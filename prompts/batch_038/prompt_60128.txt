I want a thought experiment built to spec for a Rietdijk–Putnam style argument — I'm trying to figure out how much fatalism that argument can actually buy you before it stops being legitimate, and I want a case that pushes it as hard as it goes without cheating.

Here's the setup I'm after: one person standing still on Earth, and a relay of passersby — a jogger, a cyclist, someone in a car, whatever — each moving relative to the last, and you chain thier planes of simultaneity together so each hop drags a slightly later event into "what's already real." The point of the exercise is to squeeze out the strongest fatalist conclusion you honestly can.

Constraints:
- No relativistic spaceships, no FTL, no wormholes. Every observer moves at a speed you'd see on a street, and the far-away stuff can be a galaxy or a deep-space probe, but at real ordinary astronomical distances — no made-up "a billion light years away" padding unless you justify it.
- The verdict has to be generated, not stipulated. Show the arithmetic for each hop (the vD/c² tilt), and tell me in days or years what each hop buys.
- The case has to end on one specific event that happens to the person on Earth, and you have to say flat out whether that event sits inside, outside, or on their future light cone.

Then the writeup: what the block-universe fatalist actually gets out of it, what someone who believes in an open future says back without denying special relativity, and which single feature of the setup is doing the work. Last thing — tell me what happens if I drop that feature, say by letting the relay observers move arbitrarily fast or adding more relays, and whether the argument then lands on "your decision tomorrow was already fixed," or still doesn't.
