I teach a 75-minute session in an undergraduate seminar on rational choice: eighteen students, most with no probability beyond high school, all fluent in ordinary arithmetic. The session's topic is that deciding whether a bet is worth taking and deciding how much of your bankroll to put on it are two different questions that can come apart. I want the students to run into that split rather than be told it, and I want them to reach it without ever hearing the relevant vocabulary until the end.

Design a self-contained in-class game that does this. The core device I have in mind: a repeated, favorable, even-money bet resolved by a public random device that lands in the staker's favour 60% of the time (the fairness of the device must be verifiable by the students themselves), played over a fixed number of rounds. Each student chooses their own stake as a fraction of their current tokens, secretly, every round, and can never stake more than they hold. A student who runs out is eliminated and may not re-enter or borrow. No real money is involved: tokens convert at the end into a small, cheap reward I can supply uniformly, and the conversion must be arranged so that no student's reward depends on being lucky — say explicitly how you achieve that while preserving the competitive feel.

The design must satisfy the following:

1. Every student faces the identical sequence of outcomes, so that all differences in final holdings are attributable to staking policy alone.
2. The bookkeeping has to be executable in the room by eighteen people with pen and paper. Tell me exactly how, including any tracking sheet or printed multiplier table you need, and tell me whether pairs rather than individuals are the better unit and why.
3. At some point the group is made to compute both the mean final holding across all players and the median, and to identify who is dragging the mean upward. This should be a discovery, not a remark from me.
4. A second, structurally different phase presents the same favourable bet exactly once, non-repeatably, at a fixed large stake, with no revision allowed afterwards. The point is to test whether the verdict the class just reached transfers.
5. Work the arithmetic and tell me whether the round count I suggested is sufficient for the mean and the median to separate visibly, given the staking policies students are actually likely to pick. If it is not, tell me what to change — more rounds, different odds, a different payout ratio — and what that change costs in class time.

Deliver: a minute-by-minute timeline; a facilitator script for setup that conveys the odds and the rules without telegraphing the lesson or using terms like growth rate, logarithm, or expected value until the debrief; a debrief that maps specific moments of play onto the conceptual points, states which staking policy the repeated game favoured and shows why, and distinguishes that result from the claim that the students were behaving as though they had a particular utility function; a list of what will predictably go wrong on a first run with a fix for each (I anticipate at minimum the student who stakes nothing every round and feels vindicated, and the student eliminated in round three who disengages); and a frank account of which features of the real problem this game cannot reproduce — the fact that the odds must be estimated rather than known, the fact that the relevant optimum is asymptotic rather than twenty-round, and the fact that losing tokens is not losing a livelihood — together with the sentences I should say to supply them.
