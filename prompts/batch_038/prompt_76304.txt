I spent thirty-eight years measuring things — chiefly the growth curves of microbial populations under fluctuating resource supply — and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with more seriousness than I expected of myself. Lately I have been working through the literature on the Kelly criterion and on the broader thesis that rationality ought to be understood geometrically rather than through expectations. A former student of mine, who now advises a small fund, gave a talk to a local society in which two analogies carried nearly the entire argument, and I should like them audited rather than recited back to me.

The first analogy comes from Kelly himself. His 1956 paper recast the betting problem as a problem in communications engineering: a gambler with access to a private, imperfect channel conveying advance news of the race outcome, odds quoted and fixed in advance, and a maximum attainable rate of growth of capital set by the rate at which the channel conveys information about that outcome. My former student's gloss was that betting is therefore an exercise in decoding — your edge is bandwidth — and that wagering less than the full Kelly fraction is like paying for a channel and then declining to use it: information purchased and thrown away. On that picture, under-betting is not prudence but waste, and the only serious questions concern how much information one genuinely possesses.

The second analogy is biological, and was aimed at the expected-utility camp. A bankroll compounded through a sequence of uncertain returns is like a lineage of organisms in a variable environment: what determines abundance at the end is the long-run growth rate, not the average of outcomes taken across a thousand parallel copies of that lineage, nearly all of which do not exist. To maximize expected value, he said, is to compute the mean wealth of a great many imaginary versions of oneself and treat it as a reason to accept a wager that bankrupts the single real version.

I want a genuine audit of both, not a summary. Specifically:

1. A correspondence table for each analogy. For the first: what plays the part of the channel, the noise, the bandwidth, the encoder and decoder, the quoted odds, the repeated races. For the second: what plays the lineage, the environment, the fluctuation, the abundance, the generations, and — importantly — what plays the part of death.

2. Which features of each source domain are doing real work in the argument and are simply absent from betting. I have in mind, though I want you to go well beyond these, that the communications gambler faces probabilities that are given rather than estimated, odds that are stable and unaffected by his own wagering, an information supply that is exogenous and costless, and a sequence of races that is statistically identical each time.

3. Which of the resulting disanalogies actually bear on the conclusions my student drew, and which are merely decorative. I would rather be told plainly that a mismatch is harmless than have every mismatch enumerated as though each were fatal; part of what I want is discrimination.

4. Whether each conclusion survives. Is betting below the full Kelly fraction genuinely irrational — a form of waste — once parameter estimation error, transaction costs, leverage constraints, the absorbing character of ruin, and the ordinary fact that one must also eat out of the bankroll are admitted? And does the lineage analogy establish that reasoning from expectations is a category mistake, or only that it is a poor guide in a particular regime, with the boundary of that regime left unstated?

5. A difficulty I could not resolve on my own. The lineage analogy appears to be an analogy about a population, and a population is precisely an ensemble of parallel copies, in which the extinction of any one member is survivable in a way that the ruin of a single bettor is not. Does the biological image therefore smuggle back the very averaging it was deployed against? Or does the arithmetic of multiplicative growth make the population's long-run rate and the typical single lineage's rate the same quantity, so that the objection is empty and the population framing is merely rhetorical convenience? Please work this through rather than assert either answer; I suspect the resolution turns on a distinction between what happens to the rate and what happens to the survivorship, and I should like to be shown where.

6. If neither analogy will bear the weight placed upon it, whether there is a better one for the claim that the growth rate, and not the expectation, is the quantity a sequential decision-maker should maximize — and an honest statement of what no analogy in this family can deliver. My own suspicion, formed from a working life of fitting models with error bars attached to every parameter, is that none of them speaks to the case where the probabilities are themselves the output of an uncertain estimation, and that this is the case practitioners actually inhabit.

Please argue the matter in prose as well as in the tables, and mark clearly where you are making a judgment call rather than a derivation. I am not asking for a bibliography, a literature survey, or attributions; I have the papers at hand and will supply references myself.
