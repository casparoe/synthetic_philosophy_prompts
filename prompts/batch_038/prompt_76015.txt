Ran a muscle physiology lab for thirty-some years, retired four ago, and since then I've been reading philosophy of mind — mostly through a Tuesday morning group at the county library where I'm the only person who's ever dissected anything. My question is about a pattern I keep running into and can't put a name to, and I suspect it's the sort of thing that either has a term I've never come across or has no term at all, and I'd like to know which.

Last January I cracked my tibia skiing, badly enough to need hardware. Here's the odd part: I could report the sensation perfectly. Where it was, that it was a deep throb with a sharp component on weight bearing, seven out of ten on the scale they handed me, and I'd revise it down to five by evening if they asked again. And nothing about it bothered me. Not that I was gritting my teeth — I know what that feels like from the inside, and it wasn't that. There was simply nothing to put up with, no dread of the next hour, no wish for it to be over. Then in early March the surgeon said the hardware was holding fine and I should be walking on the leg normally, and the limp was gone that same afternoon, same sensation, same numbers. Two weeks later a locum studied my films and said he thought he saw a hairline problem on the medial side, and within an hour I was back on crutches, guarding the leg, refusing to load it, planning to get a second opinion — and if you'd asked me, I'd have said the feeling was still a seven and still didn't upset me, I just wasn't taking chances. My father, who was diabetic, had something like this for years with his feet: he'd describe the burning in complete detail, would check them every night because the clinic had told him too, and I'm convinced he'd have stopped checking the day he decided the clinic was being alarmist. The protection tracks what the person believes rather than what the person feels, and it can be switched off or on by an argument, in a way that pain-driven behavior isn't supposed to be switchable.

I've read enough to know some of the neighbors. Pain asymbolia — the cases where people say the pain is there but doesn't trouble them, where the flinching and the dread have gone quiet as well, and where some accounts describe people who seem almost tickled by a pinprick and will leave a hand on something hot. Congenital insensitivity, where the signal never arrives in the first place. The old lobotomy and cingulotomy patients, who reportedly keep the sensation and lose the misery. Depression-related flattening. The people who can't find words for how bad they feel. Mine doesn't look like any of these: sensation fully intact and fully reportable, misery absent, but the guarding behavior very much present and steerable by belief. So — is there an established concept for that exact configuration? If there is, give me the term and tell me where the field draws the line between it and asymbolia, because I've seen asymbolia used loosely for something close to this. If there isn't, then coin one, and I mean a real definition with boundary conditions rather than a slogan: tell me what has to be true for a case to count, and where the edges are. At what point does it stop being this thing and start being ordinary toughing it out, or a vocabulary failure, or reverse malingering, or just a good mood? Two follow-ups, because the label by itself won't help me. Has anyone argued that what I'm describing is flatly impossible — that a sensation vivid enough to be reported as a seven carries its own motivational push along with it, so my father must have been bothered and simply didn't call it that? And if it is possible, what does it do to the idea that pain's badness lives in the feeling? It looks to me like it pries apart how pain feels from what pain makes you do, and then hands the second half over to belief, which is going to be awkward for a lot of people. Someone will surely say this is just the cognitive-evaluative component doing its everyday job — I've seen that phrase — in which case say so, but tell me why that's the whole story and not a way of not answering.
