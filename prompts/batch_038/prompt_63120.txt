I work at a small NGO that tracks official apologies and reparations programmes, so I read a lot of this stuff, and there's a pattern I keep running into that I can't find a word for. Not the bad-apology kind, where the wording is weaselly and nobody actually admits anything. I mean cases where the apology is genuinely good, and things still go a particular way afterwards.

Two instances, roughly:

One. A government apologises for a decades-old residential/boarding school system. Head of state says it, there's a ceremony, the wording names the harm properly. Within a year or two the public argument has slid onto whether the survivors' representatives are being gracious enought, whether still pressing for money is using the past, whether the apology was supposed to end it. The people who were harmed are now the ones who have to justify themselves in public.

Two. A country apologises for a famine it engineered a century and a half ago, where literally nobody on either side was alive. The apology gets treated as settling the file: after it, raising the famine in a debate about current land or migration policy draws a we-already-said-sorry response, which nobody could have got away with before the apology existed.

And the bit I find weirdest, which is what I most want a name for: in both cases the apologising state seems to end up better regarded than a comparable state that simply never did the thing. Atrocity plus apology appears to score higher than no atrocity at all — so the wrong turns into a kind of asset, with the apology as the instrument that converts it.

So — is there an established concept for this configuration, and especially for that last piece? I know it sits next to non-apology apologies and next to cheap talk, and there's something like moral licensing in psychology, but none of those fit: the words here are sincere-sounding and politically costly to say, and it's states and generations rather than individuals. What I'm after is the carving, not just a label. What are the nearest concepts in the political-apology and transitional-justice literature, how does what I've described differ from each of them, which one does the most work, and what does each one leave out? And if nothing really fits, coin something and define it properly, with the edges marked: what counts, what doesn't, what makes a case a near-miss rather than an instance. Also tell me straight if you think the third observation is an illusion becuase of how we compare cases — that would be useful too.
