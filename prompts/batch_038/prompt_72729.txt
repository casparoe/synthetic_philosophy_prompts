I lead a four-person monitoring team at a lab that ships a fine-tuned assistant to enterprise customers. Roughly what we do: before a release we validate a set of internal properties — that when the model turns down a dangerous request, a particular set of components is doing the work, and that if you knock those components out the refusal goes away — and then after every round of fine-tuning we re-run the comparison against the reference we recorded before. When the internals don't line up we raise a flag, and somebody has to decide whether the release still goes. It's ten to fifteen flags a week, most of them small; each one costs an engineer a couple of days to chase, a false positive delays a release and gets us shouted at, and a false negative means we've shipped something whose certified safety property isn't actually what's producing the behaviour. The people running the checks are ML engineers with no philosophy, and whatever you write has to be workable in an afternoon with the tools we've got — ablations, activation patching, attribution maps — not a research programme. Sign-off is mine where the answer comes out as "same mechanism, carry on", and the release committee's where it comes out as "different mechanism".

The trouble is we've no agreed answer to what the question even means. Half the team thinks two mechanisms are the same if they do the same job, in which case almost nothing we flag is a real anomaly; the other half thinks they're the same only if it's literally the same components in the same arrangement, in which case almost everything is, and the monitor is noise. My flatmate does philosophy of biology, and when I tried to explain this over dinner she said I was collapsing two different questions — whether two things descend from a common origin, and whether two things play the same causal role — and that the trouble starts when you answer one of them and announce you've answered the other. I could see that was probably right and I still couldn't turn it into anything an engineer could run. So: write the procedure. The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it terminates in "same", where in "different", where in "can't tell from what we're able to measure" and what we do at that point, and which steps are judgement calls dressed up as procedure — flag those in place rather than burying them, because the engineers will treat anything written down as a rule.

Then run it on three things we actually have. (a) Eight rounds of small fine-tunes: behaviour identical, the same components show up in the attribution maps, but the effect sizes have drifted by about a third overall and one component that contributed nothing to the reference now contributes about as much as the largest one did. No single round would have tripped anything; the cumulative gap is big. (b) We want to use our 8B model as a proxy for certifying the 70B. Both turn down the same class of requests and we can move both in the expected direction by patching, but the layers involved arent the same ones and the small model seems to route the whole thing through a component the large one doesn't use at all. Does the small-model certification buy us anything? (c) The circuit we certified as "the model discloses its own uncertainty" fires in the same components with the same effect sizes after the latest tune — every similarity number we compute says nothing has moved — except the output is now a confident assertion rather than a disclosure. Our metrics said same mechanism and the release committee wants to know why we didn't catch it. One of these three is going to come out in a way I don't like, and I'd rather find out now: tell me which, and tell me plainly where you think our current practice is just wrong. Finish with what the procedure can't settle — where "same mechanism" has no determinate answer given what we can measure — and what a team is supposed to do in that situation, since not deciding is also a decision.

Be definite about all of it. Pick which of the two questions my flatmate was gesturing at should be the primary one for safety purposes and argue for it, rather than handing me a tidy taxonomy and leaving us to choose; if you think the framing itself is a mistake, say so and tell me what to run instead. No reading list, nobody here will read one.
