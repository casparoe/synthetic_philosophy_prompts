I co-organize an interdisciplinary reading group at my university where roughly half the regulars are archaeologists and ancient historians and the other half are site-reliability engineers who describe absolutely everything as an incident. We have spent three consecutive sessions failing to make progress on the eastern Mediterranean around 1200 BCE, because the moment anyone says the word "collapse," half the room recites a five-factor environmental checklist and the other half says that what happened was a reorganization rather than a failure, and nobody can hear anything new. For our next meeting I want to open with a handout that is funny enough to loosen the room up but exact enough that the ancient historians nod rather than wince. Please write it in the voice of someone fluent in both registers at once: an SRE who has personally authored dozens of blameless postmortems and knows the template's section headings cold, and a Bronze Age specialist who has read the actual scholarly debate about societal collapse rather than the popular summaries of it.

The deliverable is a blameless postmortem, roughly 800–1,000 words, for the eastern Mediterranean system failure of the late thirteenth and early twelfth centuries BCE, and it must look like the real genre: a header block with incident ID, severity, how it was detected, duration, incident commander, affected services, and a status field; a timestamped "what happened" narrative; a "contributing factors" list; a root-cause section written as a five-whys chain that visibly fails to converge on anything; the standard "what went well / what went badly / where we got lucky" triad; an action items table with owner, priority, and due dates that are three thousand years overdue; and at least one dissenting review comment from a reviewer who objects to the framing of the entire document rather than to any particular line in it.

The substance should sit in three places. First, describe the system the way specialists describe it: a tightly coupled set of interdependent palatial and courtly economies — Mycenaean Greece, Hittite Anatolia, the Levantine port of Ugarit, Cyprus, Egypt, Babylonia — whose bronze depended on copper and tin moving long distances with no workable substitutes, so that the whole arrangement was efficient, wealthy, and brittle. Treat the destruction of individual centers as services going dark in sequence, and treat the Sea Peoples as a threat actor that no one in the document can attribute, with at least one reviewer arguing that the "actor" is better understood as a symptom of the failure than as its cause. You may allude to the famously urgent last correspondence from Ugarit, but do not put any ancient text in quotation marks anywhere in the piece: no verbatim quotations from primary sources, and if you need the gist of a letter, paraphrase it in the flat voice of an incident timeline. Second, use the root-cause section to stage the real disagreement in the field, entirely in the jargon of the form, through three reviewers. One holds that the cause was self-inflicted environmental degradation plus a checklist of aggravating factors — deforestation, drought and soil exhaustion, hostile neighbors, the loss of trading partners — and cannot resist converting the incident into a cautionary tale about choices a society made. One holds that the controlling variable was diminishing marginal returns on investment in complexity: that the palaces kept spending more on bureaucracy, specialization, storage, and monumental building for less and less net surplus, so that in several places walking away from the system was the cheaper and more rational option, and that the ticket should be re-entered as a successful cost-reduction measure. The third disputes that an incident of the kind the form requires ever occurred at all: what went offline was a thin layer of elite institutions, literacy in the palace scripts, and long-distance diplomacy, while the populations mostly stayed put, kept their languages and their gods, and in a number of places did rather better afterward. None of the three may be named, cited, or footnoted; each must be identifiable purely from what they say about blast radius, preventability, and whether the system should have degraded gracefully. Third, include a "related incidents" section with at least two further entries in the same tracking system: the Terminal Classic Maya lowlands, filed as a total outage and now considered mislabeled, since the northern Yucatán went on building while the southern centers emptied and the affected population is still present and still speaking the languages; and Rapa Nui, whose original severity rating has been revised downward twice, once because the settlement chronology turned out to be centuries shorter than the original report assumed and once because the demographic crash now reads to reviewers as post-contact disease and slave raiding rather than internal environmental suicide. Keep these in the voice of a ticketing system — severity reclassified, reporter's timeline unsupported, see linked tickets — not in the voice of a scholar correcting a colleague. At least two action items should be absurd when applied to 1200 BCE and simultaneously sound engineering advice, and at least one should be quietly devastating rather than funny.

The target of the satire is a discourse and a practice, not a person: the collapse genre's habit of compressing uneven, local, multi-generational processes into a single dated event with a moral attached, and incident-review culture's confidence that any failure resolves into causes, owners, and follow-ups. Invent the incident commander and the reviewers, and name no modern scholars and no real books. Keep the register completely straight throughout — no winking, no asides to the reader, no modern brand names, no explanatory note at the end telling us what the archaeology really says — so that a reader can reconstruct precisely what is being ironized from the document alone. Do not include a bibliography, a reference list, or in-text citations of any kind. Before you draft, spend five or six lines brainstorming which headings of a real postmortem map cleanly onto the archaeological record and which ones strain under it, since the strain is where the comedy actually lives, and then produce the document itself.
