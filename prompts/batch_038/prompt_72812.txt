Im working through the preface Jevons wrote for the first edition of The Theory of Political Economy (1871, reprinted in the 1879 second edition) and I'm stuck on one chunk of it. I'm reading a scanned copy so a couple of words came out as OCR garbage — I've flagged those in brackets but left them as they are in the scan:

"...I have attempted to treat Economy as a Calcuhis [Calculus] of Pleasure and Pain, and have sketched out, almost irrespective of previous opinions, the form which the science, as it seems to me, must ultimately take. I have long thought that as it deals throughout with quantities, it must be a mathematical science in matter if not in language. I have endeavoured to arrive at accurate quantitative notions concerning Utility, Value, Labour, Capital, &c., and I have often been surprised to find how clearly some of the most difficult notions, especially that most puzzling of notions Value, admit of mathematical analysis and expression. The Theory of Economy thus treated presents a close analogy to the science of Statikal ^lechanics [Statical Mechanics], and the Laws of Exchange are found to resemble the LaAvs [Laws] of Equilibrium of a lever as determined by the principle of virtual velocities. The nature of Wealth and Value is explained by the consideration of indefinitely small amounts..."

Could you go through this with me and then take a run at the thing Im actually hung up on?

1. Sentence by sentence first, in plain modern terms. In particular I cant parse "a mathematical science in matter if not in language" — what distinction is he drawing there, and why does he bother drawing it in a preface?

2. What work is the principle of virtual velocities doing? I know roughly what it says in mechanics, but I cant tell whether Jevons is leaning on the equilibrium part of it or on the infinitesimal-virtual-displacement part, and whether the analogy would survive if he'd just said "ordinary statics." Does the lever comparison buy him anything about exchange ratios that a plain balance-scale comparison wouldn't?

3. Here's the crux. He claims "accurate quantitative notions concerning Utility" in the same breath as saying Wealth and Value get explained by "indefinitely small amounts." Does that commit him to a cardinal, differentiable utility function, or does the machinery only ever need *ratios* of infinitesimal increments at equilibrium? My working gloss is that he's being quietly deflationary — utility-talk as bookkeeping over observable exchange ratios, an embryo of the revealed-preference move where "utility" names nothing beyond what choices disclose. A friend says that's me importing later hindsight into a passage that on its face claims the opposite, namely that pleasure and pain are genuinely quantifiable stuff. Which of us is licensed by *these sentences*, and which words decide it?

4. He lists Utility and Value as seperate items to be quantified, then singles out Value as "that most puzzling of notions," then says Value is explained by indefinitely small amounts. In this passage is Value marginal utility, or price, or a third thing? Does the text keep them apart or slide between them, and if it slides, is that a sloppiness or is the slide the whole point?

5. And one blunt question: is there any claim in here that could turn out false, or is it Victorian throat-clearing? Pick the single sentence doing the most philosophical work and tell me what would have to obtain for it to be wrong.

I don't want a reading list or a survey of what commentators have said about him — I want your reading of these sentences, and specifically where you think my deflationary gloss starts to strain against what he actually wrote.
