For thirty-nine years I worked in analytical metrology: calibration, uncertainty budgets, the whole apparatus by which one establishes that a number on a display corresponds to anything at all. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy, and I have been reading on incommensurable values and hard choices — the claim that some options can be ranked neither as better, nor as worse, nor as equally good. I confess I cannot tell what would count as an adequate theory of this, and I should like the specification before I invest another year in the literature. Not a survey of the theories: the list of requirements a theory must meet.

The purpose fixes the standard, so let me fix it. I am not concerned with aggregation across persons or with legal ranking procedures. I want a theory adequate to one agent facing a single, unrepeatable choice — an emigration, an abandoned vocation, a marriage — where there are no repeated trials from which to learn, no base rates, and no second attempt. Write the desiderata for a theory good enough for that, with the case or consideration behind each, and mark plainly which requirements are non-negotiable and which I should recognize as the local prejudices of a particular camp.

The following are the points on which I want the list to take a stand.

First, the borrowed vocabulary. In my trade two magnitudes are incommensurable when no common unit measures both exactly — the side and the diagonal of a square — yet they remain fully comparable: one is determinately longer, and their ratio is a definite number. Incommensurability there is a fact about units, not about ordering. A theory of value that adopts this word owes an account of whether it means that (options resist a common unit but are still determinately ordered) or something much stronger (no ordering obtains at all). Is that a genuine requirement, or a red herring I have carried over from the laboratory?

Second, a requirement I have not seen formulated and on which I insist. The theory must give the agent some way to distinguish a real failure of comparability from noise in her own elicited judgments. When two readings disagree, we ask whether the discrepancy exceeds the uncertainty budget before drawing any structural conclusion. A theory that declares two options on a par whenever pairwise elicitation wobbles is unfalsifiable and useless to me; a theory that dismisses the wobble as irrelevant owes an account of what would evidence parity. State this as a requirement if it is one, or explain why it cannot be.

Third, grain. Many apparent ties, and perhaps many apparent incomparabilities, may be artifacts of describing the options too coarsely. Must the theory specify how finely the respects of comparison are to be articulated before a comparison is well posed, or may it leave that open?

Fourth, location of the failure. Must the theory say whether the breakdown occurs within a single covering consideration taken alone, or only in the combining of several? If the latter, it should explain what comparing along one dimension amounts to and why that is not the whole of the problem.

Fifth, order structure. If the theory posits a fourth comparative relation alongside better, worse, and equally good, must that relation be transitive, and must better than remain transitive? Consider the familiar pattern: an exceptional item, a merely competent one, and a slightly improved competent one, where each of the last two seems on a par with the other, the small improvement never seems to close the gap, and yet the exceptional item plainly outranks the improved one. What must a theory do with such a chain, and is the small-improvement argument a requirement it must answer or a piece of machinery it may decline?

Sixth, the explanatory burden. Must it explain why such choices demand deliberation rather than a coin toss; must it explain how an agent can choose rationally without thereby making the chosen option better; must it accommodate the residue — regret, and the sense that what was forgone was not worse?

Seventh, metaphysical commitment. Must the theory say whether the indeterminacy lies in the world or in our concepts, or is that a preference rather than a requirement?

Then tell me which single requirement does the most excluding work — the one that knocks out the greatest number of otherwise attractive accounts — and identify any subset of the requirements you believe cannot be jointly satisfied, with the argument for the incompatibility. That is the part I most want to see.

Close with a paragraph each on how the main families of account fare against the list: those that reduce the phenomenon to vagueness or indeterminacy, those that posit parity as a relation in its own right, those that have the agent settle the matter by willing or committing, and any that dissolve the problem into ordinary practical reasoning. Describe each by its commitments rather than by its authors, and give me no citations, no reading lists, and no titles; I will find those myself. A table is acceptable for the requirements.
