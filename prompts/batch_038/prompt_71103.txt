I am chairing the closing session of a workshop on uncertainty in the history of economic thought, and the respondent is a philosopher-economist who has just circulated a paper on Keynes's notion of the weight of arguments. The session should engage that paper's thesis rather than Keynes in general, so please treat the following as a fixed brief and build the questions around it.

The author's position, as I read it, has three commitments. First, weight as introduced in the Treatise on Probability is not a quantity of evidence that trades off against the rational degree of belief, and it is not a measure of imprecision that could be captured by a set of probability functions in the style of Kyburg or Walley. Second, weight is better understood as the expected stability of a probability judgment under further relevant evidence: a judgment carries great weight when no evidence the agent could plausibly acquire would move it much, and little weight when it is liable to be overturned by information that is easy to come by. Third, this reconstruction is supposed to preserve what Keynes took to be distinctive about the investor's and the macroeconometrician's predicament — a defect in the resilience of the judgment itself, not the width of an interval around it.

Please draft nine to eleven questions I can put to this author in a forty-minute session, ordered so that they escalate rather than repeat. The requirements are these.

1. Coverage. Include at least one question on each of the following pressure points, and make each question specific enough that it cannot be answered by a restatement of the thesis:
   - Ramsey's charge that Keynes's account depends on an unexplained direct apprehension of probability relations, and that no extra variable of this kind shows up either in how people actually revise belief or in how belief can be measured by choice behavior. Does the author's reconstruction inherit that objection, or escape it by making weight a claim about evidence rather than about intuition?
   - The stability counterexamples. A symmetric prior about a coin is among the most stable judgments available — further evidence is not expected to move it — yet it rests on evidence Keynes would have called thin. Conversely, a judgment based on a very large but internally dependent sample, say ten thousand observations of machines calibrated by the same operator, is fragile precisely where it looks strong. Ask how the account handles both directions, and follow through on whichever concession seems likelier.
   - The redundancy worry. If weight is the expected change in a judgment under further evidence, it is itself computed out of probabilities, and so the distinct, non-numerical dimension Keynes insisted on appears to have collapsed back into the ordinary probabilistic apparatus — leaving the concept doing no work that a hierarchical model and a value-of-information calculation cannot already do.
   - The Keynes–Tinbergen dispute. Keynes objected that Tinbergen's multiple-correlation testing of business-cycle theories presupposed economic factors that were independent and additive, and relations that held uniformly across the sample period. Ask whether the author's reading explains the force of that objection, and then ask what survives of it once identification assumptions, exclusion restrictions, and graphical causal models are understood as the modern way of stating, and partly testing, exactly those presuppositions — or whether weight on this view turns out to be nothing but the analyst's trust in her own model.
   - The General Theory. Keynes treats long-run expectation as a matter on which there is no knowledge to be had, and holds that agents fall back on convention and on animal spirits rather than admit this. Ask whether an agent who recognizes that no further evidence will stabilize the judgment is facing low weight on the author's account or something categorically different, and what turns on the answer.
   - Decision guidance. If a weight judgment is not a probability, what is the decision-maker supposed to do with it? Press for an account of how two judgments with equal probability but unequal weight could ever rationalize different choices, without the difference being repackaged as a difference in probability.

2. Format. For each question, supply in one sentence the answer the author is most likely to give, and then a follow-up keyed specifically to that answer. The follow-up should be usable whichever of the two most plausible answers she gives; where you cannot do that, provide two follow-ups and say which answer each attaches to.

3. Audience questions. Two of the questions should be flagged as ones I will attribute to members of the audience: one from a Bayesian who thinks the entire Keynesian apparatus is surplus to a well-specified model plus a decision-theoretic treatment of ambiguity aversion, and one from a historian of philosophy who thinks that any reading making weight a matter of expected change in credence is inconsistent with Keynes's own insistence that probability relations hold between pairs of propositions and are apprehended rather than estimated.

4. Closing question. End with a single question asking which feature of Keynes's discussion of weight the reconstruction explains better than the imprecise-probability reading does, and what textual or conceptual finding would count against the reconstruction.

5. Constraints. No questions of the biographical or career variety, and none that merely invite the author to summarize the paper. Phrase each question for the ear, so that I can read it aloud without notes; keep each to three sentences or fewer before the follow-up. Do not ask her to cite sources or produce a bibliography, and do not assume she accepts any interpretation of Keynes other than the one described above — the questions should work even if she thinks my summary of her own paper is slightly off, since each should state the target claim it is pressing on.
