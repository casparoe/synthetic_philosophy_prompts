I serve on the steering committee of a volunteer group in a mid-sized city that is about to ask our council to consider replacing the way we elect it. I have no training in political science, but two years of these meetings have shown me something: people who all call themselves reformers disagree about things they never state out loud. One of us thinks a community that wins fifteen percent of the vote getting a seat is a triumph; another thinks the same outcome is a catastrophe guaranteeing a deadlocked chamber; neither notices they are answering different questions.

Could you construct a decision tree that sorts the people in the room rather than the electoral systems on the table? I would like roughly ten questions, each a concrete situation a committee member could react to on the spot rather than a value they could simply nod along to — what should happen when the candidate who finishes third is the only one acceptable to two rival communities, whether a five-percent threshold is a safeguard or a theft, whether a voter should be able to punish one incumbent without regard for that person's party. Every leaf should be named, should say which systems it typically points to, and should carry one sentence about the trade-off its adherents rarely mention. The tree must be able to end at a position defending what we already have, not only at reform positions. Finally, please mark which combinations of answers are logically available but apparently unheld and why, and which two leaves your questions cannot truly distinguish.
