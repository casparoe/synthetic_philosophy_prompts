Act as a philosopher of history who has actually spent time with Ranke, Carr, and Hayden White and who isn't afraid to tell me I'm confused. Think carefully. Before you give me a verdict, start by listing out every pair of my commitments below that could plausibly pull against each other — the full candidate set, even the weak ones — and only then decide which of them really do and how badly.

Context: I cover science and ideas for a magazine. Right now I'm reporting an 8,000-word history of the ulcer story — the consensus that ulcers came from stress, spicy food and too much acid, and how it got overturned by the bacteria people, Marshall and Warren, who ended up with the 2005 Nobel for it. My editor wants a short methods note to run with it, basically me saying out loud what I think I'm doing when I claim I'm telling the thing accurately. So I wrote down my commitments, and I want a consistency check before I hand it over, because I'm fairly sure some of them don't fit and I can't tell which.

1. There's a fact/framing distinction and it does real work. Whether I have the dates, the findings, and who-knew-what-when right is checkable independently of how I've chosen to tell it.
2. I can't write from nowhere. What counts as a significant fact gets picked by me, and I pick it because of what's at stake now — here, because my readers live in a world where expert advice keeps flip-flopping and they want to know whether the flip-flopping was a scandal or just what knowing looks like. I don't think that's a defect. I think it's the only way the past gets written at all.
3. Form isn't packaging. Whether I tell this as a dismissed discoverer finally vindicated, or as a field slowly correcting itself, or as a comedy of errors, does something that isn't just a different coat of paint on the same facts.
4. But when a telling goes wrong it goes wrong factually. I don't buy "that's just your narrative" as a defence. If a rival account is bad I want to be able to say it got things wrong, not that it made a taste choice I happen to dislike.
5. My framing choices don't make me a co-author of the past in anything like the way a novelist is one. I'd say I'm constrained all the way down.
6. I reject both-sides-ism in science writing — balance is not the same as objectivity — and I still want to call my piece objective. I'm pretty comfortable holding those together.
7. People who wave Ranke at me have him backwards. As I read him he was arguing against history written as a set of judgements and lessons handed down from the present, which puts him on my side of (2) rather than against it.

And then there are two verdicts I've already given out loud to two different colleagues, and I want to keep both:

A. A rival version of the same story — same dates, same findings, nothing misspoken, including the rejected paper and the self-experiment with the broth — but built as the lone genius who drank the bacteria and was vindicated against a stubborn establishment. My verdict: not wrong, just shallow and a bit cheap.

B. Another version, equally accurate on every checkable particular, but told so that the whole twentieth-century arc of gut medicine reads as inevitable progress toward the consensus we hold now. My verdict: not shallow — false. It makes the past look like it was aiming at us.

I genuinely don't know which of these I'd drop, so please don't read my ordering or my tone as a hint about what I'm most attached to. What I want is an audit, not a lecture: which pairs actually conflict, ranked by severity rather than dumped on me as an equally-bad list; which ones only look like they conflict and dissolve once you push on them; and for each real one, the cheapest revision that fixes it plus what I lose by making that revision. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most — and specifically whether any change lets me keep both A and B, or whether one of those two verdicts has to go and why it's that one.

One more thing on epistemics, and I mean this: no false confidence. Tell me what would change your mind about your ranking — what argument or consideration would make you say the package is basically fine as it stands, or that the worst tension sits somewhere other than where you've put it — and point at where your own reasoning here is most likely to break first.
