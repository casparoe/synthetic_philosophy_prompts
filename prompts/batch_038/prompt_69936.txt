I'm prepping a session on Arrow's theorem for a mixed room — a few econ grad students, a bunch of undergrads who've never seen a proof — and every worked example I can find makes independence of irrelevant alternatives look objectionable only because preferences cycle. That hands people an easy out: sure, IIA is a strange condition, but it only bites on pathological profiles, and real elections have a Condorcet winner, so who cares. I want a case that closes off that escape route.

So build me a voting scenario to these specs:

- Realistic framing, not abstract A/B/C. A five-person committee picking among three options — a search committee, a council choosing a site, a board choosing a vendor, your call.
- Exactly 5 voters, 3 options, strict rankings, no ties or abstentions.
- The pairwise majority relation is fully transitive. There is a Condorcet winner and no cycle anywhere in the profile.
- That same option also wins a straight first-past-the-post vote.
- Borda picks somebody else. State your scoring convention.
- The option you delete for the IIA check must be nobody's first choice. This is the whole point: no spoiler, no vote-splitting, no third-party canidate story. The flip has to happen with an option that not a single voter put on top.
- And the Borda winner should be nobody's last choice either.

Then show the work, don't just assert it. Give the full profile and actually count: all three pairwise contests, the plurality totals, the Borda totals. Then run the IIA check out loud — remove the relevant option, recount Borda over the surviving pair, and confirm the social ranking over that pair flipped while not one voter's ranking over the pair changed.

After the case, I want four things. Which of Arrow's conditions is Borda failing here, and is it the same failure mode as in the cycle-based textbook examples or a different one? What does a Borda defender say in reply — I suspect it has something to do with intensity of preference, but make the argument properly rather than gesturing. Which single feature of your profile is doing the work, and what happens to the verdicts if I strip it out? And how would someone who thinks IIA is fine attack the case as a piece of engineering?

One ground rule: don't rig the numbers to land where I asked. If you think these constraints can't all be satisfied at once, prove that instead of quietly dropping one — I'd much rather have the impossibility result than a fudged tally.
