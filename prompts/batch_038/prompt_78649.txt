I'm four years into a novel and I need someone to settle an argument for me — not because I want to put the argument in the book, but because I have to know which way the book is leaning before I can finish it. The setup: a midsized city retrofits every streetlight with adaptive dimming under a twelve-year energy-savings contract with a vendor. The lights come up when they recieve motion and drop to thirty percent otherwise. Beautiful for the budget. The trouble is that "otherwise" is doing a lot of work — the detection was tuned on vehicle corridors, so the places people actually stand around at eleven at night, the all-night laundromat, the bus stop with no shelter, the corner where the guys from the plant wait for the 5:15 carpool, are exactly the places the system reads as empty. Then the vendor ships a firmware update that adds a camera-based occupancy feed so the lights do come back up, and the neighborhood that complained about the dark streets is now the neighborhood complaining about the cameras. That second half is where the book lives. My partner is a municipal engineer and every time I describe this to him he says the same thing: the lights are just lights, the politics is in the procurement contract and in who bothers to show up to the council meeting, you're anthropomorphizing a firmware release. My editors note on the last draft was "careful, this is starting to feel like a thesis with characters attached." I think they're both half right and I need someone to tell me which half.

So: write me an essay on the old controversy about whether artifacts have politics. You know the terrain — Winner and the low bridges on the Long Island parkways, the claim that some technologies can only run inside a centralized authority, and then the social-shaping people who answered that whatever politics you find in a design was put there by people and can be taken back out by people. (I've got the bridge story secondhand from a college class and I think somebody later showed it doesn't hold up historically, but I may be remembering it wrong, so please don't hang anything on that anecdote.) What I want isn't a survey and isn't a lit review; it's a verdict, defended, on whether there's any sense in which politics is genuinely in the artifact rather than in the humans arranged around it. Here's the thing I'd like you to work with, and it's my own coinage as far as I know, so part of the job is telling me whether it holds water or whether I've just dressed up an ordinary idea. "Politics in the artifact" keeps splitting for me into three claims that everyone runs together. One, politics of origin: somebody's interests were baked in at design or procurement time and the object keeps cashing them out long after those people have left the room. Two, politics of requirement: the thing can only keep functioning inside a specific social arrangement — a grid, a data contract, a maintenance budget, an enforcement body — so adopting it commits you to reproducing that arrangement whether or not you meant to. Three, politics of the patch: every time the thing misbehaves and gets corrected, somebody has to decide what counts as working properly, and that decision reallocates power on the way through. My hunch, which I can't shake, is that three is the load-bearing one and that one and two are just three seen from further away — the Moses story and the nuclear-plants story only have teeth because the artifact is always being maintained, recalibrated, and defended by someone with authority over what "fixed" means. But I could just as easily have it backwards: maybe three is nothing but the ordinary politics of institutions that happens to be occurring next to a piece of hardware, in which case my novel has no spine and I'd rather find out now than in the third act.

So take the hunch seriously and then argue with it. A few things I'd like done rather than gestured at: give me the strongest version of the flat denial, not "everything is social, obviously," but an account of why a design choice feels political in a way that the placement of a park bench also does, or doesn't; tell me whether "politics" in this debate has been stretched so wide that the thesis comes out true but empty, and if it has, whether there's a narrower notion worth keeping; and say something about whether an artifact's politics can be read off the artifact at all, or whether it only becomes visible once you know who's arguing about it. Land somewhere definite. A qualified verdict is fine — something like "in the second sense, no; in the third sense, yes, and here's why the difference matters" is precisely the kind of answer I want — but no shrugging, and no resting on "well, it depends what you mean by politics" as a final position. Two thousand to two and a half thousand words, I'd guess. And please no bibliography and no citations, I'll chase down sources myself if I need to; I'd rather you spend the words on the argument than on name-dropping.
