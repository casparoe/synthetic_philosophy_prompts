I am taking a philosophy of religion course this term, and our unit on divine foreknowledge and human freedom has produced a good deal of label-swapping and very little clarity: half of my seminar section can say Molinism and open theism on demand, but could not tell you which commitment each view is giving up. For the last session I have volunteered to produce a short decision tree that sorts us by what we actually believe rather than by what we like to call ourselves.

The starting point is fixed for everyone who enters the tree. You accept (1) that God infallibly and irreversibly knows the truth about future free human actions, so that nothing can happen that He did not already know, and (2) that those actions are free in the strong libertarian sense: with the whole past, including God's beliefs, held exactly as it was, the agent could have done otherwise. You would like to keep both, and you want to be told what keeping both costs.

Please write the tree as an indented outline of six to eight branch nodes, subject to the following requirements.

1. Every node must pose a concrete case or a claim whose truth a classmate can assess without knowing where the branch leads. No node may name a position or use vocabulary that belongs to only one branch. A node might, for example, present a single situation — God knows on Tuesday that Clara will freely accept a post in Lisbon next June — or its unactualized shadow, the conditional about what Clara would have freely done had she been offered the post in Porto instead, and then ask which of two or three things the reader says about it: whether the proposition was already true on Tuesday, whether there is anything that makes it true, whether its being true is up to God, and whether God's knowing it is the reason it is true.

2. Build the nodes out of the logical space rather than out of a roster of names. Among the questions the tree must turn on are: whether propositions about future free acts have determinate truth values before the acts occur; if they do, whether their truth is grounded in something about the creature, in God's own nature, or in nothing at all; whether God's knowledge of them is prior or posterior to His decree; whether it lies within God's power to make them other than they are; and whether the necessity that threatens freedom is the necessity of the past or something weaker, together with whether unavoidability transmits across entailment. Attach a one-line gloss to each node saying which of these it is testing.

3. The leaves must include at least: Molinism proper, resting on middle knowledge; simple foreknowledge with no middle knowledge; a Boethian or timelessness leaf, but only if you think it is genuinely distinct from simple foreknowledge rather than a redescription of it; Ockhamism about soft facts; the Thomist or Báñezian physical premotion view; a theological determinism that needs no scholastic machinery; open theism; and a terminal leaf for the person who finds the grounding objection decisive but refuses to retreat either to determinism or to open theism. If you judge that one of these is not really a separate leaf, merge it and explain what collapses into what.

4. Flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot cleanly separate. I expect at least the pair of simple foreknowledge and middle knowledge, and possibly timelessness against simple foreknowledge. For each such pair, give the one further question — not already on the tree — that would most likely break the tie, or state plainly that no such question exists and that the distinction is verbal.

5. Under each leaf, add two lines. First, which part of commitment (1) or commitment (2) the position had to abandon, weaken, or reinterpret to get there. Second, the single most uncomfortable question a neighbouring leaf can put to it: for instance, the charge that knowledge of what a free agent would do has nothing there to be knowledge of, or the charge that simple foreknowledge is providentially useless because God cannot consult a knowledge that already contains His own decisions in order to make those decisions.

6. Note wherever the order of the nodes does real work — that is, where a later question is only intelligible given a particular earlier answer — and say how you have handled it.

7. Close with two or three cells of the logical space the tree leaves empty: combinations of answers to the node questions that no leaf occupies. For each, say whether nobody is standing there because the combination is incoherent, because it is unstable and slides into an occupied cell under pressure, or because it is simply unexplored. If you think one of them deserves an occupant, say what a person standing there would have to insist on.

Two constraints on presentation. The handout is for classmates who have had one logic course and no philosophy of religion, so define every term of art in a phrase at first use, and where a piece of scholastic vocabulary exists for a distinction, drop it in passing rather than recounting its history. Do not supply citations, references, or a reading list; names of positions are sufficient. Please keep the whole thing to about two pages of outline.
