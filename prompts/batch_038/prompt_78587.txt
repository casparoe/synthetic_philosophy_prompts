I am the group financial controller of a mid-sized logistics and warehousing company, and for about fourteen years most of my technical work has been about one thing: the perimeter. Which entities get consolidated, which get equity-accounted as associates, which stay off the balance sheet as unconsolidated vehicles, and what happens at the moment we lose control of one. I have never taken a philosophy course, but a friend who does warehouse automation research kept telling me I should read the extended mind paper by Clark and Chalmers, and after reading it and then reading a fair amount of commentary around it I have not been able to shake the sense that these people are having my argument in a language I do not speak. Their central move, which they call the parity principle, reads: "If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is (so we claim) part of the cognitive process." I read that and I hear a standard-setter explaining why a bright line based on legal form and majority voting power had to be replaced by a principles-based assessment of control, and why some special purpose vehicles that were structurally excluded nonetheless had to be brought onto the balance sheet. I want you to take that debate and restate it, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary I actually work in.

So please run the translation deliberately rather than by way of loose analogy. Take, one at a time: the parity principle itself; the case of the memory-impaired man and his notebook, together with the cluster of conditions that are supposed to make the notebook count as part of his mind rather than a mere tool he happens to rely on (constant availability, automatic endorsement of what he finds there, ease of retrieval, and the fact that he wrote it down in the first place because the resource had served him well); the objection from the other side that Clark and Chalmers are conflating mere coupling with constitution, and that the mark of the cognitive is drawn by the kind of process involved rather than by how tightly an external resource is linked to it; the further worry that once the trust-and-glue conditions are loosened the theory swells until almost everything counts as part of somebody's mind; and the deflationary response that the whole quarrel is about where it is useful to draw a descriptive boundary rather than about a fact we are discovering. For each, tell me what the counterpart is in my world — power over the relevant activities, exposure or rights to variable returns, and the ability to use that power to affect those returns; substantive rights as against protective rights, and the question of whether a right you cannot practically exercise is really a right at all; significant influence short of control and the equity method; joint control; the pre-2013 majority-of-voting-power bright line and what replaced it; control in substance over a vehicle whose legal form suggests otherwise; loss of control and derecognition; and the standing stipulation that the reporting entity's perimeter is drawn for the decision-usefulness of the statements, not because it carves nature at a joint. Where a piece of the philosophical position has two candidate counterparts that pull in different directions, say so and tell me which one carries more weight.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Say plainly which claims survive the translation intact, which ones only survive with distortion — and name the distortion — and which have no expression at all in consolidation language. I am particularly interested in two judgments. First, is the analogy load-bearing or merely a homonym: does accounting "control" mean anything like what these philosophers need by availability, endorsement and reliability, or am I being seduced by a shared word? Second, when you get to the things that do not translate — belief as a standing state rather than an episodic lookup, the justificatory role of a remembered item, the possibility of being wrong about what one believes, the fact that in my framework a perimeter is stipulated by a standard-setter for a purpose while these philosophers disagree about whether the mind's boundary is stipulated or found — tell me whether the failure reflects a genuine limit of my vocabulary or whether it exposes something thinner in the original debate, namely that the extended mind dispute was a perimeter convention dressed up as metaphysics all along. Keep the translation honest: flag every point where you are importing an assumption from accounting and presenting it as a result about the mind, and if my framing of the debate above is wrong or lopsided, correct it rather than working with it. End by telling me whether the accounting framework can say anything the philosophers have not said — for instance, whether the discipline of having to justify the perimeter to an auditor, and of restating comparatives when it changes, offers the extended mind debate a resource it currently lacks.
