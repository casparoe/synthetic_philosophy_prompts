I am drafting a seminar paper on Berkeley's monetary writing — the queries collected in *The Querist* — and I have constructed an argument on his behalf that now strikes me as proving far too much. I would like a diagnosis before I put it in front of anyone.

My quotations are taken from a printed edition. Because the numbering of the queries shifts between editions, I identify them by content rather than by number, and I am not asking for any bibliographic apparatus.

(1) Money has no intrinsic value; its nature is wholly functional. Berkeley asks "whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?" and "whether there be any vertue in gold or silver, other than as they set people at work, or create industry?"

(2) The function in question is the arousal and transmission of industry. Money is useful only "so far ... as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour," and Berkeley orders the dependencies explicitly: wealth is "proportioned to the industry, and this to the circulation of credit."

(3) Whatever performs that function performs the whole of money's function. He concedes as much: "whether any other means, equally conducing to excite and circulate the industry of mankind, may not be as useful as money."

(4) So the embodiment is indifferent — the credit may "be circulated or transferred by what marks or tokens soever" — and the denominations survive the disappearance of the metal: "whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?"

(5) Extend the same indifference from substance to quantity. Price is "a compounded proportion, directly as the demand, and reciprocally as the plenty." Double every ticket in a country and every price doubles with it: each wage still buys what it bought, each debt still stands at the same fraction of each income, and therefore each motive to labor is exactly as strong as before. Quantity contributes to money's function precisely what substance did — nothing.

(6) Berkeley reinforces the point by making quantity and speed interchangeable: "whether ... less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating? Or, whether, if the circulation be reciprocally as the quantity of coin, the nation can be a loser?" If any quantity can be traded off against a speed, then there is no quantity the function positively requires.

(7) Yet an addition to the stock is an addition to demand, and price varies directly with demand; rising demand for goods is just what sets people to work. So each new ticket put into circulation is a fresh spur to industry.

(8) From (5) and (6), no quantity of tickets can be excessive; from (7), any increase is beneficial. A sovereign issuing paper without limit cannot debase its money or impoverish its people. It can only fail to issue enough.

(9) That is the thesis I wanted the paper to defend: on Berkeley's ticket theory, monetary over-issue is not merely harmless but inconceivable as a category.

(10) And that is where I have lost confidence. The thesis implies that the poverty Berkeley was writing about was a failure of nerve at the press, and it implies that a claim on the industry of others grows stronger the more such claims exist. My seminar partner pressed the objection in Berkeley's own image: a counter at a card table has no worth apart from the game, but a player who can manufacture counters at will has not enriched himself — he has ended the game, since the counters no longer record anything. Every step above looks licensed by a query, and yet (9) is a claim Berkeley would certainly refuse.

Two cases compound my difficulty. In the first, a closed community of five hundred households receives an identical bundle of new tickets at every doorstep each month, the stock doubling each time; I cannot see any reason industry should change. In the second, the same tickets are issued only to those who supply the magistrate, and reach everyone else later and only through expenditure; I cannot see any reason industry should not change. I do not know whether those two intuitions can both be squared with (5) through (7), or whether one of them has to be surrendered.

So, which is the first step that fails? I would prefer to keep (1) through (4) if that is at all possible: they are the interesting part of the view and the part I want to defend, and I am considerably less attached to (8) and (9). In answering, please address the following, and rank your points by how damaging they are rather than listing them as equal worries.

(a) Whether the failure is an internal slide — a step that is valid only under a reading the earlier steps do not supply — or a genuine over-reach by Berkeley, such that he must abandon or amend one of his own doctrines to avoid it. Say which, and say what the diagnosis costs.

(b) Why the faulty step looks legitimate in Berkeley's own vocabulary, given that he is the one who declares the substance indifferent and who trades quantity off against velocity.

(c) Whether his price formula — demand directly, plenty reciprocally — is by itself sufficient to block the conclusion, or whether blocking it requires something further about confidence, about the speed at which tickets actually move, or about the order in which new tickets reach particular hands; and, if something further is required, whether it is available to a ticket theory or whether it smuggles back the "vertue" in the metal that (1) denies.

(d) How Berkeley should answer the card-table objection, if he can answer it at all.

(e) Whether the two cases I described are in fact distinguishable on his premises, and if so, what that distinction does to (7) and (8).

(f) Whether the same argument could be run against any instrumental or functional account of money, so that the flaw is a general hazard of the approach rather than a blunder peculiar to Berkeley.
