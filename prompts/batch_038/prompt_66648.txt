So I chair the professional-ethics committee at a small military academy (think ~400 officer cadets, four-year degree, most of them commissioned straight out) and I have to bring a decision paper to the academic board next month. Trying to get my head round it and I could do with someone drafting the thing so I have something concrete to react to.

The situation. Our second-year module currently teaches the broadly Walzerian line: soldiers on both sides of a war have the same moral standing, so a cadent can't be blamed merely for fighting, and what they're answerable for is how they fight — discrimination, proportionality, treatment of prisoners. Enemy combatants are moral equals, and the crime is in the conduct, not in the participation.

A cluster of faculty want to replace that with the revisionist picture — McMahan and so on. Roughly: whether you're permitted to kill depends on whether your side's war is just; a combatant fighting an unjust war does serious wrong even with spotless rules of engagement, because he's contributing to an unjust threat; and liability to be attacked tracks your responsibility for that threat rather than your uniform. Their pitch is that we're teaching a comfortable fiction and the cadets are owed the truth.

The other half of the committee make two arguments I cant wave away. One: cadets have no realistic means of adjudicating their own government's casus belli. They get a briefing, some press, and a chain of command. A doctrine that installs each individual soldier as the judge of whether his war is just seems to license precisely the selective disobedience that the rest of the curriculum spends its time arguing against. Two: the mutual-restraint story. If each side's soldiers regard the other as morally permitted to be there, that's part of what makes prisoners get taken instead of killed, and war crimes get prosecuted as crimes rather than treated as the ordinary business of the enemy. Telling cadets that the men on the other ridge are objectively wrongdoers might be correct and might also be corrosive in a way that costs lives.

There's also a compromise floating around — keep the traditional framing in year two where it does the action-guiding work, and teach the revisionist critique in year four as the deeper morality of war, on the grounds that the two levels were never meant to be the same thing. Someone will propose that on the day and I'd rather the paper got to it first.

Draft the decision paper I'll circulate: adopt, reject, or adopt with amendments. If it's amendments, be specific about what actually lands in the syllabus — which year, how many contact hours, what the assessed work looks like, whether the cadets are examined on both levels or just one. Take the losing side's strongest consideration seriously and explain why it doesn't carry the day rather than strawmanning it; the board contains people on both sides and I need the thing to survive being read by the ones who disagree with me. Give me a clear bottom line the board can vote on, not a survey of the debate. Around 800–1000 words, and please write it in British English.
