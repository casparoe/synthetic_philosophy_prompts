I run a reading group where half the people are RL/scaling engineers and half are philosophers of mind, and we've now had three sessions where the argument about whether LLMs are "agents" turned out to be four seperate arguments happening at the same time. So I want a sorting instrument: a decision tree someone can walk down and come out at a named position, ideally realizing they were talking past their opponent the whole evening.

Start from commitments pretty much anyone in the room would grant — that a model trained only on text can end up with internal states tracking a board position it was never told about; that a model's stated reason for what it just did is often confabulated; that a bare base model and the same weights wrapped in a scaffold with memory, tools and a human in the loop behave nothing alike. Then the branch nodes should be the questions that actually split the space. I want three axes to show up explicitly, either as their own questions or as coordinates you report at each leaf:

1. Locus — where the agency sits, if it sits anywhere: in the weights, in the whole loop (model + scaffold + memory + user), in the eye of whoever ascribes it, or nowhere at all.
2. Simulation vs. instantiation — is the model performing an agent the way an actor performs a character, or is it the thing that has the goals? And is "simulates an agent well" even a coherent contrast with "is an agent"?
3. Robustness — would the goals or world-model survive intervention: a paraphrased prompt, a different scaffold, a persona label, a tool it hasn't seen, a thousand extra steps.

Please build the branch questions out of at least five concrete cases rather than abstract do-you-believe-X items. Cases I'd like in there, sharpen them as you see fit: a model saying it doesn't want to be switched off while it is visibly mid-character; a model that solves a novel multi-step puzzle using tools but can't tell you why it made the move it made; a scaffolded long-horizon run that keeps pursuing a goal nobody ever specified after thousands of steps; the identical weights producing a docile assistant and a scheming antagonist depending on two lines of framing text; a model whose hidden states encode a fact its output denies.

Leaves: I want eight to twelve, each with a short label of your own coining. Don't attribute views to named people — I don't want a name-drop exercise and half the attributions would be wrong anyway. Make sure these are all reachable: a no-agency-just-token-statistics leaf; a base-model-is-a-simulator-and-the-persona-is-the-apparent-agent leaf; an agency-is-real-but-it's-a-property-of-the-whole-scaffolded-system leaf; an ascriptivist leaf on which the dispute is verbal because agency just is a stance you take; and at least one leaf for a view you think is genuinely occupied but almost never said out loud.

Then mark which cells of your three-axis space come out empty, and for each say whether it's empty because the combination is incoherent, because nobody wants the view, or because nobody has thought to defend it yet. And flag any pair of leaves your tree can't actually seperate — someone who answers every question identically but still means something different by "agent." I suspect simulator-vs-real-agent and ascriptivist-vs-flat-deflationist will be the hard ones, but tell me if I'm wrong.

It has to fit on two pages and be usable live in a room, so every question needs to be answerable in under a minute by someone who doesn't already know which leaf they're headed toward.
