For the past several years I have co-taught a graduate seminar that pairs public economists with political theorists, and one exchange in it has become so predictable that the students have begun to notice. It concerns the old incompatibility thesis, usually traced to Milton Friedman, to the effect that a country can have free movement of people or a generous welfare state, but not both. One side of the room answers with what open-borders advocates call the keyhole reply: grant the right to enter and work immediately, but decouple it from entitlement, so that new arrivals pay taxes from day one and phase into means-tested benefits over a decade or two, while the contributory social-insurance tiers are left untouched. On this reading the incompatibility is an artefact of bundling two separable policies, and once you unbundle them the fiscal objection evaporates; Bryan Caplan's version of the argument is the one the students most often bring in. The other side answers that this misses the point, because their objection was never about the balance sheet. Redistribution, they say, is sustained by a willingness to transfer resources to people you will never meet and who will never repay you, and that willingness rests on a sense of shared fate which a formally two-tiered membership is precisely designed to corrode: the keyhole regime does not preserve solidarity, it institutionalises the idea that the welfare state is a club good for a bounded set of insiders, and thereby erodes support for it among the insiders themselves. So the first side accuses the second of making a sociological claim it cannot cash out, and the second accuses the first of having answered a question nobody asked.

What frustrates me is that this is hardly a quarrel about the numbers, and the numbers are not much disputed. Everyone in the seminar knows that the sign of a migrant cohort's net fiscal contribution depends heavily on accounting conventions — whether the balance is run statically or over a lifetime horizon, whether descendants are included, how the costs of education and of non-rival public goods are allocated — and that the same underlying data can be reported as a modest net cost or a modest net gain depending on those choices. On the solidarity question, likewise, the two sides read the same contested literature in opposite directions. One points to cross-country and cross-state correlations between ethnic fractionalisation and lower trust or lower support for redistribution, and to the comparative account of why the American welfare state is thinner than the European ones, for which Alesina and Glaeser are the standard reference. The other replies that such correlations are confounded, that the American case is explained by race-specific history, the timing of enfranchisement, and labour-market structure rather than by heterogeneity as such, that within-country time-series work finds weak or null effects once controls are adequate, and that the original trust findings have not replicated cleanly. Neither side budges. I have now watched this happen three seminar cycles running, with each side claiming the other has moved the goalposts.

My working hypothesis — which I would like you to test rather than to endorse — is that the impasse is not empirical at all: the two camps are using the welfare state in different senses, as a schedule of programmes, as a fiscal balance sheet, and as a moral community held together by expectations of reciprocity, and whenever one side offers evidence in the first sense the other hears an argument in the third. Is that right? Or is the real fault line about standards of evidence, i.e. about what could possibly count as a test of the diversity–solidarity hypothesis when the experiment cannot be run and the relevant counterfactual regime of open borders plus universal benefits has never been implemented anywhere? Or is it a dispute about the correct baseline, with each side reasoning from a different reference world? Or is it, underneath, a normative disagreement about who bears the burden of proof and the risk of error when a state's justification for excluding would-be entrants is that admitting them would destabilise the institutions protecting existing members — a disagreement about the moral asymmetry between insiders and outsiders dressed up as a forecast? Please diagnose the structure of the disagreement rather than surveying the literature or adjudicating the policy: say which of these candidates is doing the most work, what turns on the choice, and what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a conceptual distinction, a better identification strategy, a change of question, or perhaps the frank conclusion that these are different questions filed under one heading, which no accumulation of evidence will merge. I would also like a direct verdict on the keyhole reply: does it genuinely dissolve the incompatibility thesis, or does it merely relocate the disagreement from fiscal arithmetic to political psychology?
