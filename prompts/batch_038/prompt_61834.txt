I am a professor of philosophy, and for three years I have chaired the ethics review panel attached to a state agency's behavioral science unit. The unit sends us roughly twenty proposals a year: defaults on benefit enrollment forms, the ordering of options on the public portal, the framing of reminder letters, and, less often, requests to add steps to a process in order to slow people down. Each proposal gets one hour. The panel consists of an agency lawyer, a statistician, two program officers, and three lay members appointed by the governor; none of them has studied philosophy, and our written reasons enter the public record and are read back to us by legislators. Depending on the program, an intervention reaches between four thousand and six hundred thousand residents, and once a default is in place it tends to stay in place for years. So whatever you produce must be applicable by a program officer and auditable by a lay member, with no technical vocabulary doing the work.

The criterion we have been using — that an intervention is permissible if it could be openly announced and would survive the objection of the people it acts on — has broken down in a way I cannot repair within our current form. Several of the proposals operate precisely because people do not attend to them. The unit's own pilots indicate that announcing the design flattens the effect in some cases and, in one, reverses it among the segment of the population that reads agency notices. The test we rely on to justify an intervention thus destroys the object it is applied to, and the panel has been papering over this by announcing its nudges generically rather than specifically. I want the procedure to confront that problem instead of assuming disclosure is costless: it should separate the reasons an intervention might fail under disclosure that carry moral weight from those that do not, and it should say what the panel does when it cannot tell which it has.

Please write the decision procedure itself: the questions in the order the panel should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the points at which it returns a flat no, and the points at which it stops and hands the matter back to a person because the question is one of values rather than facts. Mark explicitly where judgment cannot be eliminated and where you expect the procedure to misfire. State which distinctions from the literature must survive the simplification for lay use and which you would deliberately discard, and why. Include one short reflexive step: our agenda is set by the unit, proposals are taken in the order it lists them, and an item that runs out of time is carried over rather than refused — say whether and how the procedure should govern the panel's own setup.

Then run it on three proposals: (i) a default that enrolls eligible households in a utility assistance program, where opting out requires a phone call during business hours; (ii) a county clerk's request to add a confirmation step and a twenty-four-hour hold to online address changes, because of a fraud ring; and (iii) a screening-uplift program built on framing and ordering, endorsed unanimously by an elected community health board, which has asked that our public report not describe the mechanism, since describing it in the local paper killed the previous version. I expect the third to come out against my own sympathies; if it does not, tell me why I should not simply distrust the procedure. Finally, state plainly what this procedure cannot settle and must never be presented as settling.
