I'm a third-year PhD student in a criminology department and the chapter I'm writing on institutional ethics training keeps falling apart on a philosophical point I can't name properly, so maybe you can help me get it straight.

Here's the puzzle. Basically every police academy and military branch runs some version of "values" or moral-courage training — scenario drills, peer-pressure role-plays, badge-ethics courses — all aimed at building the individual officer's character so he'll be the one who speaks up when the unit goes bad. Then the same organizations turn around and engineer the situation instead: rotation policies so no unit sits in one district too long, two-officer rules, fatigue limits, anonymous reporting channels, splitting up crews that have been together a while. The first batch assumes character is trainable and is doing the explanatory work; the second assumes character is a thin veneer and the situation is doing it. That's roughly the Browning/Goldhagen fight — ordinary men caught in a machine vs. willing executioners with the right dispositions — being run by both sides at once inside one institution, and nobody seems to notice.

Can you reconstruct what each half actually presupposes about character and about situations? I want you to seperate what the practices strictly commit you to from what they merely sit comfortably with — I've got situationism in the Harman/Doris sense, Mischel-style person-situation interactionism, a virtue-theoretic picture, and a structural account all in mind and I can't tell which ones are real options here. Is the combination actually incoherent, or is there a single view that licenses both halves? And what would the character side have to give up if an institution went all-in on situational engineering — including how each view reads the handful of men who took Trapp's offer to step out at Józefów, since the training literature treats thier refusal as proof character matters while Browning treats it as proof of how overwhelming the situation was. Argument, not a literature review, please.
