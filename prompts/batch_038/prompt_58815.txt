Context: I'm running a reading group on norms of assertion. I built the argument below to show that the truth norm beats the knowledge norm, and it did the job. The problem is it did the job too well and I can no longer live with it. Notation: TN = "assert that p only if p is true", KN = "assert that p only if you know that p".

1. For any practice, the norm governing it is fixed by the practice's point: the norm is the rule such that complying with it is what gets you the point. A rule that isn't needed to get the point is not a norm of the practice, however useful it may otherwise be.
2. The point of assertion is to put true belief into circulatoin — to get your hearers to believe the truth.
3. If p is true, asserting p puts a true belief into circulation in anyone who takes your word for it; if p is false, it puts a false one into circulation. And that is all there is to say about how assertion serves truth.
4. So TN is the norm of assertion: complying with TN is exactly what gets assertion's point, violating it is exactly what misses it.
5. Call a rule N *surplus* relative to a practice P just in case N forbids some performances the point-fixed rule permits and permits none that it forbids. A surplus rule can be prudent, or a decent heuristic for agents who can't tell whether they're complying with the real norm, but it is not a further norm of P, because it adds nothing to getting P's point.
6. KN is surplus relative to assertion: knowledge entails truth, so KN permits no assertion that TN forbids, while forbidding plenty that TN permits (the true lottery proposition, the Gettiered stopped clock, the barn you happened to look at).
7. So KN is not a norm of assertion. It's a heuristic for people who can't see the truth directly; the normativity lives entirely in TN.
8. The same reasoning runs unchanged for every practice whose point is truth: belief, testimony, news reporting, scientific publication. In each case the truth rule is the point-fixed rule and any knowledge requirement is surplus, hence heuristic.
9. So "know" never marks a normative difference in a truth-directed practice: whoever hits the truth has complied, however they hit it, and whoever misses it has violated the norm, however carefully they checked.
10. That can't be right. It puts the Gettiered assertor and the knower on identical normative footing; it says the juror who convicts on a true belief formed from inadmissible hearsay has satisfied the norms of her practice; it says the doctor prescribing on a lucky true hunch has done nothing worse than the one who ran the tests; and it drains the content out of the thought that knowledge is worth more than mere true belief.
11. So something in 1–8 is false.
