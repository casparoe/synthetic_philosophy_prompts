I sit on the advisory council of our national statistics office. Next month the council votes on what will serve as the country's headline indicator of wellbeing in a new annual dashboard, and one member has tabled a motion that I need to respond to in writing. The motion has three parts: (1) adopt a single headline measure — a survey question asking respondents to rate their life as a whole on a scale from 0, the worst possible life for them, to 10, the best; (2) publish our national score each year next to the scores and ranks of other countries in the best-known international league table built on that same kind of question; and (3) adopt an explicit government target of reaching the top fifteen within ten years, on the stated grounds that only a target gives the various ministries something to coordinate around.

The council is divided, and the arguments at our last meeting ran roughly as follows.

The mover's case is that a dashboard of forty indicators with no ranking will be ignored, that a single number is what makes wellbeing legible to a finance ministry, and that the usual worry about targeting a metric simply does not apply here, because the measure is a survey of people's own experience: a government cannot itself administer the answers, so there is nothing to game.

A colleague replied that the choice of instrument largely determines who tops any such table. Life-evaluation scores track income, employment and education very closely, whereas measures built from yesterday's experiences — whether the respondent enjoyed the day, and whether they felt worry, sadness or anger — rank a number of considerably poorer countries near the top. Choosing the ladder and then setting a rank target, on her view, commits the government in advance to a growth-and-employment programme wearing a wellbeing label, without that choice ever being argued openly.

Another member's objection was about the comparison itself: national samples are small, the anchor of the scale does not translate cleanly between languages and cultures, since what counts as the best possible life differs by place, and in past editions some countries' entries have been filled in by estimation rather than by survey. Publishing a rank, he said, asserts a precision we cannot deliver and will be quoted back at us as though it were a measurement.

A fourth made what several of us found the most uncomfortable point: even a survey measure can be steered without falsifying anything. The sampling frame can drift, the wording or preamble can shift, and above all the composition of the population can change — if the least satisfied residents emigrate or otherwise leave the frame, the national average improves even though no one's life has. Under a rank target, that outcome counts as success.

One member objects to the entire enterprise, holding that it is not the state's business to instrument citizens' inner lives, and that a target invites every department to justify its budget by its contribution to a psychological score. Another wants distribution rather than an average at all: she proposes that the headline be the share of the population in the bottom two steps of the scale, on the ground that a country can raise its mean while its worst-off stay where they are. On adaptation, the council is going in circles — one member says reported satisfaction adapts to hardship and so both instruments are unreliable; another says adaptation is precisely why yesterday's experiences should be trusted over a ladder that imports people's aspirations.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the meeting. It must end in a recommendation the council can act on — adopt the motion, reject it, or adopt it in a modified form — and if it is a modified form, the modifications must be specific enough to be minuted: which measure or measures serve as the headline, exactly what may be published internationally and in what form, whether any target is admissible and if so of what kind, and what is to be reviewed after the first three years rather than left to drift. It should treat the mover's strongest considerations — legibility, coordination, and the claim that a survey of experience is ungameable — as serious arguments to be answered rather than dismissed. Finally, and importantly for something I am putting my name to: mark clearly which of the objections above are settled points among people who work on wellbeing measurement and which remain genuinely contested, and do not let a disputed position be presented as consensus.
