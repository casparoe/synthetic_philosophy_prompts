Ok so, I write science fiction, and I'm about four drafts deep in a novel about a colony that's been cut off from Earth for three hundred years. The clear liquid they drink, swim in and farm with behaves exactly like water to them — same taste, same rivers, same rain — but its a different compound, not H2O, and nobody alive there has ever seen Earth. My one academic friend read the ms and left a note in the margin of chapter two that just says, in his terrible handwriting, "meanings just ain't in the head" and underneath it "Twin Earth, look it up". He calls it the Putnam thing. My agent has since picked the line up and now repeats it at dinner parties to explain why the colonists' word 'water' is about the local stuff even though they'd be psychologically identical to us if you scanned them.

Here's my problem: I have to decide whether the book leans on that line or undercuts it, and I honestly can't tell what the line claims. There's a scene I love where a colonist who spent six years on Earth as a kid comes home and tells everyone, quite gently, that they've been wrong about what water is for three centuries, and his family treats it as a breakdown. And there's a wrinkle in my own setup that I don't think the slogan covers: the first two generations drank recycled H2O brought from Earth and only switched to the local stuff when the tanks failed, so there's an unbroken causal chain running back to the real thing. Does the word's meaning follow the chain or the current environment, and does it matter that the switch happened gradualy over decades instead of overnight?

Can you break that one sentence into the separate theses it could be standing for? I want the whole menu, not your favourite reading.

Please use these sections, in this order:

1. **What the thought experiment actually shows** — about 120 words. Just enough that I could explain it to my agent without embarrassing either of us. No history of philosophy, no names-and-dates preamble.

2. **The menu** — this is the bulk, 800–1000 words. Number the readings and give each one a short handle. For each: state it precisely, then tag it as a platitude, substantive but defensible, contentious, or flat false, with one or two sentences on why. Please include at least a version about reference rather than sense, a version about whether any such thing as narrow content survives, a version that hangs on the division of linguistic labour my friend keeps mentioning, a version about what a word's extension is fixed by, and the strong one where meaning is out in the environment the way a natural kind is.

3. **Which one my friend means** — 150 words. Which reading is he most likely invoking at dinner parties, and which one would he actually need for it to be true that the colonists' 'water' is about the local compound? Say if those are different readings, because I suspect they are.

4. **The slow switch** — 250 words. My three-hundred-year setup with the recycled tanks. Take the readings from the menu in turn and say what each implies about reference changing over time, and whether a gradual drift gives a different answer than an instant swap.

5. **The scene** — 200 words, and be blunt. Does any defensible reading entitle the Earth-returnee to be right and his family to be wrong? If not, what could I change about the setup so that it does — or should I write him as genuinely confused and let that be the point of the book?

6. **Where the slogan backfires** — 150 words on the costs of externalism a novelist should know before betting a whole novel on it. Self-knowledge especially: can my character know what he means by 'water' without doing chemistry, and if not, what does that do to first-person narration?

No bibliography or reading list please, I have no way to chase it and I don't want to pretend I will.
