I hold a version of the modernist thesis in the study of nationalism, and I would like a systematic account of what would count against it.

The position, stated as precisely as I can manage it: nations in the politically significant sense — bounded, territorially anchored communities whose members will mostly never meet one another, which understand themselves as sovereign and as the proper object of their members' primary political loyalty — are modern. They depend on a package of conditions that only became generally available in the last few centuries: a standardized vernacular carried by print, mass literacy, a bureaucratic administration reaching ordinary subjects, and forms of social and occupational mobility that make a homogeneous, anonymous culture advantageous. What earlier societies had instead were ethnic cores and elite cultural communities: dynastic realms, religious communities, urban and regional solidarities, and small literate strata sharing a high culture. I take the invented-tradition component more modestly: I do not claim that national symbols, myths, and rituals are fabricated from nothing, but that they are refunctioned and standardized in ways that leave the resulting national culture discontinuous with the older material it appropriates.

What I want is a map of this view's exposure, not a survey of the debate. Please address the following.

1. Decisive evidence. What would a pre-1500 case have to look like in order to refute the thesis rather than merely embarrass it? Set out the criteria such evidence would need to meet — social spread of the identification well beyond a literate elite; self-designation by the community's own members in their own terms rather than by outsiders or by learned writers; maintenance of a boundary against named others; territorial fixity; a claim to collective self-rule rather than loyalty to a dynasty or a faith; and the willingness of ordinary people to die for the unit — and say which of these criteria are carrying the real weight and which could be dropped without changing the verdict. Where the criteria interact (for instance, whether mass spread is even possible without print), say so explicitly rather than treating them as an independent checklist.

2. Counter-evidence actually pressed. Take in turn: the case for early English nationhood built on vernacular biblical religion and prolonged conflict with a foreign enemy; the argument that the medieval western kingdoms were genuine communities of the realm with collective identities and solidarities, not mere aggregates of personal lordship and dynastic right; continuity claims from Armenia, from Ethiopia, and from the long-running civilized-versus-barbarian distinction in China; the Jewish case as a nation without a state and without continuous territory; and the ethnosymbolist middle position, on which nations are modern but rest on persistent pre-modern ethnic cores whose myths, memories, and symbols supply the raw material. For each, classify it as decisive, awkward, or already absorbed. Where a modernist absorbs it by redescription — insisting that what the evidence displays is an ethnie, or an elite culture, or a confessional community, rather than a nation — say whether that move is principled or a definitional retreat.

3. A challenge to the terms of the dispute itself. There is an objection, distinct from any of the above, that the entire controversy equivocates: that in medieval Latin usage a natio was a learned category denoting people born in a given place, deployed in university and conciliar settings, and that the modern political nation is a different kind of thing altogether, so that disputes about when nations began are disputes about word usage wearing the costume of empirical disagreement. Assess how damaging this is to my position. Does it undermine the thesis, rescue it, or leave it untouched — and does it change what kind of evidence could count for or against it?

4. Conceptual collapse. Is my thesis true by stipulation? If nation is defined by criteria that only a modern, print-mediated, mass-literate, bureaucratically administered state could satisfy, then no pre-modern discovery could contradict it, and the thesis becomes an analytic proposition dressed as an empirical one. Note the asymmetry in how I actually reason: a pre-modern case satisfying my criteria would be taken by me to refute the thesis, while a pre-modern case failing them is taken to confirm it. Determine whether that is a genuine double standard or a legitimate asymmetry of burden, and identify what would break it — some observation on which I am committed in advance to giving ground, of a kind that has not yet occurred.

5. Predictive content. What does the thesis predict that its rivals — perennialism and ethnosymbolism — do not, particularly regarding sequence (does nationalism follow industrialization and mass literacy, or precede them?), regarding whether print is necessary or merely accelerative for national consciousness, and regarding which communities become nations and which remain ethnic cores? Flag any of these predictions that the record has arguably already falsified, including nationalisms that arose before the enabling conditions and cases where standardized print failed to produce a nation.

6. Insulation. Give me your honest judgment on whether the view as I have stated it has been qualified to the point where it risks nothing. Historical-sociological theses are not laboratory hypotheses, and a strict falsificationist yardstick may be the wrong instrument here; if so, say what the right standard is and how my thesis fares against it. A conclusion that the view is well insulated, and that this is or is not a defect, is a perfectly acceptable outcome.

Finally, rank the defeaters you have identified from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether a committed defender would concede that it counts against the view or dismiss it. I want the concessions, not only the criticisms. For calibration: the challenge I personally find hardest to wave away is the millennium-scale case — a named community persisting with continuity of self-designation, territorial attachment, and a sense of shared descent — because that kind of continuity seems to do explanatory work that a wholly modern account must explain away rather than explain.
