I cover science and ideas for a weekly, which in practice means I end up writing about responsibility — who's on the hook when a big system goes wrong — and I've spent the last fortnight down a First World War rabbit hole for a piece, reading around the July Crisis and the old Fischer business, where a German historian argued in the early sixties that Berlin had willed the war rather than slid into it. The reason I'm asking you is that my own gut verdicts on 1914 don't add up, and I want to know whether there's a principle sitting underneath them or whether I'm just a hypocrite with a deadline.

Here's what I actually think, case by case:

1. Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia, drafted in the knowledge it would be refused: culpable, no argument.
2. Germany's assurance of support to Vienna on 5 July, given before anyone in Berlin knew what Austria would demand: *more* culpable than the ultimatum, even though it killed nobody directly and could be described as simple loyalty to an ally.
3. France giving Russia the same species of reassurance at the St Petersburg summit that month, with the president and prime minister then unreachable on a slow boat home for several days: identical act-type to 2, but I blame them distinctly less, and I can't tell you why.
4. Russia's general mobilisation on 30 July, before Austrian shells had landed anywhere: roughly as culpable as 2, which I know cuts against the recieved wisdom.
5. Serbia: elements of its military intelligence backed the assassins, and Belgrade balked at the one ultimatum clause about foreign officials joining the investigation. Culpable for the murder. Not culpable for the war. That split feels stable to me but I can't ground it.
6. Britain: not culpable for starting it, culpable for staying ambiguous about whether it would fight. A failure to prevent rather than a failure to cause.
7. The German general staff insisting the mobilisation timetables left no room to stop: this excuses the individual officers in my gut but doesn't excuse the state that commissioned the plan. Asymmetric, and I don't know if it's coherent.
8. Bethmann Hollweg spending the last week of July pressing Vienna to accept a mediation formula and halt short — a man trying to cancel a cheque he'd already signed: I give him some credit but barely reduce the blame.

My tentative principle, which a colleague supplied and I've been trying on: blame tracks whoever had the cheapest exit at the moment they acted, with cost measured against their own stated aims rather than ours. The trouble is it seems to invert my list. It piles the most onto 6 and 8 — the peacemakers, who had cheap exits and good information — and it lets 2 off lightly, since walking away from Vienna was expensive in Berlin's own terms. So either the test needs an extra clause, or 2 and 4 aren't really comparable, or one of my verdicts is just sentiment.

What I'd like: state the principle or principles that best capture 1–8, precisely, and say which case motivates each clause. Tell me which sense of responsibility it's tracking — causal contribution, moral blame, political accountability, or the legal liability sense that the Versailles reparations article was doing when it made Germany accept responsibility for the loss and damage without ever using the word guilt. Say whether it's retrospective or prospective, and whether it survives being applied to agents who aren't people: does the same machinery handle the jingo press, and Serbia's distinction in 5, and — this is my real interest — a state that arms and finances a belligerant today without firing a shot, or a firm that hands a partner open-ended cover and later claims it disapproved of what they did with it. Finally, be blunt about which of my verdicts it forces me to drop. Please don't send me a bibliography; my editor has one and I can't check any of it. I want the reasoning.
