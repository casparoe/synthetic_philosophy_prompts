I am a junior and the copy editor at my high school newspaper. Six of us produce roughly twenty stories a month, none of us has studied philosophy, and our faculty advisor approves everything before it goes to print. Two years ago, after a story about a homework chatbot, the paper adopted a flat house rule: no mental-state verbs for software, ever. In practice the rule has made the writing worse rather than better. Reporters route around it with mechanical paraphrases that are just as tendentious — a recent piece said our tutoring pilot "produced a response consistent with a refusal," which tells a reader nothing while still implying there was a refusal somewhere — and readers keep writing in that the paper is pretending the software is inert when plainly it is not, since it does different things depending on what you say to it.

I would like to replace the ban with something a sixteen-year-old can actually apply in ten minutes. Please write a decision procedure, grounded in the intentional stance — the view that a system counts as having beliefs and desires insofar as treating it that way is the best available method of predicting what it will do, and that this is a level of description rather than a claim about what is inside — that tells an editor, sentence by sentence, whether a mental-state verb may stand as printed, must be hedged, or must be rewritten. I want the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and tests we can run without looking anything up: whether substituting a design-level or physical-level description for the verb changes what the sentence claims; whether the attribution lets us predict behavior in situations the reporter never described, or only redescribes the one incident in front of us; and whether the "want" or "belief" in question belongs to the system, to the people who built it, to the prompt someone typed that morning, or to the distribution of text it was trained on. Where the procedure stops with a yes, where it stops with a no, and where it hands the sentence to the advisor because the question is about values rather than accuracy, should all be marked.

Then run it on three sentences from this year's issues:

1. "The tutoring bot knows when a student is about to give up, and it changes its tone before they do."
2. "Our moderation filter doesn't understand context, so it flagged the entire news article about the book challenge."
3. "The rover wanted to reach the far checkpoint, so it abandoned the shorter route."

For each, give the verdict, a rewritten sentence if it fails, and the shortest gloss we could print as a footnote if we keep it. Please include at least one case where the procedure tells me something I will not like: I expect sentence three to survive, and I suspect it will not. Be explicit about which steps quietly assume that a successful intentional-stance prediction licenses saying the system really has beliefs, since that assumption is precisely what critics of the whole approach reject; if a step only works on one side of that dispute, say so and give us the alternative.

Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot settle — where the choice is really about how we want readers to relate to these systems rather than about accuracy — and provide a one-page version, in words, that we could tape inside the newsroom cabinet, plus a short list of verbs by default treatment with a one-line reason for each. Say which distinctions from the theory you had to drop to make this usable by people without it, and where the procedure will predictably misfire.
