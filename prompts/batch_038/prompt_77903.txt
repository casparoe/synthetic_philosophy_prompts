I've spent most of this year on a tiny volunteer working group attached to a local EA-ish society, and we're supposed to be deciding whether to shift a chunk of our budget toward insect welfare — farmed black soldier fly larvae and crickets-for-feed mostly, plus some field stuff. What's actually happened is that weve spent eight months arguing about brain size and gotten nowhere, and I want to understand why before our next meeting because I think the argument is broken in some structural way rather than just unresolved.

Here are the two camps as concretely as I can state them. The scalers say neuron count is the only proxy that actually scales across taxa, that it tracks behavioural flexibility, that a honeybee has on the order of a million neurons and a mouse something like ten to the seventh, and that anyone spending money on insects while ignoring pigs is being sentimental; on their view size isn't magic but it's the best available lever, and they point to UK law as evidence that when the evidence gets strong enough the circle does widen — cephalopods and decapod crustaceans got folded into the sentience act there after a commissioned review, which they take to show the method works when it works. The other side — call them the objectors — say brain size is a vertebrate-shaped ruler, that an octopus has most of its neurons outside its head entirely so the brain-to-mind inference is already broken by one of the very animals everyone grants sentience to, that the behavioural assays (crab shock-avoidance learning, the bee judgement-bias stuff, whether a reflex counts as evidence of anything) were designed around mammalian templates and then used to grade everybody else, and that what matters morally is whether there's valenced experience in there, not how much computational hardware. Both sides have read the same papers. Both accept the same neuron numbers. Both say the empirical evidence about insects is genuinely weak and genuinely uncertain. Nobody has moved an inch, and every meeting we replay the same exchange where a scaler says "so you'd give a nematode moral status" and an objector says "so you'd deny it to an octopus arm."

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and probably half-wrong: the dispute isn't about the facts at all, and maybe not really about insects. I think "morally relevant" is doing at least three different jobs and each side keeps grabbing a different one — relevant as evidence (a marker that should raise or lower your credence that a creature is sentient), relevant as ground (a property that itself makes experience matter), and relevant to weight (how much a creature's suffering counts once you've granted it counts at all). My guess is the scalers are answering the first question under uncertainty and the objectors keep hearing the second or third. But there are other candidates I can't rule out: that it's really a hidden disagreement about decision procedure — what you're allowed to do when the metaphysics won't resolve, precautionary inclusion versus waiting for better assays; that it's a verbal dispute over "sentience" itself, threshold versus graded; or that it's bedrock, two intuitions about whether nervous systems come in kinds or in degrees that neither side can argue for and that all the crab and bee data is just being recruited by. So: diagnose it. Which of these is it, and can you tell from what each side actually says when they argue rather than from what they claim to be arguing about? Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a distinction that dissolves it, a specific kind of empirical result that would bear on it (what assay would actually separate nociceptive withdrawal from valenced experience, and would a finding about bee-like judgement bias settle anything at all?), a change of question, or an honest argument that no progress should be expected and why. Please don't survey the literature or recommend me reading, I've read the usual suspects and that's not what I'm asking for — I want the structure of the disagreement, and an answer to the practical question of whether our group should keep arguing about brain size or drop the marker entirely and fight about something else instead.
