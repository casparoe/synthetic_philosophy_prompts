---

For Nyāya a pramāṇa is the instrument or means by which we get pramā, and pramā is a cognition that gets things right — an accurate apprehension of an object, as opposed to error, doubt, or fancy. So the pramāṇa is not the knowing itself but the means of the knowing, which is why the list of pramāṇas is really a list of the distinct ways a mind can hook onto the world. There are four: perception, which arises from contact of a sense organ with its object and is non-erroneous and, in its basic form, non-conceptual; inference; upamāna or comparison; and śabda, the word of a reliable speaker.

On upamāna, the standard example. A man who has never left the village is told by a forester that a gavaya — a wild ox — resembles a cow. Later he goes into the forest and sees an animal that looks like a cow. He thinks: this must be the gavaya the forester mentioned, this is what that word picks out. Nyāya says this cognition is not inference, because he isn't reasoning from a mark to something unobserved the way you reason from smoke to fire — he's acquiring the reference of a word through a perceived similarity. And it isn't just śabda either, because the forester's sentence on its own produced nothing; it sat there inert until the perception of the resembling animal came along and completed it. What upamāna delivers is a distinctive cognition of the relation between a word and its referent, and no other pramāṇa gives you that.

On inference: the Nyāya argument has five members where Aristotle has three. There is fire on the hill (thesis); because there is smoke (reason); wherever there is smoke there is fire, as in a kitchen (example); and this hill is just such a place, smoking (application); therefore there is fire on the hill (conclusion). Basically it's Aristotle's syllogism with two extra sentences inserted, and the extra sentences do no logical work — the example is included to make the thing vivid for an audience and to reassure everyone that the general rule is grounded in something you could go and look at, but the inference itself is carried by the reason alone, exactly as the middle term carries it in Aristotle. The structure is the same formal structure, so what counts as valid in one counts as valid in the other. What really matters is vyāpti, pervasion: smoke never turns up without fire. Once you have that, the conclusion follows, and the members are just the argument spelled out slowly.

For the other schools: the Cārvāka materialists accept perception only and reject the rest; the Buddhists accept perception and inference and refuse to treat testimony or comparison as separate sources; Sāṃkhya and Yoga add śabda to those two, for three; and the Mīmāṃsakas and the Advaita Vedāntins go up to six by adding postulation (arthāpatti) and non-apprehension (anupalabdhi). Nyāya thinks this is overgeneration. Postulation is just inference with extra steps. And the case that's supposed to motivate anupalabdhi — knowing that there is no pot on the floor right now — doesn't need a new instrument either, because absence isn't the sort of thing there is to be grasped. Absence isn't an object out in the world the way a pot is; it's just my not finding one, so "there's no pot here" is really an inference from my failure to perceive it. Nyāya's four is the parsimonious list, and the six-pramāṇa schools are multiplying instruments to cover cognitions the existing four already handle.
