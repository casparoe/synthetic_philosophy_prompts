I'm auditing a philosophy of religion seminar and this week we're on Molinism, and I've come away more confused then when I started. I think I've got the basic architecture: God's natural knowledge of everything necessary and of every possible world, then middle knowledge of the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom — the ones about what any free creature would do in any set of circumstances, which are true before God decides anything — and then free knowledge, which is postvolitional and tracks the world he actually decreed. What I can't get straight is the grounding objection, because different people in the seminar seem to be running two different objections under that one name and nobody's noticed.

One version sounds like a metaphysics-of-truth complaint: there just isn't anything that makes a counterfactual of creaturely freedom true — no state of affairs, no actual choice by anyone, not even God's will, since by hypothesis these are true before he wills anything. The other version sounds like a complaint about say-so: even if we waive the demand for a truthmaker entirely, the fact that it was already true, before any creature existed and before God settled on a world, that Alice would freely turn down the offer in Lyon, means nobody ever had any say in it, not Alice and not God, so it looks like a chunk of brute fate sitting in the middle of the logical order. Are those actually distinct objections or is one just the other dressed up? I want a careful account of where they come apart and which of the standard Molinist moves answers which one. My hunch — this is the bit I want pressure-tested — is that the Molinist has real resources against the first, since you can insist that plenty of contingent truths aren't *made* true by anything and that truth doesn't require a ground, but that those resources do literally nothing against the second, because the second isn't asking what makes it true, its asking why anyone's freedom is compatible with the answer having been fixed all along. Tell me if I've got that backwards.

Two more things I want folded in. First, the asymmetry everyone leans on: middle knowledge is prevolitional and free knowledge is postvolitional, and that's supposed to be why the objection bites on one and not the other. But what about counterfactuals concerning God's own free acts — what God himself would freely do in circumstances he never actually creates? If those are true as well, and true prior to any decree, then either they're prevolitional in exactly the same way, in which case the objection looks like it generalizes to God's own freedom and the simple-foreknowledge view ends up in the same boat as the Molinist, or there's a genuine asymmetry, and I'd like to know what it could consist in and whether it ends up helping or hurting. Second, strong versus weak actualization: I keep seeing that distinction invoked in replies and I can't tell whether it's doing substantive work against the grounding objection or just restating it — that God can strongly actualize only the circumstances and can merely weakly actualize the whole world, because which worlds are even feasible for him is already pinned down by counterfactuals he had no hand in. If you have room, also say briefly why a Thomist would think the whole dispute is a pseudo-problem, on the grounds that God knows what creatures would do by knowing his own power and how he would move them, and what the Molinist's worry is that this either collapses into determinism or fails to yield knowledge of genuinely free counterfactuals. Please run it all through one concrete worked example rather than talking in schemata, and skip the reading list — I just want the concepts made clear.
