I'm a 4th-year psych PhD student and our department runs a small speaker-series podcast. The guest in two weeks is a vision scientist whose lab has spent about a decade arguing that what you expect changes not just what you say you see but what you actually see — that expectations get into early vision itself. I got volunteered to interview her because my own project is on category learning. I have half an hour.

Her signature paradigm (she'll definitely walk us through it herself, so I want questions that go underneath it): people are trained on two invented categories of squiggly closed shapes, "kema" and "noro," that differ only in some subtle thing about where the curvature is greatest. Then they're shown an ambiguous probe sitting exactly halfway between trained exemplars, and instead of a rating or a verbal label they turn a dial to adjust a comparison shape until it "looks just as kema-ish" as the probe — a pure matching response, often under a tight deadline. Trained participants reliably set the dial further toward their trained category than untrained controls do. Her line is that a matching response under deadline can't be strategic or demand-driven, so the category knowledge has literally altered the appearance.

I don't want to just let her re-run the pitch. Write me about nine interview questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give — and say explicitly what you expect that answer to be, so I'm not caught flat-footed. Make them press on the philosophy, not only the method:

- at least one that takes seriously the worry that everything she's showing could still be response bias or demand characteristics even in the dial version, and that has somewhere left to go *after* she gives her standard reply about deadlines and unforced measures
- one on whether her effect is really about current expectation at all, or about long-run retuning from hundreds of training trials — how does she tell apart a belief-driven online effect from something more like perceptual learning or adaptation, and does the distinction even matter for the claim she wants to make
- one that makes her commit to which part of the visual system she thinks is being modulated, given that people who reject her conclusion are usually perfectly happy to grant that attention and decision stages are top-down
- one on the circularity worry: if what you believe can change what you see, then seeing stops being independent evidence for the belief. Does she accept that consequence, and if not, where exactly does she block it
- one that sets her against the stubborn illusion cases — where you know perfectly well the two lines are the same length and they still look unequal — and asks why knowledge penetrates in her paradigm but not there
- one about the motivated-perception stuff: hungry people judging food images as larger, ambiguous figures being seen as the animal you'd rather see, hills looking steeper when you're carrying a heavy pack. Keep them short and non-leading enough that she could genuinely answer them. My supervisor will be sitting in and will absolutely notice if I've stacked the deck.
