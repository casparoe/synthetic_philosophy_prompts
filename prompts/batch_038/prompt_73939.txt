I'm co-organizing a five-day residential workshop on probability and its conceptual foundations — about 18 people, roughly half philosophers, the rest statisticians and a couple of cosmologists who keep ending up in fights about measures. We have funding for one "central thread" that runs through the whole week (a shared problem we all work on together each morning, with the afternoons free), and I've been arguing with a co-organizer that the thread should be built around Bertrand's paradox and the principle of indifference. Not the undergrad version where you derive 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 for the three chord recipes and then shrug — I mean the genuinely unresolved stuff underneath it. Assume you're a philosopher of probability who has actually had to argue about priors with working statisticians and physicists, not just with other philosophers, and think carefully before answering.

Here's the candidate list I've scribbled so far. (a) Whether choosing *which partition* to be indifferent over is a separate normative problem from indifference *within* a partition — I think it is, and that most of the literature quietly collapses the two. (b) Whether Jaynes-style invariance arguments (scale/translation invariance, maximum ignorance) actually terminate the problem or just relocate it into a prior over which symmetries are the relevant ones; a friend of mine insists every proposed resolution of Bertrand's paradox ends up as a prior over priors and hence regresses. (c) Whether the "arbitrary functions" line descended from Boltzmann — the idea that a physical mechanism washes out initial-detail dependence — can deliver *epistemic* conclusions, or only facts about frequencies in the world. (d) Whether the paradox really generalizes to the measure problem in cosmology and to prior selection in applied Bayesian work, or whether those are just loose analogies that make everyone feel clever. (e) Whether indifference should simply be replaced by decision-theoretic or accuracy-driven constraints (Dutch books, expected accuracy), and whether that substitution makes a single one of Bertrand's cases come out determinate. (f) The blunt version: is "random" in the original puzzle a request for information about a chance mechanism, and if so is there anything left that's specifically a problem about *indifference*?

So: pick the thread and defend the ordering. Which one or two of these — or something I've missed — is most upstream, in the sense that the most other questions on the list change shape depending on how it's answered? Which are tractable in five days by a mixed audience that isn't going to do new mathematics? Which would actually alter what people do downstream (choosing priors in a real analysis, arguing about cosmological measures, thinking about reference classes in self-locating problems) versus which would only rearrange the philosophy literature? I'd also like you to tell me what to cut: is there a session I'm obviously tempted to schedule that would just be a waste of a morning? And name one crux where people in this area tacitly disagree but almost never say so out loud. Brainstorm a bit first if that helps, but the deliverable should be a ranked agenda with the criteria and trade-offs made explicit, plus what evidence or argument over the next few years would move things up or down my ranking. Please don't give me a bibliography or a reading list — I can hunt down sources myself — and skip the standard retelling of the three chord methods, we all know it.
