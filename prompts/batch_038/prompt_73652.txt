I head the archives at a mid-sized city's municipal records repository, and in six weeks I present a policy proposal to our Archives Advisory Committee that will have to be approved before it goes into the operating budget. I would rather find out in advance where it breaks. The proposal has four parts. First, an annual published documentation audit: we name, by creator class, the constituencies whose records we have systematically failed to acquire — in our case the dissolved tenant associations, the two transit and sanitation locals that merged out of existence in the 1990s, the neighborhoods cleared for the interstate, and the city's own seasonal contract labor pool — and state what we intend to do about each. Second, appraisal as a contestable public act: every decision not to accession a body of records gets a written rationale that is retained, published, and open to formal challenge by an affected party within two years. Third, parallel description: where a body of records has been described by our staff and also by a community organization that holds copies or maintains its own account of the material, we keep both descriptions live and side by side, including where they contradict each other on dates, personal names, and significance, rather than reconciling them into a single finding aid. Fourth, we acquire born-digital material from dissolving organizations in its existing file order, without rearrangement, accessioned together with an explicit statement of what we already know we cannot reconstruct about its original context.

The committee is emphatically not an audience of archival theorists. The chair is a retired county clerk who thinks in retention schedules and in what an auditor will say in ten years. Two members are practicing government records archivists, thoroughly Jenkinsonian about the archivist's impartiality, who will hear part two as the repository taking sides and part three as a violation of provenance and original order. One is a law professor who works on municipal liability and public records law. One is a labor historian who has used our collections for three decades and will resist anything that disturbs the citation practice and the stable finding aids her books were built on. One is a community-organizer appointee who should be my natural ally but has different priorities: she wants the money currently earmarked for the processing backlog spent on digitization and public programming, not on audits, and she is skeptical that a state-funded repository should be absorbing community-held material at all. The city attorney's office sends a silent observer who speaks only if something looks legally risky.

Here is my argument in brief, for you to attack: I am not claiming the archive is neutral but imperfect, and I am not claiming that description is a narrative. I am claiming that silence enters at four distinguishable points — the creation of a record, its assembly into an archive, its retrieval, and the significance assigned to it later — and that a repository controls only the middle two, which is why the proposal says nothing about interpretation and instead instruments assembly and retrieval. The audit is not an apology; it is a control that makes the default visible so that inaction becomes a recorded decision rather than an absence of one. Appraisal rationales are nearly free because we already write them internally and simply throw them away. Parallel description is a claim about the epistemic status of a finding aid: it is an argument, and an argument that conceals itself as a fact is worse than two arguments displayed together.

Draft my prep sheet. I want the eight or so objections this committee will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the proposal at the vote rather than by how interesting it is, and phrased in their vocabulary — they will reach for provenance, original order, retention schedules, evidential versus informational value, chain of custody, the repository's credibility as a source, and the exposure created by holding contested material, not for discourse, hegemony, or the archive-as-ideology. For each objection give a two-minute reply I can deliver standing up without notes, a candid statement of where that reply is softest, and whether something should be conceded up front to disarm it. Then tell me which single reply has to land for the proposal to survive, and which question I should hope nobody asks because I do not have an answer yet — I suspect it concerns third-party privacy and consent exposure in material acquired from organizations that no longer exist to give permission, and what happens under parallel description when two authoritative descriptions disagree about something potentially defamatory. Finally, flag any vulnerability I have not thought of, including anything the attorney's observer would notice and anything the organizer appointee could reasonably call a land grab dressed up as reparative work.
