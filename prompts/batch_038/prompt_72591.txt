I have been asked to submit a short statement of my theoretical commitments for an interdisciplinary workshop on language and thought, and a colleague has volunteered to attack it. Before I send it, I want an audit rather than a defence of the field: which of these commitments pull against one another, which pairs only look as though they do, and what the cheapest repair would be in each case.

Here is the package.

(A) **Anti-determinism.** Language does not set limits on what its speakers can think. Whatever can be thought in one language can, with enough effort and paraphrase, be thought in any other; translation may be costly, but it does not fail in principle, and I see no coherent content in the idea of incommensurable conceptual schemes.

(B) **Real effects.** There are nonetheless reliable effects of a speaker's language on performance in tasks that involve no language at all. People whose language obliges them to encode cardinal directions rather than left and right are better at remembering the orientation of an array after being turned round; speakers whose language draws a colour boundary at a different point in the spectrum discriminate across that boundary faster than within it; speakers of languages with grammatical gender attribute gendered properties to inanimate objects when asked to characterise them.

(C) **Where the effects live.** The best explanation of (B) is that language bears on cognition only at the point of formulation — the view Slobin called "thinking for speaking" — and not through any lasting alteration of the conceptual or perceptual systems themselves. Grammar obliges a speaker to attend to certain distinctions when preparing to talk, and there the story ends.

(D) **The interference evidence.** What convinces me of (C) is that effects of this kind are reduced or eliminated when participants carry a concurrent verbal load, such as repeating a syllable, while surviving a matched visual-interference control. That strikes me as direct evidence that covert verbal coding, rather than restructured perception, is doing the work.

(E) **Untranslatable words.** I also hold that so-called untranslatable words pick out genuine distinctions in experience — real kinds of thing, not merely convenient labels — which speakers of a language lacking the word cannot readily have or attend to. This is why I think such words are worth importing into English rather than paraphrasing away, and why I resist the advice to use them only as decorative flourishes.

(F) **Practice, not grammar.** Where a cognitive difference between communities does turn up, my instinct is to explain it by differences in environment, upbringing and habitual practice rather than by differences in grammar, on the grounds that human conceptual structure is largely universal and embodied, and that what one community learns from its surroundings another can learn from its own.

(G) **Modesty.** I tell readers that these effects are real but small, and that grand claims about entire worldviews being carved out by grammar are unfounded.

Two constraints on how I want this handled. First, assume I mean to keep as much of the package as possible: a revision that dissolves a tension by deleting a commitment outright does not count as cheap unless you can show that nothing less will do.
