Reading a pop-rationality book that does a real number on the Condorcet jury theorem. Roughly how the passage goes:

"Condorcet proved in 1785 that groups are smarter than their members. As long as each voter is more likely to be right than wrong and votes independently of the others, the chance that the majority gets it right keeps climbing as you add voters, and heads toward certainty. That's why twelve ordinary jurors will beat one brilliant expert, and a hundred will beat twelve. The objection everyone raises is that real voters aren't independent — same news, same culture, they copy each other in the hallway — and Condorcet knew it, which is why he conceded the theorem was a piece of pure math with no application to actual politics. True, and useless."

The opening sentences felt right to me and the ending felt too neat, but i cant say where the line is. Can you go through it claim by claim — what a political theorist or a statistician would sign off on, what's a defensible simplification, and what's just wrong? Three things I especially want checked:

1. Does the theorem actually license "twelve ordinary jurors beat one brilliant expert", or is that sneaking in an assumption about how good the jurors are that the theorem doesn't give you?

2. A friend who works on social choice says there's a second half of Condorcet's result that popular summaries leave out, something about how much confidence an already-observed verdict deserves rather than how big to build your jury. Is he making that up?

3. The book's last chapter uses all this to argue you should majority-vote the answers of five different LLMs instead of asking one. Once you've fixed up the theorem, does anything survive that move?
