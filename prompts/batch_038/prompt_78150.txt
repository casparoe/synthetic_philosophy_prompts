I'm in a philosophy of mind seminar on mental content and over the weekend I built an argument against teleosemantics that now strikes me as way to good to be right, which is exactly why I don't trust it. Background so this is answerable: we did two weeks on the disjunction problem. The frog's fly-detector fires at flies but also at BBs and dark moving specks, so on a straight covariation account what it means is something like small-dark-moving-thing, or fly-or-BB, and the frog therefore never misrepresents — which is supposed to be fatal, since a theory of content that can't make room for error isn't a theory of content. Teleosemantics is the standard fix: bring in selection history, and the disjunction gets cut by what the mechanism was shaped to do rather than by what it currently tracks. Here is my argument that the fix generalizes the disease instead of curing it.

(1) On teleosemantics, what a state is about is settled by the selection history that produced and maintains it — by what the state was selected to do, not by what it happens to covary with now.
(2) But selection doesn't select representations. It selects organisms and lineages on overall reproductive success; internal states are retained only insofar as they contribute to that.
(3) So a state's content is fixed only at the granularity at which its contributing made a fitness difference.
(4) For any real organism, a great many candidate contents are fitness-equivalent: the frog's state can be specified as tracking flies, as tracking small dark moving things, as tracking protein, as tracking edible-flying-insect-or-BB, and every one of those specifications cashes out in identical behavior and identical offspring counts over the species' whole history.
(5) Where selection history is indifferent between candidate contents, there is no fact of the matter which content the state has.
(6) So no actual biological state has determinate content — not the frog's, and not mine, since my FLY, WATER, and even GRANNY concepts each admit indefinitely many fitness-equivalent rival specifications.
(7) Therefore teleosemantics fails to naturalize content, and fails in the paradigm human cases, not just in the exotic frog case it was designed to handle.

What makes me nervous is that I want (7) to be false and I can't find a premise to drop. I'm attached to (2) in particular; I've read enough population genetics to not want to say selection literally targets representations, and (2) also seems to be the premise that makes the argument respectable — it's not leaning on swampman intuitions or on any thought experiment, it's just an inference from how selection actually works. My seminar partner's reply was that (1) is a strawman: on the consumer-semantics version, content is fixed by what the downstream systems that use the state need it to be about in order to perform their functions, so the tie in (4) gets broken by looking at the consumers rather than at contributions to fitness. I don't think that helps, and I have a dilemma for it, but I'm worried the dilemma is cheap. Either the consumers are themselves representational states, in which case they need contents of their own and we regress; or the consumers' needs are just cashed out in fitness terms, in which case we're back at (4) with extra steps and the indifference is exactly as total as before.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd like the diagnosis to be specific about why the rejected step looked acceptable in the first place, because I wrote it down and it looked fine to me at the time. Part of what I'm after is whether (4) to (5) is a real inference or a slide — my partner thinks I'm just flinching at a valid step, and I can't decide whether he's right, so argue it rather than taking my word for either side. Then I want you to price the alternatives: what does a defender of (2) who rejects the argument have to say about content granularity, and what does someone who gives up (2) owe us? Finally, say what the best version of the consumer-semantics reply actually looks like, because if it does break the tie in (4) I need to know which horn of my dilemma it escapes and what it pays for the escape — I have a term paper due on this and I'd rather the paper be about the surviving objection than about a dead argument.
