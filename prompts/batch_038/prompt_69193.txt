I would like you to take on the role of a philosopher of aesthetics who also has a genuine working command of mathematical epidemiology, and to do something for me that I suspect has never been done carefully — which is to say, please do not look for a template, and please do not assume I want the flattering version. First, who is asking. Before I turned to fiction full time I spent eight years modeling infectious disease: compartmental models, force-of-infection estimates from serosurveys, arguments about next-generation matrices, and a good deal of code nobody outside my department ever read. That vocabulary is still the one I think in, and I am fluent in it as a practitioner rather than a tourist — I do not need the basic reproduction number explained to me, and I will notice if you use it loosely. In the past year, working on a novel about a family that cannot stop performing its own grief, I have been reading Tolstoy's late treatise on art, the one in which he argues that a work is art only insofar as it infects its audience with the feeling the artist actually experienced; that the degree of infection is the measure of its success *as art*, graded by the particularity of the feeling transmitted, its clarity, and above all the artist's sincerity; and that the separate question of whether an artwork is *good* turns on whether the feeling it transmits unites people in accord with the religious consciousness of the age. I read this as a live argument rather than a period piece, and I cannot stop hearing it in my old language.

The task is this: restate Tolstoy's theory of art, as faithfully as you can, in the vocabulary of transmission dynamics. Not as a cute analogy paragraph — as a systematic translation, term by term, claim by claim. Work through at least the following, in whatever order makes the exposition clearest, and be explicit every time you have to stretch a concept to make it fit. (1) The artist as index case: Tolstoy insists the artist must himself have felt the feeling, and separately condemns work produced to order or for money as not art at all; but in my framework a pathogen assembled in a laboratory infects exactly as well as one that spilled over from a reservoir, and provenance simply does not enter the transmission equation. Where, if anywhere, does the model have a slot for provenance, and if it does not, what work is Tolstoy's sincerity condition doing that transmission dynamics cannot represent? (2) The Shakespeare problem: Tolstoy holds that Shakespeare is universally admired yet has no infectious power, and explains the admiration as a kind of mass suggestion maintained by an educated elite. In my terms this reads as a surveillance artifact — reported cases wildly out of proportion to seroprevalence, or the reverse — and I want you to decide which, and to say whether Tolstoy's criterion can even distinguish having been infected from having been recorded as infected. (3) The recovered compartment: does a second reading count as a second infection, or does his account treat communion as a discrete, non-repeatable event? If habituation has no place in his theory, tell me so rather than inventing a compartment he never posited. (4) Institutions as vectors: he treats criticism, the universities, and aristocratic patronage as corrupting influences; in my framework these look like superspreading settings that amplify transmission independently of the pathogen's intrinsic transmissibility, which makes them value-neutral rather than corrupting. Is that faithful to his point, or does it quietly replace his normative claim with my descriptive one? (5) The missing sign: in epidemiology, transmissibility carries no valence — a high R0 is neither a virtue nor a vice, it is a parameter — whereas Tolstoy treats infectiveness as constitutive of arthood and then thinks sincerity ties infectiveness to the worthiness of the feeling. Show me whether that tie has any counterpart in my framework at all, or whether it is the principal casualty of the translation.

Then give me the residue report, and please treat it as the main event rather than the coda — I would rather have two pages of residue than four pages of elegant mapping. Sort the claims into three piles: those that translate cleanly, those that translate only with distortion I should be able to see you committing, and those that have no expression whatsoever in transmission dynamics. For the third pile, take a position on each item: is the failure a genuine limitation of my vocabulary, which is built to describe populations of things that have no intentions and no experiences and therefore cannot represent what it is like to be moved; or does the failure suggest that Tolstoy's criterion was less determinate than its confident prose implies, doing evaluative work under a descriptive label? I specifically want you to be honest about where you are tempted to smuggle my framework's assumptions into his theory and present the result as translation — for instance, importing the idea that infection is measurable by third parties, or that a population-level parameter can be true of a single encounter between one reader and one book. Think carefully before you start drafting: spend your first two or three hundred words brainstorming candidate mappings and explicitly discarding the ones that merely sound apt, so I can see what you rejected and why. Use headings for the three piles. No bibliography and no citations, please — I am not asking you to reconstruct sources, and a plausible-looking page number is worse than none.
