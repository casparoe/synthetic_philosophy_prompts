I've been sitting in on a departmental reading group on human origins this semester — I'm actually in philosophy, not anthropology, and I'm mostly there for the free pizza and because I like arguments about evidence — and we've spent basically a month going in circles on whether the agricultural revolution was a mistake. It's genuinely stalled, and stalled in a way I find interesting rather than just annoying. One camp runs the Diamond line plus the older Sahlins material: adult stature drops sharply in the skeletal sequences, teeth get worse, markers of nutritional stress and infection go up, infectious disease takes off once you have sedentism plus domesticates plus dense settlements, hereditary inequality shows up in the burial record, and foragers supposedly worked something like three to five hours a day while early farmers worked harder for a worse diet. The other camp says nearly every step there compares against the wrong thing: the foragers we have ethnographic time-allocation data for were pushed into marginal environments by farming neighbours, so using them as a baseline inflates the case; the paleopathology leans on a handfull of sites and heavily on North American maize populations where the decline is dramatic but hardly typical; "hours worked" changes meaning depending on whether you count food processing, toolmaking, childcare and hauling water; and the demographic accounts say nobody chose farming in the first place, that population pressure and shrinking wild abundance made it close to inevitable, so calling it a mistake is a category error.

Here's the part that puzzles me: I dont think the two sides disagree about most of the raw findings. Everyone in the group concedes that mean stature falls in several well-documented sequences, that enamel defects and periosteal lesions increase, that infant mortality goes up, that life expectancy at birth drops, that grave goods and status differentiation appear in the record. They just refuse to reach the same conclusion, and six weeks of this hasn't moved anybody an inch. My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis, is that the disagreement isn't empirical at all — it's about the counterfactual baseline (compared to what: the Pleistocene foragers we have no data on, or the marginal modern ones we do?), about the unit that's supposed to be evaluated (individual welfare within a generation, lineage survival, or cities and writing and modern medicine as downstream goods), and about whether "mistake" even has content if the transition was demographically driven rather than chosen. There's a fourth thing I can't articulate either, something about whether a fall in average wellbeing that comes bundled with a huge rise in total population and eventually with everything we currently value counts as bad. Is that diagnosis right, or is there still a live empirical crux doing the work that I'm mistaking for a conceptual one?

What I want is the diagnosis, not a verdict on whether agriculture was a mistake and not a tour of the literature. Ground it in what each side actually says: where the two camps are answering different questions under one heading, where they're using the same word (affluence, health, cost, progress) to mean different things, where a better dataset could genuinely settle something, and where it bottoms out in starting intuitions neither side can argue for. Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a distinction that has to be drawn, a kind of evidence that would actually move things, a reframing of the question, or a case for why no progress should be expected. Format: exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no introduction and no conclusion — one on what kind of disagreement this really is, one on which parts are merely verbal, one on whether a real empirical crux survives, one on where it bottoms out in unarguable bedrock, and one on what would count as progress. I want to paste it into my notes and argue with it next week.
