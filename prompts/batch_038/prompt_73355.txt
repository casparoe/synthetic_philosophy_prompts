Six weeks ago I was sworn in as one of three citizen members of my state's Initiative Review Commission, the five-person body that decides whether a filed petition may appear on the ballot. Our enabling statute is deliberately thin: it authorizes us to reject a petition only if it is "defective in form" or "not within the scope of the initiative power," and it defines neither phrase. For my first three votes I relied on instinct and on whatever the deputy attorney general said in the meeting. A petition filed last week has persuaded me that this is untenable, and I want to understand the record I am now helping to make before I vote on it.

Our decisions over the past four cycles, with the majority's stated grounds:

1. A statutory initiative pairing a half-cent sales tax increase earmarked for bicycle and pedestrian infrastructure with a mandate that elementary schools include bicycle-safety instruction. Certified, 4–1. Single subject satisfied: every provision was germane to one general purpose, even though revenue and school curriculum are otherwise separately regulated.

2. A nonbinding question asking whether voters favored siting a federal spent-fuel repository in the state. Rejected, 5–0. The initiative power reaches only enactments; a measure that changes no law is outside it whatever its subject.

3. A constitutional amendment barring state courts from awarding punitive damages against manufacturers of devices cleared by federal regulators, and transferring civil-procedure rulemaking from the supreme court to the legislature. Rejected, 3–2, as a revision of the frame of government rather than an amendment; revisions must originate with the legislature or a convention.

4. A statute requiring photo identification at the polls with no state-funded identification program. Objectors submitted evidence that the burden would fall disproportionately on poor, elderly, and minority voters. Certified, 3–2: we do not assess a statute's constitutional soundness, because a statute remains open to judicial review before and after enactment, and preempting that review is a greater harm than the risk of an unlawful measure reaching the ballot.

5. A constitutional amendment that declared persons without lawful immigration status permanently ineligible for any state-funded scholarship or tuition assistance, repealed three named statutory programs, and barred the legislature from appropriating for any program serving that population in the future. Rejected, 3–2 — but the three votes rested on two rationales. Two members said the measure permanently disabled a core legislative power and was therefore a revision, not an amendment. One said it put the state in plain conflict with a federal statute conditioning education aid on nondiscriminatory access. Both dissenters said the commission has no substantive authority at all and would have certified.

6. A statute imposing marine fuel standards on vessels in state waters stricter than the international standards incorporated into federal law. Certified, 3–2: whether a state may exceed an international standard is a contested preemption question, and we act on superior-law grounds only where the conflict is plain on the face of the measure.

7. A statute creating a dedicated transit fund and providing that the earmark "may not be amended, suspended, or repealed except by a two-thirds vote of each house." Certified, 3–2: an ordinary statute cannot bind a succeeding legislature, the supermajority clause is therefore inoperative, and we do not reject a petition on account of a provision that will have no legal effect.

The new petition is statutory. It would prohibit the department of health from contracting with, or reimbursing, any provider that performs or refers for a specified lawful medical service; it states that the prohibition "is not subject to amendment, waiver, suspension, or repeal by any subsequent legislature, executive order, or agency rule"; and it directs the department to recover from providers all amounts disbursed for that service during the preceding three fiscal years, effective immediately upon enactment. Objectors argue that the affected providers are overwhelmingly clinics serving low-income and recently arrived patients, so the measure is class-targeted in substance; the sponsor answers that it classifies provider conduct rather than patient identity and that any disparate effect is incidental. Our counsel advises that the recovery provision may conflict with a federal grant condition requiring notice and an opportunity to contest before recoupment, but describes the question as genuinely unsettled, with authority running both ways. No single-subject objection has been filed: all three provisions concern the department's purchasing.

Here is what I need from you.

First, identify the principles that are jointly consistent with precedents 1 through 7. For each, say which of the seven it explains cleanly, which it strains or must explain away, and what it implies for the new petition. I suspect one candidate principle — that a measure is disqualified because it falls on an identifiable disadvantaged class — is already foreclosed by the contrast between 4 and 5; tell me if I have that wrong.

Second, tell me how I should vote if my aim is consistency with the commission's own record rather than with my views on the merits. Name the closest analog among the seven and explain why it governs rather than the second-closest. In particular: does the reasoning in 7, about inoperative entrenching language, extend to a measure whose retroactive recovery provision is fully operative no matter what becomes of the entrenching clause — or is that the point at which 7 stops being the analogy that controls?

Third, if the record genuinely underdetermines this, say so plainly and then state what principle the commission would be committing itself to under each possible vote: what we would be obliged to say about the next petition of the same shape, and what we would forfeit the ability to say later.

Fourth, describe one further petition — hypothetical but concrete, with its tier, its operative provisions, and the objections it would draw — whose filing would reveal which principle we have in fact been following, because the surviving readings would have to diverge on it.

I am not looking for citations, case names, or borrowed doctrine from other jurisdictions; I cannot verify them and neither can the other two citizen members. I want the reasoning about our own record, in a form I could hand them before Thursday's hearing.
