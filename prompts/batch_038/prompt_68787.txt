Here's the bit (ellipses are mine):

"But if any one will consider, he will (I guess) find, that the great advancement and certainty of real knowledge which men arrived to in these sciences, was not owing to the influence of these principles, nor derived from any peculiar advantage they received from two or three general maxims, laid down in the beginning; but from the clear, distinct, complete ideas their thoughts were employed about, and the relation of equality and excess so clear between some of them, that they had an intuitive knowledge, and by that a way to discover it in others; and this without the help of those maxims. For I ask, Is it not possible for a young lad to know that his whole body is bigger than his little finger, but by virtue of this axiom, that the whole is bigger than a part; nor be assured of it, till he has learned that maxim? Or cannot a country wench know that, having received a shilling from one that owes her three, and a shilling also from another that owes her three, the remaining debts in each of their hands are equal? ... I desire any one to consider, from what has been elsewhere said, which is known first and clearest by most people, the particular instance, or the general rule; and which it is that gives life and birth to the other. These general rules are but the comparing our more general and abstract ideas, which are the workmanship of the mind, made, and names given to them for the easier dispatch in its reasonings, and drawing into comprehensive terms and short rules its various and multiplied observations. But knowledge began in the mind, and was founded on particulars; though afterwards, perhaps, no notice was taken thereof: it being natural for the mind (forward still to enlarge its knowledge) most attentively to lay up those general notions, and make the proper use of them, which is to disburden the memory of the cumbersome load of particulars."

Four things I need from you:

1. What is "but" doing in the young-lad sentence — "Is it not possible for a young lad to know that his whole body is bigger than his little finger, but by virtue of this axiom"? Read fast I get something close to the opposite of what his argument needs, so walk me through the intended force of the question and the follow-on "nor be assured of it, till he has learned that maxim."

2. Is "which is known first and clearest by most people, the particular instance, or the general rule; and which it is that gives life and birth to the other" one question or two? Does "gives life and birth to" pick out epistemic priority, psychological genesis, or something else entirely — and does Locke answer it, or just leave it rhetorical? Also whether the "by most people" hedge is doing real work or is just politeness.

3. Parse "These general rules are but the comparing our more general and abstract ideas, which are the workmanship of the mind, made, and names given to them for the easier dispatch in its reasonings." The "made" looks like it's hanging off nothing, and I can't tell whether "which" refers to the ideas or to the comparing. Give me the parse you'd defend plus a plain-English rendering.

4. The real fight. My co-presenter's reading: the country wench is doing analogical transfer — she carries one relational pattern from the first debtor to the second without ever possessing the axiom — so Locke is inadvertently on Hofstadter and Sander's side, with abstractions as ex post compressions of concrete cases, and "disburden the memory of the cumbersome load of particulars" is basically a chunking claim. She adds that his talk of "the relation of equality and excess" between the ideas shows he already thinks relations rather than surface features carry the reasoning, which she says is straight structure-mapping. My objection: for Locke the maxim is a finished artifact that then sits in the head and gets mistaken for a foundation, whereas on the modern view there's no stored abstraction at all — the category is re-forged on the fly by each new analogical encounter, and the particulars never get discharged from memory, they keep driving the thing. Which reading is better? Is there a version of my objection that survives hers, and what's the strongest reply a Lockean could make — ideally one grounded in this passage rather than in his system generally.
