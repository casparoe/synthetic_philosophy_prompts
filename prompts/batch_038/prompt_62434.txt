I run fraud detection at a fintech and I've gotten myself into a corner that kind of makes my whole job look incoherent. Our model spits out P(fraud) per transaction and we flag anything above 0.9. A guy on my team built this argument against it and I can't find the exit:

1. If it's rational to believe p and rational to believe q, it's rational to believe p-and-q. Otherwise you're just cherry-picking which of your own beliefs you're allowed to combine.
2. A transaction sitting at P(fraud) = 0.95 is one it's rational to believe is fraud — that's the entire point of having a threshold instead of acting on the raw number.
3. We flag about 1000 transactions a day, so by (1) and (2) it's rational to believe each is fraud, hence rational to believe all 1000 are fraud.
4. But we've validated on held-out data: at the 0.9 cutoff roughly 8% of flags turn out to be legit customers. So in a batch of 1000 there are ~80 false positives, and it's rational to believe at least one of my flags is wrong.
5. (3) and (4) can't both be rationally believed. Contradiction.
6. And the threshold was arbitary anyway — go to 0.99 or 0.999 and (4) still bites once the batch is big enough. Only a cutoff of exactly 1.0 escapes it, and then you never flag anything.
7. So no threshold rule is coherent. Which would mean every calibrated probabilistic model in production is epistemically broken, and also juries and doctors.

(6) into (7) is clearly proving way too much, but I can't locate the leak. Which step do I reject, why did it look airtight, and what does each available fix cost? Obviously I'd rather keep flagging transactions than swallow (7), but I want to know what I'm actually giving up.
