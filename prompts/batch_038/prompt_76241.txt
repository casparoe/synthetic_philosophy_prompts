i want a poem about the Boltzmann brain problem, but built around a formal trick that does the arguing for me instead of the poem explaining itself.

shape: six stanzas, three or four lines each, so call it 20-28 lines, no rhyme scheme required. the constraint lives in the closing line of each stanza — stanza 2's closing line must be stanza 1's closing line with exactly one word deleted, stanza 3's must be stanza 2's with one more deleted, and so on down the line, so the refrain visibly thins out as the poem goes. start stanza 1 with something like eight or nine words so there's room to lose five. every intermediate closing line should still be grammatical English, not word salad, and ideally the words you drop are the load-bearing ones, the ones anchoring the speaker to a past or to other people, so the deletion reads as loss rather than as trimming. then the sixth stanza breaks the rule: its closing line adds words back — and this matters — not by restoring the original line, but by going somewhere new with it.

why that shape: the thinning is the fluctuation coming apart, a momentary brain running on borrowed memories. the regrowth is the actual argument, which is that a stretch of experience that stays mutually consistent and keeps letting you predict the next moment is not what a lone thermal fluctuation looks like, so the right response to "you're probably a Boltzmann brain" isnt skepticism about your own mind but rejection of the cosmology that made brains common in the first place. i want that to land as a reductio on models with an endless de Sitter-ish far future, not as a shrug of cosmic horror. the form should earn it; the last stanza is allowed to be fairly direct but the first five shouldn't read like premises in an argument.

voice and content: second person, addressed to someone who's just been told they are statistically almost certainly a disembodied brain. be concrete about the asymmetry that drives the whole thing — one brain is a vastly smaller fluctuation than an entire ordered cosmos with a 13-billion-year history and galaxies in it, so if the vacuum just sits there forever, the freak observers swamp the ordinary ones by an embarrassing margin. that comparison is the engine and the poem should make you feel it, not just assert it. don't name any physicists or papers, don't cite anything, and don't let it slide into a lecture or a numbered argument. if a specialist reads it they should be able to tell you actually understand the problem and its standard resolution; if someone who's never heard of it reads it, it should still be a good poem about being told you don't exist.
