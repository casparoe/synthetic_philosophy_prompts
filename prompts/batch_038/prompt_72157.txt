I co-teach an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar and it's my turn to set the take-home for the week on the timing of experience — the Dennett and Kinsbourne reading, Orwellian vs Stalinesque revision, the colour-phi business. 90 minutes, one question, 25 marks.

Last year's version was basically "explain the distinction between the two kinds of revision and assess it," and it was useless as an assessment: everyone reproduced the labels and sketched the little diagrams, and about half of them ended up saying something like "the brain fills in the missing bit afterwards and then you remember the filled-in version" — which is precisely the view the paper is attacking. Nobody touched the part I actually care about, which is that if the two stories can never come apart in verdicts, what is the distinction doing: is that a claim about consciousness or a claim about evidence?

So I want a question that can't be answered by narrating the phi experiment. Anchor it in a concrete proposal — an experiment a partisan of one of the two accounts would offer as decisive, something where the subject's verdict is forced early enough that there's supposedly no time to rewrite the record, or a variant like that. The student should have to (a) say what the proposal is meant to establish and why, (b) give the best multiple-drafts reply to it, and (c) say whether that reply actually dissolves the problem or just relocates it. You can invent the setup, but keep it physically and pscyhologically realistic and give real parameters — interstimulus interval, what response is required, when the response window opens and closes — so there's something to reason about rather than a thought experiment in a vacuum.

Then write the model answer, around 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 split into five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, plus one line per point on how students typically botch it. I want the A/B boundary to turn on whether they can say what the indeterminacy claim commits you to, not on how many technical terms they get right. And if the question turns out to have a defensible answer on both sides, say so in the marking scheme rather than pretending one verdict is correct.
