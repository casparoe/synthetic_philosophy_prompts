Last night a friend and I argued for four hours about Schopenhauer's philosophy of music and finished unable to say what we had actually been disagreeing about. He defends the metaphysical reading: on his account, the claim that music, unlike the other arts, passes over the Platonic Ideas and is instead a direct objectification of the will itself is a substantive thesis that does explanatory work. It is supposed to explain why music acts on us more immediately and more penetratingly than painting or poetry, why a dissonance is experienced as demanding resolution, why substituting a minor third for a major one can flip the affect of a passage instantly, and why Schopenhauer can map the deep bass onto unorganized nature and the mass of the planet, the middle harmonizing voices onto the ascending grades of objectification through plant and animal life, and the leading melody — alone possessing a continuous, freely unfolding connection from beginning to end — onto the intellectual life and effort of a human being, down to the observation that the bass moves slowly and by large intervals while the melody alone runs, modulates, deviates from the key-note and returns to it.

I hold the formalist side, in the lineage of Hanslick's mid-nineteenth-century polemic: the beautiful in music is specifically musical and consists in tones and their artistic combination; the representation or arousal of determinate feelings is neither music's content nor a criterion of its value; and Schopenhauer's parallelism is a projection laid over the score rather than a discovery in it. My stronger claim is that the metaphysics is not merely unproven but otiose — that everything Schopenhauer wants from it, including the non-representational universality of music and the picture of striving, satisfaction, and renewed striving, can be stated entirely in terms of tonal structure and our response to it, leaving no residue for the will to explain.

Write the joint statement the two of us could both sign after a genuine adversarial collaboration. It should contain:

- A numbered section of claims both of us can accept: what Schopenhauer's account actually asserts, without caricature; what music does to an attentive listener; what a purely structural description captures and where it stops; the cases in which the two accounts make the same predictions and are therefore indistinguishable in practice; and whether either of us can accept the deflationary reading on which the parallelism is a heuristic for hearing rather than an ontological claim. If one of us rejects that reading, say explicitly what the rejection costs.

- A disagreements table, one row per residual disagreement, with columns for: the disagreement; the wording my friend would accept as a fair statement of his own view; the wording I would accept as a fair statement of mine; what would have to be true for the disagreement to dissolve; and whether it is substantive or merely verbal.

- The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us. I suspect at least three are in play: whether the felt inevitability of a resolution is evidence for a parallelism between music and the inner nature of the world, or is exhausted by acoustic relations, trained expectation, and predictive response; whether the hierarchy of bass, harmonizing voices, and melody is load-bearing for the central claim or detachable from it, such that its collapse would leave the metaphysics intact; and whether the thesis that music presents striving and satisfaction as such, rather than this or that particular sorrow, says anything beyond the fact — which I also grant — that music is non-representational.

- For each crux, a case, comparison, or piece of evidence both of us would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. Include at least one test on which neither of us has an obvious stake: what music that lacks any tonal hierarchy of bass against melody does to the parallelism, or what competent listeners report about passages they have learned to hear purely structurally.

- A short closing section stating what each of us would have to give up if the other turned out to be right.

Neither position may be strawmanned: my friend must be allowed that a metaphysical account can explain something a structural description leaves unexplained, and I must be allowed that calling musical beauty immanent need not make it trivial. Paraphrase rather than quote, and do not append a bibliography, a reading list, or page references, nor invent passages. Keep every agreed claim specific enough that its negation would be recognizable to both of us as a loss.
