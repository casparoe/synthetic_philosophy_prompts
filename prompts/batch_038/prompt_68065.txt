I'm a senior double-majoring in philosophy and economics at a small college, and this semester I've been in a twelve-person seminar that spent eleven of its fourteen weeks on Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments — our professor insisted we take the first book slowly and only let us into the Wealth of Nations at the very end, on the grounds that nobody who hadn't sat through the first book was entitled to an opinion about the second. On the last day of that unit she announced she is retiring in June. Last Thursday a colleague in the economics department emailed to say the college is holding a dinner for her in three weeks and that former students will be asked to speak, and because I'm the only one of her advisees still on campus — and the only person she ever allowed to hand in the same paper three times — the student tribute has been handed to me: six minutes, after the main course, ahead of the department chair, the dean, and the presentation of a clock. Roughly a hundred and thirty people will be there. About twenty are her former graduate students, now faculty in three countries; there is the current seminar; there are colleagues from two departments, including the utilitarian with whom she has been arguing in print for fifteen years about whether the impartial spectator is anything more than a cost-benefit analysis wearing an eighteenth-century waistcoat; there is her husband; and there are her two adult children, neither of whom has ever read Smith, and one of whom has said within my hearing that his mother was a woman who was always at the university. I have never spoken to more than forty people, the function room has terrible acoustics, and my thesis chapter is due the same week, so I would rather get this right on the first draft.

What she actually taught, which I want the speech to obey rather than mention. She held that Smith's internal spectator is not an ideal observer lowered in from nowhere but a composite assembled out of the real spectators a person has actually met — the looking-glass she said society hands us, since we cannot see our own character any more than our own faces — and that it therefore inherits the blind spots of whoever happened to be watching, and can end up quietly in the pay of one's own profession. Hence her running claim that self-command is not automatically a virtue but is sometimes conformity dressed up as one. She drew, every single year, a hard line between wanting applause and wanting to be applaudable, and thought most academic careers were a slow slide from the second into the first. Her signature seminar exercise: you were not permitted to object to anyone until you had stated their position in terms its best defender would accept, to her satisfaction, and she would say that the objection could usually wait, because by then you had stopped wanting to make it. And she took from Smith the thought that a spectator can only ever come partway toward what we feel, that the person principally concerned has to bring his feeling down to the level at which the spectator can meet him, and that the resulting accord — not the intensity of what is felt — is what makes a sentiment proper; a speech that refused to come down she called "a man shouting into a room." She detested tributes. She once said at a faculty meeting that the retirement speech is the one genre in which everyone present is paid to address the crowd and nobody is answerable to the judge inside.

So: please write the speech. Eight hundred and fifty to nine hundred and fifty words, meant to be read aloud by an amateur, in a loud room. No quotation from Smith or from anyone else, no use of the phrase "impartial spectator," and no explaining of any of this to the audience — the twenty former graduate students should be able to hear precisely which of her doctrines the speech is built on and how, while her children and the dean should simply hear an unsentimental, accurate speech about a difficult and generous person, with no machinery visible. It must contain one true and unwelcome thing about her, and here is the material to work with: in 2009 she argued publicly, in a faculty meeting and then in a review, against the appointment of a young historian of science whom she judged a lightweight; the appointment went ahead anyway; that person turned out to be the most distinguished hire the college made in twenty years and will be sitting at the head table; and she never once said she had been wrong. The speech has to carry this without converting it into a virtue — nothing about impossible standards, nothing about her caring too much to be diplomatic, nothing about her being wrong the way only very serious people are wrong — and without letting the room laugh it off; it should leave the criticism and the respect standing in the same sentence, which I think is what her own account would demand, since everyone there tonight would prefer to be given only one of them. It also has to be hearable by her children, and I'd like something in it to be visible only from where they sit: for three years she drove two hours each way every Sunday to sit with her own mother while the department assumed she was simply unavailable on weekends, and the children knew and nobody at the university ever asked. Her composure should cost somebody in the speech rather than being free. It must not end by thanking her on behalf of all of us, by asking us to be more like her, or by cueing the applause — she would say the last sentence is where you find out which judge you were addressing.

After the speech, in a short passage I won't read aloud, tell me where in it each of the following does its work: the abatement of pitch; the distinction between wanting applause and wanting to be applaudable; the internal spectator as a composite that can be corrupted by the crowd it was built from; and the requirement to state a subject in her own best terms before objecting to her. Then tell me honestly which of the constraints above you think a real speech of this kind should break, and why — my roommate, who is not in the seminar, insists I should simply read a passage of Smith aloud at the end and let it do all the work, which is the one thing I'm confident she would walk out on, but I'd rather hear the case against me than have you flatter me.
