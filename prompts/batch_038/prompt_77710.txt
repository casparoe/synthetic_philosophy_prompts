So here's something I've been chewing on for the better part of a year and I finally want to put it down properly instead of just arguing about it with my wife.

Since 2021 me and my neighbour have been in a legal fight over a strip of land behind our gardens, two or three feet wide, worth a few thousand at the very most. I've got a surveyor's report that says it's mine, he's got one that says it's his, and it keeps going up — small claims, then a proper hearing, then an appeal thing. Between lawyers, a second survey, court fees and one expert witness I'm now way past what the land could ever be worth, and I still don't want to stop. Every time I think about dropping it, two thoughts arrive at exactly the same time: all that money just evaporating, and him winning. I went looking for whether this is a known thing and it turns out it is, several things at once. There's escalation of commitment. There's the dollar auction, where people bid way past a dollar for a single dollar because the second-highest bidder still has to pay their bid, so everyone keeps raising to avoid being the one left holding the loss. There's sunk cost, and loss aversion — losing a hundred hurts more than winning a hundred feels good. And I keep seeing entrapment and self-justification mentioned too.

My problem is that all of these get thrown around like they're the same condition with five nicknames, and I don't think they are. Not being able to walk away because a rival is also in and one of you has to eat the loss is a different thing entierly from not being able to walk away because you told the whole street you were going to win. And both of those are different from the case where a cold stranger would look at your situation and say no, your odds genuinely did improve, keep going. So I'd like you to build me a decision tree that sorts a person from "I'm still pouring money into this" to the particular mechanism that's keeping them in.

Here's what I'm after:

- Around ten to twelve questions as branch nodes, and about six to eight leaves. Every node has to be a question about the actual situation — is anybody watching this, would you quit if you could do it quietly and no one ever found out, if your opponent dropped out tomorrow do you keep going, has your estimate of your chances gone up or down each time you paid for something new — and never a question that reveals which mechanism the answer points to. I want to be sorted by what I'd actually do, not by what I'd like to say about myself.
- Each leaf gets its real name if it has one, or "unnamed" if nobody's bothered to name it, plus one sentence on what it predicts you'd do that the other leaves don't, plus one sentence on the cure. The cure matters most to me, because I think the fixes really are different per mechanism and I've been applying the wrong one.
- These leaves have to be in there: one where continuing is simply correct and the person isn't biased at all; one where the money already spent is irrelevant and it's purely about the audience and your own story about yourself; the rival-driven one, the dollar auction shape; and one where the mechanism is that you keep quietly revising your odds of success upward every time you invest. That last one is definitely me and I've never seen it described on its own.
- Then tell me about the empty squares. Which combinations of answers are logically possible but nobody ends up in, and why — is the combination incoherent, just unappealing, or has nobody thought to look there? For instance: can you get the rival-driven version with no rival? Can you get the face-saving one with no audience and nothing sunk yet?
- Flag honestly which pairs of leaves your tree can't separate from the inside, and if there's some extra question that would split them, give it; if there isn't, say so rather than pretending the tree is sharper than it is.
- At the end, run me through it with what I've told you above and say which leaf you think I'm standing in. Be blunt, I'm not going to be offended, I've already been called worse by my wife.

One thing: don't give me a reading list or any references. I can't look them up properly anyway and I'd rather have nothing than invented ones. Just the tree, in a form I could actually print out and work through with a pen on a Sunday morning.
