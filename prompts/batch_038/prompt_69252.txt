For the past two years I have taken part in a reading circle at our public library where about a dozen of us work through Buddhist philosophical texts in translation, using introductory secondary literature alongside them. None of us has a degree in the subject — I work in freight logistics — and this term we spent six weeks on Nāgārjuna's *Fundamental Wisdom of the Middle Way* and on the commentarial tradition around it. At the end of the term the convenor set a short take-home question and asked everyone to write a response of roughly five paragraphs, then asked me to mark one of them and return it with written comments. The writer is someone I have met twice, which is the only reason the task landed on me rather than on a friend of hers. I have to hand back a mark out of ten along with comments that justify it, and I would rather the mark did not simply reflect my own uneven grasp of the material, so I am not going to tell you what I made of her answer when I read it. Assume you have no books to hand: I am not asking for references, verse numbers, or a reading list, only for a judgment of the reasoning as it stands. Where two paragraphs pull against each other, please say so in the comment on the paragraph where the trouble originates.

Here is the question as it was set: "Explain what Nāgārjuna means when he says that emptiness is itself empty, and say what the tetralemma, the four-cornered negation, contributes to that point. Then answer the following objection: if nothing has intrinsic nature, does it not follow that nothing exists at all?"

Here is her response, kept in her own five numbered paragraphs. (1) Nāgārjuna's starting point is dependent arising. Nothing exists under its own power: a table, a person, a flare-up of anger each exist only in dependence on causes and conditions, on parts, and on the concepts and words by which we pick them out. Svabhāva, usually rendered as intrinsic nature or own-being, is exactly what such things lack — the property of being self-contained, independent, and self-defining. Emptiness is the name for this absence, so to call something empty is not to say that it is not there but to say that it is not there in the way we instinctively assume. (2) Because emptiness is what is ultimately the case about every phenomenon, Nāgārjuna distinguishes two levels of truth. Conventional truth covers the everyday world of tables and persons, which is real enough for practical purposes but only from within ordinary experience; ultimate truth is emptiness itself, the way things actually are once our projections are stripped away. Emptiness is therefore the one thing that survives the analysis, the ground left standing when everything conditioned has been dissolved, which is why later readers have been tempted to line it up with an absolute of the kind the Vedānta posits — and I think that comparison is a fair one. (3) This is where the second half of the question comes in. Nāgārjuna does not leave emptiness in place as a conclusion; he insists that emptiness too is empty, that it is only a designation depending on the very things it is supposed to be about. He uses the image of a snake picked up by the wrong end, or a medicine wrongly administered, to warn that emptiness grasped as a doctrine is more dangerous than any ordinary confusion, and elsewhere he disowns having any thesis of his own, arguing that someone who asserts nothing cannot be refuted. Saying that emptiness is empty is thus therapeutic rather than doctrinal: it prevents the analysis from terminating in a new item to believe. (4) The tetralemma is the instrument that carries this out. For any subject he examines, four positions present themselves: that it exists, that it does not exist, that it both exists and does not exist, and that it neither exists nor does not exist. He takes each in turn and shows that it cannot be sustained, as when he presses on whether nirvāṇa is a produced thing or an unproduced one, or whether the world is eternal or finite. The first three corners are rejected outright, and the fourth is where the argument comes to rest: it is the position that remains once the extremes have been discarded, a synthesis preserving what was right in the earlier alternatives while dropping their one-sidedness. The middle way is accordingly the fourth corner, correctly understood. (5) So the objection that emptiness is nihilism in disguise has things backwards, and Nāgārjuna says as much in the chapter where an opponent charges him with destroying the Four Noble Truths, karma and its fruit, and the possibility of awakening. His reply is that the opponent has it upside down: were things possessed of intrinsic natures they would be fixed, uncaused, incapable of arising or ceasing, and change, causation, and moral responsibility would all be impossible. It is precisely because things are empty that fire can burn, seeds can sprout, and practice can transform a person. Conventional truths are not true in the strict sense — they are useful fictions we live by — but they are not on that account dispensable, and emptiness cannot be understood apart from them.
