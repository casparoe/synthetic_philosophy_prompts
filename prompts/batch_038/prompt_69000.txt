I've been trying to sort out when cultural change actually counts as Darwinian — when there's a real replicator being selected rather than just people influencing each other — and I've accumulated a pile of verdicts I can't unify. Going purely on gut:

- The tune and words of a playground clapping rhyme, mangled a bit by each year's cohort, where the versions that are easiest to chant in time outlast the rest: obviously yes, this is my paradigm case.
- A family recipe never written down, learned by watching, where every cook improvises and nobody's version matches the one before it: still yes, though I couldn't tell you what the unit is.
- A motto reproduced letter-perfect on a signet ring, or a hymn sung word-for-word out of a printed book for three centuries: I want to say yes, it's a cultural replicator — but there's no variation at all, hence no selection, so maybe it's just a very durable artifact?
- A rumour that spreads *by* being distorted: each teller sharpens it, and the sharpened version spreads better than the accurate one did. I want to say selection happened here. But strictly speaking nothing was ever copied.
- The Pythagoras theorem, which each generation of students re-derives after being shown the proof and checking it. Same content, no copying, enormous stability. I'd say this is NOT Darwinian cultural evolution — it's truth-tracking, and the stability has nothing to do with fitness.
- Lapel widths cycling wide–narrow–wide: no. That's just people matching each other, there's no recipe being passed on.
- A smith improves a plough design, teaches the improvement to apprentices, who improve it further: yes, definitely evolution, but it feels Lamarckian — acquired improvements get inherited — so does that disqualify it from being Darwinian, or not?

The pair I cant reconcile is the rumour and the theorem. In both, the content is reconstructed rather than copied, and I give opposite verdicts. My half-formed hunch is that the difference is whether the reconstruction is pulled by a bias that favours some variants over others, versus pulled by a checking procedure that converges on the same answer no matter who runs it. I also vaguely remember someone arguing that cultural items aren't copies at all but something more like stable points that representations drift toward, which might dissolve the whole question.
