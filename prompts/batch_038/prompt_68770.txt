My department has one guy who reaches for the driving-on-the-right example every time someone asks why you ought to follow a social norm. The pitch is roughly: Lewis showed conventions are just equilibria of coordination games, which side of the road we use is arbitrary but self-enforcing once its common knowledge that everyone else is doing it, and promise-keeping, gift-giving, queueing and so on are the same structure — so "why should I conform?" has no content beyond "why defect from an equilibrium everyone benefits from," which he thinks is a confused question. I half buy it and half don't, and the half that doesn't has something to do with the traffic case being special: nobody is left worse off by which equilibrium we land on, and the equilibrium gets made salient by a legislature and a fine rather than by anything the drivers do. Can you audit the analogy properly — what in the traffic game corresponds to what in the norm case (players, the equilibrium structure, the knowledge conditions, the sanctions, the way the convention gets selected over time instead of decreed), which disanalogies actually bear on his inference versus which are just noise, and what features of driving the analogy quietly imports that norms lack? Then tell me whether the deflationary conclusion survives the disanalogies or whether the traffic case was carrying it the whole time, and if it doesn't survive, what a better source domain would be for thinking about how norms hold people — plus what that one would license instead. Bottom line up front in your first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question.
