I am preparing a seminar session on functionalism and multiple realizability, and I have been asked to open it with a passage from William James's 1904 essay "Does 'Consciousness' Exist?" (first published in the Journal of Philosophy, Psychology and Scientific Methods and later collected in Essays in Radical Empiricism). I can gloss the individual sentences, but I cannot assemble them into a position I would be willing to attribute to James, and I would rather not present it until I can. Below are three excerpts, in their original order, with the intervening material elided.

> To deny plumply that 'consciousness' exists seems so absurd on the face of it — for undeniably 'thoughts' do exist — that I fear some readers will follow me no farther. Let me then immediately explain that I mean only to deny that the word stands for an entity, but to insist most emphatically that it does stand for a function. There is, I mean, no aboriginal stuff or quality of being, contrasted with that of which material objects are made, out of which our thoughts of them are made; but there is a function in experience which thoughts perform, and for the performance of which this quality of being is invoked. That function is knowing. 'Consciousness' is supposed necessary to explain the fact that things not only are, but get reported, are known. Whoever blots out the notion of consciousness from his list of first principles must still provide in some way for that function's being carried on.
>
> ... Souls were detachable, had separate destinies; things could happen to them. To consciousness as such nothing can happen, for, timeless itself, it is only a witness of happenings in time, in which it plays no part.
>
> ... My thesis is that if we start with the supposition that there is only one primal stuff or material in the world, a stuff of which everything is composed, and if we call that stuff 'pure experience,' then knowing can easily be explained as a particular sort of relation towards one another into which portions of pure experience may enter. The relation itself is a part of pure experience; one of its terms becomes the subject or bearer of the knowledge, the knower, the other becomes the object known.

My difficulty is that the passage seems to cut in two directions at once, and I cannot tell which is doing the work.

1. State precisely what the entity/function contrast amounts to in the first excerpt. When James denies that there is an "aboriginal stuff or quality of being" out of which thoughts are made, is he denying that mental states have a physical (or experience-stuff) constitution, or denying only that their constitution is *specially mental*? Put differently: does the sentence rule out an identity between the state and what realizes it, or does it merely relocate the identity?

2. Here is the tension I cannot resolve. Multiple realizability, as the term is normally used, says that one and the same functional type can be had by states of many different physical types. But James posits exactly one primal stuff; every portion of the world is made of pure experience, and what varies is only the relations portions enter into. On the face of it, then, his view is the *reverse* of multiple realizability — a single realizer kind, promiscuously taking on many roles, rather than many realizer kinds for one role. Is that right? If it is, does it follow that James has no business being invoked as an ancestor of functionalism, or can role-individuation of mental types survive independently of there being many candidate realizers? Please be explicit about whether the functionalist thesis needs multiple realizability as a premise or only as a consequence.

3. To mark the point just made, I have been using two labels: *multiple realizability* for one role played by many stuffs, and — for want of a better term — *multiple placement* for one stuff playing many roles, sometimes two roles at once. Is that distinction sound, or does it collapse, since a role-swapping neutral stuff arguably just is a case of many distinct role-instances of the same underlying type? If it holds, tell me which of the two James's third excerpt supplies, and whether the passage contains anything that would let him have both.

4. The sentence "Whoever blots out the notion of consciousness from his list of first principles must still provide in some way for that function's being carried on" appears to impose an obligation on James himself. Since he has just said the word stands for a function and not an entity, who or what is the obligor here, and what would discharging the obligation consist in? Does satisfying it require producing a bearer of the function — and if so, has an entity of some sort been smuggled back in through the requirement that the function be "carried on"?

5. In the second excerpt James dismisses a consciousness to which "nothing can happen" and which "plays no part" in the happenings it witnesses. I want to know which of two objections this is. Either (a) he is objecting that such a consciousness would be explanatorily idle, a merely descriptive posit that does no work; or (b) he is objecting on the stronger ground that whatever deserves the name of a function must itself be causally embedded in what it functions over, so a timeless witness cannot be a function-bearer at all. Distinguish these readings, and say which one the surrounding text supports. The choice matters for my seminar, because only (b) would make James a functionalist in the causal-role sense rather than someone merely dispensing with a redundant posit.

6. Finally, the last sentence says that one term of the relation becomes the knower and the other the object known, while the relation itself is also a part of pure experience. Does this permit a single portion of pure experience to figure at once as knower of something and as something known — by itself or by another portion? If it does, does that generate a problem for reading James as individuating states by role, given that a role-individuated state that occupies two incompatible roles simultaneously seems not to be determinately any one state? If it does not, what in the excerpt forecloses it?

I am not looking for references to the secondary literature or for a list of places to read; please work from the text quoted and from general argument. What I want at the end is a single paragraph I could put on the seminar handout stating, in your own words and without hedging, what view of the mental James is actually proposing here and how it stands to the thesis that mental states are individuated by what they do rather than by what they are made of.
