In March I was sworn in as one of three lay members of my state's parole review panel. I was appointed partly because I spent fifteen years as a defense investigator, and I accepted the seat believing the underlying questions were settled. Eleven weeks of hearings have convinced me they are not. At the end of each cycle we must enter written reasons for our votes into the record; my first set of reasons is due in nine days, and I cannot write them until I know what principle I have actually been applying. I am hoping you can extract it for me, or tell me plainly that there is nothing to extract.

What I thought I believed going in was roughly Caruso's line: no one is the ultimate originator of the character that produced his offense, so no one deserves to suffer for its own sake; the state may incapacitate a person only where doing so is necessary to protect others, and only by the least restrictive means available; and blaming and punishing are justified as instruments for shaping behavior, never as payments owed. I also thought Dennett's objection to this — that being the sort of creature who can be held responsible is a matter of possessing certain competences, and that the practice of holding people responsible is itself among the things that produces those competences — marked a difference of emphasis rather than substance. My own votes suggest I was wrong about both.

My verdicts, in the order I encountered them.

1. A man who shot a store clerk during a robbery at nineteen and is now forty-one: twenty-two years served, model record, runs a mentorship program for younger inmates, no infractions in a decade. Release with conditions. What troubles me is that my reasoning was not that the risk is low; it was that there is nothing left for him to pay. That is a desert sentence written in the grammar of a quarantine model.

2. A man who killed his wife's lover eight years ago, openly remorseful, no minimization, whom the panel's psychologist nonetheless rated as likely to react violently if returned to a comparably humiliating situation. Keep in. My reason here was entirely forward-looking, and I do not think the remorse registered with me at all.

3. A sixty-three-year-old who drained a pension fund and ruined people who will never recover, and who maintains to this day that he did nothing wrong. His risk of reoffending is negligible; age and the nature of the offense guarantee it. Keep in — and when the chair asked why, I said something to the effect that he should stay until he understands what he did. I cannot render that in either vocabulary.

4. A man whose assaults were traced to a frontal mass lesion, removed two years ago, after which the violence stopped outright. Release. The disturbing part is that I did not experience this as an exception to ordinary responsibility but as a glimpse of the ordinary case with one variable made visible.

5. A man who committed his offense while on a sedative he had been prescribed and warned about, though the pharmacy records indicate his prescriber told him the warning was boilerplate. Partly responsible. I do not know what weight I gave the prescriber's downplaying, or whether I gave it any.

6. A man the state's proprietary risk instrument scored high, whose file — employment history, correspondence, conduct inside, the victim's own statement — pointed the other way. Release against the score. My stated reason was that the instrument describes people like him rather than him.

7. A man whose violent impulsivity has been controlled for four years on a long-acting injectable begun after his original assessment, so the instrument scores him on data that predates the change. Release, on condition he continues the medication, which he accepts. In a separate matter I opposed the same medication being imposed on a man who did not want it. I take the difference to be consent, though a man told that medication is the price of going home is not obviously consenting.

8. Two co-defendants of equal culpability on my reading, one sentenced to twenty-five years and one to nine; the nine-year man is before us and is, by every measure I trust, the more dangerous of the two. I wanted to release him because the disparity was intolerable — and I noticed that I would not have wanted the same release had both received the same sentence.

9. A woman who at twenty drove her then-partner to two armed robberies under what the file makes look like sustained coercion, and who is now, by every account including the victims', an unrecognizably different person. Release. But I did not think of it as releasing someone who was never guilty; I thought of it as releasing someone whose guilt had been spent.

The pairs I cannot reconcile: 2 with 3, since 3 cannot be quarantine and 2 cannot be desert; 6 with 2, since in 6 I rejected an aggregate statistical prediction in favor of the particulars of a man, and in 2 I accepted one clinician's prediction over a sustained record of conduct; and 8 with the whole framework, since a comparative-injustice reason for release has no place in a model whose only currency is future harm.

What I would like back is two or three candidate principles that could generate these votes, each stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have never seen, with every clause tied to the case that motivates it. For each candidate, tell me which of the nine verdicts it forces me to abandon rather than merely reinterpret, and tell me what it says about three I have not listed: a man sentenced under a statute later softened, where the softer standard would reach him retroactively while a harsher one would not; a person whose offense occurred in a single dissociative episode with no violence before or since; and a woman who is unambiguously dangerous and unambiguously no longer the person who committed her crime. Say also whether the Dennett-style objection — that a panel's entire procedure of asking whether a man has become someone who can be trusted with reasons already presupposes the competences it claims to be waiting for — alters what the principle should be, and whether it makes verdict 3 intelligible or only dresses it up. If the honest conclusion is that my set is inconsistent and no principle worth having saves it, say so and tell me which verdict to surrender first. I do not need references or a reading list; I need the principle, and I need to know how you would word it in the three paragraphs of reasoning I owe the record. Finally, tell me how confident you are in each candidate and single out the point in your answer you are least sure of. This is going into a public document, and I would rather learn where the ground is thin from you than from the chair.
