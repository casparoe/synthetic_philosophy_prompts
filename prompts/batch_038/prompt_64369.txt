A couple weeks ago two friends of mine got into it after a screening of a famine documentary and they've kind of been at it ever since, mostly over text, with me on the thread. Neither of them is dumb so I honestly cant tell who's winning.

(1) Neal said if you want to decide which atrocities get taught, memorialized, or funded for research, you have to rank them, and the only non-arbitrary ranking is by total harm — how many people, how badly, for how long. Anything else, he said, is squeamishness dressed up as respect.

(2) Priya said that's precisely the obscene move: it turns victims into a quantity and commits you to saying an atrocity against 500,000 people is "not as bad" as one against a million, which she said is not a sentence you get to say out loud about actual human beings.

(3) Neal said she's sliding between two different things, because comparing isn't devaluing. Saying A was worse than B doesn't say B wasn't horrific. We rank harms all the time without anyone taking offense — we say murder is worse than assault and assault victims don't feel demoted — and sentencing guidelines flatly cannot function without an ordering.

(4) Priya said the criminal-law analogy is the tell, since that ranking is about what the offender deserves and it's applied to somebody who can answer for it, whereas ranking atrocities is about victims who can't answer for anything. Then she said his total-harm metric is sneakier than he's admitting: it makes a person killed outright interchangeable with a person who survived twenty years of forced labor as long as the arithmetic comes out even. She asked whether he'd accept one death in exchange for sparing a thousand people a splitting headache. He said obviously not. She said then your scale isn't additive, and if it isn't additive you don't have a metric, you have a vibe.

(5) Neal conceded half of that — fine, he said, use a threshold, so that below a certain depth of harm quantities don't aggregate up into the category above. But he turned it around: Priya ranks too, whether she owns it or not. She wants some atrocities in the curriculum and others out, a memorial here and not there, and every one of those choices is a ranking. Refusing to say the ranking out loud just hands it to whatever is most recent or most telegenic.

(6) Priya said there's a difference between ranking and selecting, and that she'd select by implication — what the audience is actually downstream of, what's still live, what they could do something about — rather than by a severity score. She also said intent has to matter: a deliberate campaign to eliminate a group is a different moral kind of thing from a famine a government cynically allowed to happen, even if the famine killed ten times as many people, and any scale blind to that difference is measuring the wrong thing. Neal said that's importing mens rea into a judgment about victims, so you end up grading the wrong party, and that "implication" is just a metric that happens to flatter whoever's doing the ranking.
