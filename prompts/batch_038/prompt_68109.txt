I cover research methods and the statistical underpinnings of the behavioral sciences for a general-interest science magazine, and I also sit, as the board's only non-academic member, on the editorial advisory board of an open-access journal in that field. The editor has asked each of us to circulate a short position memo in advance of Friday's vote on a contested submission. Please draft mine.

The case is as follows. The paper reports a two-arm, pre-registered experiment with 1,900 participants per condition, comparing a brief behavioral intervention against an active control. The primary outcome is a composite self-report scale. The registration fixed a single primary hypothesis, set alpha at .05 two-sided, and committed to no interim analyses and no stopping rules; the study was conducted exactly as registered. On the primary outcome the standardized mean difference is d = 0.064, p = 0.049. Separately, the authors report a subgroup analysis involving a few hundred participants — not part of the registration — in which the effect is roughly five times larger and p = 0.0004. They present it as hypothesis-generating.

The complication is institutional. Two years ago, during the reform wave that followed the replication crisis, this journal adopted a policy requiring authors to report a Bayes factor alongside every primary significance test. The policy was never operationalized, and it has now produced a result nobody anticipated: the authors complied, and under the standard default prior on the standardized effect they obtain BF₁₀ ≈ 0.2, about five to one in favor of the null. One reviewer, a committed Bayesian, reads this as showing that the frequentist result is an artifact of the enormous sample size and recommends rejection. A second reviewer reads the same numbers as vindication: the study did precisely what the reform movement asked of it, and to reject a pre-registered test that cleared its pre-specified threshold because an unregistered Bayesian gloss looks unflattering would restore exactly the discretionary second-guessing that registration was meant to eliminate. The authors, meanwhile, point out — correctly, so far as I can determine — that the default prior distributes most of its mass across effect sizes far larger than anything reliably documented in this literature, and that a narrower prior informed by the field's typical effects moves the Bayes factor back toward, and possibly past, indifference.

The memo should run to roughly 800 to 1,000 words, in my voice, formal but legible to a board that includes both methodologists and generalists. It must end in a definite recommendation — accept, reject, or revise under conditions specific enough that the authors could act on them — together with a separate recommendation on what the board should do about the Bayes factor policy, since we will be voting on both questions. Beyond the verdict, I need it to explain to that mixed audience what each of the two numbers is actually a claim about, and why the dispute between the reviewers is not a matter of software preference.

Specifically, please make sure the memo does the following:

- Addresses whether adherence to a pre-registration changes what a p = 0.049 licenses a journal to say, and whether the argument for that result rests on long-run error rates or on something else a frequentist can appeal to when a Bayesian objects that the stopping rule and sample size are irrelevant to the evidence in the data at hand.
- Treats the prior-dependence of the Bayes factor as a substantive question rather than a technicality, and says which of the two priors in dispute — the default one or the field-informed one — you would consider legitimate for a journal to rely on, and why.
- Says what should be done about d = 0.064 on a self-report composite: whether trivial practical magnitude is a reason to reject, a reason to accept with framing conditions, or something the statistical machinery simply is not designed to police.
- Rules explicitly on the unregistered subgroup result — whether it may appear at all, and in what form.
- Gives the strongest available version of the recommendation you do not adopt, in terms its own proponents would recognize, and then states plainly why it loses.
- Identifies what piece of information, if it had been different, would have flipped your recommendation.

Two constraints. First, no bibliography, reference list, or named citations: the reasoning must stand on its own, and I will be accountable for every claim in it. Second, do not resolve the case by splitting the difference between the two reviewers; the board has enough documents that recommend hedging, and a memo that assigns equal weight to both sides is, for our purposes, no memo at all.
