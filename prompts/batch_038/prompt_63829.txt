So my dept gives out a handful of summer research grants — ten weeks, not much money, and they want a ~1200 word proposal saying what you'd actually do and why it's worth funding. Mine is on risk-weighted expected utility theory, the Buchak-style view where an outcome's contribution to the value of a gamble depends not just on its probability but on the probability of doing better than it. Can you write the proposal for me?

The half-formed idea I want it built around. People attack that theory on diachronic grounds: a rank-dependent agent apparently plans one thing now and then wants to reverse herself later once some uncertainty resolves, and there's a well-known line of argument that if you take seriously how such an agent handles temporally extended choice — commitment, planning, the way the decision problem gets framed for her — her recommendations end up collapsing back into ordinary expected utility ones, so the theory isn't a real alternative at all. I think that argument has the diagnosis backwards. My hunch is that the instability isn't coming from the risk-attitude component (the shape of the weighting function) but from the fact that a rank-dependent value isn't additive across the parts of a gamble, so what the agent is evaluating shifts depending on how you carve the situation up into acts and events. If that's right, the same agent can be perfectly stable over time once you're careful about what counts as one decision, and the collapse objection is really an argument about act-individuation that bites on everybody rather than a refutation of rank-dependence.

Related hunch, and I might have this wrong so flag it if I do: a rank-dependent valuation shouldn't in general score a two-stage gamble the same as the single-stage gamble it reduces to, unless the weighting function has some very specific shape. If that's true it's a useful wedge, because it means the question of which descriptions of the same choice problem are equivalent is already live inside the theory instead of being neutral background.

What I want in the thing: a precise statement of the question that could genuinely come out either way; why the existing positions — Buchak's own replies, the collapse objection, and the planning-or-commitment moves — don't settle it; what I'd actually do, week by week across the ten weeks, including which toy models I'd build and what I'd do with them; what a positive result looks like and what would count as the project failing rather than just being inconclusive; and an honest line about whether ten weeks is enough, and if not, what I'd cut. No bibliography needed, I'll add the refs myself, but its fine to name positions and arguments in prose.
