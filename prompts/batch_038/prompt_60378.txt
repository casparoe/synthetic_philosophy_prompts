i do compliance for a staffing agency so i read the EU working time directive (2003/88) alot, and there's something in it that has nagged me for years and i can't tell whether it's a genuine contradiction or just me not knowing the theory underneath.

the directive is built almost entirely out of quantities: 11 consecutive hours of rest in every 24, a break of at least 20 minutes once you pass six hours, 24 hours off per week, four weeks of paid leave a year, and a 48-hour weekly ceiling. But the ceiling is an *average* over a reference period, so a 60-plus hour week is fine as long as it gets evened out by short ones later. Hours move around. They get banked, spread, traded across weeks. One of the recitals even says that the concept of rest "must be expressed in units of time, i.e. in days, hours and/or fractions thereof." So rest is a number.

Then two things refuse to behave like numbers. The daily 11 hours has to be consecutive and has to fall inside that particular 24-hour window — you cant save up Tuesday's rest and take it in a lump on Friday. And the four weeks of leave can't be cashed out at all; you only get paid for untaken leave when the job ends. Meanwhile on-call time where you're required to be on the premises counts as working time even if you're asleep the whole night and never get called — the court was explicit about that for doctors. So presence counts whether or not you're doing anything, but rest only counts if it happens in the right slot.

so what is the picture of rest and leisure that makes all of this coherent? Is it a fungible stock that happens to have positional constraints bolted on, or is the ban on payment in lieu telling you something much stronger — that rest is precisely the thing money can't stand in for, that it isn't a quantity at all despite the recital? Reconstruct whatever theory actually licenses the rules. And be careful to seperate: (a) what the practice strictly presupposes, (b) what it merely fits nicely with, and (c) where it's underdetermined, so that several rival pictures of leisure — rest as recovery-for-work, rest as an end in itself, rest as a share of a common good the state rations, work as a harm to be compensated after the fact — would all sign off on the same 11 hours and the same 4 weeks.

Then compare it with the japanese approach, which comes at the same problem from the other end: there's an administrative threshold, roughly 80 hours of overtime in a month, past which a fatal stroke or heart attack is presumed to have been caused by the work and becomes compensable. Same units of time, same arithmetic, but the number does the work after someone has died instead of limiting the hours beforehand. Does that presuppose the same view of time and of what work does to a person, or a different one, and if different, which of the two is more internally consistent?

last thing — tell me what would have to change in the actual text if you swapped the presupposed view out. Say, if you decided rest really is fungible and "recovery" is a myth and only total welfare matters: which clauses survive unchanged, which collapse, and what has to be added that isn't there now?
