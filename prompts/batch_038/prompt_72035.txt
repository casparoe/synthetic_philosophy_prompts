I have ninety minutes with a municipal citizens' panel — thirty-odd residents of a mid-sized town, no political theory, including two who have served on the city council — and the topic is sortition. My aim is not to win them over to lottocracy but to get them to understand one particular design: a body of twenty-four residents chosen by civic lottery from the electoral roll, serving a single non-renewable eighteen-month term with a third rotating off every six months, supported by a professional secretariat, hearing witnesses from every side, holding binding authority over the capital budget and the right to place measures directly on the ballot, and having no power to overrule the elected council on anything else. So don't explain sortition to me — plan how I should explain this. Tell me what has to be in place before the design stops sounding like a gimmick, including how early to bring in the fact that the lottery was the democratic device in Athens while election was the aristocratic one, and how to do it so that it lands as orientation rather than as a gotcha. Then tell me which single decision this town might plausibly face that I should build the whole session around, and what that case has to have and has to lack so that neither expertise nor scandal ends up carrying the argument for me. Next, map the objections: they will arrive assuming a lottery body is either an opinion poll with extra steps or a room of unaccountable amateurs, and at least one will assume the proposal is to abolish elections, so say where in the sequence each of these should be absorbed rather than answered head-on, and how to keep the council veterans' grievances from setting the agenda. Finish with one question I can pose at the end that would reveal whether they have grasped that the term limits, the staffing, and the narrow jurisdiction are each doing real work, and with a short list of what I should announce I am not covering at this level.
