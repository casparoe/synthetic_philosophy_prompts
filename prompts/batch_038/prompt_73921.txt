I'm an epidemiologist, fifteen-odd years in, mostly chronic disease and a lot of work on risk prediction and observational studies. My daily vocabulary is causal inference: counterfactuals, DAGs, exchangeability, confounding, transportability, propensity scores, inverse probability weighting, calibration. I have essentially no philosophy training, though I've been reading around interpretations of probability lately and it's rattling me somewhat.

Two things set this off. The first is silly but persistent: propensity means something completely different in my field than it seems to mean in philosophy. Ours is a conditional probability of treatment given covariates, something you estimate from data and then check for balance — and I keep tripping over the philosophers' use of the same word as if it were a physical tendency out there in the world. The second is less silly. When I tell a patient their ten-year risk of a cardiovascular event is 12 per cent, I honestly don't know what I've just said. Its not a frequency, because they only live once and there is no long run in which they have the event twelve times out of a hundred. It doesn't feel like my degree of belief either, since it fell out of an equation fitted on tens of thousands of other people, and I'd far rather argue with the equation than with myself. So I've vaguely assumed it must be some disposition of this particular body — but I couldn't tell you what that disposition is made of, or how anyone would measure it, or why it should differ from patient to patient at the same measured covariates.

So: take the three main interpretations of probability — long-run frequency, subjective degree of belief, and physical propensity — and restate each one, as faithfully as you can manage, in the vocabulary I actually use. Estimands, reference classes, exchangeability, weighting, identifiability, prediction versus intervention. Then give me the residue report. Which claims came across cleanly, which ones you had to bend out of shape to get them through, and which ones simply have no expression at all in causal-inference language. Be scrupulous about the places where you're quietly importing my framework's own assumptions and presenting them as translation — I already suspect exchangeability is going to be asked to smuggle something. And where there is genuine residue, tell me whose problem it is: whether my vocabulary is missing something the world has, or whether the philosophers' three-way disagreement is less substantive than it looks and collapses once you insist on saying what would be estimated.

One specific thing I'd like folded in. There's an argument — Humphreys' paradox, I think its called — that if propensities are real single-case tendencies then you cannot run them backwards from an effect to its cause, even though ordinary conditional probabilities invert by Bayes' theorem without any complaint. In my world inversion is utterly routine; half of what a DAG is for is keeping track of which direction you're allowed to read a quantity. So say something about whether that asymmetry is a discovery about the world, or a symptom of propensity talk being muddled from the start.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter or a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. British English, if you don't mind.
