I direct a public-philosophy interview series attached to my department: an hour of recorded conversation with a working philosopher, published with minimal editing, and heard mostly by advanced undergraduates and interested non-specialists. Next month's guest is the author of a recent book on the Buridan's ass case — call it *The Suspended Will*. I know her position only from a review essay and a proposal, which is a poor way to prepare, so I want the questions to do the work of having read her.

Her thesis, as I have it, is that the literature on the case has run together three distinct problems: the normative question of what rationality requires of an agent whose options are exactly equally good; the explanatory question of what makes it the case that this option rather than that one is taken, given a complete description of the agent and the situation; and the diachronic question of what an agent must be like in order to get out of a stalemate at all. She holds that only the third is real, that the first two are artifacts of treating choice as an instantaneous event rather than as a process extended in time, and that Buridan's much-mocked answer — that the will, finding two courses judged equal, can do nothing but suspend judgment until circumstances change — is not a surrender to determinism but the right starting point. She also argues that the familiar decision-theoretic dissolution (when two options are indifferent, randomize; either is rational) is question-begging, since it answers a normative question nobody was pressing; and she claims that the ass is the one figure in the story that could not have the problem, suspension and renewed deliberation being capacities only a rational agent possesses.

Her signature move under pressure is to distinguish a further sense of some term — reason, indifference, choice, the will — and to say her objector has used it in the wrong one. Please write me a sequence of eight to ten questions that makes that move progressively harder to execute and closes with a question about what would count against her account.

Each question should carry one or two sentences of framing, since the listener has not read the book and I will not be able to supply context on the recording. For each, give the reply you expect her to give and a follow-up keyed specifically to that reply; where you can foresee a second deflection, add a third move.

At minimum, cover: Spinoza's handling of the case in the Ethics, where he concedes that a man held in the equilibrium would die of hunger and thirst and then declines to say whether such a man should be counted an ass rather than a man, grouping him with one who hangs himself and with children, fools, and madmen — does her account differ from his in substance, or only in vocabulary? Leibniz's principle of sufficient reason and his appeal to small perceptions below the threshold of awareness: if the explanatory demand is a pseudo-problem, is the principle false, inapplicable to action, or satisfied by the process rather than by the instant of choice? The regress of suspension: if suspending is the answer, what determines when suspension ends, and is that not the question she began with? The premise of exact equipollence, including Bayle's point that these contrived cases differ from the trivial real ones where it makes no difference which we take, and the historical point that the ass appears nowhere in Buridan's own writings while the figure goes back at least to Aristotle's man poised equally between food and drink — does that point do philosophical work for her, or merely let her disown the paradox? And al-Ghazali's two similar dates, where a quality in the agent differentiates between two like things: is her temporalized suspension simply that voluntarism in new clothes? Beyond these, give me two or three questions of your own devising on ground I have not listed, each with a sentence on why it earns its place.

I have drafted three myself. First: "Buridan devised the ass between two haystacks to defend the freedom of the will against the determinists of his day; how do you answer the charge that his own solution concedes their point?" — this is the one I am least sure of. Second: "Is the puzzle really about free will, or about rationality? Help us see why a medieval thought experiment still matters." Third: "If exact equilibrium is never realized because the world keeps changing, hasn't your view collapsed into Leibniz's — the asymmetry is always there, we simply cannot see it?" Rank the three, tell me which to cut, and say so bluntly; I would rather hear it now than on the recording.

I want your settled judgment throughout, not a menu. Tell me which two questions in your sequence are most likely to draw blood and why, commit to the cuts, and if you think my characterization of her position — assembled, again, from a review — misdescribes her somewhere, or that one of my historical ascriptions is wrong, say so plainly and correct it before I build a question on it. No warm-up material, nothing about what drew her to the subject, and nothing she could answer by reciting the dust jacket.
