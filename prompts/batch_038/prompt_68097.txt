I work on uncertainty quantification for deployed models — about eight years of it now, so I'm fluent in reliability diagrams, ECE, proper scoring rules, verbalized vs. behaviorally-implied probabilities, temperature scaling, the whole toolkit. On our team we half-jokingly call it "self-report incoherence" when a model's stated confidence and the probabilities you can back out of its actual decisions come apart, and there's a decent internal literature among us about whether that's one phenomenon or several.

So here's what I'm after. Take Moore's paradox — the "its raining but I don't believe it's raining" thing — along with the accounts philosophers give of why it's supposed to be bad: the norm-of-assertion line, the belief-aims-at-truth / self-defeat line, Evans's transparency point about answering "do you believe that p?" just by looking outward at whether p, and whatever else you think does real work there. I'd want the variants covered too: the case where the speaker says p but believes not-p rather than merely lacking the belief; the fact that the third-person version ("it's raining but he doesn't believe it") is completely unremarkable; and the fact that a Moore-paradoxical sentence can be flatly true.

Restate all of that in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can manage, and then give me the residue report in three buckets. (1) Claims that map cleanly onto something I already have — a stated-vs-implied divergence, a scoring-rule failure, a report that's unfalsifiable by construction, whatever. (2) Claims you can only render by bending my terms, with an explicit note on what got bent and how much. (3) Claims that have no expression in my framework at all.

Two things I especially care about. First, no smuggling: if the honest move is just to redescribe the paradox as "the system is miscalibrated" and declare it dissolved, then flag that you're doing it and say what work the redescription is quietly doing that the original wasn't. The tension I can't shake is that in my framework a pair of contradictory attitudes is perfectly representable and sometimes even accurate — nothing about the formalism forbids a system from being in that state, and I can measure it — whereas the philosophers seem to be saying the state can't be rationally *occupied* at all, which is a stronger and weirder claim. Second, the case that started this for me: one of our models will cheerfully emit something like "I'm 90% confident it rains, but I don't believe it" when you probe it in certain framings, and my gut says that's a broken verbalizer, nothing deep. Tell me whether my toolkit's inability to distinguish a broken verbalizer from genuine Moorean incoherence is a hole in the toolkit, or evidence that there was never much of a difference to find.

Close with a verdict for each bucket: is the residue a defect of my framework, or a sign that the original claim was less clear than it sounded when said out loud?
