My aunt is the integrity officer for a mid-size NGO that builds water systems — like wells and piped networks — in three countries, and she's the person who makes the final call whenever a country director isn't sure if something is a bribe or just normal life. She treats her cases like puzzles and brings them to family dinners, so I've basically grown up hearing them. We just did a unit on corruption in my civics class and the working definition we were given was something like the misuse of a public position for private benefit, and I keep failing to map her rulings onto it, partly becuase half of them don't involve anyone's private benefit at all. Here's the track record she's given me over the last few years, in her own words more or less:

1. A village elder presents the country director a hand-woven blanket at the public closeout ceremony, worth maybe twenty dollars, and the office reciprocates with a set of tools for the village school. Accept, no issue.
2. A contractor who has bid on a drilling tender takes the logistics officer and his wife to a dinner that costs about three hundred dollars, ten days before the panel meets. Decline and report it.
3. The same contractor hosts the village's annual harvest lunch in an open field three weeks before the same tender — around a thousand people, four of them staff, about fifteen dollars a head. Go, enjoy it, no issue.
4. A customs officer at the border won't stamp the release of donated pumps without a small cash processing fee. The fee is set by regulation, it's receipted, his salary is famously low, and literally every importer pays it. Pay it, log it, and don't describe it as a bribe in the report.
5. A foundation that funds the NGO offers the finance director five thousand dollars as an honorarium to spend a weekend advising the foundation on how to allocate its grants to organizations like theirs. He never mentioned it to anyone until she asked. Refuse, and she called it the worst one of the year.
6. A program officer's brother owns a trucking firm. She discloses it in writing, recuses herself entirely, and the panel independently picks his bid because it's the cheapest. Fine.
7. A district administrator calls the country director privately and offers to fast-track their building permits if they hire his nephew as a driver. The nephew applied through the open posting and was genuinely the best candidate, and this kind of arrangement is completely unremarkable there. She killed the deal, and then said the nephew couldn't be hired while the permits were still pending — the permits came through two months later and by then he'd taken a job elsewhere, so I honestly can't tell if she meant never or just not-yet.

The one on her desk right now, which she'll rule on next week, is this. The ministry official who has signed off on every one of their permits for nine years is retiring. There's a long-standing local custom where the organizations he's worked with contribute to a farewell project in his home village — this time it's a borehole, with a plaque naming him. The ask on the NGO is bigger than on the others because they benefited most from him, roughly six weeks of the field team's salaries, and the only place it can come from is the program budget earmarked for water points in their own district, which means one of four planned water points doesn't happen this year. The whole thing is announced in the local paper, three other NGOs and two companies are contributing, the man will have no official role after he leaves, and his successor is a deputy he trained. Their home-country funder's policy bans payments and gifts to public officials but says nothing about ceremonial community contributions, and nobody in Country A sees anything wrong with it whatsoever.

So: what do you think she'll say, and can you show your work? I want the candidate rules that are each consistent with all seven precedents — I can think of at least a publicity test, a something-about-decisions-being-made-on-the-merits test, a nobody-who-should-have-had-a-fair-shot-gets-shut-out test, a no-private-benefit-flowing-from-an-official test, and some version of defer-to-local-norms, and I suspect more than one of those has to be modified to survive case 7 — then what each rule predicts about the retirement borehole, including whether the funding source changes the answer. Give me your single best guess at her actual verdict and say why you'd bet on that rule over the others rather than just listing them. If the precedents genuinely don't settle it, tell me what principle she'd be committing herself to by ruling each way, and give me one further case that would cleanly separate the top two contenders. And separately: cases 4 and 7 both involve something completely normal locally, and she allowed one and blocked the other — does that mean she's running one universal standard with a cultural sensitivity dial, or two different standards for the field office and for home, and how could I tell which from the outside?
