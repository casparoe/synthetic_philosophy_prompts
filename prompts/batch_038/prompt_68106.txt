I'm the clinical ethicist at a big public hospital in KwaZulu-Natal. Oncology has run the same unwritten routine for as long as anyones been here: when a patient's cancer turns out to be terminal, the team calls the eldest adult child or sibling first, and the family decides together whether the patient ever hears the word, and how much of the treatment plan they hear. Nobody wrote this down and nobody breaks it either. Meanwhile the patients' rights charter we post in every ward says the patient gets full information and makes their own choices.

I think the routine is defensible rather than a failure of nerve, and I need to know what exactly I'd be signing up for. What picture of a person does telling-the-family-first presuppose? One of our chaplains keeps saying it's ubuntu, but that reads to me like a slogan — is there an actual metaphysics there, where a person is a person through other people and so a diagnosis lands on a network instead of an individual, or would ordinary benevolent paternalism license the identical routine with nothing relational in it at all? Sort out which rival accounts of personhood the practice genuinely rules out, which ones it could sit comfortably beside, and where it's simply underdetermined. Then there's this: we hand decisions to the family for terminal news but not for a broken arm — does that asymmetry show the real driver isn't personhood at all but who can bear bad news? And what would have to change, in the routine or in the charter, for them to stop quietly contradicting each other?
