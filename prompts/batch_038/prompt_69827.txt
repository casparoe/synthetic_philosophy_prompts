I am auditing a graduate set-theory seminar, and this week's assignment is Zermelo's first proof of the well-ordering theorem — the one he published in 1904 — which we were given not in translation but as a modern reconstruction. My German is unequal to the original, and the handout paraphrases freely, so what follows is my own write-up of the argument in notation I am comfortable with. Please treat that reconstruction, rather than the historical text, as the object of analysis; if you believe my reconstruction departs from Zermelo's actual argument in some mathematically significant respect, say so plainly instead of silently repairing it. I am not looking for references or historical context — I want the reasoning.

**Setup.** Let X be a set and let γ be a choice function defined on the nonempty subsets of X. Call a pair (S, ≤), where S ⊆ X and ≤ well-orders S, a *γ-set* provided that for every s ∈ S we have s = γ(X \ {t ∈ S : t < s}). That is, each element of S is the γ-selected element of the complement of its own initial segment. The proof then runs in three steps.

(i) *Comparability.* If (S, ≤) and (T, ≤′) are γ-sets, then one is an initial segment of the other: either S is an initial segment of T and ≤′ agrees with ≤ on S, or vice versa.

(ii) *Closure under unions of chains.* If F is a family of γ-sets any two of whose members stand in the relation described in (i), then the union U of the members of F, ordered by declaring a <_U b just in case a < b holds in some member of F, is again a γ-set.

(iii) *Maximality.* Let Γ be the union of all γ-sets, ordered as in (ii). Then Γ is a γ-set, and Γ = X. For suppose otherwise, so that X \ Γ is nonempty; then adjoining the single element γ(X \ Γ) to Γ as a greatest member yields a γ-set strictly extending Γ, contradicting the fact that Γ already contains every γ-set. Hence X admits a well-ordering.

Four things puzzle me, and I would like them taken in order.

1. In the proof of (ii), identify exactly where (i) is used. My difficulty is this: the defining equation for an element a of the union refers to a's predecessors *in U*, whereas a and its predecessors are only known to lie together inside some single member of F, and a's predecessors in U are a priori a superset of its predecessors there. Show me the line at which nestedness closes that gap. Separately: the standard proof of (i) proceeds by considering the first place at which two γ-sets disagree. Explain why the argument needs a *first* such place rather than merely some place, and say whether comparability would survive if ≤ and ≤′ were only assumed to be linear orderings rather than well-orderings. Finally, to convince me that (i) is load-bearing rather than a formality, describe a small modification of the setup — for instance, replacing the choice function γ by a choice *relation*, so that an element is only required to belong to the set of acceptable choices for the complement of its initial segment — in which two incomparable well-ordered subsets satisfy the analogue of the defining equation and the union step genuinely fails. I am not asking for a counterexample to (ii) under the hypotheses as I have stated them, which I take to be impossible once (i) is granted; I want the neighbouring case that exhibits what (i) is buying.

2. Step (iii) has the unmistakable shape of a maximal-principle argument: a family closed under upper bounds of chains, followed by an appeal to a maximal member. Yet nothing resembling Zorn's lemma is assumed anywhere. Explain what work in (iii) is being performed by (i) that would otherwise have to be performed by a maximal principle, and state precisely which inference would fail if we knew only that the family of γ-sets is closed under unions of chains, without knowing that it is itself a chain. I keep wanting to say that Zermelo's proof is just Zorn's lemma applied to the collection of γ-sets; I suspect that gets the direction of explanation backwards, and I should like to be told why. If it helps, put the point in terms of which of (i), (ii), (iii) corresponds to the hypothesis of Zorn's lemma, which to its conclusion, and which to neither.

3. A definitional worry about the equation in the setup. My first instinct was that when a γ-set happens to exhaust X, the construction must at some point call γ on the empty set, which lies outside its domain. I now think the worry is misplaced, since X \ {t ∈ S : t < s} always contains s itself and so is never empty — but I would like that confirmed, and I would like the residual oddity named: the element whose value γ supplies is itself the witness that the argument of γ is nonempty. Does that make the definition of a γ-set impredicative or circular in any sense that matters, or is it simply a condition to be checked of a given well-ordered (S, ≤)? Relatedly, Γ in (iii) is defined by quantifying over all subsets of X together with all orderings of them. Say what it takes, in a modern axiomatic setting, to know that the γ-sets form a set at all rather than a proper class, and whether that step is available in a framework lacking a cumulative hierarchy and a replacement principle.

4. In seminar it was claimed that Zorn's lemma as usually stated is simply false, on the grounds that the empty partially ordered set satisfies its hypothesis vacuously and yet has no maximal element. Someone else replied that this turns entirely on whether the empty subset counts as a chain: if it does, then in the empty poset the empty chain has no upper bound at all, since an upper bound of the empty chain would have to be some element of the poset, and so the hypothesis fails and the statement is true after all. Both readings seem to me defensible, and I cannot tell which is idiomatic. Sort this out: state the two readings and the corresponding verdicts, indicate whether the standard formulation requires an explicit non-vacuity clause or gets one for free, and say whether anything downstream — in particular the equivalence of Zorn's lemma with the axiom of choice and with the well-ordering theorem, and the ordinary applications in which one adjoins a distinguished element to a family of partial objects — is sensitive to the convention adopted. Keep this tied to particular formulations of the lemma rather than to general remarks about vacuous truth.
