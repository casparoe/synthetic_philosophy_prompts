My aunt retires next month after 38 years as the county archivist and I've been asked to give the toast at her dinner. Its a mess of an occasion, because the same night the historical society is unveiling a donor-funded heritage trail — twelve bronze plaques around town — and my aunt spent two years fighting the wording and lost. Plaque four calls a lumber baron a visionary who "saw the city this town would become," and the family that paid for it is sitting at the front table.

She was a Butterfield person to her bones, kept a falling-apart copy of the little book in her desk drawer. Her rule, which she enforced on every volunteer who ever wrote a label: you don't say what something led to. You say what it looked like from where they were standing in 1893, with no idea what was coming. No "first," no "the beginnings of," no adjectives quietly doing the work of a plot. She once cut a caption of mine from ninety words to eleven and it was much better.

So: four minutes, affectionate, one real laugh early. It has to land for the three actual historians in the room without turning into a lecture about historiography, and it has to let the plaque donors keep their dignity — she never thought the whig instinct was wicked, only lazy, and I don't want to overrule her on that. It also can't cheat by promising that her standards will win out eventually; that's precisely the move she objected to. One of the twelve plaques is hers. Work that in.

After the speech, tell me briefly where you felt yourself reaching for a progress-shaped sentence and what you did instead.
