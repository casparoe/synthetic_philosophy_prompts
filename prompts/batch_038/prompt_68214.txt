I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine — philosophy, psych, the occasional behavioural-economics dust-up. Working on a piece about the "can someone be too good?" argument, the one that runs: a person really devoted to doing good would end up a bore who's given away everything that made a life worth living. Nearly everyone I've interviewed on that side of it makes the same move, and I cant find a name for it, which is driving me nuts.

The move: asked why a life spent mostly making strangers better off would be a worse life, they point at one specific non-moral thing of their own. The novel they've been failing to write for nine years. The garden. A Sunday long run with two friends. And they say it in a precious register — that's what makes the life mine, take it away and there's nothing left to be good *for*.

Then two things happen. First, the same people give up the exact same thing for a promotion, or a move for a partner's job, or a book deal with a hard deadline, and nobody — not them, not me — reaches for the "my life would be less worth living" line. It gets traded, quietly, for money and status all the time. Second, if you offer to just pay for it (a funded year off to write the novel, so the moral demand is off the table), most of them take the money and *still* say the demand was unreasonable.

And you never get a number. Ask what the novel is worth against, say, restoring sight to a few hundred people, and the question gets waved off as a category mistake, treated as a profound point rather than a convenient one — even though the claim being defended is flatly comparative: more of this, less of that, worse life overall.

So: is there an established concept for a value that gets invoked only against moral demands and never against prudential ones, is never priced, and survives being paid for? I know it's adjacent to the whole is-morality-too-demanding debate, but that's about the demand; this is about the shield. It's also not quite sour grapes — nothing's been taken away from them — and not quite ordinary rationalisation, since they aren't lying, and would be horrified to be caught treating the novel as a bargaining chip.

Give me a committed answer, not a menu. Tell me the two or three nearest existing concepts and exactly where my description stops matching each, then say which one I should use in print. If nothing fits, coin one, give me a definition short enough to survive editing, and give me the boundary cases — what sits just above and just below the line, so I know when *not* to reach for it. A name I can't define is useless to me.
