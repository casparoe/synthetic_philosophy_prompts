For an edited volume I am preparing on the relationship between Victorian moral philosophy and the emerging science of mind, I need a second opinion on an interpretive thesis that I have been unable to settle by argument alone. I should like your credence in it, together with the reasoning that produces that credence.

The thesis, which I will call the Compensation Thesis, is this: Sidgwick's long commitment to psychical research — his role as a founder and first president of the Society for Psychical Research, his sittings with mediums, his willingness to treat certain anomalous classes of testimony as credible while remaining unmoved by survivalist claims generally — was substantially motivated by the hope that empirical inquiry might supply what *The Methods of Ethics* could not supply philosophically, namely grounds for believing that an individual's own greatest happiness and the greatest happiness generally are made to coincide by the constitution of things, rather than being reconcilable only by a theistic postulate he took himself to be entitled merely to hypothesize. On this reading the psychical research is not a private eccentricity running alongside the moral philosophy but an attempted repair of its concluding impasse, pursued by the only route Sidgwick thought still open to him after he had abandoned the a priori one.

Treat the thesis as having a determinate truth value: it is true just in case that hope figured among Sidgwick's substantial motives, where "substantial" means that its removal would have materially reduced the intensity or duration of his involvement. Please then give me:

1. Your overall credence, as a single number.
2. A decomposition into four to six sub-propositions whose assessment jointly yields that number. Candidates I have in mind, which you may revise, replace, or supplement: that the concluding impasse of *The Methods of Ethics* was for Sidgwick a live and deeply distressing problem rather than a dialectical concession to intuitionist readers; that he took empirical evidence of the sort psychical research promised to be capable in principle of bearing on the question of a moral world order; that his persistent refusal to endorse survival claims is better explained by demanding evidential standards than by the absence of the underlying hope; that the motivational claim survives the availability of rival sufficient motives — scientific housekeeping against fraud and credulity, obligations to a Cambridge circle of collaborators and to his wife, and a genuine interest in the frontier between psychology and physiology. Assign each a rough probability and one or two lines of justification.
3. The rule by which you combine them, and why that rule. Note the structural difficulty I want you to handle explicitly rather than paper over: the rival-motives term is not a further conjunct but a competitor, so it subtracts from rather than multiplies into the total. Say how you have represented that, and whether your combination is a product, a noisy disjunction, or a weighted judgment.
4. A sensitivity report: which term your final number depends on most, and roughly how far the total would move under a plausible revision of that term alone.
5. The Compensation Thesis is routinely conflated with two weaker neighbours — that Sidgwick personally wished to survive death, and that he regarded psychical research as legitimate science. Tell me how much of the spread in your overall credence is attributable to each of these, and where you think conflation with them does the most damage, in either direction.
6. Two of my readers already disagree sharply: one puts the thesis at about 0.30, the other at about 0.72. Given your decomposition, identify the term on which their disagreement most likely turns, and say what kind of consideration — not which document — each is probably weighting differently.
7. The one consideration that would most raise your number and the one that would most lower it, with the direction and rough magnitude of each.
8. Finally, a second-order estimate: the probability that further archival or documentary material could settle the thesis either way, as against its being permanently underdetermined because Sidgwick appears never to have stated the motive in those terms.

Two constraints. Do not supply a bibliography, citations, or page references; I will add those myself, and an invented one is worse than none. And do not smooth over the difference between uncertainty about the historical record and uncertainty about the interpretation: where a term is weak because you do not know what Sidgwick said or did, say so, and where it is weak because reasonable readers can weigh known evidence differently, say that instead. If you judge the thesis too vague to admit a single number, do not refuse it — state the precisification you are adopting and give the number for that.
