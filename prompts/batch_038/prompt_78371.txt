I'm writing up the conceptual overlap between Kuhnian incommensurability and few-shot concept induction, and I'd like a short story — roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words — that stages the tension rather than expounding it.

Premise: two research groups that have never communicated are each handed the same corpus of one hundred Bongard-style problems (twelve figures per problem, six exemplifying the target concept and six failing to) and each produces its own solution manual. A translator is later hired to reconcile the manuals. For most problems the reconciliation is trivial: the groups state superficially different rules that are easy to recognize as paraphrases. But for one problem they sort all twelve figures identically while offering rules that cannot be translated into one another at all — each group's rule is keyed to a feature for which the other group's vocabulary has no term, and each insists that what the other names is not a genuine feature but an artefact of how the panels were drawn.

Tell it from the translator's point of view across a single afternoon session. Please give at least one of the problem panels enough concrete description that a reader could in principle attempt to solve it, and show the translator trying — and only partly succeeding — to render one group's rule in the other's terms. The moment of failure should feel less like a disagreement than like a duck-rabbit figure that refuses to flip for one of the two viewers. I want the story to leave genuinely open whether the groups are talking past each other or describing one rule twice, and to make the reader feel the pull of both readings; no character should be allowed to settle the question.
