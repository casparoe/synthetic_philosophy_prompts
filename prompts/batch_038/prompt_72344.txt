Our county history museum runs four to six temporary exhibits a year plus a permanent core gallery, on a modest endowment, with a review board that includes the curator of record and, for any exhibit touching a living community, at least one advisor drawn from it. We have no settled method for what I will call the standards problem, and it now consumes more staff time than any other single issue.

The problem has a stable shape. Every draft that reaches my desk gets pulled two ways. One set of objections says we sanitize: that we reconstruct the past so thoroughly in its own terms that the harm disappears from view, and that "context" has quietly become our house word for "exoneration." The other set says we moralize: that we arraign eighteenth- and nineteenth-century people under standards they could not have entertained, that our labels have turned into indictments, and that the whole exercise is presentism of the sort the discipline spent a century learning to resist. Both objections are sometimes correct — occasionally about the same panel, in the same paragraph. At present we settle these matters by whoever argues hardest in the room.

I would like a decision procedure my curators can run on a draft — a wall label, a panel set, a catalogue essay — before it goes to the board. Specifically:

- The questions, in order, that the writer asks of the draft, with an explicit statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each and what an insufficient one looks like.
- A short fixed set of dispositions the procedure can terminate in (leave as drafted; state plainly; attribute the framing rather than assert it; supply a contextual note; restructure the panel; escalate), so that a curator knows what she is allowed to conclude.
- The distinctions that must survive translation into a staff tool. I care most about the difference between importing our present interests into the choice of what to write about at all, importing our vocabulary into the explanation of conduct, and importing our appraisal into the judgment of persons — and about the difference between anachronism and evaluation, which my board treats as one word. Tell me which of these distinctions can be dropped for usability and which cannot be dropped without breaking the tool.
- A step that tests whether the appraisal on offer was already available in the actors' own world: whether their contemporaries voiced it, whether their own religious, legal, or civic frameworks contained the resources to condemn them. Be concrete about who performs that check, how long it takes, and what the procedure does when we lack the time, the language, or the expertise to perform it.
- The points at which the procedure stops at "no change to the draft," and the points at which it hands the question to the board because the issue concerns our relationships and our values rather than the past.
- Whether the procedure needs a separate step for the case where this institution is itself a party to the history it is judging — when our building, our name, and our endowment are downstream of the conduct on display. If it does, say what such a step can realistically require and what it cannot.
- Honest marking of which steps are judgment wearing a procedural costume. I would rather have three flagged judgment calls than twelve fake decision rules.
- What the procedure assumes about whether we can step outside our own standards at all. My suspicion is that we cannot, and that a tool promising otherwise is selling something. If you agree, say what the procedure is actually managing rather than eliminating, and describe the characteristic ways that management fails silently.

Then run it on three drafts in progress. Treat them as given; do not go looking for real-world parallels or sources.

One: our entrance wall. The building was endowed in the 1890s by a local industrialist whose fortune rested substantially on leased convict labor supplied to his mills — men, overwhelmingly Black, convicted on trivial charges and worked to death under state contract. The draft calls him a self-made philanthropist of the New South and describes his mills. A community advisory group wants the convict-lease basis stated on the entrance wall itself. Two board members say that putting it there is precisely the presentist gesture we should refuse, that he was a man of his time, and that the wall will cost us donors. The procedure must tell me what the wall says.

Two: a panel on the witchcraft prosecutions in this county in the seventeenth century. The draft explains the accusations through harvest failure, local indebtedness, and the targeting of marginal women, and calls the accused victims. The defendants' and magistrates' own framework — the reality of witchcraft, the covenant, the duty to root it out — appears only in a sidebar headed "What They Believed." Is the sidebar structure legitimate, is the explanatory vocabulary ours and must it be marked as ours, and is "victim" an anachronism or a plain truth?

Three: a centenary exhibit on a local women's suffrage campaign. The organizers' published appeals argued that enfranchising white women would improve the quality of the electorate and offset the votes of newly enfranchised Black men. The board, which includes a donor descended from the campaign's leader, wants the exhibit to center these women's courage and to note the racism briefly in the closing panel as a reflection of the era. A local branch of a Black women's club has told us that this arrangement repeats the erasure. For the record, my own inclination is the board's: keep the campaign legible as a campaign, and handle the appeals in the closing panel. If the procedure rules against me, I want it to say so and to show which step forces the result.

Assume the user holds a master's in history, has read none of the historiographical debate, and has about ninety minutes per exhibit. Close with a one-page version a curator can keep at the desk. No bibliography, no citations, no named works — I want the concepts, not the reading list.
