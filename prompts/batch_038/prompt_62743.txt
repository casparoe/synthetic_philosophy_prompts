You are serving as the grader for a graduate epistemology course. Below are the essay question as it was issued to students and one submitted answer. Please grade the answer.

**Question (25 points; 50 minutes; closed book).**

Dana is a radiologist thirty hours into a shift. Two pieces of information bear on her present situation.

(i) Her department's fatigue-monitoring system, which validation studies she has read show to be highly accurate about the cognitive states it attributes, currently flags her. The flag reports that readers in the state it attributes to her miss hairline fractures at roughly twice their unflagged rate. The flag makes no claim about any particular image.

(ii) Dana also recalls an internal audit reporting that among flagged readers who nevertheless recorded high confidence in a finding, the finding was confirmed on follow-up in roughly four cases out of five, against a baseline of roughly nine in ten for unflagged readers.

Dana looks at the scan. The appearance of a hairline fracture is vivid to her, and she can correctly state the criteria she is applying. She is highly confident that there is a fracture.

(a) [8 points] For each of (i) and (ii), say what the information bears on, and explain why neither is straightforwardly evidence about whether this patient's bone is fractured. What, if anything, is the difference in evidential role between (i) and (ii)?

(b) [10 points] Suppose that in response to (i) Dana comes to believe that her high confidence is irrational while retaining that confidence. Explain precisely what this level-split amounts to, why some epistemologists think that evidence of the kind in (i) licenses it, and what it would show about the relation between rationality and coherence if level-splitting were sometimes rationally permissible. Then evaluate the claim that the enkratic requirement of rationality rules out the level-split in Dana's case.

(c) [7 points] The enkratic requirement can be read narrowly, as the principle that if an agent believes that she ought not believe p, then she ought not believe p; or widely, as the principle that rationality requires that an agent not both believe p and believe that she ought not believe p. Does your verdict in (b) depend on which reading is adopted? Explain.

**Submitted answer.**

(a) Both (i) and (ii) are information about Dana and readers like her rather than about the patient's bone. Neither describes anything about the image, and neither is evidence that there is or is not a hairline fracture. (i) reports a defect in her present condition: in the flagged state she misses such fractures twice as often as she otherwise would. It is therefore evidence about the trustworthiness of her judgment, which is what makes it higher-order. (ii) is the same kind of information with the opposite sign, since it reports that confident readers who have been flagged are right four times in five. Because (i) and (ii) bear on the same quantity — the probability that a confident reading by Dana in her current state is correct — and pull in opposite directions, they very largely offset each other. What is left for Dana is her ordinary first-order evidence: the vivid appearance of the fracture and her correct grasp of the criteria. That evidence is, as far as she can tell, exactly as good as it was before the flag, so her high confidence is unimpaired and calls for no revision.

(b) A level-split occurs when an agent holds a first-order belief while also believing, at a higher level, that this belief is irrational. Dana believes that there is a fracture, and she also believes that her believing it is irrational. The two conjuncts are not formally inconsistent, because one concerns the bone and the other concerns her own epistemic standing; that is why the split looks possible rather than self-refuting, and why its oddness, though real, is not obviously the oddness of a contradiction. Those who think evidence like (i) licenses the split press exactly this point: the flag is not evidence about the fracture, and from the fact that a belief is poorly formed it does not follow that the belief is false, so Dana's visual evidence remains as vivid as it was and she may hold on to it while conceding that holding on is unreasonable. If level-splitting were sometimes rationally permissible, it would show that rationality does not require an agent's attitudes to cohere across levels: she could satisfy every requirement applicable to each level taken on its own while being criticizable in the combination, which would mean that coherence requirements must be formulated level by level rather than over an agent's attitudes as a whole. The enkratic requirement is the principle that one's belief must fit one's evidence — that one must not believe p while one's evidence makes p improbable. Since the flag leaves Dana's evidence about the fracture untouched, her evidence still makes the fracture probable, and so the enkratic requirement does not rule out the split in her case.

(c) It does depend on the reading. On the narrow reading the requirement is conditional: it engages only if Dana holds the second-order belief, and, so long as she retains that belief, it directs her to give up the first-order belief rather than the other way round. The narrow reading thus rules the split out determinately. On the wide reading, by contrast, rationality requires only that Dana not be in the combination; it says nothing about which attitude must go. An agent who is in the combination but is disposed to relinquish one of the two attitudes on reflection therefore does not violate the wide-scope requirement, which leaves room for the level-split as a temporary or transitional state. My conclusion in (b), that the split is not ruled out, accordingly holds only under the wide reading.
