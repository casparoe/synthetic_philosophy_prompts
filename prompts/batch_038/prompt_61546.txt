For the past two cycles I have done the compliance and bookkeeping side of a federal House campaign, which means I spend my evenings classifying other people's generosity, and one pattern in the rules has stopped making sense to me in a way I cannot articulate without vocabulary I mostly lack. Here are the pieces. Uncompensated services by a volunteer are not a contribution at all: the attorney who spends a summer writing policy papers and assembling opposition research for us, gratis, has given us nothing the law counts, however far the commercial value of that summer exceeds what an individual may lawfully contribute per election, and there is nothing to cap, nothing to value, nothing to report. Yet if she performs the identical work while her employer keeps paying her salary, the value of those hours becomes an in-kind contribution from whoever paid her, valued at commercial rates and charged against that payer's limit. In the same spirit, an individual's unpaid online activity on our behalf — posting, list emails, hosting a website — is exempt outright, unlimited and unreportable even when undertaken with our knowledge, while purchasing the same output from a vendor is a capped and reportable contribution. Then there is the candidate's own money, which is formally a contribution but unlimited in amount and merely disclosed: dollars that are perfectly transferable and perfectly priced cease to be the sort of thing the law worries about the moment they belong to the person receiving them, and money a friend hands the candidate to spend on the campaign is not the candidate's personal funds but the friend's capped contribution. The salary cases sharpen the point. What a candidate earns from bona fide employment counts as their own unlimited personal funds, but if an employer keeps paying them while they are out campaigning, compensation exceeding the hours actually worked converts into a contribution from the employer; and corporations, which may not contribute at all, may nonetheless furnish unlimited free legal and accounting services aimed at keeping a committee compliant, provided they pay their own regular staff and hire nobody extra to do it.

What conception of corruption, or of political influence, or of what a campaign even is, does this pattern presuppose? My instinct is that the law is tracking not the quantity of support a candidate receives but the form in which it arrives — priced, arm's-length, transferable value on one side, unpriced personal exertion on the other — but that hypothesis runs straight into the self-finance rule, since a candidate's own fortune is every bit as priced and transferable as anyone else's. So perhaps the operative idea is that corruption requires two distinct parties, an obligation running between a giver and a receiver, which cannot exist between a candidate and herself; or perhaps the tracked variable is dependence, which is how the Court itself frames the interest that contribution limits serve, describing them as weapons against the reality or appearance of improper influence stemming from the dependence of candidates on large campaign contributions — except that dependence is precisely what an unlimited personal fortune and a donated summer of professional labor produce. Take those candidate readings seriously and tell me which the practice strictly requires, as against which merely fit it comfortably, and which conceptions it quietly rules out: equality of political voice (which the courts have said is not a legitimate reason to limit money at all), distortion of the informational environment, unequal access and official gratitude, systemic dependence on concentrated private wealth. I also want an honest accounting of how much of the pattern is philosophy-free — valuation difficulty, administrability, constitutional constraints on regulating speech, exemptions accreted over decades — and whether any residue is left for a theory of corruption to explain, because I suspect most of it is bookkeeping convenience and I would like to know if anything survives. Relatedly: if the volunteer exemption is really serving a value about ordinary participation, does the practice end up depending on a rationale the doctrine officially forbids it from invoking, and is that a live incoherence or a distinction without a difference?

Finally, suppose the presupposed view were replaced by a dependence-based or voice-equalizing one. Tell me concretely what would have to change in the rules I described: would volunteer hours have to be valued and capped, would a self-financing candidate face a ceiling, would disclosure attach to unpaid labor and to whoever is financing the volunteer's life while she performs it, and would the coordination-based line that lets outside groups spend without limit still hold up? End with the single feature of the current pattern that you think is hardest for any coherent account to swallow. Organize the answer as the candidate implicit theories, what each explains and where it breaks, the residual left over after administrative explanations, and then the replacement scenario. I am not looking for legal advice, and I know where the rules live — no case cites or citations needed, just the reconstruction.
