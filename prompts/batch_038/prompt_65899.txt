Two weeks ago I started on the product-risk team at a retail brokerage — big options book, young customer base. My first real deliverable is a two-page brief for a working group that's deciding whether we should ever again do what Robinhood did in January 2021: shut off buying in a name while still letting people sell. The framing that came down from the top already has the words "information cascade" baked into it, like that's settled about what happened. Nobody in that room has read the models and I've only skimmed them, so I want the mapping right before I write a word.

Here's the episode as I have it. Numbers are from memory and approximate — just work with these, don't try to correct me:

- GameStop closed 2020 around $19. It went vertical through January: roughly $65 on Jan 22, roughly $347 on Jan 27, then on Jan 28 it traded up toward $480 intraday and finished down about 44% near $194. Within a week it was back around $50.
- Short interest exceeded 100% of the float for a stretch, which only works because borrowed shares get lent, re-lent, and effectively counted more than once.
- The buying was coordinated out in the open, mostly on one subreddit, with identifiable figureheads making the case on camera for months beforehand. A lot of the discussion was explicitly about squeeze mechanics rather than about GameStop's actual business.
- The biggest short, Melvin Capital, took a $2.75bn investment from Citadel and Point72 on Jan 25 to stay standing and closed the position at a heavy loss days later.
- On Jan 28 several brokers went close-only in GME and a handful of other names; the stated reason was clearing collateral at the DTCC. The SEC's staff looked at all of it afterward and, among other things, didn't find that short selling was the main driver of the run-up.

Now the analysis. Run it through the canonical cascade setup — Banerjee's herd model and Bikhchandani–Hirshleifer–Welch, both 1992 — and be strict about the mapping before you evaluate anything. Who are the players? What private signal does each one actually hold, and about what? What is the "state of the world" the cascade is supposedly tracking — GameStop's fundamental value, the size of the short interest, or something else? What's the action set, and in what order do people move? That last one is where I suspect the whole thing strains: the textbook cascade needs you to watch earlier movers choose in sequence and infer from their choice what their signal was, but an anonymous continuous order book hands you aggregated flow, not a queue of individual decisions. Tell me how much of the model survives that.

Then the part I actually care about, which is a distinction I came up with and want pressure-tested — push back if it's bad. I think there are two entirely different reasons to follow a crowd here and my working group slides between them without noticing:

(a) informational herding: I buy because your buying is evidence about the state, and after enough of you I rationally ignore my own signal.
(b) payoff herding: I buy because your buying changes my payoff directly — a squeeze only pays if enough other people also buy, so the crowd isn't evidence about value, it's part of what creates the value.

If it's (b), GameStop wasn't a cascade in the technical sense at all; it was a coordination game, arguably one whose coordinating device was a public forum plus a named leader, which is closer to a Keynesian beauty contest than to BHW. Work out what each hypothesis predicts differently, especially about the collapse. Under (a) it should be fragile in the specific way the models describe — one contrarian with a strong enough signal, or a public signal that resets everybody's beliefs. Under (b) it should unwind when coordination breaks or when the payoff to being early flips. And treat the Jan 28 buy-halt as the natural experiment it looks like: it amputated the informational channel for exactly the population that was supposedly cascading while leaving the payoff channel mostly intact. Does what happened afterward discriminate between (a) and (b), or is it consistent with both and therefore worthless as evidence? I'd much rather hear "it can't tell them apart" than get a story.

Finally run it the other direction. What does this episode do to the theory — a confirmation, an anomaly the models can absorb with a tweak, or a case they were never built for: retail crowds coordinating publicly in real time on a state variable their own actions help create? And say where the theory goes silent. Is there anything in the professional side of this — shorts copying each other's positioning because being alone is career-risky rather than because of any signal — that the retail-facing models just have no vocabulary for?

One more thing, because this brief will get quoted back at me: seperate cleanly, in a way I could lift straight into the document, what's textbook-settled about cascades from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. Where there's real disagreement — whether cascades happen outside the lab at all, whether a meme-stock episode is even the right kind of case, whether the collapse vindicates or refutes the models — say so plainly and don't dress up one side as the consensus. And flag anywhere you think my (a)/(b) split is too clean.
