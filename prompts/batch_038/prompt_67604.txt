So I was the designated driver at a barbecue last month and ended up being the audience for an argument between Nadia, who owns a small personal training studio, and her brother Ellis, who's a clinical psychologist. It was about Nadia's sales script and I definately couldn't figure out who was right. Here's roughly how it went.

The script: trainers ask a new prospect what they dislike about how they look right now, get them to say a specific goal out loud and write it on a card they initial, and only then bring out the contract, which shows the 12-month price. Cancelling requires showing up in person during a two-hour window on weekdays.

(1) Ellis said the whole thing is manipulation, and offered his test: you manipulate someone when you arrange the choice so that their reflective capacities are offline at the moment of decision — the sequencing, the emotional warm-up, the initialled card. On his view it doesn't matter whether the content is all true.

(2) Nadia said nothing in the script is false and the price is printed on the contract before anyone signs, so by any ordinary use of the word she's persuading. She said his test is so broad it condemns his own job: he gets patients to commit to exposure exercises, books sessions on purpose when they're emotionally raw, and uses homework contracts.

(3) Ellis said the difference is who asked for the device and who it benefits. His patients requested the structure and can end it whenever. Nadia's prospect didn't ask for a commitment card, and he said the cancellation window exists so that most people who want out can't get to it — that's not a commitment device, that's a trapdoor.

(4) Nadia said the window is staffing, not design: three people work the front desk and they're on the floor the rest of the day. She added that inconvenient friction can't be the mark of manipulation, or every streaming subscription on earth is manipulative, including the one Ellis pays for and has never once tried to cancel.

(5) Ellis said there's an asymmetry his objection rides on — Netflix makes leaving roughly as hard as joining, whereas her script front-loads commitment and back-loads friction. Then he conceded that if she offered a genuine 30-day cancellation with one click, he'd probably drop the manipulation charge about the script itself.

(6) Nadia jumped on that: if the concession holds, her content was never the problem, so manipulation should just be defined as false-or-coerced content, and whatever's left over (contract friction) is a different ethical issue that needs a different name.

(7) Ellis said no, that definition empties the concept out — lying and coercion already cover those cases, and then there'd be no word left for things everyone agrees are manipulative, like a charity that shows you a photo of one named child instead of statistics, or a negotiator who announces he's walking away when he has no intention of walking away.

They both looked at me and I said something like "you both sound reasonable," which is the same as saying nothing. Can you go move by move through 1-7 and say who's ahead at each one and why, in a sentence or two each? Flag any move where someone scores a point that doesn't actually answer what was said to them just before — I think at least one does that. Then tell me who's more right overall, whether either of the two tests on the table (offline reflective capacities vs. false-or-coerced content) survives as a general account of manipulation, and what both of them seem to have missed entirely. I'm most curious about move 5, because it felt like the argument quietly changed shape there and neither of them noticed.
