I've spent some years with the wild animal suffering literature, and I think I've finally got the argument into a form that's hard to resist — but I want to be told where it actually breaks, not merely where it's uncomfortable.

Here's the shape of it. Most wild animals belong to r-selected species: a single female produces thousands of offspring, and nearly all of them are dead within days. Whatever else is true of it, a life consisting of a few days of exposure and ending in being eaten alive is not a life that goes well; so the vast majority of wild lives are net-negative. And if the vast majority of lives are net-negative, then any view that aggregates welfare across lives has to say that the wild is a negative quantity overall — which makes population reduction, and eventually the elimination of predation, improvements in principle rather than only in theory.

Every objection I encounter is about implementation: we don't understand ecosystems well enough, interventions could backfire, there's hubris in redesigning nature. Those read to me as prudence, not principle; "we might do it badly" can't be turned into "we shouldn't aim at it."

So: is there a principled error in the arithmetic itself, or is every real objection a practical one? Give me a definite verdict with your reasons for it, not a survey of positions and a choice left to me.
