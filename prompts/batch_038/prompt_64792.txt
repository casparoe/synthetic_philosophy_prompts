Last term our department's reading group on inductive inference split down the middle over a question I have not been able to settle since, and the two principals — I shall call them N. and R. — have agreed to let an outsider referee it. I was present throughout but have no view of my own to defend; what follows is my reconstruction of the exchange, move by move.

(1) N. opened by claiming that Solomonoff induction is what Occam's razor becomes when it is made precise instead of merely repeated: one sums over the programs that could have emitted the data observed so far, weighting each by two raised to the power of minus its length in bits. Shorter hypotheses therefore receive greater prior weight, N. said, not because anyone has legislated that simplicity is a virtue, but because there are fewer of them; and the resulting predictor can be shown to converge on the truth in the limit and to be optimal, in that nothing else of the same general kind can beat it.

(2) R. replied that a choice has been quietly slipped in. The complexity of a hypothesis is its length in some fixed description language, so the entire construction is relative to a machine; and one could design a machine on which R.'s own favourite account of the data, however baroque, is encoded in a handful of bits. The formalism, R. said, does not expel the arbitrariness from 'simplest' — it relocates it inside the choice of a reference machine, where nobody thinks to look.

(3) N. said this is precisely what the invariance theorem forecloses: for any two universal machines, the corresponding complexity measures differ by at most an additive constant and the corresponding priors by at most a multiplicative one, so the machine cannot matter in the long run. Only a bounded stretch of early evidence is affected. R., N. suggested, was objecting to something the theory had already conceded and absorbed.

(4) R. granted the constant but denied that it is negligible. It is the length of an interpreter for one machine written in the other — a wholly arbitrary quantity — and for any finite body of evidence, which is the only kind there ever is, two hypotheses whose complexities differ by less than that constant are ranked by stipulation alone. R. added that 'convergence in the limit' is a promise that never falls due, and that the guarantee is conditional on the truth lying within the class of computable hypotheses to begin with; were the world incomputable, or computable but of enormous complexity, the guarantee would be empty.

(5) N. said the circularity charge misses, because the optimality claim is a dominance claim: the universal prior dominates every enumerable semimeasure up to a multiplicative constant, and that dominance is all that 'nothing can do better' was ever meant to assert. It is comparative, not absolute, and it does not depend on any finite prefix of the data.

(6) R. said dominance is purchased with an assumption that is itself inductive. The apparatus presupposes that the data were generated by a computable process and generated in the manner the universal prior supposes. On R.'s reading, Solomonoff induction does not solve the problem of induction; it packages and labels it, which is a real achievement but not the one N. had advertised.

(7) N. accepted much of (6) yet drew the opposite conclusion: once the assumption is stated outright, the residual question answers itself. Given that the data issue from some program fed a random input tape, weighting by two to the power of minus description length is not a further postulate about simplicity at all — it is simply what a fair-coin input measure induces.

(8) R. said this relocates the postulate rather than discharging it, since 'feed the machine fair coin flips' is at least as substantive and considerably less transparent than 'prefer simpler hypotheses', and an alternative input distribution, or a uniform measure over programs up to some length bound, would induce a different weighting without contradiction. R. then drew a practical corollary: the ideal predictor is uncomputable, so what is actually deployed are minimum-description-length and minimum-message-length approximations, and no computable approximation can inherit the dominance property — whence invoking Solomonoff in principle while working with MDL in practice is an attempt to hold both ends of a rope that has been cut in the middle.

(9) N.'s closing move was that an unattainable ideal can still serve as the correct norm, because it tells an agent in which direction and by how much to err; the incomputability of the exact prior is therefore beside the point, no more discrediting than the impossibility of writing down most real numbers discredits analysis.

I should be grateful for a proper adjudication rather than a summary. Please take (1) through (9) in order and for each assign a rating — clearly right, substantially right but overstated, substantially mistaken, clearly mistaken, or something between — with a brief justification that engages the technical content rather than the rhetoric. Then tell me whether the two are talking past one another anywhere, and identify anything of consequence that both have overlooked. Two questions interest me particularly: whether R.'s machine-relativity objection retains whatever force it has after (3), given that the residual threat may concern the choice of primitive vocabulary rather than the choice of machine; and whether N.'s reply at (7) genuinely discharges the worry raised at (6) or merely restates it in the language of input measures.
