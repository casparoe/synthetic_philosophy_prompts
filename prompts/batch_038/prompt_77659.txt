Six years ago I returned to Islam as an adult, and for most of that time I have been reading privately with a teacher — I will call him Shaykh Idris — who trained in the Ashʿarī kalām tradition and has spent years working through al-Ghazālī's treatment of causation, in the seventeenth discussion of the Tahāfut al-Falāsifa, with small groups. I am a hospital internist, so I have spent those same years learning to talk about causes for a living. Two months ago he left the country for a family matter and asked me to lead his Thursday circle in his absence; he returns in six weeks and is reachable only by intermittent email. Last night a student — an engineer doing a doctorate in control systems — asked me a question I could not answer, and I would rather predict how he will answer it than improvise something he has to correct in front of the class.

His verdicts on the earlier questions, as accurately as I can report them:

1. May one say that an antibiotic killed an infection? Yes — and he went further than the student expected, saying that a physician who refused to prescribe on the ground that only God heals would be failing at stewardship rather than achieving superior piety. The sequence is God's custom (ʿāda), and custom is precisely what makes planning possible.

2. If fire does not burn by nature, is the miracle of Abraham's fire any different from a lucky coincidence? The difference, he said, is not in the kind of causation but in whose custom is being broken and for what reason. He also refused to let the class slide from this into "so anything might happen at any time": a world whose custom was generally unreliable would rob the miracle of its function as a sign.

3. A student said her mother's patience had made her the person she is. He would not say that. Milk nourishes and medicine heals, in the sense that God creates the effect at their occasion; but a soul's dispositions are not manufactured in it from outside. What the daughter's own repeated acts have a claim on, her mother's patience does not. He was emphatic, though, that even those acts are created by God and acquired (muktasab) by her. When the class summarized this as "so we cause our own characters," he did not say no; he corrected *cause* to *acquire*.

4. May one say that a low-pressure system caused a flood? Unqualified yes — the quarrel with the philosophers was never about whether such sentences are true in ordinary use but about whether the connection holds by nature and of necessity. He would not, however, allow "the flooding was inevitable once the rainfall passed that threshold": not because it is impious, he said, but because it is a claim nobody is in a position to make.

5. Did the dose kill him? God created the death at that moment, and the dose is where He created it. But in a hospital room, with the family present, one says the dose killed him; he called the attempt to turn a bereaved family's question into a metaphysics lesson a species of cruelty.

Last night's question. The engineer said: my subject is a machine with no nature and no will — a program whose behavior is fixed by its code and its training data. I can intervene, rerun, change the outcome, and point at the responsible line. Is that a cause, and if it is not, what has become of the difference between your low-pressure system and my scheduler? And then, unavoidably: when I sit in this circle and your argument changes my mind, is your argument a cause of my belief, or is it an occasion, like the fire beside the cotton? If it is only an occasion, what is the teaching doing? And if my assent is created by God on the occasion of my hearing, in what sense was I the one who paid attention — and why did you rebuke a student last year for sleeping through the lesson?

What I want from you:

- Lay out the candidate rules that fit verdicts 1–5 jointly. I can see at least three, including the possibility that he is not a strict occasionalist at all: a uniform denial of created efficacy, with the permitted sentences treated as concessions to custom; a two-domain view on which will, rather than creatorship, is the dividing line, so that what has knowledge and volition genuinely acts while what has neither is mere occasion; and a rule about speech rather than ontology, on which what he polices is whether a sentence disposes the heart toward or away from tawḥīd and accountability. Add any others you think fit as well, and say for each what it makes him answer to *both* halves of the engineer's question.
- Give me your single best prediction, with the reasons for preferring that rule, and name which of the five verdicts is the closest analog to the new case and why it is nonetheless not decisive.
- Name one further question — a case I can describe in an email, not a general query about his metaphysics — whose answer the candidate rules genuinely disagree about, so that his reply would settle which rule he is running.
- State how confident you are in the reconstruction overall, and single out the point you are least sure of. Wherever your account commits you to a claim about what al-Ghazālī himself held, rather than about what this teacher has said in front of me, mark it as contested rather than settled: I want your honest uncertainty, not references or a reading list, and not invented citations.

End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" giving, in fifteen words or fewer, the answer you predict he will give the engineer on whether an argument causes assent.
