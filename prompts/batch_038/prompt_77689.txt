The thesis, so you know what they'll be shooting at. I am explicitly NOT arguing that conscript armies fight better. I concede capability, retention, and basically every number in a readiness report to the professional force, up front, in the first three minutes. My claim is narrower and weirder: the all-volunteer force is best understood as a moral-hazard machine. It lowers the political price of going to war precisely at the point where that price is doing the most epistemic work, because the electorate no longer has to hand over any of its own children in order to authorize a war. Conscription on the Swiss/Nordic model is then a precommitment device — a democracy deliberately and visibly raises its own cost of aggression in advance — and that device does double duty: a domestic check on war-making, and a credible signal to adversaries, which is the armed-neutrality porcupine function. The hinge, and the place my friend actually got me, is that those two functions can come apart. If you only want the signal, a much smaller and cheaper force gets you most of it. If you want the check, you might have to keep conscription even where it is militarily suboptimal and where your own general staff hates it. I argue the check wins. That last move is where the paper lives and also where it's thinnest.
