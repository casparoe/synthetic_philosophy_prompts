Help, I got dragged into this by a friend who does formal political theory and I cant find where it breaks.

(1) The jury theorem: if each voter independently has a better-than-even chance p of getting a binary question right, then the majority is more likely to be right than any single voter is, and the majority's reliability goes to 1 as the group gets bigger.

(2) So bigger electorates are better instruments. Adding voters can only help, since every added voter is above .5 and independent of the rest.

(3) Notice what does and doesn't show up in (1): p is the only parameter. Nothing about a voter's information, education, expertise, or how much they've talked to other people enters the theorem, as long as they clear .5. A voter at .51 counts for exactly as much as one at .99.

(4) Deliberation is therefore not neutral but actively harmful — talking to each other makes votes dependent, which is the one thing (1) rules out. Independence isn't something you get by informing people, it's something you get by keeping them apart.

(5) So the reliable procedure is maximal numbers plus minimal contact: random mass sampling, sealed ballots, no campaigning. Information drives are at best a waste and at worst a way of correlating everybody's errors.

(6) Same reasoning kills epistocracy on the spot. Five experts at .9 each are strictly worse than five thousand citizens at .51, because the second group's reliability is approaching 1 and the first group's is stuck where it is.

I don't believe (5) or (6) — expertise obviously matters and I'd rather be judged by specialists than by a crowd — but I can't get out of it without denying (1), which I want to keep, or (2), which looks like it just falls out of (1). So which step actually goes wrong, and what do I lose by giving it up? And do (3)–(6) follow from (1) and (2), or from something extra that's been slipped in alongside them?
