Every year I teach Karen Ann Quinlan and every year I teach it wrong — as a right-to-die case, when what actually interests me is what it does to the badness-of-death debate. Here's the fact pattern I hand out: April 1975, a 21-year-old stops breathing after a party, takes an anoxic brain injury, ends up in a persistent vegetative state on a ventilator. Her parents ask that it be withdrawn, the doctors and the hospital refuse, the New Jersey Supreme Court sides with the father in March '76 but conditions it on his physicians and a hospital ethics committee agreeing there's no realistic prospect of her ever returning to a conscious, cognitive life. Ventilator comes off in May, she keeps breathing on her own, and she lives nine more years in that state before dying in 1985.

So: run that through Epicurus's no-subject argument, the Lucretian symmetry claim, and the deprivation reply — including Nagel's thought experiment about the intelligent adult whose brain injury leaves him the mental life of a contented infant, which is close enough to Quinlan to be uncomfortable. What does each view say about whether those extra nine years were good or bad for her, and if there's a harm, when does it occur? Does the experience requirement force the deprivation theorist to call those years its own kind of misfortune that nobody could feel — and if he refuses, what's the alternative account of her good, and does it survive the symmetry argument? I also want to know what the court's test quietly assumes about what makes a life worth continuing, and where all three theories simply go silent, because I suspect the parts of the case my students can't let go of are parts none of them can touch.
