I write a monthly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and this month I have been given a long-form interview slot with a philosopher-neuroscientist whose work I've been covering for some time. She is best known for a distinctive thesis about pain, which I'll summarize as she states it, so the questions can be pitched at her actual position rather than at a caricature of it:

- The sensory-discriminative character of a pain — its location, intensity, quality — carries no disvalue of its own. Unpleasantness is a separate affective component, and every bit of pain's badness is delegated to it.
- It follows that patients with pain asymbolia, who describe their pains as still present but no longer bothering them, are not enduring a diminished harm. They are enduring no harm at all, and a fully informed asymbolic would have no reason to want the sensation switched off.
- Asymbolia is therefore not primarily a deficit to be repaired but the most informative evidence available about what pain is. She puts it in terms of a lesion functioning as an instrument.
- The near-universal intuition that the asymbolic's pain must still be bad for them is, on her account, a folk-psychological residue: in intact brains the two components are welded together, so we mistake a contingent co-occurrence for a conceptual truth.
- She concedes that some reported cases show residual avoidance behavior, which she attributes to disinhibition from the same damage rather than to surviving unpleasantness.

Draft ten interview questions, in the order I should ask them, moving from a concrete clinical description toward the abstract claims about value. She is a practiced and courteous interviewee whose standard retreat when pressed on intuition is to say that the intuition is exactly what her theory predicts we would have — so please sequence the questions so that move becomes progressively harder to make, with the last question asking her to name a result that would count against her view rather than merely accommodate it.

For each question, add one follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give, and state in a few words what that expected reply is. The follow-up should target the reply, not restate the question.

Cover, at minimum: whether she treats the patients' own reports as evidence about pain's nature or as reports produced by a damaged capacity to report; where she draws the line between asymbolia and cingulotomy patients, morphine analgesia, experienced meditators who report full intensity with little unpleasantness, and the merely stoical; whether delegating all of pain's badness to a separate system leaves any sense in which the sensation itself is bad, and what that does to reasons for relieving pain in others; whether a clinical literature of a few dozen heterogeneous cases — many with additional deficits in language, praxis, or affect generally — can bear the philosophical weight she places on it; and what her view implies for creatures whose affective systems differ from ours or are not yet mature. No warm-up questions, nothing about her career, and each question should open on something specific — a case, a contrast, a piece of data — rather than on an abstraction.
