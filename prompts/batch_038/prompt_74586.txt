my brother and his girlfriend have been going at this for three weeks now, ever since a podcast episode on Athens, and it's spilled over onto me twice. Neither of them is a classicist — he teaches high school civics, she does freight logistics — but they've both read Thucydides in translation and they're not arguing badly. Here's roughly the shape of it, in order.

1. Nils: Mytilene, Sicily, and Socrates aren't three accidents, they're one defect showing up three times, and it's the same procedure each time. Thousands of anonymous voters who pay nothing personally for being wrong. Day one they decree that every adult male Mytilenean dies and the women and children get enslaved. Then they vote to invade an island whose size and population they barely know. Then they kill the one guy whose whole method was making them justify themselves. Nobody's career ended over the first decree.

2. Ada: Paying for being wrong isn't a democracy thing. Nicias argued against Sicily, got made commander of it anyway, and died in a quarry. The men voting had their own sons and their own trierarchies on those ships. A council of ephors or a tyrant's drinking companions bear even less cost and hear even fewer points of view. And the correction mechanism worked: the Mytilene decree was repealed the next morning, and after the Arginusae business the city turned on the men who'd pushed the mass execution through and some of them ran away.

3. Nils: That repeal is the worst example you could possibly pick. Thucydides says the second vote was nearly even, it went by a hair, and the countermanding ship only got to Lesbos first because the wind was behind it and the Mytilenean envoys paid the rowers to eat and row in shifts. Paches had already read the decree out and was getting ready. That's not institutional self-correction, that's a coin flip plus the weather.

4. Ada: On Sicily you're skipping that Athens actually sent a fact-checking mission to Egesta before the vote, and the Egestaeans staged a show for them — borrowed gold and silver plate from all over, Phoenician cities included, and displayed it at banquets as their own until the envoys came home raving. Any regime gets beaten by a good deception op. And the 27-day delay after the lunar eclipse wasn't the assembly at all, it was Nicias, a superstitious aristocrat in the field, sitting on a withdrawal that was already prepared. You can't bill that to the demos.

5. Nils: The vote itself is the problem and Thucydides basically hands you the mechanism: he says that in the end even the people who thought the expedition was a mistake kept their hands down, because voting against it would mark them as badly disposed to the city. That's not deception, that's deliberation failing out loud. A mass anonymous show of hands produces exactly that; a small council facing each other doesn't. And it's the same shape as the first Mytilene decree — the preference expressed wasn't the preference held.

6. Ada: Then explain the nine years. After Sicily, Athens repealed the thousand-talent reserve it had made an offense even to propose touching, rebuilt a fleet from nothing, won a major sea battle off Cyzicus, threw out the Four Hundred, and kept going until Persian money paid for a Spartan navy and Lysander caught the whole fleet beached at Aegospotami with nobody keeping watch. If 415 was the fatal error of the system, the system surviving until 404 is a strange thing for it to be capable of.

7. Nils: Recovery isn't wisdom, and the recovery was paid for by the rowers and the subject cities rather than by the voters. Socrates is the case with no wind in it and no Egestaean plate and no Persian gold. Five hundred and one jurors, convicted by something like thirty votes, then a bigger majority for death after he proposed his penalty should be free meals at public expense. And the amnesty after the Thirty arguably barred them from charging what they actually cared about — his friendship with Critias and Alcibiades — so the political case got laundered into impiety and corrupting the young, and then the jury had to convince itself the invented charge was the real one. That's the epistemics of the whole thing in one image.

8. Ada: Which concedes my point. One execution, by thirty votes, of a defendant who — on Plato's own telling — spent the penalty phase deliberately escalating. The same city let him argue with people in the agora for decades, prosecuted alot of other people on religious grounds too and sometimes won, and did all that while its oligarchic enemies were killing Athenians by the hundreds in a matter of months. Socrates is an outlier, not a system property, and building an indictment on your single most famous outlier is how you get from Thucydides to a conference keynote.
