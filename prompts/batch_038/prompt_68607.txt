You're going to be a poet who has also taught non-classical logic to grad students — equally at home with a pantoum and with a natural deduction proof. Please think carefully about this one before you start writing, it matters a lot to me.

Some background. I'm a backend engineer, not an academic. My wife was diagnosed a bit over two years ago, and every six months since she's had a scan, and every six months the letter has come back with the same four words: no evidence of disease. The oncologists are careful with that phrasing on purpose — its not "cured," and they never write cured. Meanwhile, mostly to stop myself lying awake at 3am thinking about scans, I took an evening course in logic and ended up way down the intuitionist hole: Brouwer, Heyting, the whole proof-as-construction business. And the thing that grabbed me is that in that system you aren't allowed to slide from "not not P" to "P." You can get the double negative, and it just sits there, and it never cashes out into the positive claim. Which is precisely what those letters do.

So I want to give her a poem for the two year mark (we're a few months late, whatever) and I want it to be about that. Not about cancer exactly — about the shape of the sentence.

Form: a pantoum, five quatrains, twenty lines, with the recycling done properly. Lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next; in the final stanza the second line should be the first stanza's third and the fourth line should be the first stanza's first, so the poem closes on the line it opened with. That works out to about ten distinct lines each pulling double duty. Make the opening line the phrase itself, no evidence of disease, so the machinery of the form carries it back at the end into a new position, unchanged.

Two content constraints, and these are the actual point rather than decoration:

1. No negative anywhere in the poem gets promoted to a positive. That phrase never becomes well, free, clean, cured, safe, out of the woods. If a line says something isn't there, absence is all it gets to say. The last stanza should make the reader feel the pressure toward the upgrade and still decline it — that refusal is where the turn would be, except a pantoum doesn't have turns, which is rather the point.

2. The disjunction property. At most one "or" in the whole poem, and it has to be one the poem is genuinely prepared to commit to one side of. No dangling either/or, no "either she's fine or she isn't." If the intuitionists say a proof of a disjunction has to say which, the poem should honour that.

And no jargon in the poem itself. Don't mention intuitionism, Brouwer, Heyting, Kripke, excluded middle, proofs, or logic of any kind. It has to land for someone who's never heard of any of it and who is, in fact, the person it's for. She should be able to read it as a poem about waiting, and only you and I know what the form is doing.

Process: first give me a short bit of brainstorming, four or five lines, saying which structural feature of the pantoum is carrying which logical point and which lines you're planning to make do the returning work. Then the poem. Then two or three sentences of craft note on where you think the analogy between the form and the logic actually holds and where it strains or breaks down — I want to know if I'm overreading my own idea.
