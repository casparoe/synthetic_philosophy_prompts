I am a novelist, four hundred pages into a book about the administration of memory, and I have reached the point where I can no longer tell whether my central device works or whether I have merely grown fond of it. I want to hand that device to someone else — in this case, to you — and read back a story I did not write, so that I can see whether it can bear weight when it is not being carried by my own habits.

The subject of the novel is legibility: how much of a person a state may know, and how much a person may demand that it forget. I have been reading in three traditions that are usually arranged on a single line from freedom to control, and I have become convinced the line is a fraud. The German tradition, whose constitutional court derived a right of informational self-determination from the general right of personality while striking down a national census in the early 1980s, treats being unknown as the condition of a free self, and its jurisprudence holds that a person who has served a sentence has a claim on reintegration that can outweigh the press's interest in naming him. The American tradition inverts this: it is devoted to the transparency of government, and yet it publishes the citizen — court records, booking photographs, registries, everything an aggregator will sell for pocket change — while leaving the individual with no general shield against collection. The Chinese system is usually described abroad as a single citizen score, when its ordinary machinery is far duller: lists of judgment debtors, restrictions on travel, ratings of firms. These are not points on a spectrum. They are different answers to a question the spectrum hides, which is whether a record is a weapon pointed at a person or the ground a person stands on.

There is a related thought I want the story to press against rather than state: the argument that states make populations legible in order to govern them, and the much older one that the deepest injury of the stateless is not being watched but ceasing to be anyone's obligation to remember.

Here is the apparatus. The jurisdiction is invented; do not attempt to match it to a real statute, and invent no case law. In an unnamed northern city, a municipal Office for Personal Records administers a procedure the staff call the Clean Sheet: on application, the state destroys everything it holds about a person, irreversibly, including its own ability to reconstruct it. The procedure carries one condition, and the condition is the whole novel in miniature. The erasure itself is entered. A public register records, for each grant, a name, an address, and a date — never what was erased, only that something was. The blank is legible. A person who vanishes is thereby marked as a person who vanished.

Write me a story of between 2,200 and 3,000 words set in that office across a single morning, in close third person on the adjudicator: Magda Fellner, fifty-four, twenty-two years in the post, competent, neither kind nor cruel. She has two appointments. The first is a man of about forty who has served his time and wants the Clean Sheet and knows precisely what the publicity condition will do to him on his own street, and wants it anyway; you must make his wanting it rational rather than pitiable. The second is a woman the state has never held: unregistered, misregistered, or registered under a name a commercial aggregator abroad assigned to her and will not correct, because nothing obliges it to. She has come to beg for the opposite of what the first man wants. She needs the state to remember her — to hold a durable, contestable, authoritative trace of who she is — and the law that lets people disappear is the same law that lets the office decline to remember. Fellner cannot help her, and cannot say so in words that would satisfy either of them.

The constraints matter as much as the material. No character may explain the procedure to another character who already understands it; the law must arrive only as paperwork, procedure, waiting, and the specific grammar of forms. No courtroom, no hearing, no journalist. No glowing screens, no humming servers, no drones, no oppressive weather, no reference to an algorithm as though it were a mood. The room should be drab, badly lit, and full of paper; the mercy and the violence in it are clerical. Nobody in the office may be a sadist, and at least one official must be shown doing genuinely careful, well-intentioned work. Do not resolve the second woman's case with a loophole, an exception, or a rogue act of kindness by Fellner; if Fellner bends anything, show me the person the bending lands on. Include one physical object that survives the destruction of a record. Render the outcome of the first appointment, if you render it at all, as an action the reader must interpret rather than as a line of dialogue, and do not end on a lyrical summary of what the morning meant.

Give the story a title. Then append an afterword of no more than 150 words answering two questions: which of the two injuries your story finally treats as graver — being known against one's will, or not being known at all — and at which sentence or gesture that commitment becomes visible on the page; and whether the legal machinery is load-bearing or merely atmospheric, that is, whether the story would still stand if the Clean Sheet were replaced by something ordinary.
