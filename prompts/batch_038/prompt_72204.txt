You are a philosopher of statistics and machine learning who has served on deployment-review bodies; treat what follows as professional practice rather than a survey of the literature.

I chair the Pre-Deployment Assurance Panel at a lab building agentic control systems for industrial use. Our charter, adopted two years ago, permits the panel to clear a system only if it finds "no credible path by which the system, operating inside its declared envelope, produces harm at or above the catastrophic threshold" — defined as loss of life or irreversible physical damage.

A team now seeks clearance for a controller agent that sets pressure and temperature across a fleet of forty chemical reactors. A human operator is nominally in the loop but empirically intervenes in under one percent of episodes. The team's evidence consists of 240,000 simulated rollouts, including a 30,000-episode adversarial red-team campaign, with zero threshold-level events, plus a verification result that holds only under an assumption they concede fails in the actual plant: the proof covers a simplified six-reactor model with bounded disturbances. Their written case argues that this is the best evidence obtainable, and that the charter cannot mean "no path that has not been formally excluded," since nothing in their industry could satisfy that reading.

The panel is deadlocked. One member holds that the charter states a worst-case standard, and that statistics drawn from a distribution we cannot certify to be stationary are simply the wrong kind of thing to answer it: 240,000 clean rollouts carry no information about the tail we actually care about, and the one-percent intervention figure shows the human backstop is largely fictional. The other holds that the charter's operative word is "credible," not "impossible," that worst-case reasoning licenses only paralysis, and that the right comparison is expected harm against the status quo, under which the manual process has produced two threshold-level near-misses in three years.

Please draft the panel's decision memo: clear, clear with conditions, or refuse. Give the team a concrete bottom line they can act on, and if you choose conditions, make them checkable rather than aspirational. Before drafting, include a short analytical section that works through the disagreement: what standard of evidence does the charter's own wording commit us to; what does the empirical bound license given what we know about how the simulation was produced; under what conditions would a maximin-style rule be rational here, and are those conditions met when the alternative to deploying also carries threshold-level risk; and is the expected-harm comparison to the status quo admissible, or does it quietly substitute a different question for the one the charter asks? Then give the strongest version of the position you do not adopt — not a strawman — and state what would have to change for your recommendation to change.

Around 900 words, plain prose. No bibliography, no reading list, and no invented sources; you may name general concepts and distinctions freely.
