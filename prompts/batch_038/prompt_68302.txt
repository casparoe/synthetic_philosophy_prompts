I run a once-a-month lunch thing for engineers at my company — usually 10 to 14 people, mostly backend and infra, nobody's taken a philosophy class, and at least two of them show up primed to say ethics is just vibes with extra steps. We get 75 minutes, a whiteboard, and paper/index cards. No budget for props.

Next month I want them to actually feel the pull of the doctrine of double effect — the idea that it can matter whether a bad outcome is the thing you're aiming at versus something you merely know will happen as a side effect of an aim that's otherwise fine. Not have it explained to them. They should run into it and get unsettled before anyone says the name.

My hunch for the mechanism, tell me if it's dumb: since they're engineers, have them write an actual policy spec for a system whose behavior foreseeably hurts specific identifiable people — dispatch, ad auctions, a moderation queue, a fraud-blocker, whatever — and then run a counterfactual check over their own spec. For each branch that produces harm, ask: if that harm just didn't happen, would the action still be worth taking? Some branches fail the test, meaning the harm is load-bearing and the action only makes sense because of it. Some pass. And then the kicker — two teams can end up with identical outcome distributions, identical numbers on a dashboard, and the room should notice they still judge the two specs differently and be unable to say why in terms of consequences. That's the moment I'm after.

What I need from you is the runnable thing, not a reading list for me to catch up on: team split, the constraint that forces the split so both variants get built, what goes on the cards, a minute-by-minute timeline for 75 minutes, how you get them to invent the counterfactual check themselves instead of me handing it over, and a debrief that maps each moment back onto the pieces of the doctrine — including the contrast between redirecting a threat that's already coming and using a person to stop one.

Two more things. First, what predictably goes wrong the first time it's run. My guess: one team writes its spec so narrowly that every branch passes the check and they feel clever, which I suspect is a genuine objection to the whole doctrine rather than a bug in my activity — how do I steer that so it lands as the point instead of as them winning the game? Second, be honest about what the activity structurally can't reproduce. My suspicion is that a written spec makes intention public and inspectable, while in real cases intention is private and self-deceiving, so the exercise makes the doctrine look way more decidable than it actually is. If that's right, tell me what I say in the last five minutes — and if I'm wrong about it, say so and tell me what the real limit is.
