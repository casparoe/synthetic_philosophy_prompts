I'm in an interdisciplinary reading group that's been trying for two years to have one productive conversation about the digger wasp, and we've failed. You know the story: a wasp stings a cricket, paralyzes it, drags it to the mouth of her burrow, leaves it on the threshold while she goes inside to prep the nest, then comes out and hauls it in. If you nudge the cricket a few inches while she's underground, she comes out, drags it back to the threshold, goes in and re-runs the inside routine, and will apparently keep doing this forever rather than just picking it up from where it now is. Philosophers of mind have been retelling it since the eighties — Dennett especially — and I think it's where the word "sphexishness" comes from, as a name for behavior that's all routine and no revision.

Here's the stalemate. The behavioral ecologist in our group says the example is basically folklore: the anecdote gets recycled with nobody able to point at where it came from, insects turn out to be far more plastic than the cartoon allows, and anyway whether some sequence counts as "the same rigid routine repeated" depends entirely on how finely you slice behavior — at a fine enough grain everything is rigid, at a coarse enough grain everything is flexible. So she thinks it's a bad argument wearing a nice story. The philosophers say it doesn't matter whether the wasp is real or whether any species actually does this: the wasp is doing conceptual work, it's a pump for the idea of a system whose control loop is closed to relevant changes in its surroundings, and pointing out that insects are more plastic than we assumed just concedes the point, since plasticity comes in degrees and the interesting question is where the degrees come from. Neither side has moved. Both insist the empirical facts settle it, and they don't mean the same facts by that.

So what kind of disagreement is this, really? Is it verbal — a fight over "rigid," "flexible," and "free" that would dissolve with definitions? Is it two questions hiding under one mascot, one about how behavior is controlled and one about whether anything is up to the agent? Is it a clash of evidential standards, about what an intuition pump has to be for before it's allowed to do philosophical work? Or is there a live empirical question doing all the work that both sides are gesturing at badly?

My own hunch, which I want you to attack if it's wrong: there are two axes being run together — how insensitive a system is to relevant change, and whether its behavour is determined or attributable to it — and the wasp gets drafted into both, so each side keeps answering the objection it isn't being given. If that's right, the cricket doesn't matter and the dispute should have collapsed years ago, which makes me think I'm missing something.

Give me the diagnosis argued for, not a tour of the positions, and then tell me what would actually count as progress here: a distinction, a better exemplar, an experiment, a change of question, or nothing at all — including an honest case that the whole thing is ill-posed and we should dissolve the reading group. Please don't just tell me both sides have a point.
