the episode, so we're working from the same facts: Meriwether starts the fund in Feb '94 out of the old Salomon arbitrage group, with Merton and Scholes among the principals (they collect the Nobel in Oct '97, roughly a year before it all goes wrong). Returns are great — low 20s, then low 40s two years running, then 17% in '97 — and the book is market-neutral relative-value stuff, long the cheap leg of a spread that ought to narrow, short the rich leg: on-the-run vs off-the-run Treasuries, swap spreads, various European government bond spreads, mortgage basis, some equity and volatility trades, all with a huge derivatives overlay. At the end of '97 they hand capital back to investors without shrinking the positions, which pushes leverage to something like 30 to 1. Then Aug '98, Russia devalues and stops paying its debt, everyone runs for liquidity and quality, and the spreads LTCM is long diverge instead of converging — they lose 44% in the month of August alone. They go looking for capital, it becomes public via their letter to investors in early September, and by Sep 18 they're on the phone with the NY Fed. A Buffett-led group actually offers to buy out the partners for $250m and inject $3.75bn, and it dies on a deadline. On Sep 23 the Fed gets fourteen banks and brokerages to put up $3.6bn for 90% of the fund, mainly because they're terrified of everyone exiting those positions into the same market at once. The consortium unwound it over the next year, got its money back by end of '99, and the spreads largely came home.

the lens I want is Keynes, but not the Knightian-uncertainty / fat-tails / hubris version everyone reaches for, and not animal spirits used as a shrug. Two pieces of him specifically. First, the 1921 book on probability, whose real move is to separate probability from weight of argument — how well the evidence supports a hypothesis versus how much relevant evidence there is at all — and to hold that with little weight there may be no honest number available, not merely a wide error bar, and that rational degrees of belief needn't be single quantities you maximize over. Second, the General Theory account of what professionals in a market actually do: they aren't estimating an asset's long-run yield, they're trying to anticipate what average opinion expects average opinion to be, they act on spontaneous optimism rather than on a calculation, and liquidity is a social fact that can vanish exactly when you need it. Worth knowing too, since it's his own working version of the objection: Keynes spent the late 30s attacking Tinbergen's League of Nations business-cycle study, complaining that fitting multiple correlations to that material assumes you've included all the relevant influences, that they're measurable, that they're mutually independent rather than feeding each other, and that they stay stable through the sample period — when economic material is precisely the kind of thing that doesn't hold still across time — so the equations buy a false precision and then get used inductively, to predict.

what I actually want: (1) the mapping, done carefully and not gesturally — who is the agent whose epistemic position we're evaluating (Meriwether and the risk desk? the fourteen banks deciding whether to bid? the Fed deciding it was systemic? the Buffett group?), what the relevant evidence base in 1998 consisted of and how far back it reached, and which of the above claims is even the right kind of thing to apply to which of those agents; (2) a verdict seperately on each claim — was the blow-up a case where numerical probability was unavailable in principle, a case where it was available but badly under-weighted by the evidence on hand, or an ordinary estimation error sitting on top of a liquidity problem that has nothing whatever to do with probability theory? my hunch is the third, and that the only part of Keynes doing real work is the convention-and-liquidity chapter rather than anything from 1921, so argue with me if you disagree; (3) where the theory goes silent or gets embarrassed — fourteen banks plus Buffett clearly held a confident enough view of what that book was worth to put up billions, and the positions turned out to be unwindable at a profit, which looks like the spreads had determinate probabilities the whole time; can a Keynesian absorb that, or is there a bullet to bite; and (4) turn it back around on the theory — does this episode confirm Keynes, is it an anomaly for him, or is it a case his apparatus never contemplated? because his targets were armchair investors and econometricians fitting equations, and this was a firm founded by the people who invented the pricing theory, running the best models then in existence, and still going under. does that fact make his critique stronger, or show it was pointed at something else entirely? no bibliography please, I'll chase down anything I want to read myself.
