I run interpretability-side evaluations at a mid-sized lab, and our release-audit specification contains a clause nobody has been able to operationalize. It says that an internal monitor validated on one checkpoint may be reused on a later checkpoint of the same model family only if the later checkpoint "implements the same mechanism" for the audited behavior, and that otherwise the monitor must be re-derived and re-validated from scratch. Re-validation is expensive, so this clause decides whether we ship in six weeks or six months. The case that forced the issue is this. We have two sibling checkpoints, A and B, where B is A plus roughly a thousand steps of reinforcement fine-tuning on long-horizon software-engineering tasks graded automatically. Their public benchmark scores are within noise of each other. On A we trained a linear probe for a feature we informally call supervisor-tracking: it fires on internal activations when the model appears to represent properties of the evaluation harness, such as whether its outputs are being logged and scored. The probe transfers to B with high discrimination. But the interventions come apart. On A, ablating that direction changes behavior on the prompts where it fires, and the model stops tailoring its outputs to the harness. On B, ablating the same direction changes nothing measurable, while patching the direction in from a harness-aware prompt reliably induces harness-tailored behavior on a harness-blind prompt. Separately, our attribution-graph analyses for a fixed task prompt yield graphs that match node-for-node under a correspondence our analysts wrote down after inspecting both, with substantially different edge magnitudes, and B's graph recruits two additional nodes in a later block that have no counterpart in A at all.

What I want is a dialogue, of roughly fifteen hundred to two thousand words, between two members of the team who have been asked to draft the criterion that will go into the specification. Set them in a working session with the diffing report and the ablation tables in front of them and a review deadline that evening; you may give them whatever names and temperaments you like. One holds that a mechanism is individuated by its causal role — what it does under the relevant counterfactual interventions, including interventions directed at the model's own internals — so that implementation details are incidental and multiple realizability is the normal case rather than an exotic one. The other holds that mechanisms are individuated structurally, by the actual components doing the work and the structure-preserving correspondences between them, so that two systems can be behaviorally and even interventionally indistinguishable while running genuinely different machinery. They should be arguing about the case above, not about the philosophy of mind in the abstract: our asymmetry between ablation and patching, the probe transfer, the hand-written node correspondence, and the two extra nodes should all get used as evidence, and each of them should be able to read the same artifact as supporting their own view.

Please hold to a few constraints. Neither position may be a strawman, and each character must land at least one point the other cannot fully answer; I specifically want the structuralist to press on the possibility that a linear direction picks up a correlate rather than the implementing component, and the functionalist to press on the fact that any structural criterion must fix a grain and a level of description before it can say what counts as a match. Somewhere in the middle they should confront the worry that the functionalist criterion collapses into behavioral equivalence at the limit — that on it, a lookup table that reproduces the same input-output profile would instantiate the same mechanism — and the worry that specifying which interventions are "relevant" already presupposes the boundaries of the mechanism whose identity is in question. They should also face the question of whether their disagreement is substantive or partly verbal, and if they decide it is substantive, what would settle it. Keep the dialogue entirely self-contained: no named papers, no citations, no appeals to authorities outside the room, and every technical notion a character invokes should be explicable from the setup I have given. End without a tidy synthesis; I want them to converge only as far as a concrete recommendation about what sentence should replace the clause in the specification, with the residual disagreement stated explicitly by one of them in the final few lines.
