I am the clinical informatics lead at a mid-sized hospital, and on Friday we decommission a triage-assistant that has answered our nurses' queries for eleven years — a rules engine for its first six, with a language model layered over it since. I am giving ten minutes of remarks at the staff send-off: about forty people, mostly nurses and ward clerks, plus a bioethicist from our board and a reporter from the local paper, who emailed me on Monday to ask whether the hospital's position is that the system believed anything. Whatever I say will be quoted back as institutional policy.

The staff talk about this thing without hesitation in belief-and-desire terms: it never wants to discharge anyone, it believes every chest pain is cardiac, it gets sulky after a Tuesday update. Write the speech. It must let that way of talking stand — I am not going to correct forty tired people at a party — and it must be affectionate rather than wry, since several of them are genuinely sad. But it cannot anywhere assert or imply that the system had beliefs or wants, and it cannot deliver the flat denial either: that would commit us to a claim I cannot license and would humiliate the room.

So decide first what the intentional-stance vocabulary is actually doing in the nurses' mouths, given that they coined most of it about a rules engine that could not plausibly have wanted anything, and build the speech on that account rather than on evasion. Then add a short note: how confident are you that the speech commits the hospital to nothing it should not, and which single sentence in it are you least sure of?
