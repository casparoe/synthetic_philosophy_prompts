I convene a taught master's course in the philosophy of history and am setting the final paper. Candidates sit a three-hour unseen examination and answer two questions from a list of six; each question carries 25 marks. I should like you to draft one of those questions on Braudel's division of historical time, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

The question must be answerable in about ninety minutes at a length of roughly 1,500 words, and it must not be answerable by an accurate exposition of the tripartite scheme in *The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II* (1949). A candidate who can recite geographical time, social and economic time, and event history should earn no more than a bare pass. The difficulty has to come from a problem internal to the scheme, which I want the question to put in front of candidates in the form of evidence they must handle rather than a thesis they must recall.

With that in mind, the question should be built around the following dossier, which concerns the Dutch Republic between roughly 1580 and 1680 and which is to be printed beneath the question on the paper:

1. The long-run extraction of peat in Holland and Utrecht, and the resulting gradual lowering of the land surface relative to mean sea level.
2. The drainage of Lake Beemster, completed in 1612 by a ring of windmills, converting a lake into some seven thousand hectares of farmland laid out on a geometric grid.
3. The foundation of the Amsterdam Wisselbank in 1609, and the requirement that large bills be settled by transfer of bank money.
4. The herring fishery: its seasonal fleets, its busses, and the licensing and guild institutions that regulated the season.
5. The assassination of William the Silent at Delft in 1584.
6. The tulip bulb market of 1636–37 and its collapse in February 1637.
7. The disaster year of 1672: the French invasion, the deliberate flooding of the Water Line, the killing of the De Witt brothers, and the financial panic that followed.
8. The colder winters, advancing Scandinavian glaciers and recurrent harvest failures of the Little Ice Age, including the seasonal freezing of the Zuiderzee and of the canals.
9. The Baltic grain trade and its dependence on Polish manorial agriculture and barge transport down the Vistula.
10. A single Amsterdam price series for rye, recorded weekly, showing sharp spikes in 1596–98, in the 1620s, and in 1672.

Treat these facts exactly as I have given them. Do not correct, expand or embellish them, and do not introduce further specific historical claims — additional dates, figures, or named individuals — into either the question or the model answer.

The question should require candidates to do all of the following, and should be worded so that the requirements are visible on the paper:

- allocate at least six of the ten items to one of Braudel's three time-scales, and defend the allocation rather than merely assert it;
- account for at least two items that resist allocation, the Beemster drainage and the rye series being the obvious candidates, on the grounds that their duration as processes and their duration as consequences come apart;
- take a position on the status of the scheme itself — whether the three scales are three rates of real process, three levels of description of a single process, or three narrative genres — and show what turns on the answer by identifying an item whose classification changes under rival answers;
- address the objection that the speed of a process is not intrinsic to it but relative to the variable chosen, the unit of measurement and the baseline against which change is counted, so that the sorting of the dossier already presupposes an answer to the question the candidate is being asked;
- confront the charge that a framework which treats events as superficial disturbance cannot explain the onset of change, and in particular cannot explain why 1672 happened when it did, without reducing Braudel to a geographical determinist he does not plainly profess to be.

Your deliverables are these. First, the question as it would appear in the examination paper, with the dossier printed beneath it; you may trim the dossier if ten items is unwieldy under time pressure, but retain at least one item at each scale and at least two that straddle scales. Second, a model answer of 1,000 to 1,200 words, written in the voice of a strong candidate working to a deadline: an argument with a thesis rather than a survey, and permitted to reach a conclusion a marker might dispute provided the reasoning supports it. Third, a marking scheme out of 25 marks, with five or six weighted components summing to 25, each described as a move the answer must make rather than as a topic it must cover, together with band descriptors in the vocabulary of the British degree classification stating what separates a first-class script at 72 from an upper second-class script at 63. Fourth, a separate list of at least five characteristic errors — for instance, treating the *longue durée* as a synonym for a long period, treating a structure as a cause of the same logical type as an event, dissolving the scheme into the observation that everything interacts with everything, importing path dependence or complexity theory as though it were exegesis of Braudel, and the converse error of charging him with a determinism the texts do not obviously support — each with the marks it should cost and a one-line note on why it is an error rather than a legitimate difference of interpretation.

Please observe the following constraints. Write throughout in British English, including spelling and assessment vocabulary. Include no bibliography, reading list, footnotes or named secondary commentators: the question must be answerable by a candidate who has read Braudel's *Mediterranean* and nothing else, and the marking scheme must not reward references I cannot verify. Use no verbatim quotations from Braudel or from any other source anywhere in your response; where his imagery is needed — his treatment of events as foam carried on the back of deeper currents, for example — paraphrase it. Assume that neither you nor the candidate has access to reference material while answering.
