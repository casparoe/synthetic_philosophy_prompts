I am a third-year doctoral student in decision theory, and for the past fourteen months I have been spending two days a week embedded in an applied group that builds sequential-decision agents — mostly agents that hold a budget and negotiate trades with counterparties over many rounds. My role there is to review proposed valuation specifications before deployment. Reviews come roughly every six weeks; the budgets are modest but real, and some counterparties are adversarial in the ordinary commercial sense. I am the only person in the building who has encountered the money pump argument for coherence requirements on preference, and the engineers who write the specs have not. I need a procedure they can run without me, and I need it to be right, because at present I am applying three informal habits whose relationship to the actual argument I cannot defend.

The habits are these. First, if I can write down three offered trades that carry the agent around a cycle and back to its starting position with less money, I reject the specification outright. Second, if the specification is scored over whole plans rather than over individual steps, and the agent is given a planner that optimizes over plans, I pass it. Third, if each trade carries a fixed transaction fee larger than the agent's gross gain from cycling, I pass it on the ground that the cycle is not exploitable in practice.

Please write the review procedure I should be using instead. It should consist of an ordered list of tests, each with inputs restricted to what can be read off a specification or extracted from a simulation log; a stated pass condition for each; explicit instructions for what to do when a test cannot be completed, particularly when the state space is too large to enumerate cycles and detection must be sampled or bounded; and a terminal verdict of pass, reject, or escalate to me. Mark every point at which the procedure is really a judgment call wearing the costume of a step. Where it must hand the decision to a human being because the question concerns values rather than structure — how much performance is worth surrendering for immunity, whether flexibility or exploitability-resistance takes priority — I want that flagged rather than buried.

Then run the procedure on three specifications. One: a spec whose plan-level score is a discounted sum of per-step terms and is transitively ordered, but whose deployed policy chooses greedily, so that at each of three resource states the locally best move leads to the next state in a cycle and each move costs the agent a small premium. Two: a spec that permits free and credible commitment to a whole plan at the outset, where the counterparty's offers arrive only after commitment and can leave the committed plan worse than a plan the agent rejected. Three: a spec with genuine cycles, where the operator of the marketplace sets a per-trade fee exceeding the gross gain from a full revolution.

Tell me which of my three habits the procedure overturns. I expect at least one verdict I will not welcome, and I would rather have it stated flatly than hedged, since the group is about to make the plan-level planner the default architecture for everything we ship.

Finally, take positions instead of surveying them. Specifically: does sophisticated planning — an agent choosing correctly in anticipation of its own later choices — rescue a specification whose underlying valuation is intransitive, or does the pump reappear at the level of the choice among plans; is the proper target of a review the transitivity of the ranking or merely the absence of pumpability of the policy, and can these come apart in a way that matters; and is a transaction fee a legitimate repair or a mask that a counterparty who controls the fee can lift at will? Give your reasons for each, and wherever you are relying on a contested claim, say which side of the dispute you are on and why.
