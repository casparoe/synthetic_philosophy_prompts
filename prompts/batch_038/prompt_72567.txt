I teach 11th and 12th grade at a small private school and this year they made "world philosophies" a graduation requirement. Nobody warned me. I got handed the India unit about a month ago, I've never taken a class on any of it, and I've been reading at night and trying to stay about a chapter ahead of the kids. It's mostly going okay except one thing: we're doing the Nyāya stuff on sources of knowledge, and my students have gotten completely stuck on an argument I can't referee. Half of them think that if you can describe how you found something out, it counts as its own separate source. The other half think almost everything collapses into either looking at it or reasoning about it. They talk past each other every single class.

What I want is a decison tree I can print and hand out. Input: a student says "I know that P" and can describe how they came to know it. Output: which pramāṇa that actually is, or a clear verdict that it isn't knowledge from a source at all. Build it from the logical space first — what are the real questions that split the candidates apart — and only then hang the positions and the schools on the leaves. I don't want a list of six terms with definitions, I want something that sorts their answers.

Here's my rough map, please correct it wherever I've got it wrong and tell me where:

- the candidates I have are perception, inference, comparison/analogy, postulation (working something out because nothing else explains an otherwise weird combination of facts), non-apprehension (knowing an absence), and testimony/word
- as far as I've got it, the materialists take one, the Buddhists take two, Sāṃkhya and the Jains take three, Nyāya takes four, and the Mīmāṃsakas and Advaitins take six, with one Mīmāṃsā sub-school taking five because they think absence is just perceived. Is that right? What about the Vaiśeṣikas, who I think are partners with Nyāya but don't agree with them here?
- testimony seems to need splitting into at least two leaves: a case where you're trusting a person you have independent reason to think is reliable and competent, versus a case where the words are supposed to carry weight without any author behind them at all
- my notes mention a fight about whether the reliability of a source is built into it or has to be established separately afterward. I think that belongs somewhere in the tree but I can't figure out where
- my notes also mention conditions for a sentence actually conveying knowledge — the words needing each other, needing to be mutually compatible, needing to be heard close together, and something about the speaker's intended point, which I think one school rejects as a condition. Fold that in as a sub-branch on the testimony side, or tell me why it doesn't belong on this tree at all
- and Nyāya apparently has special categories of perception for odd cases, like seeing sandalwood as fragrant or somehow perceiving a general property, plus a yogic variety. I don't follow these. The tree either needs a place for them or needs to say why they aren't separate leaves.

Constraints on the thing itself:

- Max twelve branch questions, it has to fit on one page front and back. Go as deep as you need on the testimony side, that's where they fight.
- Every node has to be phrased as a question a seventeen-year-old can answer honestly without knowing which way the answer sends them. Nothing like "do you accept non-apprehension as an independent pramāṇa?" — that's the label, not the question.
- Every leaf gets: the name, which schools treat it as genuinely independent versus reducing it to something else, and one sentence on what accepting it actually costs you. If a leaf is "this isn't knowledge from any source," say what it is instead — memory, recognition, doubt, error, hypothetical reasoning, whatever.
- Route these five things that came up in class through your own tree, showing the path for each. Don't just assert the endpoint:
  1. A kid said "there's no coffee in the pot," and another said she didn't know any such thing, she just looked at an empty pot.
  2. My cousin keeps gaining weight but swears he never eats during the day. So he must be eating at night. The kid who brought this up insists it's not inference because there's no general rule connecting the two, and I couldn't answer him.
  3. I'd never seen a quince in my life. My grandmother described it as like a lumpy pear that smells like perfume. Last week I picked one up at the market and knew exactly what it was.
  4. Maya saw her cousin at baggage claim after nine years and says she didn't work anything out, she just saw that it was her.
  5. One kid said: if you tell us the exam is Tuesday we know it, but if the textbook says the earth is 4.5 billion years old we also know it, and those are two different situations. I want the tree to handle the difference without me having to explain it.
- Flag any two leaves your tree cannot actually separate, and say what would separate them if anything could. And give me at least three corners of the space that no position occupies — combinations of answers that are available but nobody takes — with a one-line diagnosis each of whether the corner is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's just unattractive, or because nobody's bothered.
- No citations, no sūtra numbers, no bibliography, I can't check any of that anyway. Sanskrit term in parentheses once at each leaf and then English after that.

Give me the tree as a plain indented outline, then the five routed cases, then the flags and the empty corners. Very last line of your whole answer, on its own, has to be the call on case 4, the recognition one, since that's the one I need to settle with the class Monday: `Verdict: ` followed by exactly one of your leaf labels.
