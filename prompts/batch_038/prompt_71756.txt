Retired three years ago after a career in comparative physiology — thirty-odd years of fish and invertebrate nervous systems, mostly the unglamorous end — and I've gone and fallen down the philosophy hole in retirement. Currently chewing on Dennett's four kinds of creatures from Kinds of Minds: Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian. I've written down where I've actually got to and I want a proper consistency check before I take it along to the philosophy group I've joined, which contains one actual academic philosopher who will not be gentle with me.

Here's the list.

1. The ladder is a ranking, and I want to use it as one. I want to be able to say that a bee has less mind than a rat and a rat less than a chimp, and I want the rungs to be the reason I can say it. Half the appeal for me is that it's not just "more or less consciousness" waved around — it's an ordering with something behind it.

2. But I'm also completely signed up to the point that the rungs are about different *ways* of getting information about the world, not different amounts of the same stuff. A parasitoid wasp's built-in repertoire tracks its host more precisely, in its own domain, than anything a lab rat ever manages.

3. I take it that a creature goes up a rung when it acquires an additional selection process stacked on the old ones: genetic selection, then reinforcement of the individual's own behaviour, then the offline testing of internal models so the animal can try things out without dying, then the use of designs stored outside the head — words, writing, instruments, other people. Each step is a difference in kind, not degree. That's why I think the taxonomy dodges the "it's all a continuum" crowd.

4. I'm a naturalist and I want every rung to be empirically checkable. In principle you should be able to settle where a species sits by running experiments on it, and if no experiment could ever settle it, the rung isn't earning its keep.

5. I gather people have trained tethered fruit flies to steer away from one visual pattern and toward another because one of them gets paired with heat, and bees obviously learn all sorts of things in a Y-maze. By my criterion in 3 that makes a fly Skinnerian. But I don't for a moment think a trained fly has more mind than a garden spider, and I'd put the spider on the bottom rung. I don't want to give up either half of that.

6. I hold that the Gregorian step is what lets us have thoughts we literally could not otherwise have. And I also think most of what looks like human cleverness is offloaded onto paper, libraries, instruments and each other — strip a bright person naked into the woods and they're not much better then a good chimpanzee.

Go through these and tell me which ones are genuinely pulling against each other and how hard, which ones only look like they are, and what the cheapest repair is in each case. Rank the tensions by severity — don't hand me a list where everything is equally a bit worrying, because that's no use to me. And I want you to commit: tell me which single item you would drop or rewrite and give me your reason, rather than laying out the respectable options and leaving me to pick. If you think I've simply got hold of the wrong end of what the rungs are supposed to be doing, say that plainly. I'm a scientist, I'd much rather be corrected than agreed with.
