I'm a novelist — third book came out in the spring and I'm about sixty pages into the fourth. Not an academic. I read one Davidson essay once about eight years ago and retained roughly a vibe, plus a lot of second-hand chatter about charity from friends who did the actual reading, so treat me as fluent in my own framework and near-illiterate in his.

My framework, in detail, because I want the translation to be honest rather than loose. I write close third person and I work to a private rule I call the behaviour-only draft: every scene gets written once containing nothing but what a camera and a microphone could catch — gesture, dialogue, what people do with their hands, who leaves the room first. Only then do I let myself interior access, and whatever interior I add has to be something an attentive reader could in principle have arrived at from the behaviour-only draft alone. If it isn't, I either plant more behaviour earlier or I cut the thought. Related to this I keep a charity budget per character: a rough allowance for how much inexplicable belief and self-contradiction a reader will absorb before the person on the page stops reading as a person and starts reading as a device. Some characters get a huge budget (the self-deceiver, the man in the grip of something he won't name), some get almost none (the woman in one scene at a funeral), and spending it badly is the main way I know a draft is going wrong.

So: restate the radical interpretation project — the thought experiment about making sense of a speaker from scratch, the work charity does inside it, the way belief, desire and meaning get tied together so you can't pin any of them down independently of the others, the worry that the result is underdetermined, plus the later business about an interpreter who already knows everything and about two creatures and a shared object (triangulation, if I've got that right) — in my vocabulary above. Do it properly, piece by piece, actually using behaviour-only drafts and charity budgets and the rest, not just sprinkling my words over his argument and hoping.

Then the residue report, which is the bit I actually want — its most of why I'm asking:

1. Which of his claims translate cleanly, such that a reader equipped only with my craft terms would come away with the same thing?

2. Which ones translate only with distortion, and what specifically the distortion is in each case.

3. Which ones have no counterpart at all in how I think about character. My suspicion is that the constitutive part is the big casualty: my charity budget is a pragmatic and aesthetic constraint I get to choose and vary, whereas his charity is supposed to be a precondition of there being thought to interpret in the first place. Tell me whether you agree, and whether that difference is a real difference or whether it collapses under pressure.

4. Where my vocabulary might be richer than his. I have an author who made the character and therefore knows exactly what is in her head; I have readers who withhold charity deliberately and on principle; I have free indirect style, where the narrator's idiom and the character's bleed into one another and you genuinely cannot say whose words you're reading. Do any of those put real pressure on him, or are they all artefacts of the fact that I invent both sides of the conversation and he doesn't?

5. Flag every place you catch yourself smuggling in one of my assumptions and presenting it as his. Especially this one: the behaviour-only draft assumes there is a determinate inner life behind the behaviour that the draft is merely hiding. That may be close to the opposite of what he's arguing, and if it is I'd rather it be said plainly than papered over.

Finish with a verdict I can argue with. Where the translation breaks down, is that because my craft vocabulary is missing something — some distinction that only comes into focus once you have it — or because parts of the radical interpretation set-up were less substantive than they sounded, so that the residue is exactly where the sound and the substance come apart? Don't be diplomatic. I would genuinely rather be told the novelist's version is a cheat than told we were both right in our own ways.

British English throughout, please. Somewhere around fifteen hundred words, more if the residue section needs the room. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I won't chase anything up, and rather then being useful that part of an answer is always just noise to me.
