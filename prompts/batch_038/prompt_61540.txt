**What Kind of Deliberation Gets to Make Reasons?**

Williams's internalism is usually stated as a conditional: an agent has a reason to act only if there is a sound deliberative route from her subjective motivational set to her so acting. Most of the standard objections aim at a weaker target than this, since the motivational set is not a list of occurrent desires and the route is allowed to change what is in it. The debate consequently stalls: the externalist produces an agent who seems to have a reason and no motive, and the internalist replies that a sound route would have produced the motive, and neither side can say what would settle the matter. My suggestion is that the dispute is not really about whether reasons must be anchored in motivation, but about the closure conditions on the route — that is, about which operations on an agent's motivational set count as sound deliberation rather than as psychological drift.

Write S\* for the set of motivations reachable from S by sound deliberation. There are at least three expansion operators that could be built into the route. The first is correction: revising S in light of which of the agent's non-normative beliefs are false or unsupported. This is what handles the familiar case of the man who wants a gin and tonic and reaches for a glass of petrol he takes to be gin — the sound route eliminates the false belief, and the desire does not survive its elimination, so he has no reason to drink. The second is imagination: presenting to the agent, vividly enough to elicit a response, possibilities she had not entertained. The third is generalisation: extracting a pattern from elements of S and adopting a principle that generates concern for cases falling under the pattern but not currently cared about.

**Claim 1 (vacuity).** If imagination and generalisation are unconstrained, S\* can be made to contain almost anything, and internalism loses its bite. For any purported external reason, the internalist can insist that some sufficiently long route from S would have produced the relevant motivation. Since no one can survey all routes, the thesis becomes unassailable in exactly the way that makes it uninformative: the externalist's counterexample is always met by positing a longer journey, and the internalist's thesis is never risked.

The way to keep internalism substantive is to impose what I will call the conservation constraint: deliberation may reconfigure the material in S — correcting it, combining it, ordering it, refining it, spelling out what it already involves — but every element in the output must be either a specification or a generalisation of something in the input. No new source of concern may be imported. Correction obviously satisfies this. Imagination satisfies it whenever the possibility imagined is a way of elaborating something the agent already cares about. Generalisation is where the constraint bites, because from the same input many generalisations are available. Someone who cares intensely about two particular people can generalise to persons as such, or to these two plus anyone they care about, or to beings with a similar history. Nothing in S selects among these.

**Claim 2 (the soundness horn).** That is the problem. Which generalisation is the right one is a normative question, and its answer is not to be found in S. So the conservation constraint requires a norm of correct generalisation that is not itself derived from the agent's motivations, and internalism leaks: it denies external reasons at the level of ends while presupposing something external at the level of the route. The internalist must then either hold that the soundness of a route is fixed by normative facts that obtain independently of what the agent cares about — which concedes the externalist's central claim, in a different place — or hold that it is fixed by S after all, in which case she owes an account of how S picks out one generalisation rather than another without arbitrariness.

Consider a case meant to test the constraint. Tomas is thirty-eight, restores stringed instruments, and has no desire to have children. His motivational set includes absorption in his work, two long friendships, a passion for early music, a settled dislike of obligation, and strong affection for his niece. People tell him he has a reason to become a parent, that he will regret it otherwise. Under imagination he can be brought to picture fatherhood concretely enough to feel something; the question the conservation constraint poses is whether the concern that this elicits is a specification of the affection he already has or an import from outside it. My claim is that in cases like this the constraint typically comes out indeterminate rather than violated, and that this — not falsity — is what explains why such reason claims feel simultaneously insulting and not merely confused. The speaker is not asserting something clearly untrue; she is proposing a generalisation of Tomas's own concerns that his concerns do not settle.

Two summaries of Williams frame the above, and I am not confident of either. First: on Williams's account, an agent has a reason to act only if acting would satisfy one of the desires she actually has at the time, which is why the doctrine looks so restrictive and why the correction operator has to be introduced as a patch. Second: Williams's conclusion is that external reasons statements are not so much false as meaningless — that there is nothing such a statement could be saying, so the externalist owes an account of her own meaning before she owes an argument. Relatedly, I take it that internalism does leave room for blaming the agent who lacks the motive, but only in a thin sense: we blame him because blame is one of the instruments available for producing in him the motive he lacks.

The upshot I want to draw is conciliatory. Internalism is best read as a thesis about the first-person legibility of reasons — a consideration is a reason for an agent only if she could take it up in her own deliberation — and the conservation constraint is a constraint on uptake, not on what makes considerations normative at all. Read this way, both sides can accept nearly everything the other says. The externalist is right that there are normative facts that do not depend on any agent's motivations. The internalist is right that nothing can be a reason for an agent unless it could get a grip on her. The internal/external debate, on this reading, was never a debate about reasons; it was a debate about the psychology of deliberation conducted in the vocabulary of normativity.
