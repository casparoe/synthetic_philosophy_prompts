Me and my brother rewatch The Godfather every December, usually with a lot of talking over it, and this year I went down a rabbit hole on the phrase "dirty hands" in politics and now I can't watch the movie the same way. I don't have any philosophy training, I'm a warehouse supervisor outside Columbus and I just read stuff at night when I can't sleep, so please don't write at me like I'm in a seminar. I'm not looking for a reading list either, I want the argument.

Here's what I gathered, correct me if I've garbled it. Michael Walzer wrote an essay in 1973 called "Political Action: The Problem of Dirty Hands," and the basic move is that a politician sometimes has to do something that is genuinely wrong — not just regrettable, wrong — in order to protect the people he's responsible for, and that doing it can still come out right overall, but the man who does it stays guilty and doesn't get to feel good about it. The guilt is supposed to be the mark of a decent one, and Walzer even floats the idea that the dirty-hands politician ought to accept punishment for what he did. Then there's a piece by Thomas Nagel called "Ruthlessness in Public Life" where the point, as far as I could follow, is that the interesting case isn't the leader who does terrible things while convincing himself they're fine — it's the one who sees clearly that what he's doing is wrong and does it anyway without flinching and without lying to himself about the cost.

So here's my problem. Michael Corleone looks like a textbook case from the outside — he steps in, does what he keeps saying he doesn't want to do, and justifies all of it as protecting the family — but the longer I sit with it the less sure I am the dirty hands idea actually fits him, and I want someone to argue it out instead of telling me both readings have merit.

Stuff I want you to actually work through:

- Which of Michael's moves in the first movie clear the bar? Take them one at a time. Killing Sollozzo and that crooked police captain plausibly saves his father's life. The baptism business, where he stands at the font renouncing the devil while his people hit every rival boss at the same hour, is strategic and hard to call anything else. But Carlo, and Tessio, and the fact that he deals with his brother-in-law and then has to face his sister — I can't find the "for the community" part of those at all. Are those still dirty hands, or is that just a man settling scores with the vocabulary of necessity draped over it?

- Where in the movie does Michael feel any of the guilt Walzer says he should? As far as I can tell, nowhere, not once. So is the film telling us Walzer's condition is a sentimental fantasy, or is it telling us Michael fails the test and that's precisely why he's damned? And the last shot, the door closing on Kay while his captains kiss his hand — is that the punishment the philosophers say a dirty-hands man should want, arriving from outside because his conscience never produces it? Or is it just a great final image and I'm reading too much into it?

- Can a crime family even be a community in the sense the theory needs? Walzer's cases are people holding public office with public responsibilities. Michael constantly borrows political language — the family business, consigliere, wanting a senator with Corleone in the bag someday, telling Kay they'll be completely legitimate in five years. I wonder whether the movie is doing something deliberate there, dressing private murder in the clothes of statecraft so the audience feels like it's watching a tragedy about power instead of a story about criminals. Does that borrowing actually work on the theory's terms, or is the whole point that it can't?

- What about the others as counter-cases? Vito also killed and also ordered killings, and he ends up in a garden chasing his grandson with an orange peel in his mouth. Kay is the one with clean hands and she pays the most for it. Tom gets pulled off the wartime job for not being hard enough. Sonny runs on pure impulse and is shredded for it. If the film has a verdict about which of these ways of living alongside violence is survivable, what is it, and does it line up with Walzer and Nagel or cut against them?

- My brother thinks Michael is a tragic hero in the Greek sense and that's why the movie works. I think he's closer to a con man who sold his own family a promise of a clean future in order to keep them financing a dirty present, and the five years never arrive. Tell me which of us the film supports and point at the scenes that decide it.

And if the honest answer is that dirty hands is the wrong frame for this movie, say that and tell me what frame does fit, plus exactly where you had to bend Walzer or Nagel to get them to apply. I don't want the theory stretched until it fits the film — I'd rather hear that the movie is about something the theory can't handle. Don't bother quoting lines back at me, I've seen this thing forty times, just tell me which scene and what's happening in it. If you want to pull in the end of Part Two, the empty house and the lake, go ahead, but keep the weight on the first one.
