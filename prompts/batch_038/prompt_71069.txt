In three weeks I present a forty-minute paper at a two-day workshop on the long-run future, hosted by a policy institute rather than a university department. The room will hold about twenty people: two philosophers who work on existential risk and population ethics and who are committed longtermists, fluent in expected-value reasoning though not formally trained in statistics; an economic historian of technology; a growth theorist who models directed technical change and reads the persistence-and-institutions literature closely; several public-policy graduate students; and one funder whose portfolio is split between AI governance and pandemic preparedness. None of them is a Bayesian statistician, and none has read the anthropic-reasoning literature in any depth, so any reply of mine that depends on it will have to be delivered without technical apparatus.

The thesis is negative and, I believe, stronger than anything in the current debate. In brief: the hinge of history hypothesis — as canonically argued in MacAskill's What We Owe the Future (2022), alongside the related time-of-perils framing — is not merely hard to confirm but evidentially self-undermining. My argument runs in four steps. First, hinge claims require their advocates to assign a comparatively high probability to the proposition that their own era is the era of maximum influence over the long-run trajectory of civilisation. Second, the historical record suggests that the capacity and disposition to formulate such a claim is produced by recurrent conditions — rapid technological change, a self-conscious historiography, a literate class with an explicit sense of futurity — rather than by conditions that obtain only in eras that are actually decisive. Third, therefore the probability that an era generates a confident hinge claim is far higher conditional on those recurrent conditions than conditional on that era in fact being decisive, which makes the claim itself a poor likelihood ratio: learning that a period takes itself to be pivotal should sharply lower, not raise, our estimate of its pivotality. Fourth, the deflation bites hardest exactly where the contemporary case is strongest, because much of the felt evidence for the present hinge is a sense of unprecedented transformation plus a value lock-in mechanism whose apparent permanence is itself judged by era-relative standards; my conclusion is not that the future does not matter but that the abductive support usually offered for this era's specialness has been largely explained away.

Please prepare me for the question period rather than evaluate my argument. I want a prep sheet containing:

1. The seven or eight objections this particular audience will actually raise — not the generic critiques circulating in online longtermism debates — ordered by the product of how likely each is to be asked and how much damage it would do. Attribute each to its likely source and phrase it in that person's own idiom: the growth theorist's version will press on identification and on whether persistence estimates can support the lock-in premise at all; the historian's will press on the second step, since most pre-modern societies arguably had no concept of trajectory change to formulate; the longtermists' will insist that their case rests on structural facts about capability timelines and coordination failure rather than on any felt sense of unprecedentedness.

2. For each objection, a reply deliverable in about ninety seconds of speaking, followed by a single line naming where that reply is softest — the point at which one competent follow-up would corner me.

3. A note on which objections share a common root, so that I can defuse several of them with one passage, and what that passage should assert.

4. The concession I should make explicitly within the first ten minutes, on the grounds that withholding it will read as bad faith to the two longtermists and as naiveté to the growth theorist.

5. The question I most hope nobody asks: say why it is worse than the others and give me a holding answer that stalls without conceding the paper.

6. The one objection that, if handled badly, loses the room even if it is not in principle the most damaging — and what about the setting makes it decisive here.
