I would like a rigorous assessment of how exposed a view I hold actually is — not an exposition of the phenomenon that motivates it.

The phenomenon: two spatially separated lights of different colors are flashed in rapid succession, and subjects report not merely apparent motion between the two locations but a change of color partway along the illusory path — a change they therefore seem to have experienced before the second light came on. The view I hold is the one Dennett and Kinsbourne draw from it. There are two ways such a report could be generated. On the Orwellian model, the subject genuinely experienced the first color persisting toward the second location, and that experience was subsequently revised in memory. On the Stalinesque model, whatever content reaches consciousness is assembled only once the relevant information is available, so nothing answering to the report was ever conscious and there is nothing to revise. I hold that no experiment could decide between them, and that this is structural rather than a limitation of present methods: every candidate datum — verbal report, confidence rating, speeded discrimination, eye movement, neural signature decoded after the fact — is itself a later process, and so can deliver only what survives the editing. From this I conclude that asking which model is true presupposes a single determinate stream whose contents arrive at a fixed place and time, and that giving up that presupposition costs us nothing worth keeping.

Tell me what would count against this. In particular:

1. Which empirical findings would actually separate the two models? Does the Stalinesque story, unlike the Orwellian, commit the system to a processing delay with measurable consequences for reaction times, or for the ranges of spacing and asynchrony over which the illusion appears and dissolves? Would a non-report measure tracking the premature color be decisive, and what exactly would it have to look like? Are there paradigms — backward masking, interleaved catch trials, forced choice under time pressure, priming or affective measures — in which the two models genuinely diverge rather than merely differing in gloss?

2. Which conceptual objections should move me? The charge that the premise on which my argument rests — that reports are always downstream of the events they report — smuggles in the very delay it is supposed to establish. The charge that the two models make different commitments about the timing of experience even if they agree about every report. The charge that my move from indistinguishability to there being no fact of the matter is verificationism that proves too much, licensing the same verdict about whether experience is unified or about the order of two near-simultaneous events. And the charge that a third-person criterion — the time and content of some neural event — could settle when an experience occurred without any report being able to reveal it, which would make the models distinguishable in principle after all, merely not from the inside.

3. What does my view predict that the Cartesian theater view does not, and does it risk anything? Can a multiple-drafts framework absorb every new result as simply another draft, and if so is that a defect — given that its rival appears equally absorbent, or is the asymmetry that only one of the two claims to be empirically respectable?

4. Which of these would a committed defender of the indistinguishability thesis concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss as a misreading? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say plainly where you think the view is genuinely exposed and where it is insulated by stipulation.
