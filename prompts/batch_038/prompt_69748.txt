I coordinate the assessment for a second-year graduate course on the epistemic foundations of game theory, and last month's reading group left the seminar split in a way I now have to examine on: one faction holds that cooperation surviving deep into long centipede-style games refutes common knowledge of rationality outright, the other holds that the backward-induction argument cannot even state a prescription at nodes it has declared unreachable. Write one essay question for the final, answerable in 90 minutes and worth 25 marks, that forces candidates to reason about a concrete case rather than recite either slogan.

The question must be entirely self-contained, since candidates sit it with no sources: give a finite alternating game of your own design with an explicit node count and payoffs, together with a public announcement procedure that generates mutual knowledge of rationality to a specified finite depth and no further, with that finite depth itself known to both players. It should then ask candidates to identify the earliest node at which the player to move is compelled to end the game, to explain what does and does not follow about the nodes preceding it, and finally to say what a player should do and believe on arriving at a node the argument had ruled out.

Supply a model answer of roughly 800 words and a marking scheme that allocates the 25 marks across must-hit moves, names the specific errors that should cost marks — including any candidate who treats common knowledge as interchangeable with universal knowledge, or who invokes trembling-hand refinement or the small-probability-of-an-irrational-opponent story as a label rather than an argument — and states plainly what separates a first-class answer from a merely competent one.
