Third-year phil/history joint here, taking a methods seminar on explanation in the human sciences, and I've hit a wall on Mill. We're in the induction book of A System of Logic (1843, so the prose is doing my head in), on the chapter about the plurality of causes and the intermixture of effects. I've gone through the stretch we're supposed to comment on and restated it move by move in my own words, because at this point I can't tell whether I'm following him or just pattern-matching on the vocabulary. Here's my breakdown:

1. He starts from the point that even if the same cause always has the same effect, the reverse doesn't hold: one kind of effect can be produced by several different kinds of cause. His examples are the sort of thing where heat can come from friction or from electricity or from burning stuff, and death can come from a huge range of agencies.
2. So you can't read the cause off the effect, and this is where I think he says the method of agreement gets shaky, since two instances that agree only in the effect might have arrived there by completely different routes. My notes say he wants any conclusion from that method backed up by difference or by deduction, but I'm not sure I've got his reason right.
3. Then a second, separate problem: effects are almost never produced by one cause working alone, they're the joint result of many causes at once, and their contributions get tangled together. That's the intermixture of effects.
4. Here he splits cases in two. In one kind each cause goes on doing what it would have done by itself and the joint effect is just the sum, like two forces moving the same body, so you can build up the whole from knowledge of the parts. In the other kind the causes together produce something new whose properties are unlike any of them separately, like a chemical combination, and then the separate laws are as it were superseded and you can't compose your way up to the joint effect at all.
5. Finally he gestures that this isn't only a point about chemistry, and I *think* he's setting up the claim that the moral and social sciences belong to the first kind rather than the second, which is what would let you reason there from general laws of human nature. But I can't find him actually saying that in this chapter.

Two questions on the passage itself. (a) Is any of 1-5 a distortion, especially 2 and 5, and does 5 belong to this chapter at all or is it something he argues much later? (b) What exactly is the difference he's drawing in 4 — is the second kind a failure of the causal laws themselves, or only a failure of our ability to compose them? He seems to want the second, and I dont see how that squares with saying the separate laws get superseded.
