So, quick background: I ran a mass spec lab for thirty-five years, retired three years back, and have fallen into philosophy headfirst, mostly decision and game stuff, because that felt like the door a scientist could get through without tripping. I've been working through iterated deletion of dominated strategies and I've hit a snag that I suspect is obvious to people who do this for a living, except nobody I know does this for a living. Maybe you can settle it for me in dialogue form.

Here's the situation I've been chewing on, borrowed from my own world so I can picture it. Two research groups — mine and a rival's — are the only two anywhere who can chase a particular measurement. Each has to commit to one of three programs for the next five years:

- H: build the big high-field instrument
- L: build the big cryogenic one
- S: the safe program, incremental work, steady stream of modest papers

Payoffs are in whatever units you like — priority, prestige, output. The situation is symmetric, so the same matrix applies to both groups:

|  | rival: H | rival: S | rival: L |
|---|---|---|---|
| **you: H** | 4 | 3 | 0 |
| **you: S** | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| **you: L** | 0 | 3 | 4 |

The thing that's been bothering me: S is not beaten by H (H gets 0 where S gets 1), and it's not beaten by L (L gets 0 where S gets 1). But S *is* beaten across the board by a fair coin flip between H and L, which pays 2 against their H, 3 against their S, and 2 against their L, all strictly better than 1, 1, 1. So the textbook move is to say the safe program is strictly dominated after all, delete it for both groups, and carry on with what's left.

Write me a dialogue between two people who genuinely disagree about whether that deletion is legitimate. Not a skeptic and a believer where the skeptic is dumb — I want both of them sharp enough that I can't tell who's right when I'm done.

One of them holds the standard line: S is strictly dominated, out it goes, and since the rival is also rational they'll delete it too, and this cascade is just what rationality amounts to when two people are thinking about each other thinking.

The other one thinks the mixed-strategy deletion is not nearly as innocent as it looks, roughly on these grounds: a mixture isn't an act anybody performs, it's a description the analyst writes down; a real institution can't fund "half a high-field instrument and half a cryogenic one," and it certainly can't flip a coin to pick its five-year program; so what's doing the work in the argument isn't a fact about the players' rationality but a fact about what set of plans the analyst decided to admit into the model. And each round of deletion quietly demands more than common knowledge of rationality — it demands common knowledge that both sides are able and willing to reason with randomized plans at all.

Things I'd like them to actually wrestle with, in this order or whatever order the argument drags them into:

1. Whether the "you can't really randomize a research program" objection is an objection to dominance reasoning, or just a claim that the strategy set was drawn wrong — and if it's the latter, whether that concedes the philosophical point while winning the practical one.
2. The other reading of a mixed strategy, the one where it isn't a coin flip the player performs but a way of encoding what the *other* side is uncertain about. Does that reading rescue the deletion of S, or does it quietly change what the deletion even means? I have a feeling this is where the real fight is and I can't get it into focus myself.
3. Once S is gone for both groups, whether there's anything left to delete — including whether weak dominance would take them further in this specific game, and whether the usual order-dependence worry about weak deletion actually bites here or only in the abstract. Have them do the arithmetic rather than gesture at it. If one of them notices that the coin flip turns up again at the end of the analysis in a slightly embarrassing role, let them say so.
4. Where they land. I don't need a conversion, but I do want either real movement or a clean statement of what the disagreement has been reduced to — something like "we agree on the game and disagree on X," where X is stated precisely enough that I could go check it.

Ground rules: no citations, no named papers or authors, nothing I'd have to look up — I want this to stand on its own on a printed page. Keep it around 1200 to 1500 words. Let them interrupt each other, let them score cheap points, let them get something wrong and have the other one catch it. I'm going to hand this to my reading group (two retired engineers and a veterinarian) so the prose should be readable out loud, but don't water down the content — they're smarter than they look, and so am I. Also please double check the numbers in the table above as you go, because if I've built a game that doesn't do what I think it does, I'd rather find out from you than from the engineers.
