I'm prepping a talk for a small reading group — mostly analytic epistemologists with basically zero background in Indian philosophy — and the topic is whether the Naiyāyikas were right to refuse arthāpatti a seat at the table. Write me an essay that picks a side and actually defends it. No landing on "there are serious considerations on both sides," please; a conditional verdict is fine (something like "irreducible for cases of type X, reducible for type Y") but it has to be a verdict.

Since the audience won't know the terrain, set it up yourself early on: the four pramāṇas Nyāya accepts, what arthāpatti (postulation/presumption) is supposed to be, the stock Devadatta case, and the Nyāya move of saying that the whole thing is just anumāna in disguise — you know, fatness plus no daytime eating gives you a pervasion like "gaining/maintaining bulk requires eating," run that as a syllogism, done. Mention that the Mīmāṃsakas and Advaitins resist this, and that the Buddhists go further and cut the list down to two. Don't spend too long there tho, the interesting part is below.

Here's a proposal I've been kicking around and want the essay to take seriously whether or not you end up endorsing it. Call it the **conflict-minimality** account. A cognition is arthāpatti rather than anumāna iff (i) the knower already holds two items of knowledge that are jointly unsatisfiable given background constraints they accept, (ii) the cognition repairs the clash, and (iii) among the repairs on offer it picks one that changes as little as possible. On this picture the difference between the two pramāṇas isn't in the content of what you end up believing — both routes can deliver "Devadatta eats at night" — it's in what triggers the cognition and what the live alternatives were. Inference starts from a mark and a pervasion; arthāpatti starts from an incoherence inside your own commitments.

Two things I want pressure put on. First: does this buy you anything, or can the Nyāya reply that clause (i) is just a description of the inference and clause (iii) is pragmatic noise about which inference you happened to make? Second, and maybe more damaging: notice that the standard Nyāya reduction seems to need the conflict anyway. Where did the pervasion come from? If the only reason I'd ever think to look for a pervasion here is that two of my commitments are already in collision, then the collision isn't a rival to inference, it's upstream of it — which might actually be an argument for irreducibility, or might be an argument that the whole distinction is drawn in the wrong place. Say which.

Then run your verdict over a battery of cases and tell me, for each, what your view predicts and whether that prediction is comfortable:

(a) Devadatta, the stock case.

(b) Caitra is known to be alive, isn't in his house, and isn't seen anywhere — so he's somewhere else. Is the clash here even a clash, or just an absence of information?

(c) A locked room, a soaking wet umbrella in the hall, nobody's been outside all day. You land on "it rained." Inference from a mark, or postulation? I suspect the interesting answer is that this case is ambiguous in a way that's diagnostic.

(d) The one I actually care about: a colleague says they've had lunch in the canteen every day this month, and you know the canteen has been shut for renovation all month. You conclude they were lying or confabulating. Note that nothing here is observed — the clash is between two bits of testimony plus a fact about a building. If your account handles this, does it handle it as arthāpatti, or as a testimony-vs-perception fight where testimony just loses?

(e) A case where the minimal repair isn't the true one. Your friend's phone has been off for two days; the cheapest fix is a dead battery; the actual explanation is that they've gone on a silent retreat. Does clause (iii) mean arthāpatti is built to produce falsehoods under pressure? And if a pramāṇa can be systematically wrong in a way inference isn't, is it a pramāṇa at all or just abduction with extra steps?

(f) Anupalabdhi — no pot on the bare floor. Does your verdict about arthāpatti force a verdict about non-apprehension too? If you can reduce one you'd better explain why you can't reduce the other, and if you can't reduce either, say what's keeping the list from just growing forever.

I also want a paragraph on the slippery slope: if arthāpatti collapses into anumāna, what stops upamāna from collapsing too, and what stops the Buddhist two-pramāṇa view from winning outright? Is there a principled line between "reduce this one, keep the rest" and "reduce everything"?

House rules: no bibliography, no list of texts or scholars for me to go look up, nothing that depends on me having read the primary literature — I haven't and neither have you got a way of checking. Use Sanskrit terms freely but gloss them the first time. Roughly 1200–1600 words. End with your verdict stated plainly, in a sentence or two, plus the single strongest objection to it that you're willing to concede without changing your mind.
