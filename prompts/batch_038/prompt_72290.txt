**The take-home question** (from a seminar titled *Scepticism and the Modal Turn*; maximum 1,000 words; marks allocated 40 / 30 / 30):

> (a) Set out Nozick's truth-tracking analysis of knowledge, including the role played by the belief-forming method in his formulation of the subjunctive conditions. Explain why, on that analysis, an ordinary perceptual belief such as *I have two hands* can be known while *I am not a handless brain in a vat* cannot, and show precisely how this commits the tracking theorist to denying that knowledge is closed under known entailment.
>
> (b) In seminar we entertained an objection from a stipulated interlocutor, "Dr Vellani" — a fictitious character invented for the course pack, whose argument is to be assessed on its merits and is not to be looked for in the literature. Vellani argues as follows. Nozick's third condition says: if p were false, S would not believe that p. The contrapositive of that says: if S were to believe that p, then p would be true. And that is exactly the safety condition. Since a conditional and its contrapositive cannot differ in truth value, sensitivity and safety are one requirement written in two directions. It follows that safety-based accounts of knowledge inherit Nozick's failure of closure, and that safety theorists forfeit the anti-sceptical result they claim for their view. Assess Vellani's argument: does it succeed, fail, or partly succeed?
>
> (c) Otto believes that 7 + 5 = 12 because he has counted it out on his fingers. Say what Nozick's third and fourth conditions come to for this belief, and what follows for the tracking account's treatment of necessary truths. Does the method clause do any work here? Does it do work elsewhere — in particular, does holding the method fixed bear on the closure question raised in (a)?

**The candidate's response** (approximately 660 words):

> (a) Nozick replaces the traditional justification condition with two subjunctive conditions. On his account S knows that p only if: (i) p is true; (ii) S believes, via some method M, that p; (iii) if p were not true and S were to use M, S would not believe via M that p; and (iv) if p were true and S were to use M, S would believe via M that p. Condition (iii) is the sensitivity condition and (iv) is the adherence condition; together they say that knowledge consists in the belief's covarying with the truth across the relevant possibilities rather than in the believer's possession of internally accessible reasons. This is the descendant of the relevant-alternatives tradition, and as Dretske had already argued, knowledge requires that one's belief be sensitive in just this sense, since what matters is whether the alternatives that are actually in play are ruled out.
>
> Applied to the sceptic: take *I have two hands*. Were I handless, I would not believe I had hands — I would see the stumps, or feel the empty sleeves. So (iii) is satisfied, and with (i), (ii) and (iv) in place the tracking theorist says I know it. Now take *I am not a handless brain in a vat*. In the nearest possibility in which that proposition is false — in which I am such a vat brain — I would still believe it, since the vat experience is by hypothesis indistinguishable from the ordinary one. So (iii) fails, and I do not know it. Closure says that if I know p, know that p entails q, and come to believe q by competently deducing it from p, then I know q. *I have two hands* entails *I am not a handless brain in a vat*, and I know that it does; so anyone who accepts closure must say I know the second. Nozick refuses, and concludes that closure is false. Dretske makes the same move with relevant alternatives: the vat possibility need not be ruled out for me to know the ordinary claim, yet the entailment to its denial does not transmit.
>
> The upshot is that the sceptic's argument — I do not know I am not a vat brain; if I knew I had hands I would know I was not a vat brain; therefore I do not know I have hands — is blocked at its second step rather than its first. Tracking therefore does not refute scepticism so much as decline to play its game: it concedes the ignorance premise and rejects the inference. That is why the resulting conjunction, knowing the humdrum claim while failing to know its transparent consequence, is widely thought to be intolerable even though none of its components looks individually false.
>
> (b) Vellani's objection seems to me to succeed, and it is the most interesting thing about the whole framework. Condition (iii) states: were p false, S would not believe p. Contraposing yields: were S to believe p, p would be true — which is precisely the safety condition, that a belief is safe only if, were the believer to hold it, it would be true. Contraposition preserves truth conditions, so the safety theorist is committed to the very same modal requirement as Nozick, merely written in the opposite direction. It follows that safety-based accounts inherit the failure of closure, and that the safety theorist cannot claim the anti-sceptical advantage usually claimed for her view: she must say, with Nozick, that I do not know I am not a vat brain. The difference between the two camps is then stylistic rather than substantive, which is a disappointing conclusion but the correct one.
>
> (c) There is no possibility in which 7 + 5 is not 12. The antecedent of (iii) is therefore never satisfied, and (iii) is vacuously true; (iv) is trivially satisfied as well. So the tracking account says Otto knows the necessary truth, and the method clause can do no work in this case, since the clause only narrows which possibilities are relevant and there are no possibilities here in which the proposition is false. That is a welcome result: arithmetic knowledge does not need a special story. Where the clause does earn its keep is in cases like the grandmother who sees her grandson healthy and believes he is well; were he gravely ill she would still believe he was well, because she would be told so, but if we fix the method as seeing him then in the nearest possibility in which he is ill and she sees him she does not believe he is well. Once methods are held fixed in this way, closure is restored for any deduction carried out within a single method, which is why Nozick can maintain that failures of closure are the exception rather than the rule. The sceptical case is exceptional because the premise and the conclusion are believed by different methods.
