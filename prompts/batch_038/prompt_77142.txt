Act as a philosopher of law who has also actually sat on disciplinary panels — someone who knows the mid-century Hart–Devlin argument about legal moralism cold but writes for volunteers rather than law students. Before you draft anything, spend a few lines brainstorming the ways a procedure like this usually fails: what it'll get systematically wrong, where a panelist will just relabel their own gut feeling as a completed step, and where it can be gamed by whoever complains loudest. Then the situation. I'm the elected standards officer for a member-owned food co-op, about 2,400 members across four stores. Our bylaws contain a clause nobody wrote carefuly in 1998: a member can be suspended or expelled for "conduct seriously at odds with the co-op's values and the trust of the membership." Complaints come from members against other members; the panel is five or six volunteers with no legal training; we get ninety minutes per case and see roughly fifteen a year. The stakes are real but not criminal — someone loses their membership, their discount, and their community. Twice a year the whole membership can overturn a decision at a general meeting, and I write the reasoning that goes in front of them, so it has to survive being read aloud to a room of people who disagree with it.

The cases killing us are the ones where nobody can point to a victim. Last year: a member who bulk-bought co-op goods and resold them at a markup on an online marketplace; a member who wore a T-shirt with a political slogan on a volunteer shift, which a handful of other members said was so hateful they'd stopped shopping at that store; a member who kept photographing the produce aisle and posting critiques of our pricing; and a petition against a member whose adult-content side income came out. In three of the four the complainants' grievance is basically that they find the conduct disgusting or degrading to what the co-op stands for. I'm personally a harm-principle person and my instinct is to throw all of them out — but half the panel doesn't share that instinct, and honestly the disintegration worry feels different for a 2,400-person voluntary association than it did for Devlin's England: we have no monopoly of force, people chose to be here, and our entire offering is a shared set of expectations about fair dealing that we arguably do have to defend or we're just a supermarket with a membership card. So I dont want a procedure that smuggles my priors in through the vocabulary. I want one that takes Devlin's side seriously enough that a Devlin-leaning panelist would sign it, while making Hart's objections do actual work rather than decorative work.

Here's what I need. A decision procedure the panel can run in order, on paper, inside ninety minutes: the questions to ask, in sequence; what counts as a sufficient answer to each; where the procedure stops with "no case to answer"; where it hands the decision back to the panel because the question is one of values rather than fact; and what has to be written down at each stage so the reasoning is auditable later. Tell me which distinctions from the debate have to survive the simplification — the gap between what a community actually feels and what it can defend feeling, the difference between revulsion and a moral judgment, the empirical question of whether enforcement preserves a norm or just pushes it underground and destroys trust in the panel, the fact that moral change in a community isn't the same thing as the community dying — and which ones I can safely drop for a room of volunteers. Also say what the procedure does when our values document is silent or contradicts itself, and when the outraged group is small and vocal and everyone else is quiet. Then run it on three of the cases above — the reselling, the T-shirt, and the petition about the adult-content member — and be straight with me about where it lands, including on the one where I'm going to hate the answer. Finish with what the procedure can't do: the cases it'll get wrong, the ways it'll be misused, and the point at which I'd be fooling myself if I thought a checklist settled this. Finally, two things on confidence: say how confident you are in the load-bearing moves, and single out the one you're least sure of; and flag anywhere you're reconstructing Hart or Devlin rather than reporting them — if the version you're working from is a contested reading, or if applying an argument about criminal law to a private association's expulsion process is a stretch, say so plainly instead of letting it ride.
