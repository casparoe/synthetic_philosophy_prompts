Credit ratings on mortgage-backed securities were, for roughly a decade, produced by statistical loss models. In 2007–2008 they failed en masse, and the failure is usually narrated as a story about greed, or about complexity, or about a housing bubble nobody saw coming. I want it analyzed instead as an instance of the reference class problem, and I want the analysis to run in both directions.

Here is the skeleton of the episode as I understand it; correct me where I am wrong, but do not let the essay depend on disputed details. The major agencies assigned AAA and other investment-grade ratings to tranches of securities backed largely by subprime mortgages. Those ratings came out of models calibrated on the historical performance of comparable mortgage pools, and the history available to the modelers was a period in which U.S. house prices had risen nearly everywhere and had never declined simultaneously and nationwide. A broad, sustained price decline was therefore not in the sample. The ratings carried enormous weight because regulation, capital rules, and investment mandates were written in terms of them, and because a AAA on a structured tranche was supposed to mean what a AAA on a corporate bond meant. When the pools performed catastrophically, part of the defense offered was that the triggering event had no precedent in the available statistical record — that there was, in effect, no reference class to consult.

First, the mapping. State the reference class problem as precisely as you can: a single event falls into indefinitely many classes, each with its own base rate, and nothing in a purely frequentist account of probability selects among them. Then lay out the candidate classes actually in play for the agencies — recent U.S. mortgage pool performance; longer and wider histories of housing finance including episodes where prices did fall; the class of leveraged, correlated, model-dependent structured products; the class of everything previously rated AAA — and say what each would have licensed, and why the data alone did not settle the choice.

Second, the diagnosis. Distinguish at least three readings of the failure: a better reference class was available and was not used; no adequate reference class existed, so any point estimate was arbitrary; and no frequency estimate was the right kind of output, since the question that mattered — how losses would correlate once leverage and common exposure were taken into account — was structural rather than statistical. Argue for one of these, and say what evidence would separate them.

Third, where the lens breaks. Give an honest accounting of how much of the episode was about reference classes at all, and how much is better explained by things the framework cannot see: the issuer-pays business model, regulatory dependence on the ratings themselves, competitive pressure from issuers shopping their deals. If the reference class framing absorbs these only by being stretched until it means nothing more than bad judgment, say so.

Fourth, a feature I think the standard telling misses. The ratings were not passive descriptions. Once published, they organized the portfolios whose correlation and leverage made the historical sample unrepresentative, so that acting on the base rate altered the very class the base rate had been drawn from. Analyze whether this makes the practice self-undermining in principle or only self-undermining at scale, and what would have to hold for a statistical rating practice to remain stable under its own widespread adoption.

Finally, the asymmetry. If the absence of a usable reference class made the outcome unforeseeable, does the same absence also show that no reasonable estimate was available beforehand? Can the no-reference-class defense do both jobs at once — excusing the disaster and excusing the estimate — or does invoking it in one place commit you to something you cannot accept in the other?

Do not close on a balanced summary. Tell me what you think the episode establishes about base-rate reasoning where the thing being estimated has no natural kind, and whether the notion of a reference class survives the case intact, needs a non-frequentist grounding, or should be abandoned in favor of something else.
