I am delivering a paper at an interdisciplinary colloquium in a philosophy department next month, and I should like a Q&A preparation sheet rather than a defence of the view.

The thesis is that Strawson gets the order of explanation backwards among the reactive attitudes. His taxonomy treats the impartial or vicarious responses — indignant resentment, moral disapprobation, the attitudes we take up on behalf of someone other than ourselves — as analogues of the personal ones, and the literature since has largely followed suit: the paradigm is my dignity, my expectations of regard, my standing to resent. I want to argue that the third-personal form is basic, and that the argument runs roughly as follows.

1. The cases that do the justificatory work in the debate — resentment on behalf of a comatose patient, of the dead, of a severely cognitively disabled person, of a non-human animal — involve victims who cannot hold the personal attitude at all. If the personal attitude were primary, these would be extension cases needing an account of how standing transfers; on my view they are unmixed instances of the primary form.
2. The primary form is not *I take this as a slight against me* but *someone's claim to regard has been disregarded, and I stand for it*. Standing to resent is therefore a matter of being in a position to represent a claim, not of being its subject. This is why a stranger may properly resent, and why the victim may properly fail to — through self-blame, premature forgiveness, or sheer exhaustion.
3. It follows that the propriety of resentment tracks the beneficiary's claim rather than the resenter's emotional economy, which lets me handle hypocrisy, complicity and tainted standing without bolting a separate standing condition onto a personal-first picture.
4. It also follows that the attitudes are far more socially variable than Strawson's naturalism permits: because the basic attitude is calibrated to how legible a beneficiary's claim is within our practices, we systematically fail to resent on behalf of those our practices do not render visible. The framework is natural in its roots, conventional in its reach.

The room will contain: two senior moral philosophers in the Strawsonian tradition, who hold that these attitudes are natural commitments not properly up for review, and who will suspect that I have quietly turned resentment into a judgement and dissolved the distinction between the participant stance and the objective one; one relational theorist of blame, for whom blame is a revision of one's judgements and intentions in light of an impaired relationship, and who will want to know what resentment adds; one theorist of second-personal authority, who will claim that I have simply re-described his apparatus without its structure of accountability; one sceptic about moral responsibility, who cares only whether my account can survive the abandonment of desert; and three or four graduate students working at the interface with moral psychology, who will arrive with findings about in-group bias in third-party punishment, the effect of outcome severity on indignation, and the malleability of blame attributions.

Please produce, in this order: a short list of the concessions I should make in the opening five minutes so that certain lines of attack lose their charge; then roughly ten objections, ranked by how damaging they would be if I fielded them badly rather than by how likely they are, each written as its objector would actually put it from the floor, in that person's idiom and first person; a reply to each that I could deliver in under two minutes; and a candid note on where each reply is softest. Mark explicitly which objections, if they land, would oblige me to give up the priority thesis altogether rather than merely to restate it more carefully, and flag any pair you expect to arrive as a coordinated pincer, with a line for declining to answer them separately. Close with the exchange I would most prefer not to have to improvise, and an explanation of why it is difficult.

Do not include a bibliography, a reading list or any citations, and do not put words in quotation marks unless they are mine. Where a reply depends on an empirical claim about how resentment actually behaves, label it as an empirical commitment I would have to defend rather than presenting it as settled.
