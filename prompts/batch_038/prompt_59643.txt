You are a philosopher specialising in action theory and free will, with a working knowledge of how philosophers of mind and of biology have used insect behaviour to mark the line between the mechanical and the agential. Spend a paragraph working out what actually varies across the cases below — sensitivity to reasons, sensitivity to perturbation, endorsement, history of acquisition, the range of counterfactual situations in which the agent would do something else — before you commit to any principle. Treat my list as data to be explained, not as views to be agreed with.

Here are my verdicts on rigidity and freedom, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. The digger wasp of the textbook story: she drags a paralysed caterpillar to the mouth of her burrow, goes inside to check, and if the caterpillar is moved a few inches while she is in there she comes out, hauls it back to the mouth, goes in again, and loops indefinitely. No freedom, no responsibility — even though she is doing precisely what she is trying to do. (I also half-suspect the anecdote is bad natural history, since later observers report that such wasps do not always loop; tell me if that undermines the case rather than merely illustrating it.)
2. A sat-nav that re-routes me down the same closed road, cheerfully recalculating each time: unfree in the same way.
3. A chess engine that plays from an opening book but abandons it without hesitation when the position is unfamiliar: something has plainly been added, yet I would not call the engine free.
4. A judge who applies a sentencing rule to a case she can see is a travesty, saying her hands are tied: blameworthy, hence free — although her behaviour is exactly as rigid as the wasp's.
5. A guest who refuses a slice of cake at a wedding and wounds her host: rigid, mildly blameworthy, and yet she insists she could not have taken it. I find I do not know whether to believe her.
6. A monk who weighed leaving, chose the vow, and now reports that breaking it is unthinkable: free and praiseworthy, and genuinely unable to do otherwise.
7. Someone raised in a closed community who reports exactly the same — that transgression is unthinkable — and behaves identically to the monk: not blameworthy, and less free, so far as I can tell, on the very same dispositions and the very same modal profile.
8. The reformer whose conscience is captive and who therefore cannot recant: unable to do otherwise, and yet my paradigm of free action.
9. The person whose one fixed policy is to reconsider every policy: the freest agent I can describe, or sphexish one level up?

State the principle or the small set of candidate principles that best captures this pattern, clause by clause, saying which case motivates each clause. Then give its verdicts on two cases I have not listed: an agent who freely bound himself in advance — the mast and the sirens — and is now inflexible by his own earlier design; and an agent whose only flexibility comes from an external scaffold, a checklist or a supervisor who prompts the reconsideration, where I cannot tell whether the flexibility is his or the scaffold's. Finally, be explicit about which of my nine verdicts the best principle must overturn, and why the cost is worth paying rather than buying the verdict back with an extra clause. Where the literature bears on this, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on the problem, and do not present a contested position as the consensus.
