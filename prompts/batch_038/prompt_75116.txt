Six years ago I moved from a northern European country, where the price on the menu is the price you pay and nobody leaves anything extra anywhere, to a mid-sized American city. Relatives and old friends now visit me several times a year, and each of them asks some version of the same question: what am I expected to do here, and what happens morally if I do not do it. I have answered a dozen times, and I have realized that my answers do not compose into anything I could state. I would like you to try to state it. I am after a principle, or two or three competing candidates, that captures the pattern of my verdicts — and, where it cannot, an account of which verdicts I have to abandon and why.

Here is what I actually think, case by case.

1. A visitor who eats a sit-down dinner, is served competently and courteously, and leaves nothing on the table has done something wrong. I do not think the wrongness is exhausted by the missing money.

2. A visitor who buys a coffee at a counter, receives the cup ninety seconds later, and is presented with a payment screen offering three percentages, does nothing wrong by pressing the button for no gratuity. I feel a flicker of something when I do it myself. I do not think the flicker is guilt.

3. Cash left for whoever cleans a hotel room: I do it, and I think a first-time guest who does not is blameless. But having done it on my first night, I now think I would wrong the same person by not doing it on my third. My own history seems to change the moral shape of an act that the custom itself leaves unchanged.

4. Crossing against the signal on an empty street at two in the morning: permissible. Crossing against the signal into moving traffic: wrong — though I cannot tell whether I object to the rule-breaking or to the hazard, and I suspect I would still object if the road were empty and the hazard absent but a police officer watching.

5. In the country I came from, pedestrians wait for the green figure at deserted intersections, and I wait when I visit, though I consider the waiting pointless. When my seven-year-old nephew is beside me, the requirement feels several times stronger. That difference makes me suspect the obligation attaches to the audience rather than the act.

6. Cutting into the order of people waiting at a bus stop where there is no barrier and no ticket, only an understood sequence of arrival that everyone present can track: seriously wrong. Cutting into the same order at an airport gate where a machine has already recorded everyone's place: still objectionable, but noticeably less so. If what mattered were that others lose their position, the ranking should run the other way.

7. A visitor in a country where it is customary to hand a small sum to the traffic officer who has stopped you for a defective light: I think he should refuse, and that complying would be wrong. The custom is entrenched, widely observed, and part of many people's income, exactly as the restaurant custom is. I have no account of why that similarity does not carry the same verdict.

8. A visitor who could not reasonably have known the local practice and does not follow it: not blameworthy. The same visitor, on a second evening, after I have explained it to him at length: blameworthy if he still does not follow it. Nothing about the practice or its beneficiaries changed between the two evenings; only what he knew.

9. A colleague who considers the whole arrangement an unjust transfer of the employer's wage bill onto customers, says as much to the servers, and continues to eat out three times a week while leaving nothing: I do not think he is doing wrong, but I think he owes something standing in place of the money, and that without it his position collapses into case 1.

10. I think it is imperious of me to reproach a visitor over which utensil he uses, whether he brings anything to a dinner he has been invited to, or where he sits in a taxicab. Yet I cannot identify a difference in kind between those practices and the gratuity — only a difference in whether anyone's rent depends on the outcome.

11. A crossing where roughly half the town ignores the signal, because the wait is famously long: I ignore it too, without discomfort. A practice everyone around me observes without exception: I follow it, and I cannot find anything wrong with the act of breaking it. I worry that my verdicts are tracking what people in fact do rather than anything about the acts themselves.

The principle I have been reaching for, and which I know is inadequate, is roughly this: breaking a local convention is wrong only where the convention exists to solve a problem that my non-compliance would make worse, for identifiable people who are relying on me. It handles 1, 4, and 6 reasonably well. It wrongly permits 2, since the person behind the counter is paid the same way and is relying just as much. It wrongly permits 7. It is silent on 8, on 3, and on the asymmetry in 11, and it condemns the colleague in 9, which I am not willing to accept.

What I would like from you:

- State the repaired principle, or the two or three candidate principles that divide my cases differently, precisely enough that I could apply one to an unfamiliar practice without coming back to you. For each clause, tell me which of my verdicts it is there to accommodate.
- Say frankly which of my verdicts the best candidate contradicts. I expect at least one of 2, 3, and 9 has to go; tell me which, and what replaces my confidence in it.
- Apply the result to four cases I have not raised: a passenger who rates a driver five stars and pays nothing on a platform that prompts for an extra amount; a cyclist who rolls through a stop sign on a deserted residential street in a jurisdiction that has considered legalizing exactly that; a traveler who pays the posted price in a market where bargaining is the norm and the seller expects it; a guest who takes the seat next to a stranger in a half-empty carriage when every other seat is free.
- Tell me whether the visitor's position differs in kind from the resident's, or only in what he knows and what he has already done — and, separately, whether my standing to disapprove of him is the same thing as his having acted wrongly, since case 10 makes me think it is not.
- If the principle has to appeal to what a practice is for, tell me how to fix what it is for when its origins and its present function have come apart, as I believe they have with the restaurant gratuity.

I am not looking for reading suggestions or for the names of the debates this belongs to. I want the carving.
