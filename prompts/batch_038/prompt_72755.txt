I keep a notebook of comparative judgments in the margins of my drafts, and after four novels I have noticed that the entries do not add up. I should like someone to tell me what principle, if any, they are tracking.

**One.** Last winter I cut a ferry chapter down by degrees. The first cut removed a paragraph about my narrator's father's hands; the chapter came out tighter and I judged the result better. The second removed a comic exchange with a deckhand; better again, though I was fond of the exchange. The third removed the gulls. Over three weeks I made twelve such cuts, and at every one I judged the new version better than the version it came from. These are the verdicts I am surest of, because each was made in the room, with the two pages side by side, and none of them was hurried. When I finally set the twelfth version beside the first, though, the first is plainly the better chapter: the twelfth is a skeleton with the weather left in. I do not believe I was wrong at any individual step.

**Two.** Two candidate last lines for that chapter do different work, and I judge neither of them better than the other. I have reasons on both sides and can state them. Yet I would not accept a swap: if an editor substituted the rival line while my back was turned, I would insist on the change being reversed and say the book had been worsened. This is the case I keep going back and forth on. Meanwhile there are comma-level variants of an ordinary paragraph where I rank one above the other without hesitation, cannot say why, and would not defend the ranking under questioning.

**Three.** A friend observed that if my judgments run in circles of that sort, someone could walk me up a ladder of drafts and finish the afternoon holding my first version and owing me a coffee. I said that sounded like a pleasant afternoon. He said that was the trap closing. I remain unmoved on my own account; but I am presently writing a man who does exactly this with racehorses, and he is meant to be a fool.

**Four.** My comparative vocabulary slides. Asked whether a version is better than the one it came from, I answer one way; asked whether it is better than the book I meant to write, another. Asked whether it is better as a sentence and worse as part of a chapter, I say both without embarrassment — a phrase can be the finer sentence and make the poorer paragraph.

**Five.** I judge a spare, flawless novella better than a long, generous, digressive novel. I also judge that same novella worse than a slightly longer work carrying one brilliant digression. And I would judge a second brilliant digression an improvement on that. I have not run this ladder to its end, and I do not intend to.

What I want is the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that governs these verdicts — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case you have never seen without writing to you again. For each clause, tell me which of my verdicts it exists to preserve. Then tell me what it says about three situations I have not raised: a ladder whose steps I would judge confidently but whose endpoints I have never compared; a ranking I can make and cannot justify; and whether my novel is better than an admired book that is doing nothing remotely like what mine is doing. Finally, be candid about which of my verdicts the principle must overturn. I would rather surrender one of them than be handed a principle with eleven clauses in it.

If there is a name for the view you land on, give it, but I am after the principle rather than the pedigree. Please keep your answer under nine hundred words, and close with a short note saying how confident you are in what you have offered and naming the single claim in it that you would most expect a better philosopher to overturn.
