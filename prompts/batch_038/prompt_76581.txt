**Dirty Hands Without an Audience: Remorse as the Second Stage of the Act**

The debate about dirty hands has settled into a fairly stable shape, at least as the terms of the puzzle go. A political agent confronts a situation in which the only available means of securing something of real moral importance — averting an attack, holding together the coalition without which a settlement collapses — requires him to do something that is, by any ordinary reckoning, wrong: to authorise an interrogation that amounts to torture, to deceive people whose trust he holds, to break a promise on which someone's fate depends. He does it. Walzer's contribution was to insist that we should not tidy the situation up in either direction. The agent was right to act as he did and is nevertheless properly judged for having done it; his having been right does not render him innocent. The standard formulation of the difficulty is that one and the same act appears to be both required and forbidden, and the familiar responses — role morality, threshold deontology, the suggestion that the puzzle is merely verbal — are attempts to break the apparent contradiction.

My proposal is that these responses share an unstated assumption: that the moral assessment of the dirty act is complete at the moment of acting. Once the deed is done, on this picture, there is a fixed moral fact about it, and everything that happens afterwards — the agent's anguish, his resignation, the inquiry, the memoir — is commentary on that fact. I want to deny this. On the account sketched here, the residue that dirty hands theorists make so much of is not evidence left over from a completed wrong. It is the second stage of the wrong itself.

Consider what the residue is supposed to do. Almost nobody writing in this tradition thinks the anguished statesman innocent; the anguish is precisely what stops him being either a monster or a saint. But then we should ask what work the anguish is doing, and the answer suggested by the cases is that it is doing moral work, not merely expressive work. Contrast two officials who authorise exactly the same thing. The first is visibly marked by it: he does not enjoy the discretion his office affords him, he insists the decision be recorded in his own name, he resigns once the necessity has passed. The second is entirely untroubled; he regards the episode as a technical success and mentions it only when doing so is useful. We do not merely find the second man less likeable. We think something further has gone wrong in his case, and we think it has gone wrong in the act, not only in the character. My suggestion is that this is because the act is incomplete.

On the account I am proposing, a dirty hands situation calls for a two-stage performance, and both stages belong to what morality requires. Stage one is the transgression: the deception, the betrayal, the authorised cruelty. Stage two is the agent's submission to condemnation and the community's supply of it — the accounting, the naming, the punishment where punishment is appropriate, the refusal to let the matter be folded silently into the general business of governing. What is required, all things considered, is the transgression-plus-reckoning package; what is forbidden is the transgression taken on its own. The paradox dissolves because there is no single act of which we must say both that it is required and that it is forbidden.

Three consequences recommend the view. First, it explains why the unrepentant agent is worse than the anguished one without our having to say that two men who did the same thing performed different acts. Second, it explains the peculiar character of exposure. A covert operation that is later revealed seems worse than one that never comes to light, even though, on a view that fixes the moral facts at the moment of acting, discovery cannot alter anything about them. On my view, discovery does alter something: it makes the second stage possible, and until it is possible the wrong is unfinished. Third, it makes sense of the thought that the community shares the taint. The second stage is not something the agent can perform alone; a resignation nobody accepts, an apology issued into a vacuum, does not complete the transaction. If the community declines to supply condemnation — because it finds the transgression too useful, or too embarrassing, to examine — it becomes party to an incomplete act, and that is what the talk of shared dirt is gesturing at.

Two difficulties occur to me and I have not resolved them. The account makes the wrongness of an act partly a matter of how things go afterwards, which sits oddly with the idea that a wrong is something an agent does. And it seems to imply that a convincingly staged performance of remorse, delivered by an agent who feels none, could complete the second stage as well as the genuine article; but if so, the account makes sincerity irrelevant to a process that appears to be entirely about sincerity. I flag these as places where I suspect the view needs help rather than as objections I consider decisive.

My requests, in order of importance:

1. **Novelty.** Is the central thesis genuinely distinct from the familiar insistence that dirty-handed agents must be judged for what they have done, or have I simply described a social practice and then relabelled that description as a claim about the wrongness of the act? If it collapses into the existing view, I should like to know at which step the collapse occurs.

2. **The dissolution move.** Please examine the claim that what is required is transgression-plus-reckoning while what is forbidden is transgression alone. Does this actually remove the contradiction, or relocate it? In particular: does the account now make the permissibility of an act depend on facts unavailable to the agent at the moment he must decide, and is that fatal rather than merely awkward?

3. **Accuracy.** Have I mis-stated the dialectic? Is the characterisation of Walzer fair? Is it true that the standard responses treat the moral assessment as complete at the moment of acting, or is that a straw man of my own construction which makes my position look more original than it is? Please say so plainly if I have set the debate up in a self-serving way.

4. **Slippage.** In the second of the three consequences I move from the observation that a revealed operation seems worse to the claim that revelation changes something about the wrong. Flag any other place where I slide between appearance and reality in this manner, or between what we feel about an agent and what is true of his act.

5. **Objections.** Are the two difficulties I flag the right ones to be worried about, or is there a worse objection that I have missed altogether? If there is a case that breaks the account rather than merely straining it, I would much rather have that case described to me now.

6. **Verdict.** Is this worth developing into a full paper, or is the interesting content really only a paragraph with a lot of scaffolding around it?

I am not after references or a reading list — I can find the literature myself. What I want is a judgement on whether the argument works, and a diagnosis of the places where it does not.
