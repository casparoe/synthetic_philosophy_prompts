I've been sitting on the Knobe effect for going on two years now — the chairman who only cares about profit, the environment getting harmed or helped as a side effect, people saying "intentional" in the harm case and not in the help case — and I'm becoming convinced the argument about what it *means* hasnt stalled in the normal unsettled-but-progressing way. It's stalled in a way where the phenomenon keeps getting more robust and the accounts keep not converging. The asymmetry survives reworded vignettes, other languages, within-subject designs where people see both cases back to back, and subjects who know perfectly well what the effect is and insist it doesnt apply to them. Meanwhile there are at least four live explanations:

- moral considerations are built into the concept of intentional action itself, so the asymmetric judgments are competence, not error;
- it's pragmatic: blaming ascriptions carry a conversational load that praising ones don't, so the asymmetry is about what saying the sentence does;
- it's affect — a motivated blame-attribution that a sufficiently neutral measure wouldn't turn up;
- the real variable isn't moral badness but whether the side effect expresses the agent's own evaluative stance, and harming expresses more than helping.

Two things set me off. First, in our reading group the pragmatic defender and the concept defender sat down and genuinely tried to construct a case where their views predict different verdicts, and after an hour they couldn't. Everything the concept side offered as evidence — persistence under reflection, cross-linguistic stability, robustness to wording — the pragmatic side could absorb as entrenched conversational habit, and everything the pragmatic side offered — attenuation under odd response formats, wording sensitivity — the concept side could wave off as surface performance noise. Second, we ran a pilot adding a third condition, a chairman who eats a modest profit loss to protect the environment and says he doesn't care about the profit, and the intentional-rate for the good side effect shot up well above the standard help case for maybe a third of subjects. Both camps claimed it as theirs on the spot.

So here's my hypothesis, and I want you to attack it: this isn't a disagreement about the psychology at all. It's two research programs with different explananda sharing the word "concept," and what looks like an empirical fight is actually a fight about what a theory of concepts is even supposed to be a theory of.

Is that diagnosis right, or is there a hidden empirical disagreement wedged in there that could be settled — something about whether the effect survives measures with no conversational structure, or about individual differences, or about how it develops in kids — that both sides would accept as decisive *before* seeing the result? And if you think I'm right, what would actually count as progress here: a new paradigm, a formal model that makes the competing accounts into distinguishable objects, a redivision of the question, or is this the sort of standoff where nothing counts as progress and the honest move is to stop asking? Argue for the diagnosis using what each side really claims, don't give me a tour of the four accounts. American English, please.
