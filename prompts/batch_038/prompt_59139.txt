I'm in a decision theory seminar this term and we just spent a week on the prisoner's dilemma and Hofstadter's superrationality, along with the thought experiment he hangs it on — the one where an eccentric trillionaire gets 20 people in a room and announces that if exactly one of them sends him a collect telegram by noon the next day, that person gets a billion dollars, but if none arrive or more than one arrives nobody gets anything, and they're not allowed to coordinate. Superrational answer there is each person sends with probability 1/20. Our TA then mentioned that Hofstadter actually ran a real-world version of this in a Scientific American column in 1983, the Luring Lottery, and that it went badly, and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it.

The rules as I've got them: you entered by mailing a postcard, and writing a 1 on it counted as one entry. You were explicitly allowed to enter as many times as you wanted just by writing a bigger number on your single postcard. The prize was a million dollars divided by the total number of entries recieved, awarded to whoever submitted one randomly drawn entry — so entering more times raised your odds but shrank the pot. The column apparently suggested the superrational move was to estimate how many readers would respond (something like 5 percent of the readership), roll a die with that many sides, and mail a postcard only if you rolled a 1. What happened instead: a bunch of people entered astronomically large numbers, several wrote a googolplex, some filled their postcards with expressions designed to evaluate to the biggest number that would fit in the space. The prize diluted to a fraction of a cent and they couldn't even determine a winner. Reportedly the publisher and owners were nervous about betting the company on this, and Hofstadter's follow-up column that fall had a title to the effect of irrationality being the square root of all evil.

Can you work through this as a case study of the theory rather than just retelling it? Specifically:

1. Is the lottery even the same game as Platonia? Map it out — who the players are, the strategy space, the payoff structure, and what the symmetry assumption is doing. In Platonia the payoff is all-or-nothing on exactly one sender; here the pot splits. Does the roll-a-die-with-N-sides prescription still fall out of superrational reasoning once the payoff is diluted like that, or does the split-pot structure change the answer? I want the reasoning, not just a verdict.

2. The googolplex people. It looks to me like they weren't playing the lottery at all — they were playing a biggest-number contest that happened to share a maildrop. If that's right, then the core premise of superrationality, that everyone reasoning superrationally about the same problem converges on the same answer, needs an unstated extra assumption about everyone first identifying the same game. Is that a genuine gap the episode exposes, or am I just manufacturing an excuse for the theory?

3. The population problem. To roll the die you need to know who's in your group and how big it is, and the group here was whoever felt like mailing a postcard — self-selected, unknown size, 5 percent being a guess. Plus the readership obviously contained lots of people who were rational but not superrational, which by Hofstadter's own lights means a superrationalist facing them should defect. How much of the failure is just the preconditions not being satisfied, and how much is a problem with the reasoning itself?

4. Then the verdict: does this episode refute superrationality, count as an anomaly the theory can absorb, or was it never a possible test of it in the first place? And please be willing to say where the theory is simply silent — I'd much rather have that than have it stretched to cover everything.

5. Finally, if you were designing a follow-up experiment that could actually test the superrational claim, what would it look like, and what would you have to control for that Hofstadter didn't?

Not after sources or a reading list — I've got the columns. I want the analysis.
