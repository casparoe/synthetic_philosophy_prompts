I convene a graduate reading group that runs a recorded interview series on non-Western epistemology, and next term's guest is a philosopher whose recent monograph offers a reconstruction of the Nyāya account of the sources of knowledge. Her thesis is deliberately two-fronted: against the Mīmāṃsā expansion she argues that there are exactly four irreducible *pramāṇas* — perception, inference, comparison, and testimony — so that both postulation and non-apprehension must be analysed away; and against the Buddhist contraction she argues that testimony in particular cannot be collapsed into an inference from the speaker's reliability. I have forty minutes with her, and I want questions that engage the machinery of the position rather than inviting her to narrate it.

Please draft eight interview questions, each immediately followed by a follow-up. Every follow-up must be keyed to the reply she is most likely to give: write it conditionally, in the form 'if she answers by …, then press …', so that I am not left holding a question she has already defused. Devote at least one question to each of the following; the remaining two are yours to choose, provided they are of equal weight and at least one of them addresses how she defines knowledge itself — and, in particular, whether a true cognition produced by a defective process still counts as knowledge on her account.

1. Postulation. In the stock case — a man known to be stout who is known not to eat during the day — the Mīmāṃsaka says the resolution of the tension is not inferential, because the required pervasion is available only by way of the very postulation being reduced. Ask her where the pervasion comes from, and press on whether her reduction smuggles the postulate in as a premise.

2. Testimony. She must answer the Buddhist reduction without conceding that warrant for a speaker's reliability is itself testimonial. Ask what grounds that warrant, and whether her reply commits her either to circularity or to a regress she has no independent route out of.

3. Memory. On her account memory yields nothing not already cognised and is therefore not a *pramāṇa*; yet the operative cause of an inference is the subject's awareness of the mark as pervaded by what is to be proved, and that awareness depends on a pervasion she can only remember. Ask what the epistemic status of that remembered link is, and whether the causal chain of inference can run through something that is not itself a source of knowledge.

4. Absence. She owes an account of how an absence — which has no causal powers of its own — can enter the perceptual relation she requires. Ask how her appeal to the counterpositive does the work, and what stops every perception from being a perception of indefinitely many absences at once.

5. Extraordinary perception. To secure pervasion from finite experience she posits a mode of perception that reaches every instance of a universal. Ask whether this explains anything or merely relabels the difficulty, and what is left of the notion of perception once it has been stretched that far.

6. The stopping rule. Ask what principled criterion of *pramāṇa*-hood generates four items rather than two, three, or six. If she answers that the list is fixed by which capacities resist analysis into the others, ask what is doing the analytic work — and why comparison survives that test while postulation does not.

Format: open with a single sentence stating the through-line — the one problem all eight questions are circling — so that I can see the sequence has an argumentative arc rather than being a list. Then number the questions 1 to 8, each with its follow-up beneath, introduced by 'Follow-up:'. Keep every question to at most three sentences and every follow-up to at most two. Keep the whole response under 900 words. Write in British English, italicise the Sanskrit terms, and gloss each on first use. No warm-up or biographical questions.
