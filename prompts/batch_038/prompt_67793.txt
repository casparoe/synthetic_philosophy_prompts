My reading group on the metaphysics of spacetime ended the semester in a bad place. One of us assembled the argument below, whose premises we each found individually hard to refuse and whose conclusion we each found worse than any of its premises. I have not been able to locate the failure.

(1) Substantivalism says that space — or spacetime — is a substance: its points are individuals existing independently of whatever matter occupies them, so a world is completely described only when one has said which body is at which point.

(2) If (1) is true, then a world W* in which the entire material contents of the universe are displaced ten miles to the east of their positions in our world W, with every inter-body distance, angle, relative velocity and force preserved, is numerically distinct from W: the same bodies stand in the same relations to one another but in different relations to the points of space.

(3) No observation could ever tell W from W*, because everything observable is a relation among material bodies and every such relation has the same value in both.

(4) Two worlds that could not be told apart even in principle are not two worlds but one. A difference that could never be manifested anywhere is not a difference at all.

(5) So W and W* are a single world, the individuation of points required by (2) is no part of the furniture, and relationism is right. The bucket's concavity must then be explained by relations among bodies — on Mach's line, by rotation relative to the mass distribution of the universe as a whole.

(6) But (4) is a general principle, and it reaches far past the shift. A world that sprang into being five minutes ago, complete with fossils, memories and starlight already in transit, is observationally indistinguishable from ours, so by (4) it is ours, and there is no past beyond its traces. An empirically adequate Bohmian universe and an orthodox one are observationally indistinguishable, so by (4) there is no fact as to which obtains. An unobserved region of a distant galaxy is observationally indistinguishable from countless rivals, so by (4) it has no properties at all.

(7) That is verificationism in its global form: no fact outruns the observable.

(8) We are realists. There is a fact about the state of the universe before there were observers, and empirically equivalent rivals can genuinely differ about what exists. So (7) is false; and since (7) is entailed by (4), (4) is false.

(9) With (4) gone, nothing stands in the way of (2). W and W* are genuinely distinct worlds and space points are genuinely individuals, so Leibniz's shift argument fails. It never showed substantivalism to be incoherent; it showed only that substantivalism has undetectable consequences, which realism entitles it to have.

(10) The modern descendant of the shift falls for the same reason. The hole argument against general-relativistic substantivalism is the shift argument with a diffeomorphism confined to a region in place of a rigid translation of everything: it yields two models agreeing outside the hole and differing inside it, and asks the substantivalist to say whether they represent one world or two. Rejecting (4) is precisely to say two. So the hole argument no longer threatens determinism; it merely reports that our theory, like our metaphysics, permits distinctions we cannot observe.

(11) And the bucket now stands unopposed. The water's concavity tracks rotation with respect to space itself rather than with respect to any body, and Mach's appeal to the distant masses earns nothing, since no experiment performed at the bucket discriminates his story from the substantivalist's.

(12) Therefore substantivalism is not merely undefeated but established, and the whole controversy is settled by a general metaphysical principle — realism about the unobservable — with no further input from physics.

What I want to keep, if I can: (4), which still looks to me like nothing more extravagant than the demand that a metaphysical distinction be a distinction of some possible thing; and (8), which I am not prepared to surrender. What I cannot accept is (12), and I am nearly as unhappy with (10) and (11). Yet I cannot find a step that is simply false, and the author insists that anyone who gives up (4) has already conceded him the shift argument, and anyone who keeps it has already conceded him verificationism.

Please diagnose the argument, using exactly these sections in this order:

1. **Bottom line** — at most 150 words. Identify the step or steps that fail, or, if you judge the chain valid and some plausible premise must simply be surrendered, identify that premise. Say in one sentence what kind of failure it is.
2. **Reading the steps** — 500 to 700 words. Walk the chain from (1) to (12), and at each contested transition state what would have to be true for it to go through and what is in fact true. Pay particular attention to the words doing the work in (4) and to what (9) and (10) claim to have inherited from (8).
3. **Pricing the repairs** — 400 to 600 words. Consider at least three distinct premises or transitions one might reject in order to block (12). For each, state what the repair buys and exactly what it costs — including which further commitments in the spacetime debate it forces you to abandon or reinterpret — and then say which price you would pay and why.
4. **The bucket and the hole** — 200 to 300 words. On your diagnosis, say what survives of Newton's bucket argument and of the hole argument once the shift argument has been dealt with, if it has been.
