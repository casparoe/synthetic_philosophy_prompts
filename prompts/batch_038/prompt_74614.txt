I cover science and ideas for a British magazine, and I have been commissioned to write a long feature on the revival of panpsychism — the view that experience, or something precursory to it, is basic in nature rather than something that switches on at the level of brains. My editor wants the piece to have a historical spine, and I keep coming back to the objection William James presses in the first volume of his 1890 *Principles of Psychology*, in the chapter where he takes up the mind-stuff theory then current: the proposal that the world is composed of minute units of feeling which, aggregated in the right way, amount to the minds of higher organisms. Because I am working from a library scan and would rather not misquote, what follows is my own close paraphrase of the argument, not his sentences; please treat the wording as mine, and where you think my rendering has drifted from what James actually argues, say so plainly. My paraphrase: the theorist in question holds that feeling accompanies matter throughout nature, and that the feeling of an organism is constituted by the feelings of its parts. James's reply is that adding feelings together does not yield a feeling of the sum. Gather as many of these elemental feelings as you like, order them however you please, and press them together as tightly as the picture allows: what you end up with is still a collection of just that many feelings, each with its own private being, and no additional item consisting of one subject aware of the collection. If each unit is already a subject with its own point of view, the points of view remain many, because the being of a feeling is its being felt by someone, and the theory supplies no candidate for who feels the aggregate. He also seems to think the analogy such a theory leans on — that chemical compounds have properties their elements lack — cannot carry the weight here, since a compound's novel properties can be specified from outside by an observer, whereas a novel feeling would have to be somebody's in order to count as a feeling at all.

For my own purposes I have been sorting the combination problem into four distinguishable puzzles, and I want to know which of them James's argument is really aimed at, because I suspect it gets cited as though it disposed of all four at once. My four: (i) subject combination — how many subjects could become one subject; (ii) quality combination — how elementary phenomenal qualities could compose into the rich, structured qualities we actually have, such as a continuous stretch of colour out of many micro-hues; (iii) perspectival combination — how many points of view could become a single point of view on one world; and (iv) binding — how the contents of an already unified subject get organised into one scene at a time rather than a succession of disconnected awarenesses. Four questions follow from that. First, which of (i) to (iv) does the paraphrased argument genuinely engage, and which does it merely sound as though it engages? Second, is James arguing against panpsychism as such, or only against an aggregative, constitution-first version of it — one on which the composite subject is nothing over and above its components and the components are already full-blown subjects? I ask because I want to know whether the argument still bites on positions James did not have in front of him: a view on which the fundamental units are precursory of experience rather than experiential, so that no actual subject need be summed; and a view that reverses the direction of dependence, holding that the whole is prior to its parts, which then inherit their character from it rather than lending theirs to it. Third, how much work is the gathering-and-packing imagery doing? Is it an argument — that aggregation preserves the number of items and therefore cannot deliver unity — or a vivid way of registering that we cannot imagine the transition, which would be a considerably weaker claim? How would you tell the two apart? Fourth, a neuroscientist friend of mine insists that what James describes is simply the binding problem in nineteenth-century clothes and that there is no distinct combination problem. I want to push back carefully rather than rhetorically: is she right, partly right, or wrong, and what precisely separates explaining how a subject's contents are bound from explaining how the subject itself comes to be?

Please answer as a professional philosopher of mind who also has a genuine command of the history of the discipline — someone equally at home explaining this to a general reader and picking apart its logical form for a specialist. Begin with a short stretch of thinking out loud: before committing to an interpretation, sketch two or three competing readings of what James is doing in this argument, and say what would decide between them. Then work through my four questions in order, making the structure explicit — premises, conclusions, and the places where a premise is doing more work than James seems to notice. Finally, and this matters to me as a journalist, be scrupulous about the epistemic status of everything you assert. Separate what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested, and do not let a view that happens to be popular, or that you find persuasive, masquerade as a consensus; where reasonable specialists disagree, say so and say roughly what the disagreement turns on. Flag my reconstruction wherever you think it likely distorts the original, and flag any point where your confidence in the historical detail ought to be lower than your confidence in the philosophy. I do not want references, a reading list, or footnotes — I cannot check them and I am not asking you to supply them; I want your reasoning, and your willingness to say where it runs out.
