The chapter of my dissertation that I present in three weeks argues that historical counterfactuals have a shelf life. The venue is a two-day methods workshop co-hosted by an economics department and a history department, roughly twenty-five people, and the composition matters: the clear majority are applied microeconomists and econometricians who work inside the potential-outcomes framework and think almost exclusively in terms of identification assumptions, pre-trends, exclusion restrictions, and estimands; three or four comparative historical sociologists who do process tracing and multi-case comparison and regard formalism with suspicion; one philosopher of science who works on explanation and modelling and does know the counterfactual literature properly; two graduate students who run agent-based and complex-systems models and will be friendly but merciless about technical detail; and an assigned discussant, a senior economic historian with a standing reputation for scepticism about simulation-driven history.

Here is the argument they will be attacking. (1) The macro-social processes that cliometricians model exhibit sensitive dependence: small differences in initial conditions amplify into large macro-differences at a rate that can be estimated in a calibrated model. (2) On the familiar nearest-world semantics, the closest world in which some historical event did not occur is a world in which the event is absent *and* the micro-state has been perturbed in order to remove it; under sensitive dependence that perturbation is by itself sufficient to generate a different macro-outcome well inside any horizon of interest. (3) Therefore, beyond a characteristic time — I call it the divergence horizon, defined as the point at which perturbations smaller than the resolution of the historical record push the outcome outside its observational error — the claim that had X not occurred, Y would not have occurred, is not merely hard to know but has no determinate truth value. (4) In the econometric idiom this is neither sampling error nor omitted-variable bias: it is indeterminacy of the estimand. Matching on pre-treatment covariates presupposes smoothness — nearby covariate values, nearby potential outcomes — and sensitive dependence is precisely the failure of that smoothness. (5) My diagnostic runs an ensemble of perturbed simulations of a calibrated model and reports a fragility curve: the proportion of ensemble members in which the sign of the outcome of interest flips, as a function of horizon. (6) I classify candidate pivotal events as load-bearing (the outcome moves inside the horizon) or saturated (the outcome arrives anyway along redundant routes), and I claim the historiographical habit of nominating dramatic pivots is biased toward the load-bearing class, so the diagnostic will demote famous events and promote mundane ones. (7) The recommendation is to state the horizon explicitly, decline counterfactual claims beyond it, and, where the horizon is shorter than the question demands, report the question as unanswerable rather than answer it with wider error bars.

Prepare my Q&A. I want roughly ten objections, ordered by the damage they would actually do in that room, and for each: which segment of the audience raises it and how it would be phrased aloud by them — an econometrician will not put it the way a process tracer will — a reply I can deliver standing up in about two minutes, and a candid flag on the softest joint in that reply. Tell me separately which of the objections are simply right and should be conceded rather than defended, and which two I should concede in the opening five minutes to pre-empt the worst lines of attack. Identify the single reply that has to land for the chapter to survive, and draft it properly. Then give me the question I am dreading — the one that would be fatal if anyone thought to ask it — with an honest verdict on whether an answer exists.

Two further requests. Do not go easy on the technical criticism: if the sensitive-dependence claim cannot be defended against a short, noisy, non-stationary historical series, or if I am conflating chaos with mere stochastic unpredictability, say so and tell me what to say instead. And name one sentence I should avoid uttering, because it reliably triggers a specific objection, together with the safer formulation. Assume I know the literature; I need argumentative content, not references.
