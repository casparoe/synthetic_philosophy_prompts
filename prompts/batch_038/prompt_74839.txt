Im a 4th year PhD student in philosophy of physics and this term Im TA-ing our third-year Space, Time and Relativity module, which is about half philosophy students and half physics students. The lecturer dumped the final exam on me and I have to write the essay question on the Rietdijk–Putnam argument / the block universe. Everyone in the class has seen the Andromeda walk example in lecture — the two people passing on the street, one heading toward Andromeda, and the claim that their planes of simultaneity disagree about whether the fleet has already launched. So a question that just asks them to state and explain that example is useless, they'll have it memorized from the slides.

Here's what I want. Write one essay question, plus a model answer of about 600 words, plus a marking scheme. Total 25 marks, students get 45 minutes on it, no calculators, no formula sheets — so the question has to be fully self-contained and can't turn on anyone doing Lorentz arithmetic.

The crux I want the question to turn on is the logic of the "reality relation" rather than the physics. So have the question hand the students a little schematic premise set inline — something like: co-reality is reflexive, symmetric, and transitive; whatever is simultaneous with an observer's here-now is co-real with that here-now; and there's no privileged inertial frame — and then make them do real work with it. Specifically I want them to have to:

- trace where in that premise set the argument actually gets its grip, and say which of the three properties of co-reality is doing the load-bearing work when you go from "co-real for the walker" to "real now for the person standing still" (they need the fact that the two observers share a location, and they need to see that the chaining step is what needs the property, not the tilt itself);
- evaluate the Stein-style move of hanging reality off individual point-events rather than off observers or persons, and say whether that's a coherent notion of reality or just a way of refusing to answer the question;
- and say what the argument does and does not establish — I want a top answer to be explicit that every event the argument touches is spacelike separated from us, and to explain why that blocks the slide from "the future is already real" to "the future is causally fixed" or fatalism.

For the stipulation, give them the number rather than making them derive it: an observer moving toward Andromeda at about 1 m/s, with Andromeda at roughly 2.5 million light-years, has a plane of simultaneity out at Andromeda that is displaced by on the order of three days relative to a standing observer's, in the direction of Andromeda's own later times. I want that in the question stem because there's always some student who loses the thread thinking the effect is too small to matter, and I'd rather they had no excuse.

For the model answer: no citations, no references, no reading list, no "for further see" — I'll bolt the bibliography on myself, and anyway I want it to stand alone. It should be the kind of answer I'd happily hand back as exemplary, and it should not just walk the premises in order.

For the marking scheme, I want it to actually separate a first from a B. So break the 25 marks into named moves with the marks for each, and then separately list at least four specific errors or dead-ends that should cost marks, with how much each costs and a line on why its a mistake and not just an omission. At least one of those should be the error of thinking reciprocity sinks the argument — i.e. that because the walker's slices are equally tilted relative to the stander's, the argument proves too much and therefore nothing. And at least one should be a physics error rather than a philosophy error, since half the class will come at it from that side.

One more thing: keep the question stem itself short, under about 200 words, even with the premise set in it. Our exams are printed on one side and I dont want this spilling.
