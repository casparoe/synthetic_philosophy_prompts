I'm a third-year philosophy student co-running a reading group on the ethics of borders, and this week we took up Dummett's writing on immigration — in particular the thought that a people is entitled to determine what its own way of life will become, which he takes to ground a state's right to limit immigration, sitting alongside his insistence that the obligation to admit those fleeing persecution is not up for negotiation. My reconstruction of the first half of that case may be uncharitable to him; if it is, say where, but please evaluate the argument as I have stated it rather than the one he might have given. I deliberately avoided any premise mentioning states, borders, citizenship, or coercion, because I think most objections to the communitarian case simply assert that states are special, and I wanted to see whether the case could be made without that. The trouble is that what I ended up with proves far too much:

(1) A people's way of life — its language, its institutions, the customs and settled expectations that accumulate out of a common history — is a good, and its members are entitled to care about its continuing.
(2) Who joins a group largely determines what its way of life becomes; so a group entitled to care about the continuation of its way of life is entitled to decide who joins it.
(3) That entitlement belongs to those who must live with the consequences of the change, and not to those who stand outside the group and will not.
(4) Therefore a political community may limit immigration to whatever level, and on whatever criteria, it judges necessary to preserve its way of life.
(5) Nothing in (1)–(3) mentions states, borders, citizenship, or coercion; they are stated about groups and ways of life generally.
(6) So they apply to any group with a way of life: a region, a town, a neighbourhood, a school, a congregation — and also to a majority within a state whose way of life is said to be changing because of the presence of a minority, in exactly the way it is said to change because of new arrivals.
(7) So a town may keep people out, a school may keep them out, and a majority may disenfranchise or expel those whose presence alters what its way of life is.
(8) And since (2) leaves the criteria to the group, while (1) makes the group's own estimate of what threatens its way of life the estimate that counts, nothing in (1)–(3) rules out race or religion among the criteria.
(9) (7) and (8) license segregated schools, towns that keep out anyone of the wrong colour, and at the limit ethnic cleansing.
(10) No principle that licenses those things can be true.
(11) So (1)–(3) are false, or at any rate (2) and (3) are; a state's right to control its membership needs some ground other than the value of a shared way of life, or no ground at all.

Two things I cannot get past. First, every proviso I try to insert fails: if I say the group's judgment must be reasonable, or that only ways of life worth preserving generate the entitlement, I have either begged the question against the town that sincerely judges otherwise or conceded that (1)–(3) were never doing the work. Second, the refugee exception seems to cut against the argument rather than soften it. Refugees change a way of life as much as anyone else, so if (1)–(3) really grounded (4) there would be no reason for the exception to be absolute; the fact that a communitarian wants it to be absolute suggests the operative consideration is need rather than character, in which case (4) was never supported by (1)–(3) to begin with.

I would rather keep (1) and (3) than lose them, because (3) looks to be doing honest work in cases I find persuasive — an Indigenous nation settling who its members are, a small island state facing the relocation of its whole population. But I cannot accept that (11) is the only alternative to (9). So: which step do you reject, and why did it look unobjectionable when I wrote it? If you think the argument is valid and that I simply have to choose a premise to surrender, say which choice costs least and price the alternatives — what I give up by rejecting (1), by rejecting (3), and by rejecting the move from (5) to (6), respectively. I would also like to know whether that move can be blocked by anything other than an assertion that states are different, and what becomes of the refugee objection on your preferred repair.
