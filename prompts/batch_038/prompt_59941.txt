I've been reading rationalist blogs since the old Overcoming Bias days and I like to think I've gotten decent at noticing when a slogan is being asked to do work it can't actually do, but last week I got completely steamrolled and I still can't reconstruct why. Our forecasting group chat was arguing about whether the whole superforecaster thing — Tetlock's tournaments, prediction markets, calibration curves, all of it — has any implications for whether the future is "open." One guy, smart, physics PhD, said roughly: obviously the future is open, that's precisely what makes prediction hard, and anyone who thinks a model could call geopolitical events ten years out at respectable calibration is confusing openness with mere ignorance — a genuinely open future has a hard ceiling on how well it can be predicted, full stop. Another person said pretty much the opposite: the tournament results prove the future *isn't* open, it's already written, openness is just a feeling we have from the inside. And I sat there realizing I had no idea wether these two were even disagreeing, because I couldn't say what "open" means coming out of either of their mouths.

So: can you lay out for me the different precise claims "the future is open" could be making? I want the actual menu, not a summary of the debate. I've got a partial list already — the physics-y one about indeterminism, whatever the Aristotle sea-battle people mean when they say there's no fact of the matter yet about tomorrow's naval engagement, the free-will-ish one about "I could have done otherwise," the block-universe one where the future exists just like the past does, and the deflationary one where it just means nobody knows yet — plus I strongly suspect there's a technical branching-time sense in which several futures are all genuine possibilities even though one of them is what actually happens, which sounds incoherent to me and I'd like that one explained properly. State each reading as a single precise sentence I could check, and then for each one say: is it trivially true, substantive and defensible, or just false? Is it compatible with determinism? And — the part I actually care about — does it entail any ceiling on how accurate prediction can be, and if so why? My hunch is that almost none of them do and the physics guy is smuggling something in, but I can't say what.

Then wrap up by telling me which reading each of the two guys was most likely invoking, whether there's any single reading on which they'd genuinely contradict each other rather than just talk past one another, and what the one-sentence comeback would have been that ends the arguement. Related: "the past is fixed and the future is open" is supposed to name some deep asymmetry in the world, and I want to know whether any of your readings actually delivers that asymmetry, or whether they all turn out to be either trivial or symmetric once you say them carefully. Assume I can follow technical material — I know roughly what bivalence is — but don't assume I've read the philosophy literature, I know the sea battle thing entirely from blog posts.
