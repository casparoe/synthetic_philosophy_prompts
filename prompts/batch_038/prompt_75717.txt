My town council is deciding whether to fund a flood-defence scheme whose costs fall now and whose benefits mostly arrive around the 2150s, and I have been trying to think the question through for myself rather than take anyone's word for it. What I have landed on is this: the social discount rate used in such calculations splits into a pure time-preference term plus a term reflecting expected growth in consumption, weighted by how quickly marginal utility falls — a decomposition that goes back to Ramsey. In our local debate both camps accept the same figures for expected growth, for that weighting, and for the chances of catastrophe; they differ only on whether the pure time-preference term should be zero or positive. I would like that dispute dramatised rather than explained.

Write a dialogue of about 900 words between two advisers to the council, one arguing that the term must be zero and one defending a positive rate. Both should be equally competent and neither should be set up to lose; each must answer the point just made rather than restate their own case. Since the empirical inputs are shared and held fixed, the defender of a positive rate must at some point produce a reason that cannot be recast as an adjustment to expected growth, to the utility weighting, or to the probabilities of catastrophe — and the other adviser should test explicitly whether it survives that recasting, conceding what must be conceded if it does. Please also have the zero-rate adviser give the sharpest available diagnosis of what a positive rate is doing morally, namely that it treats the calendar position of a life as itself a reason to count that life's welfare less, and let the defender answer that diagnosis head-on. For what it is worth, I have also come across the suggestion that discounting may be legitimate in proportion to how weakly some future person is psychologically connected to us, though that strikes me as an argument about one's own future rather than about strangers in 2150; either adviser may take it up, but neither may win by an appeal to authority. Close with each adviser stating, in one sentence, what the other would have to give up in order to be persuaded.
