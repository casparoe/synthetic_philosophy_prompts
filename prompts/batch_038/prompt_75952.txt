I am preparing a handout for a graduate reading group in the philosophy of economics, and I have grown dissatisfied with the two standard ways in which the Kelly criterion is introduced. The first is the popular gloss — stake a fraction of your bankroll equal to your edge and you will maximise the long-run growth of your wealth — which never explains why anyone should care about long-run growth as such, rather than about expected wealth or about the distribution of terminal outcomes. The second is the economist's rejoinder, that growth-rate maximisation is merely expected-utility maximisation wearing a logarithmic utility function, and therefore carries no independent normative weight at all. Both are too quick, and everything interesting lives in the seam between them. Rather than write another exposition, I would like you to compose a dialogue of roughly 1,500 to 1,900 words in which two equally competent advisers argue the question out over a single concrete mandate. Please write it in British English, in a formal but readable register, and allow the characters to do explicit arithmetic where that clarifies a point.

The mandate is as follows. A charitable trust holds £2,000,000 in liquid capital. Under its deed, the whole of the capital must be transferred to a named beneficiary in exactly twenty years; there is to be no consumption, no further contributions, and no borrowing. Once each year, a single counterparty offers the trust the same wager: it may stake any fraction of its current capital, and the stake either returns twice its amount as net profit, with probability 0.60, or is forfeited, with probability 0.40. The probability is not a metaphysical certainty but an estimate drawn from the counterparty's record over some 150 broadly comparable past wagers, so it carries a standard error of roughly four percentage points. One further clause matters greatly: if the capital ever falls to £500,000 or below, the trust is wound up at once, the residue passes to a different and far less sympathetic beneficiary, and the trustees are personally removed. Breaching that floor is thus a genuine absorbing state rather than an abstraction. Finally, the trustees have been put through a battery of certainty-equivalent elicitations and appear to behave, over a single year, like agents with a constant-relative-risk-aversion utility over wealth with a coefficient of about 0.4 — that is, they are noticeably less risk-averse than a logarithmic agent would be.

The two advisers should hold the following positions, each stated in its strongest form and neither permitted to caricature the other. The first maintains that, for a repeated, multiplicative and irreversible decision process, growth-rate optimality is a preference-independent normative constraint rather than a disguised taste: the ensemble average of the trust's wealth across counterfactual copies of itself diverges sharply from the time average along the single trajectory the trust will actually inhabit; ruin, where it is absorbing, is not a bad outcome to be traded off but the loss of the very arena in which outcomes matter; and expected-value reasoning asks a real agent to be moved by an average over lives she will never lead. The second maintains that growth-rate maximisation is nothing but logarithmic utility over terminal wealth with the arbitrariness hidden, that the much-trumpeted coincidence between patient expected-utility maximisation and growth-optimal staking is an asymptotic artefact of the length of the horizon that fails outright under bequests, consumption, finite patience or utility unbounded above, that a single agent's decision problem is properly assessed by her own expected utility because that expectation already integrates over her uncertainty as to which path she occupies, and that talk of "typical" trajectories smuggles in a probability weighting of its own. I would like them to press each other on at least the following: what the trustees' elicited risk aversion of 0.4 actually implies when applied myopically to the annual wager, and what it implies when applied properly across the full twenty years with the floor in force; the awkward fact that under purely proportional staking and in the absence of the covenant ruin is literally impossible, so that the customary appeal to avoiding ruin does no work in the standard telling, whereas here the covenant reintroduces it; a one-off wager with positive expected value that a logarithmic agent would decline, and whether that reveals a defect in logarithmic utility or a vindication of it; whether prudence about the estimated 0.60 is required by the growth-rate criterion itself or is merely ordinary uncertainty about one's parameters, and whether conceding that collapses the distinction between the two views; and, at every turn, which of their claims are mathematical and which are normative. Please end with both advisers stating the stake fraction they would in fact recommend to the trustees, together with an explicit diagnosis of which strands of their disagreement had any operational consequences and which turned out to be merely verbal — but do not let them settle into an easy reconciliation in which both were right all along. Give each of them a name and set the scene in the trustees' committee room shortly before the annual decision is taken. Do not cite or name any papers, books or articles, and do not append a bibliography or reading list; the argument should proceed from the structure of the case and from first principles.
