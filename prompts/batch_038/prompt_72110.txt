**Question.** 'A chaotic system is deterministic but not predictable. Chaos theory therefore does not threaten determinism; it threatens the inference from determinism to predictability, and with it the coherence of Laplace's demon.' Assess this claim. In your answer say whether chaotic unpredictability marks a limit on what can be known or a limit on what there is to know, and whether the distinction makes any difference to the free will debate.

**Candidate's answer.**

The claim in the question separates two things that are habitually run together: that a system is governed by deterministic laws, and that its later states can be inferred from a description of an earlier one. Chaos theory grants the first and denies the second, and in doing so it removes the inference on which Laplace's thought-experiment depends. So much I take to be uncontroversial. What I want to argue is that the consequences reach further than this standard line allows, and that a chaotic world is not deterministic in the sense the determinist actually requires.

The mechanism is familiar. In a chaotic system — the weather on Lorenz's model, a double pendulum, the logistic map at the parameter value where its behaviour is fully chaotic — trajectories that begin arbitrarily close together diverge exponentially, at a rate fixed by the system's Lyapunov exponent. The useful horizon of a forecast is therefore bounded by the accuracy of the initial measurement: knowing the state to *n* significant figures buys a horizon that grows only logarithmically in *n*, so that doubling the precision of one's instruments gains one further Lyapunov time and no more. This is why Poincaré's insight, reached half a century before the computer experiments that made it famous, is not a point about technology. It holds for any finite observer however well equipped, and it would hold even if the observer were not made of matter at all.

It is here that the usual framing of the debate seems to me too generous to the determinist. That framing says: the state at one time fixes the state at any later one, but we cannot know the state exactly, so prediction fails while determination survives — the first half a claim about the world, the second a claim about us. Consider, though, what the demon must do. It must hold a representation of the initial state to infinite precision, since any finite representation is eventually swamped by the instability. No physical system can store infinitely many bits, and there are general results to the effect that a bounded region of space containing finite energy can encode only finitely many; results in computable analysis likewise show that certain partial differential equations admit computable initial data whose later states are not computable. If the exact initial condition is not the sort of thing that could be physically represented or computed, it is unclear in what sense it could be a fact about the world. And if the exact initial condition is not a fact, the later state is not fixed by it, and the system is not deterministic in any sense that would matter to the debate. The position I am defending might be called determinism without determinacy: the laws keep their deterministic form while the states they govern cease to be determinate. On this account chaos supplies the anti-determinist with what quantum mechanics is normally invoked to provide, and does so without appealing to any contested view of measurement.

That last point is worth pressing. Once chaotic instability has made the future open, the question whether quantum mechanics is itself indeterministic loses most of its bite, since the two resources would be redundant. Whichever interpretation of quantum theory one adopts — collapse, many worlds, or hidden variables — the classical world in which we deliberate and act is dominated by instability, and it is instability rather than measurement that does the work of opening the future. This is an advantage of the present account over the quantum one, as it does not require us to take a side in a dispute that has gone unresolved for decades.

Finally, the asymmetry. Chaotic instability is not time-symmetric in its effects: small differences in the past are amplified into large differences in the future, whereas the converse does not hold, and it is this that makes the future open in a way the past is not. Chaos and the second law of thermodynamics point in the same direction here.

If this is right, the free will debate should be recast. The traditional worry is that determinism leaves no room for genuine alternatives; but a chaotic system is one in which an agent's own deliberation is not, even in principle, a predictable consequence of what preceded it. Unpredictability of that radical kind is what the incompatibilist was reaching for, and the compatibilist's insistence that freedom survives determination now looks beside the point. The issue was never determination, but predictability.
