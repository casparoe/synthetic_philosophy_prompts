I take notes for my hospital's patient safety committee, and after about a year of it I can usually predict what our chair Priya is going to tag as a "contributing factor" in the final report. Next month's case has me stuck though, and I want to figure out where she'll land before I have to draft the summary.

Her calls so far:

- Post-op patient with an order for neuro checks every hour. Nurse did them roughly every four. Epidural hematoma picked up six hours late, permanent deficit. Contributing factor: yes — and she wrote it up as "a four-hour interval between assessments," not as the nurse's failure to do them.
- Pharmacist missed a tenfold overdose on the verification screen, and the second nurse check missed it too. She listed both omissions, separately, in the same report.
- Resident never re-ordered a lactate that the lab system auto-canceled. Our timeline showed the result wouldn't have changed anything, he was already booked for the OR. Not a factor. Her line in the summary was that there's a difference between something going undone and something going undone that mattered.
- A patient's daughter, sitting at the bedside, didnt hit the call light when her dad started to stand up. He fell, fractured a wrist. Priya ruled it out, partly because the daughter isn't staff and partly because the nurse was two minutes down the hall and the fall looked unavoidable on the timeline.

Two things I've noticed. She has never once written a factor as a bare "so-and-so failed to X" — its always a gap, an interval, a bypassed check, something you can point at. And in every case where she said yes, the person had a documented job to do. I can't tell if that second thing is load-bearing for her or just an accident of which cases we happen to get.

Here's the new one. 79-year-old, med-surg, fall-risk order specifying hourly rounding. Two call-outs that night so the unit ran one nurse to eight patients, and there's a hospital policy that lets you stretch rounding intervals during short-staffing, except nobody documented that they were doing it. Our nurse rounded about every two hours. Separately: the patient's bed exit alarm had been silenced on the day shift because it kept going off and the family asked, and the night nurse never re-armed it, even though the shift-start checklist explicitly says to confirm alarms are armed. Around 2 a.m. the patient got up, fell, subdural, long ICU stay. On the timeline either fix probably catches it — the nurse was in the adjacent room and would have heard an armed alarm, and an hourly round would have put her in the room at about the right time.

So which of those two does she tag: the stretched rounding, the un-rearmed alarm, both, or neither? I'm less interested in the tag than in the rules. I can think of a couple of ways to read her record that sound different but never actually come apart on the cases we've had, and I have a feeling this one splits them.
