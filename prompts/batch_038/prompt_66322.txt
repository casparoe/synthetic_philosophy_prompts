You are a philosopher who works at the intersection of formal epistemology and the philosophy of perception, and who has sat through enough job talks to know which questions get asked in a room as distinct from which ones get asked in print. Please think carefully before drafting. What I need from you is a preparation sheet, not a defence of my view and not a survey of the problem of uncertain evidence.

The situation. I am a fifth-year PhD student going on the market, and in six weeks I give a job talk at a department whose two strengths are formal epistemology and the philosophy of perception. My talk is cross-listed with the statistics department, so I also expect three or four Bayesian statisticians who regard subjective probability as a modelling practice rather than a normative theory. Call it nine or ten formal epistemologists, several of whom work directly on updating rules and arrive with firm expectations about what a correct answer looks like; four or five philosophers of perception working on perceptual content, non-inferential justification and phenomenology, who mostly do not write down probability axioms and will not follow a derivation at the board; and the statisticians, one of whom I am told has little patience for philosophy and whose question could end the talk if I fumble it.

The thesis. When perceptual evidence is uncertain, we should not model the episode by probability kinematics over a first-order partition at all. Roughly:

1. Jeffrey's rule is licensed only under the rigidity condition: the experience must leave one's conditional probabilities on the partition of possible looks intact.
2. In the paradigm cases — the bird at dusk, the faded paint chip in bad light, the consonant heard through a wall — rigidity fails, because the very shift in my confidence across the partition carries information about my present discriminative position, and that position bears on what further looks should lead me to expect.
3. So the right model holds those first-order conditionals fixed and conditionalizes instead on a self-ascription: that I am, right now, more inclined to judge goldfinch than canary. Its evidential weight is set by my antecedent credences about how reliable my discrimination is in conditions like these.
4. The payoff I sell in the talk: this explains why the deliverances of perception can rationally be resisted — something the Jeffrey model cannot accommodate, since it takes the new partition probabilities as given inputs rather than as assessable claims — while preserving a kind of perceptual authority, because the self-ascription itself is not optional.
5. A corollary I intend to advertise rather than bury: on my view there is no updating on looks for which the agent has no prior self-model. I will own that cost.

Two objections I already expect, so please do not spend the sheet on them: the limit worry, that if my prior reliability is high the detour collapses into ordinary conditionalization on content and so changes nothing in the normal case; and the bootstrapping worry, that my priors about my own reliability were themselves formed from perception. Tell me which of the two is worse in this room, and then give me the ones I have not thought of.

Deliverable, in this order:

- A mapping paragraph of five or six sentences before the sheet: which sub-audience generates which kind of objection, and why the formal epistemologists and the statisticians will attack the same feature of my view for different reasons.
- The sheet: eight to ten objections ordered by the damage they would do in this particular room, not by how often they appear in the literature. For each: who raises it; the objection phrased the way that person would actually phrase it, in their own vocabulary, including the technical apparatus the formal people will use and the phenomenological language the perception group will use; a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds; and one line on where that reply is softest and which claim I must avoid making in order to keep it.
- Which two objections are best defused by conceding something in the first five minutes, with the sentences of concession written out.
- The question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that exposes a structural problem rather than a gap — with an honest two-sentence answer and a way to register it gracefully without surrendering the talk.
- My instinct is that someone from the perception group will press the speckled-hen case, and that someone else will say I have reinvented the proposal to conditionalize on the totality of one's experience. Tell me whether either instinct is right for this audience, and what the difference is between a self-ascription and one's total evidence.

Constraints. No bibliography, no reading list, no named attributions — my committee knows the literature and I only need the shape of the exchange. No hedging about how the answer depends on the details, and no suggestion that I consult a specialist. Be technically precise where precision is warranted: the formal people will punish any vagueness about what rigidity actually requires and about which quantities my rule is holding fixed. Assume the Bayesian framework itself throughout; do not spend my ninety seconds defending it.
