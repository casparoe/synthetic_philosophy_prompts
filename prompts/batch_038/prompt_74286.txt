The question was worth 40 points and read as follows:

*Rawls holds that citizens of a nearly just society have a duty to comply with its laws. Identify what grounds that duty on his account, and explain whether it matters if the ground is a natural duty rather than an obligation of fair play. Then take up Nozick's objection that benefits cannot bind a person who has not accepted them: does that objection also undermine Rawls? Here is the student's answer:

---

Rawls begins from the point that we are not obliged to obey the law simply because it is the law. Like Hart, he distinguishes being obliged, which is what happens in the gunman situation when a threat moves you, from having an obligation, which arises only within a practice whose rules people accept from an internal point of view. The real question is what generates the obligation in a society that is nearly just.

For Rawls it is the principle of fairness. Society is a cooperative venture for mutual advantage, and each of us has accepted its benefits — the security of a legal order, the courts, the roads, the public goods we rely on every day. To keep taking those benefits while refusing the corresponding burdens is to free-ride on the fellow citizens who restrain themselves so that we can be safe. So the duty to comply is owed to other citizens rather than to the state as such, and it is owed by anyone who has voluntarily taken up what the scheme provides. Rawls limits the range: the duty binds only in a nearly just society, and civil disobedience becomes available when a majority persistently violates the equal basic liberties.

Nozick's objection is that this gets the moral geography backwards. He asks us to imagine a neighborhood that installs a public address system for entertainment and assigns each resident a day to run it. When my day comes around, am I obliged to serve just because I enjoyed the broadcasts on everyone else's day? No: a benefit cannot obligate unless I have accepted it, and merely receiving something is not accepting it. But the force of the example is general. It does not merely embarrass the fair-play account, it defeats it, and Rawls with it, because any argument that moves from my having received a benefit to my owing a burden is exposed to the same case. What survives is at most a weak, content-dependent reason to support particular just arrangements, which is nowhere near a general obligation to obey.

Raz supplies what fairness lacks. On the service conception, an authority is legitimate when its directives help subjects comply better with the reasons that already apply to them than they would judging for themselves, and legitimacy entails an obligation to obey, since the subject now has a reason to do as the authority says in place of weighing the reasons on his own. Because legitimacy is assessed authority by authority, this also answers the particularity problem: a general duty to support just institutions cannot explain why I must obey this state rather than an equally just one abroad, but the service conception can, since it is indexed to the actual relation between me and my own government.

Finally, fidelity to law. Rawls defines civil disobedience as a public, nonviolent, conscientious yet political act contrary to law, addressed to the sense of justice of the majority and undertaken to bring about a change in law or policy, normally after legal channels have failed. Willingness to accept the penalty shows that the disobedient has not rejected the legal order as a whole; the act stays within the bounds of fidelity to law. That is why Rawls confines it to serious violations of the equal liberties, and why it must be directed at the law being broken: the act is a piece of speech to one's fellow citizens, and it can carry that content only if the law violated is the law protested.
