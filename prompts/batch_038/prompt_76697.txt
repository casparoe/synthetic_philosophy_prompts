I teach an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of mind at a small liberal arts college, and the week we reach the timing of experience goes wrong in the same way every year. We read the classic argument that there is no single place and moment at which consciousness "happens," and half the class accepts it as a genuine paradox about the brain while the other half is quietly convinced the professor is running a word game on them. What I would like to hand out alongside the papers this year is a piece of fiction, because I have come to think the difficulty is imaginative rather than intellectual: no one in the room can feel the force of the claim that two incompatible stories about what went on inside a person's own head might be permanently indistinguishable from inside that head. I have searched for a story that produces that feeling and cannot find one, so I would like to commission it from you.

The story should be built around a real perceptual illusion. A subject sits in a darkened room facing a screen. A dot of one color — say red — is flashed at one position, and a fraction of a second later a dot of a different color — say green — is flashed some distance away. At certain spacings and delays, subjects do not report two flashes at all: they report a single dot travelling from the first position to the second, and they report that it changed color partway along that path. The trouble is that nothing could have told them so. At the moment when the travelling dot is, in their telling, midway and turning green, the green flash has not yet happened; the information is not in the world yet, and so cannot have reached anyone's eyes. Two rival explanations are available, a distinction philosophers sometimes label the Stalinesque and the Orwellian. On the first, the brain waits, collects the later information, and only then assembles the whole little narrative — motion, mid-path change of color — which is delivered to consciousness already edited, the way a doctored photograph reaches the press as an original. On the second, the subject really does consciously see a red dot travelling, and when the green flash finally arrives the memory of that experience is rewritten after the fact, so that what the subject now sincerely recalls having seen is a change that occurred too early; the record is amended downstream of the witnessing, the way an archive is quietly corrected overnight. On the way the argument is usually put, both accounts predict exactly the same thing the subject will say when asked: there is no verbal report, no confidence rating, no forced-choice guess that could separate them, because any such test is itself just another report.

Here is what I want. A first-person story of roughly two thousand words, set in a university vision laboratory in the mid-1990s, in a basement suite whose air handling hums through the wall; the display is a CRT monitor with an audible refresh and a slight geometric distortion at the corners, and the narrator is a graduate student from another department entirely who answers the paid-subject notices because the alternator in her car has gone, and who therefore keeps coming back for session after session until she becomes the lab's reliable one, the subject everyone builds a protocol around. Keep the period honest and do not let modern instrumentation into the room — no eye tracker, no scanner, nothing capable of settling the question by looking inside the skull, since the point is precisely that the question is not settled that way. Do not name either of the two explanations above and do not permit yourself any philosophical jargon whatsoever; the reader should be able to reconstruct the puzzle from what happens in the lab and from the way the narrator talks about it, with no exposition addressed to them. At some point the narrator must take the problem seriously enough to try to catch the revision in the act — to devise her own clever test from inside the chair, out of nothing but what a subject has access to — and the attempt must fail, with the reason for its failure emerging from the events rather than from anyone explaining it. Give her at least one colleague in the lab who maintains that the distinction between the two accounts is empty, mere bookkeeping about a process that has no witness, and at least one person outside the lab — a partner, a sibling, an older neighbor, anyone plausible — who maintains that it is the only distinction worth making; let neither of them win. Finally, fold in one episode wholly outside the laboratory in which the same structure turns up in the narrator's ordinary autobiographical memory, something small and personal whose date and details she cannot quite vouch for, and treat it with exactly the seriousness you give the trials rather than as a spooky coincidence. The story should end in a way that leaves which account is true genuinely undecidable for the reader while making unmistakably concrete what the narrator stands to lose depending on the answer. No epigraph, no author's note, no explanation appended at the end: if it needs one, it has not done its job.
