ok so i'm doing a philosophy of social science module this term and we've just had the block on natural kinds and social construction, and I keep thinking about my sister's job because of it. She's in the people team at a big high-street retailer — about 30,000 staff — and they've rolled out this mandatory "generational intelligence" programme. Every manager does a half-day session. The deck sorts the whole workforce into five named cohorts with fixed birth-year bands, and each cohort gets a profile: what motivates them, how they like feedback, their attitude to authority, whether they'll stay. There's a Gen Z onboarding pack they now sell on to other companies in the sector, plus a reverse-mentoring scheme pairing the youngest staff with the over-55s.

Here's the bits I can't stop thinking about:

1. The bands aren't equal. The boomer cohort gets split into early and late, with seperate profiles, but everything after that is chopped into fixed 15-year slabs, and the cut points aren't the same as the ones you see quoted in the papers.

2. The oldest band in the deck — the one for people born in the 1930s — is still there with a full personality profile attached, even though the company employs essentially nobody in it. She says nobody has ever asked why its still in there.

3. Someone born in the last year of a band gets that band's whole profile, and someone born twelve months later gets a completely different one. Nothing in the training admits the boundary is a choice.

4. There's a slide near the end saying no one should be reduced to their generation and that the profiles are only a starting point. But the thing they're actually selling is the profiles.

5. She's seen the company's own engagement and attrition data, and the analysts cut it by tenure, role and region — never by birth year. Apparently when someone did try birth year it barely moved anything once you controlled for the others.

So: what does this practice presuppose about what a generation is? I don't mean what it says out loud, the deck is careful, I mean what has to be true for it to make sense as it's actually run. Does it need generations to be real kinds with some stable underlying nature, or would something much weaker license it — a loose statistical cluster, a shorthand everyone agrees to use, an admitted fiction that still pays off? Which rival pictures does it quietly rule out, including the possibility that naming a cohort is part of what brings it into being, so the categories aren't found but partly made by things like this training?

And then the two that puzzle me most. Is the empty oldest band evidence the whole thing isn't tracking anything real, or can a category with no members in it still be doing legitimate work? And is that disclaimer slide compatible with the rest of the practice, or does the thing contradict itself in a way that matters?

Finally, tell me how much of the half-day session would have to change if you swapped the presupposition out — say for generations as real but fuzzy and historically contingent clusters, versus generations as pure bookkeeping for demographic events like the post-war birth spike. And be honest about where the practice is underdetermined, ie where several incompatible theories would license exactly the same deck and the same exercises, because I suspect alot of it is and I don't want the easy answer.
