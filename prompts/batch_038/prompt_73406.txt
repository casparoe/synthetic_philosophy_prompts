ok, context first: I'm an EM at a ~60 person backend company. We run on-call rotations and we do a "blameless postmortem" after every Sev-1 and Sev-2, so roughly two or three a month land on me or one of the other three EMs. Our postmortem template literally says *no names, no judgements, only system factors*, and for about a year that felt enlightened. Now I think it's quietely broken in two directions and I want to replace it with something I can hand to another EM.

Direction one: because we can't say anything about a person's choices, everything gets written up as if the system did it, and the fix is always "add a guardrail" or "add a lint rule" even when what actually happened is that somebody decided to skip review because they were annoyed. Same person, two outages in six months, and the process has nothing to say except "action item: sync offline."

Direction two: when something does go wrong, the person gets frozen out anyway. They stop getting invited to design reviews, they get moved off the interesting services, nobody says it out loud. That feels worse for them then an actual conversation would have, and I can't point at where in our process it happened because it didn't happen in the process.

So: I want a decision procedure my EM group can actually run on an incident, grounded in a real idea instead of vibes. The one I keep coming back to is Peter Strawson — reactive attitudes versus what he calls the objective attitude, i.e. the difference between being angry at someone as a person and treating them as a thing to be managed, fixed, or worked around. I have zero formal philosophy background, I read a summary and skimmed an article, so please don't assume I know the details. What I think I got out of it: there's a real difference between "they didn't know / were pressured / it was an accident" and "they aren't an agent we hold responsible here at all," and those two should NOT end up in the same place procedurally — but I also think treating someone purely as an object to be optimized is a cost we're paying, not a neutral default, which is exactly what our no-names rule does.

What I want back:

- the questions to ask in order during an incident review, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where the procedure stops at "no further action" versus where it hands the decision to a human because it's a values call, not a facts call
- an explicit fork for the two things above — the excusing-style cases vs the not-an-agent-here cases — and what each one licenses you to do and what it forbids
- the step that guards against the freeze-out failure: something that says when you may NOT take the managerial/objective stance toward someone, even though it'd be easier
- what to literally say out loud in the review meeting and to the person afterwards, in plain language, plus a version of the finding that can go in the doc without a name in it
- where HR has to be looped in and where looping them in would be an overreaction
- every place you're making a judgement call dressed up as a step, labeled as such
- and mark anywhere you've invented a distinction that isn't actually Strawson's, because I genuinely can't tell the difference

Then run it on three cases: (a) a senior engineer who force-pushed over a colleague's work mid-incident and maintains they were right to do it; (b) a new grad three weeks in who followed the runbook exactly and the runbook was two years stale, so the steps made the outage worse; (c) the two-outages person from above who everyone has quietly stopped pinging. For (c) I expect the procedure's answer to be something I don't want to hear — give it to me straight rather than sanding it down.

Constraints: 45 min per incident, run by an EM who has never read any of this, no philosophy names or citations in the artifact itself (the reasoning should be baked in, not footnoted), and the output has to survive being said to someone's face without sounding like a therapy bot.
