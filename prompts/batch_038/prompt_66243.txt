Quick background, since it shapes what I'm asking for: I'm a retired analytical chemist. Thirty-five years of quoting measurement uncertainties, coverage factors, the whole GUM apparatus, and since I stopped going into the lab I've gotten embarrassingly deep into the philosophy of statistics. I now run an informal reading group at our town library — about ten people: two retired engineers, a nurse, an actuary, a couple of community-college students, and a man who just likes the biscuits. Ninety minutes, every other Thursday. They like doing things with their hands, and I can raid my old lab's supply closet for beakers, plus I've got poker chips and paper bags at home.

I want a runnable game, not a talk. The thing I want them to run into has two beats.

Beat one is the usual Ellsberg-style pull — they'll pay more to bet on a bag whose composition they know than on one they don't, even when the arithmetic says they shouldn't. But I don't want to just re-run Ellsberg; half of them have seen it on the internet and it'll feel like a trick.

Beat two is the one I actually care about, and I want it to land as a gut-punch. They post a range for some bet. Then a piece of evidence arrives — evidence they were told up front was definitely going to arrive, so nothing ambushes them. They update. And their range comes out WIDER than it went in. Not wider because they're being sloppy; genuinely wider, and I want them to discover that by doing the bookkeeping themselves before anyone says the word "dilation" out loud.

Here's what I need:

1. The physical design. How many containers, exact chip counts, who draws what and when, how many rounds, and a minute-by-minute timeline for 90 minutes. Cheap materials only, nothing I have to order.

2. A way to get honest ranges out of them. My recollection is that weather forecasters score interval forecasts so that you get dinged both for being narrow-and-wrong and for being wide-and-right — please pin down a concrete payoff scheme along those lines that I can score with pen and paper in under a minute per person per round, no real money, and show me why posting the full range every single time isn't a winning strategy under it.

3. A wrinkle of my own I'd like folded in: a small bonus for ranges that still cover the truth after the update has happened, i.e. rewarding a range for surviving the evidence rather than just being right on the day. I cooked this up myself and I suspect it's gesturing at whatever those second-order "safety" or "reliability" conditions are that get bolted onto imprecise probabilities. Tell me honestly whether it actually does that, or whether it's really just a sneakier width penalty.

4. The arithmetic, written out and checked. The set of distributions corresponding to the bags, the lower and upper probability of the target bet before the evidence and after it, for each thing they could possibly observe. I want to see the numbers and be convinced the widening really occurs with the chip counts you picked, not just in principle. If it needs more chips than fits in a beaker, say what the minimum container size is.

5. A debrief that maps specific moments in the room onto the concepts — when I'm allowed to introduce credal sets and the name of the phenomenon, and separately, where my old Type A versus Type B uncertainty vocabulary lines up with this framework and where it will actively mislead them.

6. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including the actuary, who will absolutely try to collapse everything to a single number and argue the intervals are just a guarentee-free way of admitting you don't know anything. How do I handle that in the moment without turning it into a lecture?

7. Straight talk on which feature of the idea the game cannot reproduce, so I know what I have to supply by mouth. My own worry is that the whole thing ends up measuring their attitude to risk and nothing at all about belief — tell me if that's right and whether the design can be adjusted to blunt it.
