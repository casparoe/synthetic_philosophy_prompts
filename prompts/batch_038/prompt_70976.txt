Our book group is doing Fear and Trembling next month and one of the blokes in it listened to a philosophy podcast about it and sent round his summary of the argument, which goes:

'Kierkegaard's point is that God's commands override morality whenever God issues one. The ethical, for him, is basically the set of rules a community agrees to live by, and since God is the source of those rules he's free to lift them for whoever he likes. That's what the teleological suspension of the ethical amounts to: Abraham is about to murder his own son, which no moral code permits, but because God has ordered it the act stops being murder and becomes an act of faith. Structurally it's a tragic hero story — Agamemnon giving up Iphigenia so the fleet can sail, that kind of thing — except that the greater good Abraham serves is God rather than his people. What's being tested is whether Abraham loves God more than Isaac, and faith here just means beleiving something flatly absurd against all the evidence, with no grounds for it at all. Which is why Kant was right to say you should treat any voice telling you to do something like that as an illusion: if faith means believing without evidence, then Abraham and any fanatic who claims God spoke to him are in exactly the same position, and there's nothing in the book that lets you tell them apart.'

Half of me agrees with him and half of me thinks he's flattened it into a divine-command-theory pamphlet with a Bible story stapled on, but he's read far more Kierkegaard than I have and I can't say where it goes wrong. Could you take his paragraph sentence by sentence and tell me what a Kierkegaard scholar would accept, what's a harmless simplification, and what actually gets the argument backwards? Two things especially I can't work out. First, whether de silentio (I gather its a pseudonym, and that apparently matters somehow?) is offering this as a general rule anyone could apply to themselves, or the opposite of that. Second, whether faith in the book really is just believing against the evidence, because I've got a foggy memory of something about Abraham expecting to get Isaac back, a "double movement", and two different kinds of knight, and none of that seems to fit what he's written. And on his last sentence: it sounds like a serious objection and maybe it is, but does it follow from the accurate parts of his summary, or only from the bits he's got wrong?
