I am writing to you in a rather exposed position, and I should explain why before stating my request.

Two years ago I signed a witness statement in an internal disciplinary process against a colleague. I believed every sentence of it at the time; I had good reasons, as I understood them; and I said so to people who were entitled to rely on me. He lost his post. Some months later, documents surfaced that made the central claim in my statement simply false, though nothing about my evidence at the time had changed. I have read enough epistemology to know that the question of whether I did something impermissible is not the same question as whether what I said was true, and I have not been able to settle which of the two I am supposed to be ashamed of. In trying to think it through, I have gone back to Ian McEwan's Atonement, which I first read at nineteen and which is, I now see, a novel almost entirely about this problem.

Please act as a philosopher who works at the intersection of epistemology and literary criticism: someone fluent in the contemporary debate over the norms of assertion and equally at home doing close reading of a twentieth-century British novel. Think carefully rather than quickly. Before you write the reading itself, begin with a short piece of brainstorming in which you list the moments in the book that are genuinely assertoric and those that merely look assertoric, and note, for each, what verdict the leading candidate norms would deliver. Then develop that into a full critical reading, roughly fifteen hundred to two thousand words, addressing the following.

First: Briony's identification of Robbie to the police on the dark drive. Assess it under the knowledge norm, under a truth norm, and under the weaker reasonable-belief or justified-belief norms, and say clearly where the three verdicts come apart and which one the novel's own sympathies appear to track. Second: consider whether her real failure is an assertoric one at all. The fountain scene watched from the window, the intercepted letter, the encounter in the library — none of these is an assertion, yet each fixes the content of what she later says. Does the novel suggest that the norm governing assertion depends on norms governing interpretation and imagination that the assertion literature ignores? Third: the audience. Lola's compliance, the family's readiness, the police's uptake, and the fact that a thirteen-year-old girl of that household was believed on almost nothing while Robbie's denials were believed by almost no one — does the permissibility or the harmfulness of her assertion depend on the entitlements and prejudices of her hearers? Fourth: the dying young French soldier in the London hospital, whom Briony lets go on believing that she is the girl whose photograph he carries. Her statements to him are false and, on the standard account, impermissible; nearly every reader takes them to be the most decent thing she does. What does that scene cost the knowledge norm, and can any contextualist or audience-sensitive refinement save it without making the norm do no work at all? Fifth, and most difficult: the 1999 section. Briony reveals that the reconciliation she gave us in Part Three never happened, that Robbie died at Dunkirk and Cecilia months later in the bombing of the tube station where she was sheltering, and that the book we have read is hers. Does a novelist assert anything, and if the invented reunion is not an assertion, in what sense is it a retraction or a repair of the assertion she made at thirteen? And what does the disclosure do retrospectively to the reader's own entitlement to have believed Parts One and Two?

Be faithful in both directions. Do not reduce the novel to an illustration of a debate, and do not bend the debate to flatter the novel: if McEwan is really making a claim about imagination and the opacity of other minds that has nothing to do with the norms of assertion, say so, and say where the reading must stop. Note also the structural complication that Parts One and Two are rendered in free indirect discourse, so that it is not obvious whose assertion they are. End by telling me what the novel contributes to this debate that argument alone does not, and then, briefly and without consolation, what it implies about my own statement: whether my failure was epistemic or moral, and whether either diagnosis leaves anything for me to do now.

Two constraints. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; naming philosophers and positions is welcome, but do not invent titles, dates, or page numbers. And do not quote the novel — I cannot check you, and I would rather you describe a scene accurately than cite it from memory. If you are unsure of any plot detail, flag the uncertainty rather than smoothing over it.
