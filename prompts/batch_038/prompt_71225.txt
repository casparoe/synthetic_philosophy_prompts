The Anglo-French Concorde programme is the episode that gives the Concorde fallacy its name, and so it is worth asking whether it actually exhibits the structure it is usually cited for. Treat it as a case of Shubik's dollar auction and test the fit in both directions. The facts I have in mind: a 1962 treaty committed the British and French governments jointly; development was estimated at roughly £70 million at signature and had run to £1.5–2 billion by entry into service in 1976; a forecast market of some 350 aircraft and up to 100 airline options collapsed to twenty airframes, flown only by British Airways and Air France; Boeing cancelled its rival supersonic transport in 1971; the fleet was retired in 2003.

Begin with a literal mapping: who are the two bidders, what is the prize, what are the bids, and who is the auctioneer? Identify the moment, if there is one, that corresponds to a bidder crossing the value of the dollar — after which further funding could not be justified by any plausible forecast of aircraft sales — and say what each government's continued participation owed to the other's.

Then say where the analogy breaks. Dollar-auction bids are irrecoverably forfeited and the prize's value is fixed and known to both players; Concorde's spending went largely to domestic firms and workers, and its value included items no ledger captured — industrial capability, prestige, the bilateral relationship, technology that fed later programmes. Does the case belong to the sunk-cost fallacy, to the dollar auction, to both at different stages, or to neither? Finally, consider what the example's canonical status does to the theory: can an episode recruited to name a fallacy also function as evidence against it, or has it been insulated from refutation? Say what could have settled in advance whether continued funding was rational ex ante rather than merely regrettable in hindsight, and note where the theory is simply silent.
