I am a novelist, and for three years I have chaired the conduct and appeals panel of a British writers' federation of some four thousand members. Please write in British English, and write in the voice of a moral philosopher who specialises in blame and responsibility and has sat on professional disciplinary bodies: I need a document my four fellow panellists could act upon, reasoned from the case rather than from authorities. Think the matter through carefully, and open with a brief brainstorming section — three or four rulings the panel could plausibly issue, each with what it costs — before committing to one.

The case. A poet-critic spent eighteen months campaigning publicly against a bestselling novelist, alleging that a memoir presented as the novelist's own testimony had been substantially written by an uncredited collaborator. The campaign worked: the novelist lost a shortlist place and a festival invitation. Last month the panel received documentation from a former assistant establishing that the poet-critic's own prize-winning sequence, eleven years ago, was likewise substantially drafted by an uncredited collaborator. She does not contest the fact; she says it never occurred to her at the time, while the collaborator says she was reminded of it and laughed it off. The novelist has now resigned from the federation and written asking that nothing whatever be published about her. Our code requires that complaints be brought 'in good faith and for the purpose of upholding professional standards, not for the settling of private grievances'. One further fact, which two colleagues would prefer not to confront: the federation has quietly dropped three comparable complaints in ten years, one of them against a sitting member of this panel.

Draft the panel's written opinion. It must recommend a concrete outcome — what we find, what we publish, and by when — and it must separate two questions I suspect we have been blurring: whether the poet-critic's own conduct destroys her standing to have made the accusation, and whether an institution with our record has standing to condemn anyone. Take seriously the claim that standing is a genuine moral phenomenon rather than the tu quoque fallacy in disguise, and take equally seriously the objection that hypocrisy never makes a wrong right, so that a panel setting aside a well-founded complaint on standing grounds has abdicated its job. Then tell me which of these considerations must lose, and why.
