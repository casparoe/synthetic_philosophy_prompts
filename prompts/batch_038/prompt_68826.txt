I lead a weekly reading group in a philosophy department — mostly people whose physics stopped in high school, plus two who have read popular accounts and will arrive already convinced that the wavefunction is a physical wave. I have fifty minutes, a whiteboard, and no mathematics beyond drawing a two-state system, to teach the distinction between psi-ontic and psi-epistemic views of the quantum state. Plan the session rather than explaining the material to me. I want the order the pieces have to go in and what each depends on; the single running example that should carry the whole thing (I am inclined toward a classical analogue of incomplete knowledge, something like a shuffled deck, before touching a qubit — tell me what such an example must have, what it must lack, and at which point it begins to mislead); the misconceptions the group will form, above all the slide from "epistemic" to "in the mind, therefore subjective," and the exact place in the sequence where each should be headed off; whether the indistinguishability of non-orthogonal states and the no-cloning result belong before or after I state the distinction, since I suspect they are what make the epistemic view attractive rather than merely possible; and where, if anywhere, the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem belongs, or whether naming it is better than explaining it at this level. Impose one constraint on the design: no double-slit experiment and no Schrödinger's cat, since both make the state look like a thing spread out in space and quietly settle the question I am trying to leave open. Close with one question I can pose at the end that would show the group has grasped the distinction rather than the slogan, and a short list of what I should openly decline to explain at this level. Please write in American English.
