I am in the second year of a doctoral programme that sits awkwardly between a history department and a philosophy of science unit, and next week I have to table a draft chapter at our work-in-progress seminar. What follows is the argumentative skeleton of that chapter — roughly a thousand words, which will eventually have to become about six thousand — and it is currently at the stage where I can no longer tell whether its central move is a genuine distinction or a verbal contrivance that I have dressed up. My supervisor has read it twice and given me two incompatible pieces of advice: that the paper is too modest and needs to say what it is against, and that it is too ambitious and should drop its political section entirely. I would rather have your view before I decide which of them to obey.

The draft, in full:

§1. In June 1816 a party of English travellers, shut indoors by days of rain and cold near Lake Geneva, agreed to amuse themselves by each writing a ghost story. One of them began what became *Frankenstein*. The weather had a cause: Mount Tambora, on the island of Sumbawa, had erupted the previous April with a violence that cost tens of thousands of lives and put enough sulphur into the stratosphere to depress temperatures across the northern hemisphere. It has become conventional to say that the volcano helped to write the novel. Sentences of that shape now populate some of the best history being written. A bacillus reaches the ports of the Mediterranean in 1347 and carries off perhaps a third of Europe's population, thinning the labour force, raising wages, loosening serfdom. A fissure opens in Iceland in 1783 and the harvests fail from Reykjavík to the Continent. A volcano in Peru erupts in February 1600 and there is famine in Muscovy within two years. What, exactly, does such a sentence claim?

§2. I think two claims are being run together. The first is a claim about causal leverage: some non-human process supplied a large share of the difference that made the outcome what it was. This is an empirical claim, in principle assessable, and often true. The second is a claim about narrative agency: that the item can occupy the subject position of our explanations, that it is a protagonist whose doings and sufferings give the account its shape. The second does not follow from the first. Much of the rhetoric of environmental history slides from one to the other without notice — in the founding texts of the genre, McNeill's *Plagues and Peoples* (1976) and Crosby's *Ecological Imperialism* (1986), the slide is usually benign and often deliberate — and I shall call that slide 'actant inflation'.

§3. A test. Call a purported actor substitutable if it can be replaced by any member of a family of functionally similar causes without altering the form of the explanation in which it figures. 'Drought' in most accounts of societal collapse is substitutable in this sense: salinisation, soil exhaustion, the interruption of a trade route, a succession crisis and an epidemic will each slot into the same narrative grammar, and it is the grammar doing the explanatory work rather than the item. Where an account cannot be re-run with a substitute, the item has earned its place as a subject. Note that this line does not coincide with the line between the human and the non-human: a named institution or a particular statute can be non-substitutable, and a generic natural process substitutable.

§4. The test has an awkward consequence, which I shall not hide. *Yersinia pestis* is not obviously substitutable. It is a particular organism with a particular transmission ecology, and good plague narratives track its differential behaviour: seasonality, the rat–flea–human relay, the contrast between urban and rural mortality, the way a second wave differs from a first. On the test, plague looks more agential than climate, which is the reverse of the intuition with which I began. My patch is a second condition: an actor must have what I shall call a response repertoire, meaning that the account tracks how the cause behaves differently in different contexts rather than merely that it is present. Climate, in most uses, has no repertoire; cold is cold. A pathogen, an institution and a person do. I suspect this is an ad hoc rescue and I expect to be told so.

§5. There is a further problem about measuring leverage at all. To say that a cause supplied a large share of the difference requires a comparison class. Climatologists have one: they run ensembles and compare a world with the forcing to a world without it. Historians have a single run and no way of obtaining a second. Leverage claims in history are therefore not measurements but judgements about the robustness of a counterfactual — about how many of the nearest possible worlds in which Tambora does not erupt still contain the novel. That is a legitimate thing to estimate, but it is not a quantity, and the rhetorical force of the sentence about the volcano and the novel depends on readers mistaking it for one.

§6. I want the political claim to be narrow. Determinism has a bad name for good reasons: early twentieth-century climatology was enlisted in the ranking of peoples, as in Huntington's 1915 attempt to correlate climate with the level of civilisation, and Febvre's assault on that programme in 1922 remains the right starting point for scepticism. But the harm I want to isolate is not that environmental accounts make non-human things too powerful. It is that they make alternatives unthinkable. An explanation that forecloses counterfactuals licenses quietism, whatever its subject matter; and the reason to prefer possibilism to determinism is not that it flatters human beings but that it keeps the space of alternatives open for assessment. On this view *Guns, Germs, and Steel* (1997) is objectionable not because it gives too much weight to geography but because the weight it gives is presented as unalterable.

§7. Objection. Actor-network theory, following Latour, refuses precisely the asymmetry I am trying to police: actants need not be human, and there is no reason to demand of a microbe that it have intentions before admitting it to the explanatory cast. My distinction, the objection runs, re-imports intentionality through the back door under the respectable name of grammar. My reply is that the distinction is ontologically neutral. I am not asking what plague wants; I am asking which slot an item occupies in an account, and whether that account tracks its behaviour across contexts. That is a question about the form of our explanations, not about the furniture of the world, and an actor-network theorist ought to be able to accept it without embarrassment.

§8. A caveat about my evidence, since several of my examples are weaker than they look. The 1600 eruption of Huaynaputina is the largest in South American history, and its connection to the Muscovite famine of 1601–1603 is a hypothesis rather than a finding. Icelandic mortality after the Laki fissure eruption of 1783–84 is estimated at anything between a fifth and a quarter of the population, and the eruption's responsibility for the severe European winter that followed, or for famines in Japan, is disputed. The Little Ice Age is a periodisation whose boundaries move with the region studied, and whose very name entered the scientific literature only with François Matthes in 1939. The pathogen behind the Antonine plague has never been securely identified, and palaeogenomic work has recently made the antiquity of smallpox itself doubtful. I have used these cases as illustrations; a reader may reasonably ask whether an argument whose illustrations need this many hedges can carry the weight I put on it.

§9. Provisional conclusion. Write non-human causes in the material register — as conditions, forcings, background — unless you can show both non-substitutability and a response repertoire. Where you can, the item has earned the subject position, and there is no reason to withhold it out of squeamishness about minds.
