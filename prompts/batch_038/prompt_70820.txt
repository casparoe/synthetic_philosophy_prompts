I have served four years on the Clinical Standards Committee of a nonprofit behavioral health system in the American Midwest — roughly 45 clinicians, about 11,000 client contacts a year, mostly Medicaid and contracted student health. I should disclose my formation: I spent fifteen years reading rationalist blogs and forums, so I think in base rates, Goodhart's law, and pre-registered decision rules, and I came to Ian Hacking's looping-effects material late, through philosophy of science rather than through clinical training. Our committee is not especially philosophical, and I need a document that will survive being read aloud to it.

The case. A vendor has offered us a 16-item self-report instrument it calls the Sustained Depletion Inventory (SDI-16), intended to flag adults meeting a proposed category it calls depletion syndrome: persistent exhaustion, detachment from previously meaningful activity, and a felt decline in one's own capability, with no requirement that any of it be tied to work. The proposal before us is to administer the SDI-16 at every adult intake beginning next fiscal quarter, to create a matching intake category, and to route positive screens into a new eight-session group protocol.

The facts the committee has been given, all of which I accept as accurate for purposes of the memo: in our four-month pilot (n = 612 adults), 23% screened positive. The rate was not uniform. Of clients who said they first heard of our system through an online community organized around the depletion label, 58% screened positive; of clients referred by primary care, 11% did. The vendor's validation sample was recruited largely from those same online communities, so the criterion against which the cut score was set is not independent of the population the instrument is now being used to find. Three clinicians report that clients who arrive already carrying the label describe their experience in the instrument's own vocabulary, answer the items like a checklist, and respond poorly to the behavioral activation work that otherwise suits exhaustion. One clinician reports the opposite: for a client with years of unhelpful contacts elsewhere, being handed the label was the first thing that made her feel described rather than diagnosed, and her engagement improved. Separately, a state care-coordination grant pays a per-case supplement for each newly identified client in a recognized category, so adoption is revenue-positive; one member has already said in writing that this is a textbook Goodhart trap, and another replied that a financial incentive to find cases does not make the cases fake.

For context the committee will recognize: the international disease classification keeps burnout out of the disorder list altogether, treating it as an occupational phenomenon, and its entry explicitly limits the term to the occupational context and warns against applying it to experiences in other areas of life — whereas depletion syndrome carries no occupational restriction whatever. We also all remember the widely reported wave of functional tic-like presentations among adolescents during the pandemic years, which most observers traced to the circulation of symptom videos, and we have all read the argument that dropping the bereavement exclusion from the depression criteria would convert ordinary grief into caseload. Half of us think those precedents settle the matter. The other half think they are being used as an all-purpose veto.

What I want is the memo I will circulate before the meeting: a recommendation — adopt as proposed, adopt with specified conditions, reject, or defer — with the reasoning attached and a bottom line the committee can act on. Three requirements.

First, make the looping problem operational rather than atmospheric. I want you to work with a distinction I have been pushing and to tell me whether it earns its keep or is a distinction without a difference: prevalence loops, where a label changes how many people fall under it; phenomenology loops, where a label changes what it is like to be one of the people who fall under it; and criterion loops, where a label changes what evidence we accept as establishing that someone falls under it at all. Say which of the three are actually live here, which are red herrings, and what each implies for the decision — including whether the conditions you would impose differ depending on which loop you are trying to interrupt.

Second, handle the 58/11 split honestly. It is compatible with at least two readings — that the label is recruiting and shaping cases, and that an underserved population has finally found a door it recognizes — and the committee will want to know what we could measure during a first year that would actually discriminate between them, not merely what would make us feel more rigorous. Give me a small set of pre-specified metrics and a decision rule for the twelve-month review, including which result would oblige us to stop.

Third, give the strongest case against me more than a paragraph's worth of courtesy. As I understand it: our existing categories loop just as hard as this one and have simply had decades to feel natural; refusing to name a kind of suffering because naming it might multiply it is a way of preferring a tidy map to the people currently outside it; and the clinicians most confident about contagion tend to be those least burdened by the clients it would exclude. If your recommendation is conditional or negative, say plainly what it costs those clients and why the cost is worth paying.

Constraints on the document: 1,000 to 1,300 words, plain prose, no bibliography, no citations, and no named authorities unless a name is doing real work — I cannot look anything up, and the memo should not assume its readers can either. Write it so that a smart clinician who has never encountered the phrase looping effects can follow every step, and so that the researcher whose instrument we are deciding on could answer our reasons point by point.
