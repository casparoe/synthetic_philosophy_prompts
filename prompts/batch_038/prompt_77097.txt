I spent thirty-eight years in calibration, most of it at a national measurement institute, and since retiring I've fallen into philosophy head first — epistemology mainly, which turns out to be full of arguments I've already had, just without anyone drawing the uncertainty budget.

Here's the thing that won't leave me alone. Every measurement I ever signed a certificate for sits in a traceablity chain: my working gauge against a reference standard, that against a better standard, and so on up until you reach a realisation of the kilogram or the metre. And since the 2019 redefinition, that bottom rung is bizarre: the Planck constant is fixed by definition at exactly 6.626 070 15 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and the speed of light at exactly 299 792 458 m/s, both with zero stated uncertainty — but you can only cash those definitions out through an actual apparatus, a Kibble balance or a silicon sphere or a frequency comb, and that apparatus comes with a fat error bar. So the foundation of the whole system is a stipulation with no uncertainty resting on a realisation with plenty of it. Nobody in my lab ever thought that was a scandal. We just called it a primary standard and got on with the day.

That's the regress problem, and I want to build a diagnostic for the reading group I've joined — mostly retired engineers and lab people, not one of whom has read any epistemology, and I don't want to lose them in jargon.

So: a decision tree of the positions on the regress, built from the logical space rather than from a list of names. Ask the questions first, then put the positions where they land.

Seven to nine branch nodes, nine to twelve leaves, at least three of them empty. No node may contain a label — nothing like "are you a foundationalist?" — and every node has to be answerable by someone who's never heard of the debate. Where you can, phrase them in the idiom of a lab chain; where you can't, use ordinary concrete cases.

Please make sure these turns appear as nodes, because they're the ones I keep failing to sort out on my own:

1. Is the demand for a further reason binding on everything, or only on some things? I.e. is the regress generated by a principle you're actually obliged to accept, or is the principle itself the suspect move?
2. Must the bottom of a chain be certain, or is it enough that it doesn't owe its warrant to a further link? My primary standard is definately not certain — it's a realisation with an error bar — and yet the chain above it is fine. Which foundationalism does that support?
3. Is the objection to coherentism really about circularity, or about the absence of independent routes? Would a mesh with several mutually independent paths between any two nodes still be objectionable? If yes, make "single loop" and "redundant mesh" different leaves; if no, say why not.
4. Can a chain be endless and still finite in total effect — each further reason contributing less, like a convergent series — and if so, is that infinitism, or foundationalism wearing a disguise? I want that distinction to be a node, not buried in prose.
5. Does the regress run over propositions believed, or over abilities — a standing capacity to produce one more reason if challenged? Those look like different views and I can't tell whether they are.
6. Does warrant come in degrees, so that "justified" is a threshold on a quantity that can be raised indefinitely without ever needing a stopping point?
7. Is the chain-shaped picture itself the mistake? If warrant is conferred by a process being reliable rather than by reasons the subject can cite, does the regress simply fail to get going — and is that a leaf or a refusal to play?

Leaves I want to see, at minimum: strong or classical foundationalism; modest/defeasible foundationalism; coherentism (split per node 3 if you think it splits); infinitism, with the convergent variety separated from the plain endless one if that's a real distinction; the externalist dissolution; the rejection of the generating principle; and the sceptic who thinks the regress goes through and nothing is justified. Then fill in whatever else the nodes permit, including the empty cells.

For each empty cell, tell me whether it's empty because the combination is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody has simply got round to it — and if it's the last, why you'd want to live there.

Finally, be honest about the tree's limits: name any pair of leaves it can't actually separate, and say what extra question would separate them if you walked the tree back.

Please give me the whole thing as a single JSON object, in British English spelling, with exactly this schema:

- `how_to_use`: two or three sentences on how to run this on a real person, in order.
- `nodes`: array of `{ "id", "question", "what_turns_on_it", "options": [ { "answer", "goes_to" } ] }` — where `goes_to` is either another node id or a leaf id, `question` is the label-free wording a reader sees, and `what_turns_on_it` is one sentence on which disagreement that node is actually probing.
