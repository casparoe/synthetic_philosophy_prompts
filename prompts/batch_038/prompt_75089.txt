Question: does Grice's line between what is said and what is implicated mark a real joint, or is it a philosopher's artifact?

The worry I want you to take seriously, and then tell me whether you think its fatal: you can't get to what is said without already doing pragmatic work. Disambiguation, fixing reference, and the enrichment in cases like someone at a party saying every bottle is empty when they mean every bottle at the party — all of that looks like the same kind of inference that generates implicature, only run earlier and more automatically. If the supposedly prior, purely semantic level is reachable only by running the pragmatic machinery, then tests like cancellability aren't adjudicating a genuine boundary, they're just recording how confident we feel about a reading.

Against that: something has to bottom out. Otherwise its hard to see what a competent speaker knows and a hearer recovers, why irony, metaphor and understature are recognizable as departures from something, and how we diagnose a miscommunication as a failure at one level rather than another.

Weigh both sides properly and come down on one. Don't end on "the distinction is useful for some purposes but not others" — if your verdict is that it survives, say exactly what survives and where the line goes; if it doesn't, say what should replace it. Then test that verdict on one hard case, something like "John has a brain," where the flat literal reading is perfectly available but everyone hears a compliment, and show whether your account predicts the right result there.
