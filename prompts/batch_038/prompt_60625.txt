I'm in a value theory seminar this semester — we've spent the last few weeks on suffering, commensurability, population ethics, that sort of thing — and the final paper is 5,000 words, due at the end of week 10. I want to lock down the topic before I sink a month into it, so write me the proposal I'd hand my professor. Not a topic area: an actual plan for work I could do in the time I've got.

Here's the half-formed thing I keep circling. Take the lexical threshold view: there's some intensity T such that any pain at or above T can't be outweighed by any quantity of goods below it, however many, however long they run. Every version of the view I've seen treats T as a fixed point on a single intensity scale — the same cutoff for every subject, in every context. Both halves of that feel wrong to me and I want to go after the second one.

Consider a pair of cases. Start with an hour of pain that's genuinely awful but still sits below T — one that can, in principle, be outweighed by enough mild pleasure spread across the rest of a life.

- Case A: that hour happens, then the person stays awake and conscious and goes on to live an ordinary, mildly pleasant life.
- Case B: the exact same hour happens, and then the person drops into six months of dreamless unconsciousness before anything resumes at all.

On the standard additive picture these contain the same amount of badness. B just has a neutral stretch in it, and neutral stretches don't change the sum, so A and B are equally bad — or A is worse if you think the mild pleasures in A do any work. But my intuition runs the other way: B is worse. Pain that just sits there with nothing diluting or interrupting it seems to count for more than the same pain embedded in a continuous stretch of experience. If that's right, maybe T isn't a property of raw intensity at all but of how undiluted the badness is — whether and how much it gets reset. The threshold would be endogenous to the temporal profile of a life rather than a fixed mark on the scale. That's the seed, as coherent as it currently is.

Turn it into a proposal with:

1. The question, stated so it actually has a determinate answer instead of being a mood.
2. Why the positions already in play — threshold views, the sorites-style pressure on where exactly T sits, the intransitivity worries, the general debate about whether pleasure and pain are commensurable on one scale at all — don't already settle it. I want the gap to be real, not manufactured.
3. The method, and specifically why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives. Three things I'd be doing: (a) building a toy model where the threshold is a function of the temporal profile, and checking whether I can keep the ordering transitive, block the sorites without just relabelling it, and stay off both trivial endpoints — a T so low the view collapses into straight negative utilitarianism, and a T so high it's effectively no threshold at all; (b) pinning down what does the resetting — is it interruption as such, memory of the pain, anticipation of it, narrative unity? These come apart and they have different consequences, so the proposal should say which one I'd have to commit to and what that costs; (c) testing whether the view gives sane verdicts in actual cases — palliative sedation for refractory suffering at the end of life, where clinicians deliberately induce unconsciousness to stop symptoms they can't otherwise control; anesthetic depth and accidental awareness under anesthesia; and unbroken pain versus the same total amount broken into episodes with symptom-free stretches between.
4. What a result would look like and what would count as the project failing. Be blunt here — I'd rather find out in week 1 than week 9. One thing I already suspect: the view might end up saying unconsciousness is good rather than neutral, which is exactly the kind of consequence that makes people reject the whole setup, so I want to know whether that's a bug I can engineer around or the actual price of admission.
5. Realistic scope. What fits in 5,000 words and ten weeks alongside four other courses, and what gets cut.

Deliverable-wise I'm imagining a formal model, a short table of verdicts showing where my view and the standard one agree and where they come apart, and a clear-eyed failure section.
