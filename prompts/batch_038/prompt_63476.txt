I run the staff side of a statewide coalition — 34 member orgs (two food-bank networks, a nurses' local, four congregational groups, a bail fund, a riverkeeper, a couple of disability-rights shops, a business association) sharing a $2.1M budget, a lobbyist, and a joint-position process. Almost nothing we do is a straight moral argument; it's trades. Every month something lands on my desk shaped like "we'll drop our objection to your data-sharing bill if you put our translation-access rider in the omnibus," or "we'll co-sign your permitting letter if you stay quiet on the clinic protest buffer." I set the agenda, and while the bylaws need two-thirds of the steering committee for any position statement, in practice I decide what gets brokered at all — so the real decision is mine and it happens before the vote. Roughly one formal trade a month plus two or three informal ones, twenty minutes per item in a room of nine people who are not philosophers and do not want to be treated like they are, and we've had two walkouts in five years, both after a trade someone later described as us selling them out. I have a poli-sci degree and no ethics training, and what I currently do is vibes plus a running mental ledger, which has failed twice.

Build me a decision procedure my steering committee could actually run on a proposed trade: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with "don't broker this," and where it hands the decision back to the room because the question is about values rather than facts. Ground it in whatever's actually established about moral compromise and gains from trade — the line people draw between ordinary compromises and "rotten" ones, i.e. agreements that set up or maintain a regime of cruelty and humiliation; the difference between trading something you're merely uncertain about and trading something you're sure of; the worry that some trades change what the thing being traded means, or express an attitude flatly inconsistent with the value it's cashed out against; the separability problem, where two issues look tradeable but aren't, so no compensation can actually balance them; and the difference between splitting the difference and building a position neither side started with. Don't make me chase references, just build the distinctions in and tell me which ones you kept and which you dropped as unusable. It also has to handle the four ugly features of my situation specifically: (1) trades here are structurally one-way — our labor members keep giving on policing and surveillance because the civil-liberties side has nothing they want on prevailing wage or procurement, so the "exchange" is a concession wearing a trade's clothes and the ledger never ballances; (2) we pay in IOUs across committees, and membership turnover has voided the ledger twice, so the procedure needs a rule on whether a future committee can be bound; (3) our biggest funder's program officer keeps suggesting "package deals" that always happen to favor their priority area, so I need a way to test whether a trade is ours or theirs; (4) one member has standing-threatened to walk if we take any position on a certain topic at all, which means we're sometimes trading with someone's exit instead of trading on behalf of the people a position actually affects — and several of our trades spend things that belong to non-members who aren't at the table. Finally it needs a publicity test that's a real test and not a slogan: can the deal survive being stated in one sentence in a joint press release that a hostile reporter reads aloud.

Run it on three cases, one of which should come out against me: I want to see the procedure refuse a trade I'm confident is good for the coalition's survival, stated flatly, with no softening and no "but you might still choose." The cases: the data-sharing / translation-access swap described above; a permitting letter we'd co-sign for the business association in exchange for them dropping opposition to a paid-sick-leave ordinance, where the people who'd benefit from the ordinance aren't members and won't be in the room; and the funder's package deal, which folds a housing-siting compromise, a police-oversight provision, and an environmental-justice rider into one statement. Mark every step that's really a judgment call wearing a procedural costume with something like [JUDGMENT], and say what to do when we simply can't answer a question in twenty minutes — does the default run to refuse, defer, or split.

I want you to commit. Pick the thresholds, pick which test wins when two of them conflict, and give your reasons rather than laying out the schools of thought on compromise and letting me choose; if you think one of my four "ugly features" isn't actually a problem, say so and argue it. Format, in this order: **Bottom line** (~150 words, what the procedure is); **What has to survive from the theory, and what I cut** (~300 words); **The procedure** (700–900 words, numbered questions, each with its sufficient-answer condition and its stop point); **Where it hands the decision back to the room** (~200 words); **Worked applications** (the three cases, ~800 words total); **What this procedure can't do** (~250 words, including the failure mode where it gets used to launder a decision I'd already made).
