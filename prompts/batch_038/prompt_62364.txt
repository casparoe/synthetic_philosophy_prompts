I'm TA-ing a third-year interdisciplinary course on time (roughly half philosophy students, half physics) and in two weeks I get 50 minutes on whether the past and the future exist. The physics half have all done SR and most of them think the debate is already over — one of them told me after lecture that the block universe is just what relativity says and presentism is a holdover from religion. The philosophy half have mostly only met McTaggart, so they come in assuming the issue is whether time really flows, and several of them will happily say time flows while denying that this has anything to do with ontology, and I don't think I can get them to see why that's a mistake without burning half the session.

So don't explain presentism and eternalism to me, I know the territory well enough — plan the session for me. What I need: the order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what (I suspect I can't do the truthmaker objection until they've got "exists" and "is real" pulled apart and until they've accepted that truths need truthmakers at all, which is not obvious to a physics student, but I might have that backwards); which single case should carry the whole thing — and here I want you to argue *against* using the Andromeda/special-relativity argument (Rietdijk–Putnam?) as my opening move even though that's exactly what my physics students will demand I start with, and then tell me what to use instead and what that example has to have and what it must not have; the misconceptions, split into the ones they walk in with and the ones my own explanation will create, each tagged to the point in the sequence where I should head it off (I'm already bracing for "eternalism means fatalism, so nothing I do matters" and for someone reinventing the moving spotlight about five minutes after I've insisted the block is static); one thing I should deliberately refuse to explain at this level and say so out loud, with the sentence I'd actually say; and a closing question that separates students who got the ontological issue from students who just memorised a spacetime diagram.

Give it to me as something I could teach from — rough minute counts per stage, and what I say or write on the board at each transition — not an essay about the philosophy of time. End with exactly one line beginning "The hinge:" naming the single moment in the sequence that everything else depends on.
