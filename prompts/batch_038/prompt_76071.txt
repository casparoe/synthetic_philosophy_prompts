The question was worth twenty marks, the four parts equally weighted at five each, and read as follows:

"In On Certainty, Wittgenstein responds to G. E. Moore's Proof of an External World. (a) Explain what Moore took himself to be proving and on what grounds, and explain why Wittgenstein thinks the proof fails to do what Moore wanted. (b) Explain the notion of a hinge proposition, saying what kind of thing a hinge is supposed to be and how it stands to doubt. (c) Wittgenstein compares the relation between settled propositions and ordinary empirical ones to a riverbed and its water. Explain the analogy and say what it does and does not imply about the permanence of hinge certainties. (d) Does this picture make Wittgenstein an epistemic relativist — someone for whom what counts as known varies from framework to framework, with no neutral standpoint from which frameworks could be compared? Argue for your answer."

Here is the student's response, unedited:

"(a) Moore's aim was to establish the existence of external objects against the sceptic by the most direct route available. He raised his hands and announced that here is one hand and here is another, from which it follows that there are at least two external objects, and hence an external world. He insisted that this met the ordinary conditions of a rigorous proof: the premises differ from the conclusion, the premises are known to be true, and the conclusion follows. He also conceded that he could not prove the premises, and thought that did not spoil the proof, since a proof does not have to be a proof of its premises. Wittgenstein's objection is not that the conclusion is doubtful but that the premise is not the sort of thing that can be known in the way Moore claims. When Moore says that he knows that here is a hand he is not reporting a well-supported empirical finding: there is no evidence he could produce that would be better than the thing itself, and there is no live doubt in the vicinity for the claim to be excluding. Doubt, for Wittgenstein, needs a background that is not itself in doubt; to doubt everything at once would not be to doubt at all. Moore's mistake, then, is to treat a proposition that functions as part of the frame of enquiry as though it were a result arrived at inside that enquiry.

(b) The framing propositions are what Wittgenstein calls hinges. The image is that our questions and our investigations turn on them the way a door turns on its hinges: all the movement is in the door, and the hinges have to stay put for there to be any movement at all. Hinges include such things as that the earth existed long before my birth, that I have a body, that this is my hand, and that the chair in the next room has not ceased to exist while I stopped looking at it. They are not items of knowledge, and not strictly beliefs either, since a belief is something one can be shown to have got wrong and these cannot be shown to have got wrong in that way. They are held fast by everything around them rather than by evidence of their own, and they show themselves in how we act: my certainty that this is my hand is not a vivid inner feeling but an unhesitating way of behaving. Hinges are what we are trained into, and agreement in them is the precondition of agreement in judgments rather than a further matter to be argued about.

(c) The riverbed is Wittgenstein's way of insisting that this is not a static picture. Some propositions have hardened and act as channels through which the fluid propositions of ordinary enquiry run, and the relation between the two alters over time: what was fluid can harden, and hardened ground can be cut away and re-cut. So hinge status is not eternal, though neither is it something an individual can revise at will. What I take this to show is that hinge-hood is a matter of how entrenched a proposition has become: a proposition becomes a hinge to the extent that it has survived testing and can no longer usefully be doubted, so hinge certainty is the limiting case of confidence — confidence high enough that further doubt stops paying for itself. The difference between an ordinary empirical belief and a hinge is one of degree rather than of kind. It follows that what Moore should have said was not that he knows there is a hand here but that he believes it, and that hinges are justified not by evidence but by the indispensable role they play in making evidence possible.

(d) On that reading the answer is no. Hardening happens through shared training and shared ways of acting rather than through anyone's choosing, and since the hardened ground is what makes it possible to have evidence at all, there is no standpoint from which frameworks could be compared, because the comparison would itself require a shared bed. The sceptic and Moore are not two frameworks but two ways of trying to speak from inside one. The relativist error is to treat a hinge as though it were an opinion that a framework happens to hold, when in fact it is what makes any opinion of that framework's intelligible. Wittgenstein is not saying that what counts as known varies from framework to framework; he is saying that what makes anything known is not itself a candidate for being known."
