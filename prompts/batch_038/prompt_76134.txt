The question we set was:

> Keynes's *A Treatise on Probability* (1921) treats probability as an objective logical relation between a body of evidence and a conclusion, and holds that the "weight of argument" — the amount of relevant evidence bearing on it — is a dimension distinct from the probability itself, the relevant comparison turning "upon a balance, not between the favourable and the unfavourable evidence, but between the absolute amounts of relevant knowledge and of relevant ignorance respectively." In his 1937 QJE paper "The General Theory of Employment" and in Chapter 12 of *The General Theory*, he holds that for the prospective yields of a capital asset many years hence there is no scientific basis for forming any calculable probability. Explain the role of weight of argument in the Treatise, and assess the claim that the later doctrine of uncertainty is simply that doctrine carried over into macroeconomics. Does the later Keynes still need the objective-logical-relation account, or would his argument about investment go through just as well on a subjectivist basis? (1,500 words.)

And here's the student's answer:

> Keynes rejects the two standard accounts of probability. He is not a frequentist, because on his view probability attaches to propositions relative to evidence rather than to events in the world, and he is not a subjectivist either, because for him a probability is a genuine logical relation between a set of premises and a conclusion, something the mind apprehends by direct judgement rather than something constituted by anyone's feelings. Crucially he denies that such relations are always numerical. Many of them are only ordinal, and some are not even comparable: there need be no fact of the matter as to whether the evidence for one hypothesis is stronger than the evidence for another. His attack on the principle of indifference supports this. Just because we can list n alternatives and see no reason to prefer one does not license assigning each 1/n, since the alternatives can be partitioned in different and mutually inconsistent ways, and the principle then generates contradictory results.
>
> Alongside probability Keynes puts weight of argument, which is best understood as the probability that our probability estimate is correct. Adding relevant evidence can move the first-order probability in either direction, but it always raises the weight, and weight is what makes a judgement worth acting on. That is why an estimate based on a thin sample is not, for Keynes, simply as good as one based on a large sample with the same ratio.
>
> The later work applies exactly this. In the 1937 paper and in Chapter 12 of the General Theory Keynes argues that when an investor tries to estimate the yield of a capital asset ten or twenty years out he is not dealing with anything like drawing balls from an urn. The relevant evidence is missing, so no calculable probability exists. This is an information problem in principle solvable: if we had long runs of reliable data about the future, the probability would be determinate, and better statistics and forecasting would progressively fill the gap. What actually happens instead is that investors fall back on convention — assuming the existing state of affairs will continue unless there is specific reason to expect change — and on spontaneous optimism, what Keynes calls animal spirits, plus a state of confidence that can collapse suddenly and for no calculable reason. Hence the volatility of investment, hence the premium on liquidity, and hence Keynes's case for a somewhat comprehensive socialisation of investment.
>
> On continuity: the doctrine is plainly the Treatise carried into macroeconomics, and this is not seriously disputed. Weight of argument becomes the state of confidence; non-numerical probability becomes uncertainty. One complication is Ramsey, whose 1926 critique showed that degrees of belief are measurable by betting behaviour and that Keynes's objective probability relations were otiose. Keynes accepted this, so by the time of the General Theory he was a subjectivist about probability, and the uncertainty argument is really a subjectivist one: expectations are psychological states, not logical relations. This does not damage the economics, but it does mean the continuity thesis is best stated as continuity of a subjectivist position rather than of the Treatise's objectivism.
