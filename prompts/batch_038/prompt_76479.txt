I was at a two-day procurement workshop last month — donor-funded, half the room was ministry people, half was us auditors — and on the second evening two of the participants got into it in the hotel bar and I ended up just sitting there with my drink while they went several rounds. Neither of them was stupid, thats the thing. I'd like someone to tell me who was right about what.

The case they were arguing over comes from the district where one of them works. The council votes to award a road contract. The winning firm is not the lowest bidder; it's the one whose owner, after the award, hires the nephews and brothers-in-law of four of the council members who voted for him, and also re-roofs the district secondary school out of his own margin, no invoice. The road got built, apparently to spec, and locals seem pleased with it. The auditor — call her M — says this is three counts in one file: procurement fraud, undisclosed conflict of interest, and a disguised bribe, since the school roof is a payment to the council in a form that also buys them popularity. The other one, a sociologist who's spent years in the district — call him A — says calling it corruption is lazy and possibly counterproductive.

Their exchange went roughly like this:

1. M: public office comes with rules, and the rules say award to the lowest responsive bidder and take nothing from your vote. He broke them and came out ahead by breaking them. That's what corruption is, it doesn't need more argument than that.

2. A: your first move assumes the thing in dispute. Benefit — benefit to whom, on whose account? In that district the man who wins a contract and does *not* hire his cousins is the one people call corrupt, because he's taken something and given nothing back. He's discharging obligations everyone there recognizes. You're grading him against an ideal of impersonal office-holding that only makes sense where the state actually delivers impersonally, and there it doesn't.

3. M: even granting all your anthropology, there's a loser in this and he isn't hypothetical. The firm that bid twelve percent lower and had no relatives to hand out is out of the money. That's a real distributive harm to people outside the network, and the school roof doesn't offset it — a different group gets a nice building while the excluded bidder eats the loss.

4. A: notice you've shifted from rule-breaking to harm, which is telling. And on harm: that road is better than the one the low bidder would have laid. Everyone in the district knows the cheap firms skimp the base course and it washes out in two seasons. Your harm is the difference between two imaginary roads, one of them worse. On the actual transaction, nobody was made worse off except a firm that would have delivered a defective asset.

5. M: then be consistent. Campaign donations buy access in your country too. Lobbying, the revolving door, a university that admits a donor's child, a minister who steers contracts to the region that elects him — all legal, all converting money into advantage under a veneer of rules. If kin-hiring escapes the label because it's socially embedded, so does all of that, and you've emptied the word out.

6. A: fine, empty it out — or rather, admit it's a word for whichever form of elite advantage we happen to dislike. But look what that does to your verdict. Consistency doesn't get you to "the district officer is corrupt," it gets you to "everyone is corrupt," and that's not a finding, it's a shrug. Whereas I can say something operational: there's no feasible alternative arrangement in that district, so writing him up gets him replaced by someone who keeps the roof money for himself.

7. M — this is the one I keep turning over: your last point only works if the losers stay quiet, and they didn't. Two council members who voted against the award went to the press, and the excluded bidder filed a complaint. So the "local standard" you keep invoking isn't one standard, it's the standard of the people who came out ahead, and you've taken their side and called it culture.

They both laughed and went for another round and that was the end of it. I write the reports for a living so this is supposed to be my subject, and I came away genuinely without a verdict.

Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step — not just who wins overall? Specifically: which of M's moves are stronger than they look and which are actually bad arguments (5 smells like a tu quoque to me but I couldnt say why it isn't, and I suspect 3 does more work than M realizes); whether A's 2 and 7 can both stand or whether 7 guts 2; whether 4 is a legitimate move or just changes the subject from the office-holder's conduct to the outcome; and whether there's a defensible version of "corruption" that survives 6 — one that isn't just "whatever our written rules say" and isn't just "whatever the locals say," and that can still explain why the school roof matters. Then say what both of them were missing, if anything.
