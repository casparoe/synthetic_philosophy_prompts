Forty years as an experimental neurophysiologist, and philosophy has become my retirement project. I have been sitting with Avicenna's Flying Man: a person created all at once, fully formed, floating in air with his vision shrouded and his limbs kept out of contact with one another, so that no sensory channel reports anything at all — who nonetheless, on Avicenna's telling, would affirm the existence of his own self while affirming no limb, no organ, no length, breadth, or depth.

I want a number from you, and I want the derivation. Give me your credence in this proposition: the Flying Man scenario, taken as a standalone argument and setting aside whatever else in Avicenna's system is brought in to support it, provides good evidence that the self-awareness constitutive of being a subject does not depend on the body or on sensory functioning.

Please decompose that credence into terms you would actually multiply, covering at minimum: (i) the probability that a subject genuinely in such a state would affirm a self at all, rather than affirming nothing; (ii) the probability that such an affirmation would be evidence about what the self depends on, rather than only about what a subject so deprived could represent; (iii) the probability that Avicenna's own conclusion — a soul subsisting as a distinct immaterial thing — is licensed, rather than the weaker claim that first-person givenness is underived from perception; and (iv) any further terms you think belong in the product. Show the arithmetic, not just the total, and say what would move the total most.

For reference, I sit near 0.2, and I suspect my deficit lies almost entirely in (ii). It strikes me that a subject receiving no bodily signal whatsoever would have nothing by which to single himself out from what is merely occurring, so that what he is aware of may be no more determinate than that awareness is happening. Tell me where you think that intuition overreaches, and whether it is the kind of thing that could be settled empirically at all.
