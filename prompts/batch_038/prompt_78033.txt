Third draft of a novel with a city council in it — nine members, and the whole middle of the book is them circling a decision about a piece of waterfront land. I keep finding I've written scenes where somebody argues hard for a thing they dont actually want, because arguing for it makes the thing they *do* want more likely to happen. A friend of mine who does political science says there's a formal result about exactly this, something like Gibbard-Satterthwaite, which apparently says you can't build a voting rule that's immune to that kind of play unless one person is effectively running the whole thing from behind.

Here's my problem. I can read the plain-English writeups of it and they just slide off me. I don't think in theorems, I think in scenes — in what a character wants versus what they say, in who's the engine of a plot, in what an audience knows that the characters don't. My maths stopped somewhere around high school algebra. So: can you translate the theorem into that vocabulary, as faithfully as you're able? I want to know what a "social choice function" would be in a story, what it would even mean for a fictional world to be strategy-proof, what the "dictator" looks like on the page as opposed to in the definition, and why the thing needs at least three options before it bites — is there a story-shaped reason for the three, or is that purely an artifact of how the proof runs?

Then the part I actually care about, which is the residue. Once you've done the translation, tell me what fell out of it. Which bits of the theorem have no counterpart in craft terms whatsoever — and for each of those, tell me whether that's because narrative thinking is missing a concept it ought to have, or because the theorem's version of the concept is thinner than it advertises itself as. I'm not looking to be flattered and I'm not looking to be told my novel is secretly about social choice theory. Be blunt. Three things especially:

1. The theorem seems to require preferences that are fixed, fully ranked, and transparent to the person who has them. Every character I've ever managed to write is wrong about what they want, or wants two things that can't both be had, or reverses mid-scene for reasons that aren't new information. Is that just a difference in subject matter — economists do one thing, novelists another — or does it mean the theorem isn't actually about people at all?

2. My friend also said that if everyone's preferences happen to line up along a single axis, you *can* get a non-manipulable rule, something about a median voter. My hunch is that most novels are quietly like that: one wound, one question, everybody arranged along it, which is why the council in my book feels so orderly even when they're all scheming. Is that hunch doing real work or is it a category mistake? What would the single-axis restriction correspond to concretely in a story — subject matter, cast size, setting?

3. Where do I sit in all this? I'm the one who decides the outcome, which sounds like precisely the dictatorship the theorem rules out, except nobody in the book is being manipulated by me, because none of them know I exist. Is that a real difference or am I just flattering myself?

No bibliography and no citations, I'll add anything I need myself and honestly I wouldn't read them. I just want to come away knowing whether this theorem can tell me something about those council scenes that I can't get from anywhere else — or whether I should stop thinking it's deeper than it is.
