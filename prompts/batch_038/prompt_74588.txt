I'm about two-thirds through a novel set in a run-down narrowboat yard in the Black Country, and I've hit a wall that isn't really a plot problem, it's a thinking problem. Three of my characters have each put four years and their savings into restoring boats that are plainly never going to work: one is doing it for her dead father, one is a health economist who can recite the line about sunk costs being irrelevant and still keeps paying for a storage unit he doesn't need, and one is a young bloke who's convinced that walking away would make the last four years retroactively worthless. At the moment they're just three shades of stubborn and my editor will spot that in a heartbeat. What I want is to know what each of them actually believes, in terms that aren't just he's irrational or she's sentimental.

So could you build me a decision tree, or some kind of sorting instrument, for the question of whether it's ever rational or right to keep going because of what you've already put in? I'd like it built out of the logical space first rather than out of a list of names. Give me maybe seven to nine branch nodes, each one a concrete question a character could answer without knowing where it leads — the sort of thing where you ask: if a stranger offered you the finished boat tomorrow, with none of the four years attached, would you take it? Would you tell a mate in the same position to carry on? Does the fact that you started tell you something about the project you didn't know before? Are you trying to protect the value of the past years, or the shape of your own character? Then lay out the leaves and name them, and I want the awkward ones in there, not just the textbook pairing of clear-eyed forward-looking agent versus biased human. I'd want room for someone who thinks past spending is genuine evidence and so carrying on is a decent inference; someone who holds a standing rule of never honouring sunk costs because case by case they can't trust themselves; someone for whom abandoning a project changes what the earlier years meant rather than costing more; someone whose objection to quitting is about the people who helped, or about shame, and has nothing to do with payoffs; and a deflationist who says there's no such thing as a sunk cost at all because how you book the four years is up to you. That last one is basically my health economist, and I need to know whether he's muddled or just standing in a corner nobody's bothered to label.

A few housekeeping things. Where a combination of answers is possible but nobody actually holds it, put the empty cell on the tree and say in one line why it's empty — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. And if two of your leaves can't really be seperated by the questions you've given me, say so rather than pretending the tree is sharper than it is. No references, no reading lists, no names of papers — I won't chase them up and you'd only be guessing; I want the machinery, and the machinery has to work standing on its own. Please write the whole thing in British English, since I'll be pasting chunks straight into my notes and the other spelling would drive me spare. Oh, and keep the wording of the nodes plain enough that a reader with no philosophy whatsoever could answer them while standing in a boatyard in the rain.
