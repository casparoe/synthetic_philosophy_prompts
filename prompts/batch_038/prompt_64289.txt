Six months ago I left a philosophy MA program to take a decision-support job at a mid-size regional health system, which in practice means I build the models that tell managers whether its worth collecting more data before committing to something. Last month we rolled out a flagging tool — a score that fires automatically in the chart when a patient looks like they're deteriorating — and the units using it are, on a handful of process measures, doing worse than the ones that aren't. I've been asked to explain to leadership why better information could possibly make things worse, and I went down a rabbit hole on Good's theorem and ended up writing something way too philosophical for the two-page memo I'm supposed to hand in. I'd like comments on the draft before I butcher it into something work-appropriate. I'm not after a reading list or citations — I mostly want to know whether the argument holds up and where it's soft.

Here's the draft:

**The theorem isn't about evidence.**

Good's theorem says that if you can either choose now among acts A1…An, or first learn the answer to some question E and then choose, learning first is never worse — provided the evidence is free. The proof is one line. Deciding now gets you the max over acts of the average across answers to E of that act's posterior expected utility; deciding after learning gets you the average of the maxima; and the maximum of an average never exceeds the average of the maxima.

But free evidence isn't the only thing the theorem needs. It needs (i) the evidence to be genuinely costless, (ii) the payoff of each act in each state to be unaffected by whether you learned E, (iii) your post-learning self to conditionalize and then maximize posterior expected utility, and (iv) the menu of acts to be the same before and after.

My claim is that (ii), (iii) and (iv) are not three separate technical conveniences. They are one hypothesis said three times: the person who decides after learning is the same agent as the person deciding now — same values, same options, same discipline. Good's theorem is not a theorem about the goodness of evidence; it's a theorem about diachronic self-identity that got mislabeled. When it fails, what failed is the identity, not the datum.

The standard counterexamples bear this out. In the suspense cases — you can read a plot summary that tells you whodunnit before choosing between the novel and a biography, and knowing ruins the novel — the payoff of "read the novel" depends on whether you already know, so (ii) breaks; the post-summary you is a different reader. In the Newcomb-flavored cases where a predictor's setup keys off whether you'd look, (ii) or (iv) breaks again. Even the non-Bayesian route — there's a good paper from around 2016 arguing that free evidence can be bad for agents with imprecise credences — works by giving up (iii): an agent who updates a set of priors doesn't have one stable ranking to maximize. In every case it's something about the self, not the evidence, that goes.

Which suggests the fix isn't to weaken the theorem but to widen the menu. If the act set is allowed to include self-directed acts — precommit now, blind myself, decline to look, defer the decision — then the option to learn always dominates deciding now, trivially, because declining is one of the available acts and declining reproduces your uninformed self exactly. So the quantity worth caring about is never the expected value of information; it's the expected value of the *option* to be informed, and that's non-negative by construction. Genuinely negative value only shows up when evidence is non-declinable: when it arrives whether you want it or not and can't be unread.

That is exactly the situation my hospital built. The flag auto-fires, it lands in the chart, it can't be suppressed or deferred, and once it fires the clinician is on the hook for having seen it. The signal may be fine; the design made it non-declinable. So the rule I want to hand leadership: the value of a decision-support system is not a function of its accuracy alone, it's a function of whether its outputs can be refused, and a tool that can't be refused can have negative value even when the underlying signal is informative. The fix for a bad flag is not a better model, it's a declinability switch.

End of draft. Specific things I want pushed on:
