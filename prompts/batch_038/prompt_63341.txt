I work on inventory and routing at an e-commerce company — basically making sure stock that already exists somewhere in the network actually reaches the people who ordered it. A few months back I tagged along to a public lecture on famines (my girlfriend's thing, she's doing a development studies MA) and the speaker kept hammering a point that felt very familliar to me from my day job: famines usually aren't a matter of there being too little food, they're a matter of the food not getting to the people who need it. He name-checked Sen and the entitlements idea and Bengal in 1943. I walked out with a clean mental model: a famine is a last-mile failure. The warehouse is full, the trucks don't show, requests get dropped somewhere between the fulfilment centre and the customer. I've even been using that framing at work, as a way of talking about how a system with plenty of total capacity can still starve parts of itself — which people seemed to find compelling, partly because it sounds so concrete.

Then over the holidays I cracked open a copy of Sen's book, got maybe two chapters in, and now I worry she was right when she said I'd reinvented the thing he was arguing against. My worry, as far as I can articulate it: in the logistics picture the food is sitting still and something physical is blocking it, and there's an implicit well-meaning-but-incompetent operator who could fix it if he had better telemetry. But if the actual mechanism is that people lost their ability to pay, then the food was never stuck — it went exactly where the money was, on purpose, and the market did its job correctly. That would mean I've put the failure in the transport layer when it belongs in the demand layer, and that's not a small imprecision, it changes who you point at.

Can you audit this properly for me? I'd like the correspondences spelled out — what in my analogy plays the role of the warehouse, the trucks, the dropped requests, the network operator, the traffic — and then where each mapping fails, separating the ones that are just loose talk from the ones that actually flip a conclusion. Two things I especially want nailed down. First: does "famine is a distribution problem, not a production problem" survive the audit, or is my analogy the thing carrying that slogan? Because I've noticed the logistics framing makes the obvious fix sound like better roads and better delivery, whereas the people invoking Sen seem to want the fix to be cash transfers or public works employment — a completely different intervention — and I suspect that gap is telling me something about which part of the analogy is load-bearing. Second, and this is the bit I actually care about: does the analogy quietly decide the blame question? A jammed supply chain points at incompetence, and "there wasn't enough food" points at the weather, and neither of those is the same as "someone let this happen," which is where the famine-as-political-crime framing seems to come from. I'd rather you commit than hedge: tell me whether my analogy is salvageable, mostly misleading, or useful for one purpose and actively distorting for another, and if you think there's a better systems-flavoured analogy for the same idea, propose one and say up front where it would break in turn. Also flag anywhere you're leaning on a contested reading of Sen or on a case historians have pushed back on — I got the impression from chapter two that his Ethiopia and Bangladesh material isn't universally accepted, but I don't trust my memory of it.
