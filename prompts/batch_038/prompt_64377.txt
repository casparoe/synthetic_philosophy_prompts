I am applying for a one-year postdoctoral fellowship in political philosophy and need a proposal of roughly 1,500 words on a question about the concept of corruption. My starting intuition, which I have not yet managed to state cleanly, is this. The standard philosophical and legal test for telling a bribe apart from a permissible gift asks whether the transfer is tied to some particular official act. That test seems to presuppose a picture of the officeholder as a sequence of discrete decisions, and it may be a local artifact of one administrative tradition rather than a universal standard. My suspicion is that two distinct features are being run together. The first is the determinacy of the link between the transfer and what the official does. The second is the legibility of the relationship in which the transfer sits: whether the ongoing exchange is publicly known and describable by third parties, or private and deniable. On a two-axis picture, diffuse and enduring gift exchange and clientelist patronage, which fail the act-specific test and so look legally clean, can do more damage to impartiality than a discrete payment, because they alter an official's dispositions over time rather than purchasing a single decision; while highly legible ceremonial exchange, which is often treated as inherently suspect, may be comparatively benign.

Please turn this into a proposal rather than into an argument. It should contain: a precise statement of the question, formulated so that a determinate answer is possible; an account of why the existing positions — the act-based analysis of bribery, the view on which corruption consists in subordinating a public role to private interest, and the relativist response that treats prevailing anti-corruption standards as culturally parochial — leave the question open rather than settled; the method, including what I would actually do month by month, and a defence of why a conceptual taxonomy coupled to a set of discriminating cases is the right instrument here rather than the obvious alternatives, such as comparative legal analysis or survey work on cross-cultural judgments about gifts; a description of what a successful result looks like and of what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of whether twelve months is realistic for it. Do not include a bibliography or a reference list — I will supply those myself — though you may name positions and arguments where doing so helps.

Two further requests, about commitment rather than content. Rank the proposal's own substantive claims by how confident you are in them, and say explicitly which parts of the framework you expect a referee to attack first. And do not simply build on my distinction if it will not bear weight: if you think legibility is not an independent axis at all but a correlate of determinacy, or if what I am calling the dispositional damage of ongoing exchange is just the act-based wrong spread thinly over time, say so plainly at the outset and propose the repaired version of the question instead of a polished proposal around a distinction that cannot hold.
