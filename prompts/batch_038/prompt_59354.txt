I am presenting a paper at a workshop session on analogy and conceptual representation, and I need a question-period preparation sheet aimed at a specific, largely unsympathetic room. Please treat the background gloss below as given rather than as something to reconstruct; the point of the exercise is the argument and the audience, not your recollection of the literature.

**Background I assume.** The Copycat family of models answers letter-string analogy problems — a source string, a transformed source string, and a target string, with the task of producing the transformed target. The architecture is tripartite: a slipnet of permanently available concepts whose link weights shift with activation, a workspace in which temporary perceptual structures (bonds, groups, correspondences) are built and demolished, and a coderack of stochastic codelets posted and run under the influence of both. Control is by parallel terraced scan, modulated by a scalar temperature intended to measure the workspace's current resistance to further change. Slippage — letting a concept slide to a near neighbour in the slipnet when a rule cannot apply cleanly — is presented not as a controlled relaxation of determinate rules but as the ordinary operation of the mechanism. The same architecture has been re-instantiated in other microdomains, including number sequences and melodic expectation.

**My thesis.** The letter-string microdomain is degenerate in a precise sense: it collapses several distinctions that a less contrived domain separates, and Copycat's apparent achievement of principled slippage depends on that collapse. It follows that the fluidity in these architectures is not emergent from the terraced scan. It is pre-loaded by the choice of a microdomain whose conceptual geometry has been matched, by hand, to the distances stipulated in the slipnet. The microdomain is therefore not a neutral testbed but a hidden theoretical parameter, and the family has never been evaluated on the degeneracies it removes rather than the answer distributions it reproduces.

**The microdomain I build the argument on, which I invented for the paper.** Tokens are pairs of a shape and a fill. Shape is one of triangle, square, pentagon, hexagon, read as three, four, five, six sides. Fill is one of hollow, half, solid. There are exactly two successor relations. The shape-successor advances the side count and is cyclic: the successor of hexagon is triangle. The fill-successor runs hollow to half to solid and then terminates; the successor of solid is undefined. Adjacent tokens sharing a shape or sharing a fill may bond into groups, and groups inherit a successor, as in the letter-string world. So the two slippable dimensions have different topologies: one is a closed cycle with no endpoint and no global maximum, the other is a bounded chain that runs out.

**Why I think this bites, in three steps.** First, in the letter-string world the rightmost position, the ordinal maximum, and the terminus of the successor chain coincide, and the successor relation is one-dimensional and acyclic, so slipping among rightmost, last, and highest costs nothing and looks like conceptual flexibility. In my domain they come apart: advancing the token with the greatest side count yields a triangle, the token with the fewest, so ordinal extremity and positional extremity decouple, and a rule phrased in terms of the maximum has no analogue in the fill dimension at all. Second, temperature is supposed to be a single scalar governing how much further search the system tolerates, and it cannot play that role when the dimensions available for slippage have incompatible topologies. Either temperature becomes dimension-relative, in which case the terraced scan loses its global control signal and decomposes into several independent searches, or it is computed on one dimension and thereby silently privileges that dimension — which is a hand-coded bias, not an emergent one. That is the pincer, and I regard it as the core of the paper. Third, the constructive claim: a microdomain should be specified by stating its conceptual geometry explicitly — how many successor relations, whether they are cyclic or bounded, which extremal descriptions coincide — and models should be compared on domains chosen to break those coincidences.

**The audience.** Roughly forty people. The session chair is a connectionist who thinks hand-built slipnets are a dead end and that any puzzle of this kind is already handled by in-context learning in a large pretrained model. There is a categorization experimentalist in the prototype-theory lineage who runs adult and child studies and is sceptical of armchair microdomains. There is a computational statistician who reviews for a modelling journal and cares about what counts as a model comparison when the output is a distribution. The rest are a mixture of ML practitioners and one philosopher of cognitive science who will want to know what my positive account is, given that I have objected to hand-set parameters.
