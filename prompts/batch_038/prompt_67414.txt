A professional regulator is revising the integrity clause in its disciplinary code. The clause in force penalises a registrant who makes a statement he or she knows to be false. The proposed replacement would penalise a registrant who, by words, omissions, ordering or selective emphasis, communicates with the intention that another person come to hold a belief the registrant knows to be false, whether or not every word uttered was true. Before the consultation closes, two camps have been asked to collaborate adversarially on the drafting question.

Camp A (the asymmetry camp) holds that lying is a distinct and graver wrong than merely misleading with true utterances: a lie abuses the practice of assertion itself and violates a duty owed to the addressee as hearer, whereas a literally true answer leaves the hearer's own inferences merely unguided, and the hearer retains some responsibility for the inference she draws.

Camp B (the uniformity camp) holds that culpability attaches to the intention to bring about a false belief and to the foreseeable harm that follows from it, so that a carefully true answer can be exactly as bad as a lie — and worse where it involves more forethought, exploits the hearer's reasonable expectation of cooperative communication, and leaves the hearer with nothing false to point to afterwards.

Draft the joint statement these two camps could both sign after a genuine, good-faith collaboration, addressed to the regulator's drafting committee. Please include the following.

1. A short shared glossary giving the working definitions of assertion, of what it is to say something as distinct from to implicate it, and of the intention to deceive that both camps can operate with, together with an explicit statement of what each definition deliberately leaves open.

2. A numbered list of agreed claims — substantive ones, not platitudes. At least one must be a claim each camp had expected the other to resist, and at least one must cut against Camp B's own preferred formulation of the clause. Mark which agreed claims are load-bearing for the eventual drafting recommendation.

3. A disagreements table with the columns: the disagreement; its formulation in wording Camp A would accept as a statement of its own view; its formulation in wording Camp B would accept as a statement of its own view; whether the disagreement is substantive or in part merely verbal, with reasons; and the crux, if any, whose settlement would move at least one side. For each crux, state the case, argument or evidence both camps accept as bearing on it, and record each camp's prediction in advance.

4. A shared case bank of at least six scenarios of the kind the regulator actually adjudicates — for instance, a clinician asked whether a treatment will work who replies that the evidence base is still developing; a manager asked whether a colleague's post is safe who describes the reorganisation as under review; a witness asked whether he was at a named address who denies ever entering a building by that name when the building is registered under another. For each scenario record each camp's classification, its comparative ranking of wrongness against the others in the bank, and its reason. Where the two camps reach the same verdict for different reasons, say so, and say which reason the committee should rely upon.

5. An explicit treatment of two pressures the committee must resolve. First: Camp B will argue that a speaker who can arrange the literal content of her utterance at will, and does so in order to produce a false belief, is lying by a slower route — Camp A must answer why the extra step through true words changes the moral character of the act rather than merely its concealability. Second: whether the hearer's opportunity to ask a follow-up question carries any moral weight, how much, and whether that weight survives in situations of unequal standing where follow-up questions are socially or practically unavailable.

6. A recommended wording for the clause that both camps can sign; or, if no single text is available to them, the narrowest formulation each can tolerate, together with an account of which camp's residual objection the committee should treat as decisive and why. Note any part of the dispute you judge to have been merely verbal, and what the two camps would have said had they been using the same words all along.

7. A closing paragraph in which each camp states what it would accept as evidence or argument that its own position is wrong, and whether there is anything both agree would actually settle the matter.

Argue the positions rather than reporting who holds them; do not cite or appeal to authority, and do not supply a bibliography.
