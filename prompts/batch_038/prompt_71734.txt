I run my team's book club at work — twelve ML engineers, all fluent in gradient descent, none of whom has ever sat through an argument about formal systems. Next Thursday I've got 45 minutes and a whiteboard to get them to understand the MU-puzzle and what Hofstadter means by jumping out of the system, because its supposed to set up the rest of the quarter's reading. I half-remember the puzzle from high school and never actually got why it mattered, so plan the session for me rather than explaining it to me.

What I need the plan to nail:

- The order the pieces have to go in, and what depends on what. I'm guessing they need the idea of a derivation before they can care about an invariant, but I don't know how much scaffolding people who've never met a formal system actually need before the puzzle is even a puzzle to them.
- Which example carries the thing. Is MIU itself the right carrier, or do I prime them with something they can see through in ninety seconds — the mutilated chessboard, maybe — and if so what does the priming example need to share with MU and what must it deliberately lack? I want the criteria, not just a pick.
- The misconceptions, in the order they'll show up. I'm near-certain someone says 'that's just a search problem, run BFS', someone says 'so add the invariant as a fifth rule and you're done', and someone will steer straight to 'this proves humans aren't computers' (the Lucas/Penrose move). Tell me exactly where in the sequence each gets headed off, and for the second one be honest with me: does it actually defeat the point, or is it the point?
- What I should flat-out refuse to cover at this level and say so out loud — I'm guessing Gödel numbering, but tell me what else I'm going to be tempted to include and shouldn't.
- One question I can ask in the last five minutes that separates people who got it from people who just enjoyed a cute puzzle.
