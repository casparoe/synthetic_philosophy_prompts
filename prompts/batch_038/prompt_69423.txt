I write literary fiction and I've got a story I cant crack. It's about a woman who spent four years planning her whole life around a house she finally bought, and then, within a week of getting the keys, stops planning anything at all — deliberately, as a kind of convalescence. She makes every decision one at a time, refuses to hold herself to intentions she formed earlier in the day, and won't let herself look at how today's choices add up. I want the plot to be a money pump. A dealer at the canal-side flea market works out that her tastes run in a circle: she'd hand over her grandfather clock and twenty dollars for a brass diving helmet, hand over the helmet and twenty for a crate of chipped porcelain dogs, and hand over the dogs and twenty to get the clock back. He only needs two of the three objects on his stall to run the whole thing, which I like as a detail, and he just walks her around that circle, gently, lap after lap, until she's standing exactly where she started, clock and all, a hundred and eighty dollars lighter. I want a reader to be able to diagram it on a napkin, so keep it to three objects and a small payment going her way at no point — she pays every time — and make every single trade feel like something she'd happily do considered on its own, right up to the last one.

The hard part is that I dont want the dealer to be a villain or the story to land as a fable about being irrational. He can be kind about it; he can even warn her. And I want her refusal to plan to feel like a position someone could hold rather than a cognitive defect, so the ending stays genuinely unclear: she has the clock back, she's poorer, and it's an open question which of those facts is the point. Length 1,800–2,500 words, close third person, present tense, no exposition about preference theory — the circle has to come out of what she touches and what she looks at too long. I'm also honestly not sure my setup is coherent. If she really looked ahead she'd see the whole sequence coming and refuse the first trade, which is why I thought the pump only gets people who choose myopically — but I have a vague memory of an argument that even a far-sighted, sophisticated agent with cyclic preferences can still be pumped under some conditions. So if it turns out my character has to be both clear-eyed about the pattern and still walk into it, in a way that makes the economics fall apart, say that plainly instead of writing around it and papering over the crack. Same if you think this is unavoidably a fable no matter how I stage it, or that a pump needs something my setup doesn't supply.

Start with the story itself — no preface, no note about what you're doing, no title unless it earns one. Then after it, give me one short paragraph on where you took liberties: which parts of the pump you bent to make it survivable as fiction, whether you had her running myopically or with foresight and why, and any place you're leaning on a contested reading of what makes the cycle irrational, or of whether it is irrational at all. I'd rather have that caveat than a seamless piece that quietly changes the subject.
