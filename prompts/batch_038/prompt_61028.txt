I'm slowly working through a normalized transcription of a letter Newton wrote to Richard Bentley in the winter of 1692–3 — Bentley was drafting the sermons he gave as Boyle lecturer and had sent Newton a set of positions to comment on. The transcription keeps Newton's spelling and puts braces where the editors had to expand something, so it reads a bit odd. Two stretches right next to each other have me stuck.

First:

"Tis unconceivable that inanimate brute matter should (without the mediation of something else which is not material) operate upon & affect other matter without mutual contact; as it must if gravitation in the sense of Epicurus be essential & inherent in it. And this is one reason why I desired you would not ascribe {innate} gravity to me. That gravity should be innate inherent & {essential} to matter so that one body may act upon another at a distance through a vacuum without the mediation of any thing else by & through which their action or force {may} be conveyed from one to another is to me so great an absurdity that I beleive no man who has in philosophical matters any competent faculty of thinking can ever fall into it. Gravity must be caused by an agent {acting} consta{ntl}y according to certain laws, but whether this agent be material or immaterial is a question I have left to the consideration of my readers."

And then immediately after:

"Your fourth assertion that the world could not be formed by inn{ate} gravity alone you confirm by three arguments. But in your first Argument you seem to make a petitio principij. ffor where{as} many ancient Philosophers & others as well Theists as Atheists have allowed that there may be worlds & parcels of matter innumerab{le} or infinite, you deny this by representing it as absurd as that there should be positively an infinite arithmetical summ or number which is a contradiction in terminis: but you do not prove it as absurd. Neither do you prove that what men mean by an infinite summ or number is a contradiction in nature. ffor a contradiction in terminis argues nothing more then an improperty of speech. Those things which men understand by improper & contradictious phrases may be sometimes really in nature without any contradiction at all. A silver inkhorn a paper Lanthorn an iron whetstone are absurd phrases & yet the things signified thereby are really in nature. If any man should say that a number & a summ (to speak properly) is that which may be numbered & summed; but things infinite are numberless or (as we usually speak) innumerable & summless or insummable & therefore ought not to be called a number or summ: he will speak properly enough & your argument against him will I fear lose its force. And yet if any man shall take the words number & summ in a larger sense so as to understand thereby things which in the proper way of speaking are numberless or sumless (as you do when you seem to allow an infinite number of points in a line) I could readily allow him the use of the contradictious phrases of an innumerable number or summless summ without inferring {from thence} any absurdity in the thing he means by those phrases. However if by this or any other argument you have proved the finiteness of the universe it follows that all matter would fall down from the outsides & convene in the middle."

Three things I cant get straight:

1. The inkhorn / lanthorn / whetstone move. It looks like he's saying a self-contradictory phrase doesn't show there's no such thing, since "a silver inkhorn" is a contradictory phrase that still picks out a real object. But then he gives two ways of taking number and summ — the proper way (what can be numbered and summed, so infinite things are innumerable and insummable) and a "larger sense" — and says Bentley's argument loses its force on the first reading and is harmless on the second. So where does Newton actually land? Is he granting that an actually infinite collection is possible, or only that Bentley has proved nothing? In another of the Bentley letters he runs the thought experiment about matter evenly scattered through the heavens and what happens depends on whether that space is finite or infinite, so I can't tell which side of the finitude question he's on. And what's the status of the points-in-a-line example — is geometry allowed to have actual infinities while nature isn't?

2. The agent sentence. People always quote the "unconceivable that inanimate brute matter" bit as Newton refusing to let gravity be intrinsic to matter becuase he needs God to hold the system together. But then he says whether the agent is material or immaterial is left to the reader. If he's genuinely relaxed about it being some material medium, what theological work is the passage doing at all? And how does "without the mediation of something else which is not material" square with that openness — I suspect the "not material" is doing something other than what I'm reading into it, but I can't say what.

3. Do the two stretches connect? It looks like Newton demolishes Bentley's first argument and then shrugs and says "however, if you have proved the finiteness of the universe, your conclusion follows" — giving back the thing he just undercut. Is that dialectical generosity, or is the infinity quarrel doing some work for his own view that I'm missing?
