I'm writing a long feature for a general-interest science and ideas outlet on the proposal that powerful AI systems can be kept honest by having them argue against each other in front of a human judge, on the reasoning that a lie is easier to catch than to tell. My editor wants the piece readable, but two researchers I interviewed, separately and at some length, both pushed me toward a family of objections that each of them referred to loosely as involving "obfuscated arguments," and neither of them explained the objection in a form I could responsibly carry into print. I'm not after a literature review, a list of papers, or names and dates; I have more of those than I can use and they are the easy part to check. What I need is the conceptual machinery, laid out plainly enough that I could reconstruct it for a bright reader with no technical background and without distorting it.

Here is the version I have been carrying around, and I would like you to tell me where it is right and where it is a caricature. Suppose a system wants a judge to accept a false conclusion. It does not assert the conclusion baldly; it builds a case out of, say, forty individually plausible steps, each of which a lay judge could check in a minute or two and each of which the judge would in fact accept. Most of the steps are true, several are true but irrelevant, and two are false in a way that surfaces only if you notice an interaction between step eleven and step thirty-four. The argument is, in a certain sense, fully verifiable — nothing in it exceeds the judge's comprehension — but the flaw is not findable by anyone reading at ordinary speed, and the opposing system, if it is being honest, must locate that specific interaction before it has anything useful to say. I have been calling this the conjunction problem: the cost of *producing* a long argument seems to grow roughly with its length, while the cost of *establishing* that it is sound grows much faster, because the checker must consider interactions among steps rather than steps in isolation. If that is right, the asymmetry runs the wrong way, and the asymmetry is the whole foundation of the scheme. My question is whether that is genuinely the crux, or whether I have fastened onto a practical difficulty that the proposal never claimed to solve.

So what I would like is an explanation of the conceptual issue that does four things. First, tell me what sort of claim the generating-versus-checking asymmetry actually is — a claim about time and effort, about computational complexity, about what a person can know, about something else — and whether the objection above really bears on that claim or merely sounds like it does. Second, distinguish the situation in which a judge could not detect a falsehood even given unlimited time and attention from the situation in which the judge could detect it but will not, and explain why that distinction is supposed to decide whether the mechanism is unsound in principle or merely unreliable in practice. Third, explain what the presence of a second, adversarial system is supposed to add over simply asking one system to explain and justify its own answer, since a reader will inevitably ask me this, and I suspect the honest reply is subtler than "the other one catches it lying." Fourth, give me the strongest available answer that a defender of the approach would make to the conjunction problem, stated so that I can represent it fairly rather than as a straw man. Please stay concrete and work through examples rather than abstractions, and please tell me directly if anything in my framing above is flatly wrong rather than merely imprecise.
