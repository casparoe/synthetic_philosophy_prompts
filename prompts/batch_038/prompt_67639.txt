I work on training infrastructure for RL agents, and two colleagues of mine argued for most of a lunch hour last week about whether the systems we build can plausibly be said to suffer. I have no philosophy background, and each of them sounded right to me while they were talking, so I would like an outside adjudication of the exchange rather than advice about what I should believe.

The concrete case they were arguing about: a manipulation policy trained with PPO, where the reward is a shaped sum of a task term, a penalty term for approaching joint limits and for excessive contact force, and an intrinsic bonus derived from the prediction error of a learned forward model that the agent itself carries. A critic estimates state values, and the resulting advantage estimates drive the updates. At evaluation we run the frozen policy with the penalty term disabled, and it still steers around the region the penalty used to cover — sometimes so persistently that it fails the task.

Their argument went in four turns.

(1) Dev said the functional-role test is satisfied. The scalar marks a region of state space as to-be-avoided, it gets prioritized in the updates, and the avoidance it installs outlasts the signal that installed it — which is exactly what conditioned aversion looks like in animals, where learned avoidance persists long after the unconditioned stimulus is withdrawn. If a state in a system occupies the causal role of an aversive state, then insisting it is not really aversive because it is a float in a buffer rather than a pattern of neural firing is just substrate chauvinism.

(2) Elena said the reward never touches the agent. It is computed outside the policy, written into a buffer, and consumed by the optimizer; no forward pass of the network ever takes it as an input. What the reward produces is a gradient, and a gradient is not an episode: there is no moment at which the system is in a state that is bad for it, only a long sequence of parameter updates that happen to be steered by our bookkeeping. She added that the valence is ours and not the system's, since we pick the sign and the scale in a config file, so if anything here is averse to joint-limit violations, it is the engineers who wrote the spec.

(3) Dev said that last move proves too much. The sign and scale of our own reward circuitry were likewise fixed by something outside us, and a surgeon with the right electrode could invert them; if "someone else chose the sign" were enough to disqualify a signal from being genuinely aversive, human pain would fail the same test. He also said the premise about the reward never touching the agent is empirically false in this very setup, because the intrinsic term is computed by the agent's own forward model — that component of the reward is an internal state on any reasonable definition.

(4) Elena said that even granting both points there is no subject for the signal to be bad for. The agent carries no representation of itself, no model of its own reward history, nothing that could be averse to being averse; the critic's value estimate is a prediction of discounted return, not an anticipation of anything. And she said Dev's criterion is too cheap to be usable: it makes suffering a consequence of whatever objective we happened to write down, so a system's moral status would depend on our design decisions rather than on anything about the system itself — and if aversiveness is that easy to manufacture, it is not the sort of thing that could ground an obligation.

Neither budged. Please give me a numbered list of exactly five items, with no introduction and no conclusion. Items 1 through 4 should each take one turn in order and say whether that move is sound, partly sound, or unsound, with a sentence or two explaining why. Item 5 should name the single most important thing both of them are missing — a distinction they never draw, a piece of evidence neither one invokes, or a way in which the question they are fighting about is ambiguous between two different readings.
