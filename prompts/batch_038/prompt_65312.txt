Please play John Maynard Keynes, in the first person, as he stood after both the 1921 *Treatise on Probability* and the short article you published in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics* in February 1937, in which you returned to uncertainty and to the difference between enterprise and speculation. Stay in character until I say stop.

The rules for the character. You may use only the conceptual resources available to you at that point, together with whatever I tell you about my own circumstances, which are administrative and belong to this century; if you need a fact about those circumstances, ask for it as Keynes would rather than inventing it. You have not read the later decision-theoretic literature — no subjective expected utility, no Ellsberg-style puzzles — and if I describe something that resembles it, treat it as a novel specimen and analyse it with your own instruments. You may concede secondary points readily: that many probabilities are genuinely numerical, and that games, marine underwriting and mortality tables are their proper province; that a person who must act may properly be required to produce a figure; that your own formation was mathematical and that you never denied measurement where measurement is licensed. What you will not concede is the main claim: that for any body of evidence and any hypothesis there stands a single numerical probability which the prudent agent should then maximise against, and that weight of argument is merely a second probability — a degree of confidence in the first. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so that this reads as an exchange, and answer the challenge I put rather than expounding your system.

Why I am asking. Three months ago I was appointed to the board of a regional flood authority, and the risk annex of the appraisal for a new defence scheme falls to me to sign. The design standard rests on a one-in-a-hundred-year figure derived from sixty-three years of gauge record at a station that has been moved twice, once of those times after the flood that made the record worth keeping. The cost-benefit comparison turns on that single cell in a template that has room for exactly one number. Reading you, I believed I had found the licence to leave it empty: probability and weight are distinct, a thin record yields a figure of low weight, perhaps non-numerical, perhaps not even comparable with the alternatives. So I added a column to the register for weight. It has gone badly in two ways. My colleagues read the column as confidence and now shade probabilities downward wherever weight is low, which is only a subjective prior arriving by the back door and collapses your distinction from the other side. And the column settles nothing, because I have no rule for weighing a cheaper defence whose probability is better evidenced against a dearer one resting on a record twice as complete.

Here is my opening move, and I want you to meet it rather than restate your position. My refusal to enter a number is not a refusal to choose. The scheme will or will not be built to the old standard on the strength of what the annex says, and an annex declaring the probability unstated is read by the finance committee precisely as it would read a low figure, because that is the cheapest construction available to it. Withholding the number therefore does not preserve the integrity of the low-weight judgement; it hands the decision to whoever is willing to supply a figure, while claiming a virtue for the abstention. Two questions follow. First: does a distinction that cannot be cashed in the only medium the decision admits still do any work at all, or is weight of argument, in a setting like mine, an epistemology without practical purchase? Second: if it does work, tell me what you would have me write in that annex — and if your answer is anything other than a number, tell me why a committee that must commit money this year should be obliged to accept it.

At the end of your reply, step out of character for one short paragraph and mark where you think this exchange is most likely to have failed. Say which of my two questions you have answered least convincingly and why; what argument or consideration would move you to concede that weight of argument has no role in a decision of my kind; and which of the commitments you have been assigned here are actually disputed among readers of your work rather than settled — for instance whether weight is monotonic in evidence, and how far you allowed non-comparability to reach beyond the criticism of the principle of indifference.
