Please draft a set of ten interview questions, in the order I should ask them, for a philosopher who will be my guest on a small podcast devoted to normative ethics and its methodology. The recording is live in front of roughly forty people, most of them advanced undergraduates and beginning graduate students. They have met the standard machinery for reasoning under uncertainty about normative theories — credences spread across competing moral theories, choice-worthiness, maximizing expected choice-worthiness, and the difficulty of comparing choice-worthiness across theories whose scales are not obviously commensurable — but nobody has pushed them on the prior question of whether such uncertainty exists at all. I want the questions to engage the internal structure of my guest's position rather than invite her to expound it.

Her position, developed across several papers, is this. Moral truths are knowable a priori in the sense that anyone who fully grasps the relevant non-moral facts and the moral concepts, and whose attention is not distorted by interest or emotion, believes them. It follows that there is no such thing as uncertainty whose object is a moral proposition: no credence-like state of the kind we have about the weather or about a coin. What presents itself as moral uncertainty is always one of three things — uncertainty about descriptive facts, uncertainty about one's own attitudes or emotional responses, or what she calls a blind spot, a local failure to see something one is perfectly positioned to see. Blind spots, she argues, are not degrees of belief: they do not update smoothly on evidence, they do not license betting behavior, and they cannot enter deliberation as probabilities. From this she draws two further claims. First, the apparatus of maximizing expected choice-worthiness is built on a category mistake, since it treats a defect of attention as if it were a probability. Second, blame for serious moral error is not a matter of unlucky credences but of culpable failures of attention, which are themselves assessable in the way we assess negligence. She is well known for the fact that every apparent counterexample to her view gets reclassified into one of her three routes, and she does this quickly and without embarrassment.

Requirements for the questions:

- Each question should be speakable aloud: one or two sentences, with at most a brief clause of setup. No mini-essays, and no question that is really an argument against her in disguise. Where a question does load a premise, tell me it is loaded so I know what I am conceding by asking it.
- Each question must be accompanied by a short note stating the answer she is most likely to give, and by one follow-up keyed to that predicted answer. Follow-ups must be self-contained, because in a live setting I will rarely get a second follow-up.
- At least one question each on the following: a surgeon-style case in which the state she calls a blind spot seems to change what the agent should do even though the agent cannot say what she is uncertain about; disagreement with a reflective, sincere, and philosophically competent opponent who denies what she takes to be self-evident; moral deference, that is, why deferring to a better judge on a hard case can be right and yet seem to cost the deferring agent something she cannot recover by being told the answer; the ordinary, non-monstrous historical wrongdoer who absorbed his community's norms without any obvious distortion, and whether her view commits her to saying he knew better; whether maximizing expected choice-worthiness is on her account nonsense or a useful fiction, and if a useful fiction, in what sense there is no moral uncertainty; uncertainty involving concepts that are partly normative, such as sentience, personhood, or what counts as serious harm, where the descriptive facts are agreed on all sides; and the mechanism question — what observation, in principle available to someone outside her theory, would distinguish a blind spot from a low credence.
- In addition, include one question that stipulates the descriptive facts as fully known and the agent as attentive as she could possibly be, so that only the blind-spot route remains available to her, and presses on what is left to explain the disagreement; and one question asking what a genuine case of moral uncertainty would look like on her account, and whether there is any possible evidence that would show her view to be false.
- Finish with a question about what she tells an agent who must decide today and is, on her own diagnosis, in the grip of a blind spot: what is the decision rule, and does it not quietly reintroduce the probabilities she has ruled out.
- Nothing biographical, nothing she could answer from her curriculum vitae, and nothing that lets her restate the thesis in new words. You may describe well-known arguments and cases, but do not attribute claims to named contemporary philosophers or quote from anyone's work.

After the questions, add a closing paragraph of your own, addressed to me rather than to the audience. Say which of the ten questions you expect to fail and why — which one she can dissolve with a distinction I have not anticipated, or which one turns out to be a verbal dispute once she answers it. Say what you think her single most effective reply to the whole set would be. Then say what would change your mind about the design: what information about her actual commitments, if I sent it to you before the recording, would lead you to reorder, rewrite, or drop questions, and which of your predicted answers you are least confident in. Be explicit about any place where your rendering of her view risks being uncharitable or is quietly a strawman, since I will be asking her to answer the version you have written, not the version she holds.
