Consider the blindsight literature that began in the mid-1970s: patients blind in half the visual field after damage to primary visual cortex who deny seeing anything there, yet — pressed to guess — locate stimuli presented to the blind field and discriminate their orientation and motion well above chance, without confidence and without spontaneous use of the information, and on some readings without any experience at all. Critics have argued that the same data show degraded conscious vision rather than its absence. Analyze this episode through the conceivability argument for philosophical zombies against physicalism, running the analysis in both directions.

Be disciplined about the mapping. A zombie is stipulated to be functionally and physically identical to its conscious twin while lacking experience; a blindsight patient is identical in neither respect. What, then, can the argument claim from a partial dissociation rather than a full duplication: does an actual instance of visual information guiding behaviour without phenomenology strengthen the conceivability premise, leave it untouched because the functional profile is wrong, or weaken it by suggesting that experience tracks some specifiable functional condition such as integration, global availability, or reportability? And what does the episode show about the argument itself — a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case it never anticipated? Say plainly where it falls silent, above all on the criterion problem, where the patient's own denial of experience is the only evidence either way.
