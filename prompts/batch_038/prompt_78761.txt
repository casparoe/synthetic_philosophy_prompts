Hey — I'm a features writer, mostly science-and-ideas stuff for a general interest mag. I'm two thirds through a piece on why the idea of a rules-based international order keeps colliding with the fact that nobody can actually enforce it, and Hegel has ended up as my villain. Before I file I want to check I haven't built a strawman out of a guy I last read properly in a survey course.

Here's the chain as it currently stands in my draft:

- In the Philosophy of Right, in the war section (I think it's §324 and the remarks attached to it), Hegel says something to the effect that a people living in permanent peace would go rotten, the way the sea goes foul if the winds stop moveing it. So on my reading he thinks states have a standing reason to fight every so often, and that a state which never goes to war is failing at something ethical, not merely being imprudent.
- He also says states stand to each other the way individuals stand to each other in a state of nature, with no judge and no sheriff above them. So there are no real constraints on what one state may do to another — conquest, annexation, whatever works. World history is the world's court of judgment (a line I'm told he lifted from a Schiller poem), which on my reading means the winner was right, full stop.
- And the whole thing is just the master-slave business from the Phenomenology scaled up to states: two self-consciousnesses only get recognition from each other by staking thier lives, so a treaty can never be more than a temporary truce between powers waiting for the next round.

Which gives me my kicker: a Hegelian state has no principled reason to respect anything resembling the laws of armed conflict, and the entire post-1945 legal apparatus is, in Hegelian terms, a polite fiction with a flag on it. The Hegel scholar I interviewed pushed back hard and said I'd collapsed two different books into one argument, but he explained it in a register that went straight over my head and I want a second opinion.

Two things I'd most like you to weigh in on:

1. Does Hegel actually think war is instrumentally good for a nation's health — i.e. an empirical claim about stagnation and morale that you could in principle go and test on the historical record? Or is the wind-and-sea business doing something else entirely, in which case what exactly is the claim, and does it license anything like my "states should pick fights" reading?
2. Does the no-sheriff-above-states point commit him to no limits on conduct in war? I keep suspecting there's a gap between "there is no power that can make a state keep its treaties" and "states owe each other nothing," and that Hegel says something about wars between states that recognize one another being conducted differently from wars of extermination — but I've lost the note and I may be remembering something I wanted to be true. Please tell me if I am.

And one modern case for my sidebar that Hegel obviously never faced: a small state that hasn't fought a war in two centuries because it sits under a bigger power's nuclear umbrella, so its citizens are never in fact called on to stake their lives for it. Under the reading my interviewee was gesturing at — the one where war is the moment at which private property and individual life stop presenting themselves as the final authority and show themselves as subordinate to the state — does that country lose something, keep something, or is the question malformed? I'd much rather you say it's malformed and explain why than manufacture an answer.
