I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for months I have been explaining the same dispute to readers in the same clumsy way: whether the design of a technology can itself constitute a political act. Most readers arrive convinced the answer is obviously no — people have politics, objects don't — and then they encounter Langdon Winner's essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?", with its Long Island overpasses built to specifications that discouraged buses from reaching a celebrated public beach, and its pneumatic molding machines installed in a Chicago reaper works in the 1880s at a cost that made no economic sense unless the point was the organized men they displaced. Rather than make the argument again in my own voice, I want to satirize what happens when the thesis is absorbed by institutions that have to operationalize it.

Please write a parody of a draft municipal procurement standard, roughly 750 words, formatted as numbered clauses with a definitions section and a short annex of worked examples. It is issued by an invented body — something on the order of a Joint Committee on the Political Certification of Procured Objects — and it requires that every article a city purchases file a statement of its political orientation before it may be installed on public property. The register should be flat, procedural, and entirely sincere: scope, definitions, scoring rubric, evidence requirements, appeals, review cycle. No winking, no authorial aside, no explanatory footnote telling the reader what is being mocked.

The humor has to come from the committee taking the idea seriously and administratively. In particular, I'd like the piece to dramatize — without ever naming — several of the problems specialists actually fight over:

- The rubric must distinguish objects that are political because someone decided they should be, with the decision on the record, from objects that are political because of the social arrangements they require in order to operate at all: centralized, hierarchical, expert-staffed, difficult to shut down. These earn different scores, and the annex should show a rooftop solar array and a transformer substation receiving visibly different treatment for reasons the form never quite articulates.

- There must be a field for documented design intent, with a clause instructing the assessor that where intent cannot be established from the record, "indeterminate" is to be entered and may not be inferred from the object's effects. Include at least one annex entry in which the effects are undisputed, the intent is not, and the form handles the situation badly.

- The neutral option must turn out to be unavailable. Somewhere in the definitions, an object certified as having no political orientation is thereby certified as endorsing the arrangement already in force, so that neutrality acquires a score of its own.

- The politics of an object shift with who maintains it, whether spare parts arrive, and whether the shift is staffed. The form has no field for this, and the annex should exhibit the resulting absurdity.

- By its own scope clause the standard must certify itself. Give us the committee's minute on that problem.

- One entry should concern a pedestrian crossing button that is not connected to anything.

- Somewhere near the end, responsibility should migrate quietly from a person to a thing — a clause in which the object is recorded as having declined a citizen's request. This should be the darkest note in the piece, delivered in the same bureaucratic monotone as everything else.

Constraints: no real living people, no real agencies or vendors, and nothing that requires the reader to have read Winner to follow the joke. The target is the position and the compliance genre that has adopted it, not any individual. Close on an administrative detail — a comment deadline, a next meeting date, the standard's own revision number — and let the reader do the recoiling.
