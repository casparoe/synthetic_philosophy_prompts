I want one problem-style question on Good's theorem, the result that a costless observation is never expected to leave you worse off as long as you'll re-maximize on what you learn. What I'm trying to kill is the reflex answer: more evidence is always valuable, so run the study.

My rough setup, clean it up however you like: an agency has to adopt one of two regulations by Friday. It can run a study at zero budget cost, results in hand Thursday, and it will certainly choose by maximizing expected utility given whatever it then knows. But once run, the study is public record, and one of the two regulations is only politically viable while nobody knows what the study found. So Thursday's act-set isnt Wednesday's act-set.

Deliverables: the exam question itself (add whatever numbers you need to make the expected utilities determinate), a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme out of 25 that says precisely what separates an A from a B. I want the scheme to catch the two mistakes I keep grading — reading "zero budget cost" as "zero cost" full stop, and concluding that the case refutes Good's theorem rather than that one of its hypotheses fails in the case. For the second, name which hypothesis each wrong move is dropping: fixed act-set, fixed utility function, conditionalization, or whatever else you think is doing the work.

Write it in American English. And at the end give me your own confidence level in the diagnosis the marking scheme is built around, plus the single claim in it you'd least want held against you.
