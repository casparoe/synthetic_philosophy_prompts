I supervise an eligibility unit in a county human services office, and for most of the past two years my week has been spent reviewing whether clients have satisfied the work requirements attached to their cash assistance and, where they have not, signing the paperwork that reduces or ends the grant. Three weeks ago I approved a sanction against a mother whose child has a significant disability; the underlying failure was a missed appointment she had explained in a voicemail that nobody logged. I have not stopped thinking about it. In the spring our office runs a half-day ethics training for new eligibility staff, and I have been asked to give one session. I want to build it around A Christmas Carol, because everyone in the room already knows the story and it costs me nothing to get them inside it — but I want to use it honestly, and I am no longer sure that I can.

Here is the reading I have sketched, and I would like you to test it rather than flatter it. When the two collectors call on Scrooge in the first stave, he does not refuse them in the manner of a man who believes the poor undeserving; he asks whether the prisons and the union workhouses are still in operation and whether the treadmill and the Poor Law remain in force, and he answers, in effect, that the state has already provided an institution and that anyone unwilling to enter it cannot really need it. That is, as I understand it, precisely the logic of the workhouse test that the Poor Law Amendment Act of 1834 was built on, and of the principle behind it that Edwin Chadwick's commission articulated and historians call less eligibility: that the condition of the pauper inside should be less desirable than that of the lowest-paid independent labourer outside, so that only the genuinely destitute would apply. On this reading Scrooge is administering the screening procedure privately, and the book's cruelst line — his suggestion that those who would rather die should do so and "decrease the surplus population" — is the screening procedure with its euphemism stripped away.

If that is right, the ending worries me rather than reassures me. The conversion does not appear to abolish the test of deservingness; it privatizes it. The judgement survives, but it is now exercised by one man's reading of character instead of by a commission, and it is passed or failed according to whether the applicant is cheerful, grateful, and pleasant to look upon. Three questions follow, and I would like each taken seriously rather than smoothed over.

First: is Bob Cratchit constructed as deserving precisely by being the kind of poor man who does not complain? His household is pious, grateful, and festive in destitution, and Tiny Tim's death is averted by a rich man's change of heart. Does the book have any place at all for the ungrateful, the unattractive, the addict, the person who is rude to the person holding the file — or does its sentimental machinery exist to make the deserving/undeserving distinction available to the reader in the first place? Relatedly, what should I make of the two wretched children the Ghost of Christmas Present produces from beneath his robe, whom he names Ignorance and Want and warns Scrooge to beware, saying that doom is written on the boy's brow unless the writing is erased? That looks like a structural indictment no personal conversion could answer. Does it defeat my thesis, or is it ornament the plot never cashes out?

Second: does anything in the book amount to an institutional alternative rather than a converted individual? I keep coming back to the moment readers usually glide past as sentiment — Scrooge raising Cratchit's wages. An employer changing pay is not the same act as an employer giving alms, and it may be the only structural thing in the book. Does that rescue the ending from being a purely charitable solution, or is a discretionary raise still discretion, with all the arbitrariness and humiliation that discretion implies for the person on the receiving end of it? And is Scrooge's nephew the real alternative, given that his defence of Christmas turns on treating people below you as "fellow-passengers to the grave, and not another race of creatures bound on other journeys"? If that is the book's thesis, then the error it identifies is not a miscalculation about who deserves what but the prior move of classifying a category of persons as another kind of creature — which is a much harder thing to build an eligibility system on.

Third: where does the book simply refuse a policy reading? It is a ghost story about a man confronting his own death, and at some point my framing may be doing violence to it. I want the honest critic's answer, including if that answer is that this is the wrong book for my session and that I should choose something else.

What I most want to know is what the book shows about the experience of being assessed that the technical literature on conditionality and sanctions does not. The person across from me is being read as a moral specimen before she is being read as a case. And the book does something the administrative literature never does: it gives the assessor a causal history — his schooling, his apprenticeship, the woman he lost — as an account of how he became a man capable of signing such a page. Is that a genuine insight about discretion, or is it an excuse dressed as psychology?

Throughout, please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this material — the history of the 1834 settlement, Dickens's own politics, and the critical debate over the book. I am aware that scholars disagree about whether Dickens is attacking political economy or only unkindness; do not let a contested reading travel as though it were the consensus, and tell me where your own confidence is low. If I have garbled any of the history above, correct me before you build on it. Please write in American English, and please do not send me references or a reading list — I want your reading.
