A reading group has spent a year on Kyburg's lottery paradox and Makinson's preface paradox, and its two camps have been arguing past each other rather than at each other. The first, call it the Threshold camp, holds that to believe outright just is to assign a credence above a contextually fixed threshold, and therefore that a perfectly rational agent's belief set may be jointly inconsistent: what the two paradoxes show, on this view, is that consistency is not a requirement of rational belief. The second, the Cogency camp, holds that rational outright belief must be jointly consistent and closed under obvious consequence, and therefore that the threshold premise is precisely where the error lies.

Draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration ought to have produced — a document both camps could sign without wincing. It should contain:

1. A numbered section of agreed claims: what both accept about the two cases as normally described; what both accept about the relation between credence and outright belief that falls short of the disputed premise; what both regard as desiderata on any solution; and which candidate solutions both jointly reject as cheats, with the reason each rejection is not merely partisan (for instance, whether advising the agent to believe nothing outright, or to eliminate outright belief in favor of degrees of confidence, counts as a solution or as a change of subject).

2. A disagreements table. For each live disagreement, give a neutral statement, then the statement as the Threshold camp would phrase it, then as the Cogency camp would phrase it, such that each side recognizes its own position in its own column.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions whose resolution would move at least one side. Address at least: (a) whether deductive cogency does any work that credence alone cannot do, given what belief is for (practical reasoning, assertion, memory, planning); (b) whether a threshold can be fixed non-arbitrarily — high enough to exclude each individual lottery proposition, low enough to include ordinary empirical beliefs; and (c) whether the two paradoxes stand or fall together.

For crux (c), have the collaboration work with the following framework rather than trading intuitions. The two standard cases differ along two independent dimensions: whether the premise that something must give is known or only highly probable, and whether the individual beliefs rest on one common statistical source or on many independent ones. That leaves four corners, of which the standard cases occupy only two: the lottery is the known-and-common-source corner, since it is known that some ticket will win and the evidence for each individual belief is a single uniform distribution; the preface is the probable-and-independent corner, since each claim was checked on its own merits and the expectation of error rests on track record. Have the document specify the other two corners and pre-register each camp's predictions about them:

- The raffle whose prize may go unclaimed, where the draw can fail or the winning ticket need not have been sold, so that believing of every ticket that it will lose is jointly consistent even though each belief remains only highly probable. Does a paradox survive, and if so which one?
- The manuscript whose author has been told by an editor she has no reason to doubt that exactly one claim in it is false, though the editor will not say which, so that the expectation of error is no longer merely probable but known while the individual claims still rest on independent checking. Does the preface now have the shape of the lottery, and does that change what a solution must do?

Add one further test under this heading: replace the preface book's thousand independently checked claims with a single thousand-step argument in which each step is highly probable given its predecessor. Ask whether evidential independence is doing any real work in the paradox, and record what each camp predicts about whether the chained manuscript generates the same difficulty, a worse one, or none at all.

For every crux, name the case, argument, or piece of evidence both camps agree would bear on it, write down each side's prediction in advance of the discussion, and state what each side would accept as refuting its own position.

4. A short closing section identifying whatever part of the dispute is merely verbal — in particular whether belief, acceptance, and high credence are being used interchangeably across the two columns — and stipulating the vocabulary the joint statement itself adopts, so that neither camp finds its view described in the other's terminology.
