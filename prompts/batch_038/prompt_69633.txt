I run a weekly reading group that's half philosophy grad students and half ML engineers who build classifiers, and we keep having the same unproductive argument: whether a model can "know" anything. Before we get anywhere near that I want to sort people by what they actually believe about knowledge and closure, because I'm pretty sure most of them are in different camps and don't realize it.

So: build me a decision tree, rooted in case intuitions, that lands someone on a named position in the closure/tracking debate. Some constraints:

- Every branch node has to be a concrete case or a yes/no claim about one, answerable by someone who's never seen the literature. No node like "do you accept closure under known entailment?" — they get sorted by their gut reactions and only told afterward which doctrine those reactions add up to.
- Work your way through the zebra-at-the-museum case, a fake-barns style county, a stopped clock, a grandmother-type case where the subject's belief would have been false but the surroundings would have hidden that, and at least one case where the method actually used is reliable but the subject would easily have switched to a bad one if it hadn't been available. Make that last one up yourself; my version is: Maya checks a trustworthy news app every morning, had it been down she'd have read a satire site instead, today it's up and she forms a true belief. I want that case doing real structural work in the tree, not parked off to the side, because its the fault line in my group — whether you hold the method fixed is basically whether you evaluate a classifier on the process it actually ran or on its whole design space.
- Leaves should be actual positions: closure-denying relevant alternatives, tracking with method-relativity, safety-based neo-Mooreanism, contextualism, the interest-relative/pragmatist view, plain skepticism, and whatever else you think the space forces on you.
- Build the logical space first: list the questions and the combinations of answers they permit, mark the combinations nobody occupies, and say for each empty cell whether it's empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody has gotten there yet.
- Flag every pair of leaves your tree can't really distingush, and say what extra case would separate them — or admit if maybe none would.
- Since half my group cares: mark which leaves could in principle be occupied by something that isn't a person, and which ones quietly assume a subject who can choose methods.
- Finish with a scoring sheet: given someone's answers at the nodes, which leaf they're at, plus the one-line diagnosis you'd give them.

Eight to ten nodes, leaves named, no reading list — this is a whiteboard thing.
