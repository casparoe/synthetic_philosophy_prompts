A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about Clever Hans, and neither of us put the disagreement clearly. I'd like it reconstructed properly. The theory in play is sphexishness as Dennett uses it: the digger wasp that stings and paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the mouth of its burrow, leaves it on the threshold while it goes inside to inspect, comes out — and if the cricket has been moved a few inches during that inspection, hauls it back to the threshold and repeats the whole routine, indefinitely, never simply picking the prey up and carrying it in. Sphexishness is supposed to be the thing free will is the absence of: not mechanism as such, but a routine that keeps executing after the world has changed in a way that makes it pointless. My friend holds that Hans is a paradigm sphex; I hold that he is not, and that the theory has nothing to say about him.

Take these as given rather than trying to reconstruct them. Hans belonged to Wilhelm von Osten, a German mathematics teacher who toured him without charging admission; a commission appointed in 1904, including a veterinarian and the director of the Berlin zoological gardens, examined him and concluded that no trickery was involved. Carl Stumpf's assistant Oskar Pfungst then ran controlled tests: other questioners besides von Osten, the horse isolated from spectators, blinders varied so that Hans sometimes could not see the questioner, and trials in which the questioner did not know the answer. Hans answered correctly roughly 89 percent of the time when he could see a knowledgeable questioner and about 6 percent of the time when he could not; the operative cue was an involuntary build-up of postural tension in the questioner as the hoof-taps approached the right number, released at the final tap. Pfungst then reversed the roles, playing the horse himself with human subjects concentrating on a number, and found he could detect their answers from a slight upward jerk of the head they could not feel themselves making. Von Osten was never persuaded and went on exhibiting Hans.

I want the mapping done in both directions. Where is the rigidity actually located — in the horse, in the horse-plus-questioner pair, or nowhere at all? Hans handled novel problems from unfamiliar people across arithmetic, calendars, spelling, and musical intervals, which looks like substantial behavioral flexibility and generalization; say what sphexishness can and cannot absorb that fact. Then tell me what Pfungst's reversal does to the criterion: if the same cue-reading signature turns up in a self-conscious adult human whom nobody would deny free will, is sphexishness a property of behavior or of internal architecture, and if the latter, could a method that manipulates only externally observable conditions ever have detected it? Finally: who in this episode does the theory actually pick out as sphexish, is the double-blind norm that grew out of the case best read as an institutional prosthesis for flexibility individuals demonstrably lack, and at what point does the theory go silent rather than merely strained? I would sooner be shown that my friend is right than be reassured.
