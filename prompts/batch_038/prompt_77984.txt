You are a professional philosopher specializing in moral responsibility and the justification of punishment. Please work through the candidate principles explicitly before committing to a prediction, and be candid about where the record is genuinely ambiguous rather than forcing a tidy answer.

A colleague of mine is writing about whether the retributivist notion of just deserts can be saved, and she has given me her verdicts on four cases. I want to reconstruct the principle she is actually applying, and to predict what consistency commits her to on a fifth.

Her record:

1. A mid-level manager who overbilled clients systematically for six years — sound mind, comfortable upbringing, caught by a routine audit. Fully blameworthy; a substantial custodial sentence is what he deserves.
2. A man with untreated paranoid schizophrenia who assaults the neighbor he is convinced is poisoning him. Not blameworthy in the ordinary sense; any confinement must be justified as treatment or prevention, never as something he deserves.
3. A nineteen-year-old raised amid chronic violence and neglect who commits armed robbery. Blameworthy but heavily mitigated — roughly a third of the sentence the manager's offense would merit, on the grounds that "he had far less to work with."
4. A man in his fifties with no prior history who begins abusing a child; imaging shows a right orbitofrontal tumor, which is resected, after which the urges and the conduct cease entirely. Not blameworthy; no punishment, though supervision until stability is confirmed is acceptable.

She calls herself a compatibilist of some kind but will not say whether she thinks desert is basic, and she keeps citing Dennett on the one hand and Caruso on the other as the two poles she is trying to sit between.

New case. A former accountant ran an embezzlement scheme twenty-two years ago that wiped out the retirement savings of some forty people. She was fully competent, knew precisely what she was doing, and had an unremarkable upbringing. She was never charged — the scheme surfaced only last year, in the course of an unrelated investigation. In the two decades since, she has made complete restitution out of her own earnings, has no subsequent offense of any kind, and every actuarial and clinical assessment now in hand puts her risk of reoffending at effectively zero. She does not disown the past conduct; on my colleague's reading of the transcript, the person who did it is psychologically continuous with the person who would now face sentencing.

Please deliver four things. First, lay out the candidate rules that are jointly consistent with verdicts 1–4 — at minimum a Dennett-style account on which responsibility tracks graded normative competency, a Caruso-style skepticism that abandons basic desert but retains punishment on quarantine or public-health lines, and a moderate reasons-responsiveness account — plus any further rule you think fits the record better, and say what each rule must make of verdict 3, which I suspect is doing more work than it looks. Second, state what each rule predicts for the new case, separately as to her culpability rating and as to her recommendation about punishment. Third, give your single best guess at her verdict, explaining which rule you favor and what in the record tips you toward it. Fourth, if the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, name one further case that would most sharply separate your top two rules, and say what each rule would say about it.
