Six months into serving as the patient representative on the ethics committee of the regional hospital where I work as a records clerk, I have been asked to help draft a single page stating when a patient in our building is to be considered dead. I have no training in medicine or philosophy. What little reading I have done on my own began after my mother was declared dead on neurological grounds in an intensive care unit while her chest went on rising and falling under a ventilator, her skin stayed warm, and the nurse spoke of her as though she might still hear me; I have never been able to reconcile the declaration with what I saw. The page we are writing has to cover both routes used here: patients whose brains have ceased to work while the heart is kept beating by a machine, and the two or three patients a year from whom we retrieve organs once the heart has stopped on its own and will not be restarted.

What I want from you is not a defence of any one standard but the specification that any candidate standard must meet before it deserves to be argued about — the list of requirements, each with the case or consideration that makes it a requirement. To save you repeating my own errors back to me, here is what I have come up with so far, and I would like to be told plainly which of these are genuine requirements and which are prejudices I have picked up from my mother's bedside: the standard must be applicable by one clinician at the bedside using nothing more elaborate than a torch and a stethoscope; it must pick out a moment rather than a stretch of time; it must yield the same answer whether or not the family has consented to donation, since otherwise we would be choosing when someone dies; it must not be overturned by the arrival of some machine that could have kept the body going longer; it must be statable in language a twelve-year-old in the waiting room could follow; and, in a formulation I cannot yet sharpen, the body must have stopped being one thing and become a collection of parts each of which is merely still working — I have been calling this the orchestra test, on the thought that an orchestra has stopped even while the instruments are still making noise, and I want to know whether that is a real principle or a comfortable metaphor that hides exactly where the difficulty lies. Add whatever requirements I have missed, in particular those arising from the fact that a body meeting our neurological standard can go on digesting food, healing surgical wounds, mounting a fever against infection, growing, and in reported cases sustaining a pregnancy for weeks, and from the fact that the same page must license us to remove organs from a body that is warm and perfused.

Then do the three things I cannot do. First, tell me which pairs of requirements cannot be satisfied together — I suspect at least that a standard must fix a determinate moment and must also track the loss of whatever it is about a human being that makes their death a loss, and that these come apart in the patient who has been in an unresponsive state for a year and in the one whose heart has stopped for four minutes and might yet be brought back; and I keep circling a further worry, that our draft says the cessation of function must be irreversible when what we can actually establish at the bedside is only that it will not be reversed, because we have decided not to try, and those look like the same word doing very different work. Second, say which single requirement excludes the largest number of otherwise plausible standards, since that is the one we should argue about longest in the meeting. Third, briefly and without a scorecard, say which requirement each of the three standards in circulation — whole-brain cessation, the loss of the capacities that make a person, and the permanent cessation of circulation and respiration — gives up, and tell me how much of the entire list is driven by transplant practice: if our hospital never retrieved an organ, which requirements would drop away, and would that leave us with a more honest page or a less usable one?
