I am three-quarters through a novel whose moral engine is the two ways a hospital can declare a person dead, and I have come to the point where what I need is a document rather than more reading.

Two chapters run in parallel. In the first, a nineteen-year-old woman is pulled from a cold lake, suffers an anoxic injury, fails apnea testing on the third day, and is declared dead by neurologic criteria; her heart is beating; her liver and kidneys go to two recipients; her mother spends the remainder of the book convinced she has buried a living girl. In the second, a fifty-eight-year-old man with a devastating but non-qualifying brain injury is taken to an operating room, extubated at his wife's request, stops breathing, loses his pulse, is observed for five minutes, and procurement begins. The novel's claim is that the two families undergo the same thing while the institution treats the two deaths as different in kind. My beta readers split on whether that claim survives contact with the medicine and the philosophy, and I have concluded that what would settle it is the text the two camps I have been reading would produce if they sat down together in good faith.

So please draft that text: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between, on one side, a defender of current practice who holds that death by neurologic criteria is genuine death — the organism as a whole has irreversibly ceased to function, and the ventilator is doing work the body can no longer do for itself — and who holds that the circulatory pathway, standoff interval included, is equally sound; and, on the other side, a critic who holds that neither determination establishes what it purports to establish: that the brain-dead patient is a living human being who has irreversibly lost the capacity for consciousness; that the chronic cases, in which brain-destroyed children have gone on for years on ventilation while healing wounds, regulating temperature, growing, and mounting immune responses, falsify the loss-of-integrative-unity story; and that the dead donor rule is therefore doing covert normative work in the place where it presents itself as reporting a biological fact.

I want the document itself — not a verdict on who is right, and not a dramatized exchange. Please give me:

1. An agreed-claims section, numbered. This should include what the model statute's two clauses actually require and what they leave open; what the clinical examinations establish and what they do not; whether the neurologic and circulatory pathways are one determination under two descriptions or two determinations with different content; what status each camp assigns to the patient in a persistent vegetative state, and where that exposes a divergence they have been reluctant to state; and what each side concedes about the other's strongest case. One wrinkle to build in: my drowning patient would have to be rewarmed and cleared of confounding factors before neurologic testing could proceed at all. I was told this by a reader who is a neurologist, and I want to know whether that constraint, properly stated, is itself something both camps would sign, and what it does to their dispute about what the tests show.

2. A disagreements table, with these columns: the issue; the formulation Camp A would sign as a statement of its own position; the formulation Camp B would sign as a statement of its own position; whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal; the crux to which it reduces; the test, argument, or case both accept as bearing on that crux; and each side's prediction about the outcome of that test, recorded in advance.

3. A cruxes section. I suspect at least three: whether "irreversible" means could-not-be-restored-by-any-available-means or will-not-be-restored-given-the-decision-not-to-try; whether the organism-as-a-whole criterion does independent work or merely redescribes the bedside tests that were used to operationalize it; and whether the standoff interval is justified by the absence of documented spontaneous return of circulation or by the intention not to resuscitate, those being different kinds of justification. For each crux, state which camp's position would be damaged by which finding, and by how much. Add any crux I have missed that you consider more central than these.

4. A short section identifying which parts of the dispute are merely verbal, with your reasons, and saying what is left over once the verbal part is removed.

5. A line-by-line assessment of five sentences from the manuscript, stating for each whether both parties could sign it, only one could, or neither could because it is simply confused: "She is dead, and she is warm." "We waited five minutes because we had already decided not to start again." "He isn't dead; he's finished." "The machine is breathing, and nothing is being breathed for." "If he had refused the ventilator an hour earlier and died in exactly this way, we would have called it death, and we would have been right."

I would rather have a joint statement with three honest gaps in it than one with forty comfortable agreements. Where you judge that no claim can be signed by both parties, write that no joint claim is available and say which side refuses and why, rather than softening the sentence until it commits nobody. And if any part of my request rests on a confusion of mine — if the parallel between the two chapters is a false symmetry because the two determinations are answering different questions, or if asking these two camps for a joint statement is incoherent because one of them denies that there is a fact of the matter about which either could be jointly wrong — say so plainly at the top, explain the confusion, and tell me what I should ask for instead. Do not hand me a polished document in order to please me.
