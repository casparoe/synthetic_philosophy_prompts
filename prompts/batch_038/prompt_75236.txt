So I run a monthly argument-night with a few friends — one of them does a PhD adjacent to decision theory, another works in climate policy — and this month we each had to write down our actual position on individual action and collective harm so the rest could take it apart. Here's mine. I'd like to know whether it hangs together before Saturday, and I'd rather have the audit than a lecture on the literature.

(1) I'm a strict vegetarian and I don't fly, and the reason I give people isn't symbolic. Markets are lumpy: a store restocks chicken in batches, a processor fills an order in thousands of birds, so any single purchase carries some small probability of triggering a large order. Probability times magnitude gives a real expected harm, so abstaining actually reduces expected suffering rather than just expressing disapproval. (2) That said, I fully concede that in the actual world my not buying that pack of thighs changes nothing — the bird gets raised and killed either way, and I don't pretend otherwise. One of my friends says (1) and (2) can't both be true, that if I admit the actual sequence is untouched I've quietly admitted my abstention is a gesture with a fancy story attached. I don't know that he's right, but I can't say why cleanly. (3) I also reject the idea that an act becomes permissible just because it makes no perceptible difference: if a thousand people each turn a dial up one notch and the result is agony, nobody gets off with the claim that their own turn was imperceptible. The invisibility of my contribution doesn't launder it. (4) On climate, though, I think the individual-emissions frame is a mistake. One person's driving and heating makes no difference worth the name, so I put my effort into political organizing and donations instead, and I think telling people to take shorter showers is counterproductive — it lets them feel they've done something and takes pressure off the levers that matter. Depending on the day I either say the individual difference is negligible next to what policy could achieve or that there just isn't one. (5) On voting: I do vote, but I explicitly deny that the tiny-chance-of-being-decisive argument is a good reason to. The chance my ballot flips anything is something like one in tens of millions, and I think multiplying a number that small by anything is motivated arithmetic. I vote because it's how I take part in a shared practice and because I'd rather be the sort of person who votes. (6) Underneath all of this I'm a consequentialist about the bottom line: what makes an act right is its expected good consequences, and I give animal welfare real weight, not token weight. (7) I also think cost matters — the vegetarianism costs me essentially nothing at this point, so it's easy — but I don't think morality can demand that I torch my career or my relationships, and once the cost to me gets high I think I'm permitted to stop. (8) The line I draw between (1) and (5) is that the probabilities just aren't comparable: there are a few thousand units between me and the next bulk order, and there are tens of millions of voters between me and a flipped election. So its not that I apply expected-value reasoning differently in the two cases, it's that the inputs are wildly different.

Go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and how badly, ranked by severity rather than dumped on me as equally bad. For each real tension, say what would have to give and what the cheapest revision is, and then tell me which single change would clean up the most. I'm fairly sure at least one of the things that looks like a tension here isn't one at all, so please flag those separately and say why they dissolve — I'd like to be able to answer the objection in (2) rather than just stop making it. And be careful about epistemic status: there's a lot of live disagreement about whether indivdual acts matter in cases like these, so I want you to separate what's basically settled among people who work on this from what's genuinely contested, and not serve me one contested line as if it were the consensus. Where you think a tension is real but the standard reply to it is decent, say so and give the reply; where you're unsure whether something on my list is a substantive commitment or just sloppy phrasing on my part, ask me instead of auditing the sloppiness.
