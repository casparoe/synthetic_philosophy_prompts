Background: I spent thirty-eight years in a state lab doing ecotoxicology, mostly freshwater, mostly the uncharismatic invertebrates nobody fundraises for. Retired four years ago, started reading philosophy to have something to do, and I've gotten properly stuck on the precautionary principle. Partly because people keep asking me about it — I sit on a county advisory board now and the moment something uncertain comes up someone says "well, you're the scientist, does precaution apply here?" and I have no good answer except gut feel.

So let me lay out the gut feel and see if it adds up to anything.

A company applies to register a fungicide. Good mammalian data, good fish data, no amphibian data whatsoever, and the application area is frog country. I say make them run the amphibian study before anything is permitted. No hesitation.

Same shape of ignorance, different chemical. A compound whose breakdown pathway you can work out from the chemistry, and where measured concentrations in the watershed come in four orders of magnitude below anything that has ever produced an effect in anything. The dataset is thinner than the fungicide one. I say register it.

An invasive weed is already in the lake and spreading, and it will close out the fishery inside a decade. The only tool that works is a herbicide with real unknowns of its own. I say use it. Which looks like it contradicts the first case, except that doing nothing is a decision too.

A single creek population of a caddisfly, found nowhere else, would be permanently gone if we approve a diversion. Straightforwardly irreversible. I say that isn't a reason to stop the diversion, and I know how that sounds coming from me.

A contaminant that would sterilize the invertebrate community of a whole watershed, but where recolonisation from upstream refugia in thirty years is ecologically plausible. I say stop it — even though by the irreversibility column the caddisfly case looks worse and I let that one through.

Terminal illness, experimental drug, essentially no long-term data. I'd take it, and I'd let my wife take it, and I don't think that's weakness on my part.

A one-in-ten-million estimate that a treatment works up anoxic and kills a reservoir. Catastrophic if true. I say shrug and proceed, and I could not defend the ten million to you with a straight face.

The rough rule I've been carrying around is: act when you can't bound the downside and the price of buying information is small next to it; don't act when the inaction is itself the unbounded risk. But that misses the caddisfly entirely, and it doesn't say why the fungicide and the second chemical come out different when the second one has less data behind it. The Sunstein book pushed me the other way — towards thinking this whole principle is just regulatory panic dressed up — and maybe he's right, but he writes as though overreaction were the only failure mode available, which after thirty-eight years I know isn't true.

What I want: is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? State it precisely enough that I could hand it to the board without embarrassment — I want to know what the clauses are, which of my cases forces each clause in, and what it says about situations I haven't thought of yet. And tell me plainly which verdicts I have to give up if I want something simpler rather than something baroque. I'd rather lose one than carry around a rule with nine conditions.

Two extra things. First, a distinction from my working life that I'm not sure survives into the philosophy: there is a big difference between "we measured it and the confidence interval is wide" and "we have no idea what the mechanism even is." Both get filed under uncertainty, which always struck me as sloppy, and I'd like to know whether any respectable principle actually needs the difference or whether my intuitions are just tracking vividness.

Second, the case I keep flip flopping on, where I most want your principle's verdict. A chemical gets banned, and industry swaps in a close structural relative with no data at all on it. The definitive chronic study would take eight to ten years. In the meantime the substitute is in every stream in the state, and there's a real suspicion — nothing more — that it shares the mechanism we banned the first one for. Waiting costs us eight years of exposure we can't recover; acting now might cost us a compound that was perfectly fine and the benefits it was delivering. I've argued both sides of this out loud in the last year. Which way does your principle send me, and does it send me the same way if the suspicion of a shared mechanism is stronger?
