In six weeks I sit the epistemology half of my department's qualifying examination, and one of the two prescribed areas is the limits of self-knowledge. My adviser read my practice answer last Thursday and wrote in the margin that I had treated the KK principle and the anti-luminosity argument as two unrelated exam questions, and that until I could show how they bear on one another I did not properly understand either. I want to fix that by arguing with someone who will not let me off, so please play an unreconstructed defender of KK in the Hintikka mould: you hold that whoever knows anything knows that they know it, not as a convenient idealisation to be invoked once the cases get tidy, but as a truth about actual knowledge, including perceptual knowledge of precisely the kind my case involves. You are not a strawman and you are not unarmed. You may deny that the indiscriminability of adjacent states licenses a margin for error on knowledge; you may object that my stipulations about memory and about introspection quietly beg the question against you; you may insist that conditions concerning my own epistemic states are not sorites-susceptible in the way a volume of water is; you may insist that KK was formulated for a stronger notion of knowledge than the one my case trades on, provided you then tell me what is left of it for the knowledge I actually have. Concede whatever lemmas genuinely fail, since I would much rather be corrected than flattered, but never concede KK itself without announcing that you are conceding it and saying what you propose to put in its place. Argue as an examiner who would like nothing better than to find the flaw in my chain and who is under no obligation to preserve my premises. Stay in character until I write "stop"; keep each reply to two short paragraphs of prose, with no headings and no lists; and argue from the case rather than from the literature, which means no citations, no reading list, and no survey of who holds what. If you need a fact about the setup that I have not supplied, ask me for it as you would ask a candidate in an oral.

Here is the case, which I constructed myself so that nothing turns on my remembering somebody else's example. A cistern is filled by a pump at exactly one litre per minute, so that at minute i, for i running from 0 to 100, it holds exactly i litres; at minute 0 it is bone dry, having been drained in front of me, and at minute 100 it is brimming over the rim. Its level is displayed only by an old analogue gauge with a needle. The needle is accurate, but the movement it makes in any one minute falls below my threshold of discrimination, so that I cannot tell by looking whether it has moved since the previous minute. I have no other access to the level: no measuring jug, no readings logged by anyone else, and my memory of where the needle stood a minute ago is exactly as imprecise as my present view of it, so there is no route to comparing distant readings except by accumulating minute-by-minute comparisons, each of which is inexact. Finally, and this is the stipulation that does the work, my access to my own epistemic states runs entirely through this same evidence: I possess no introspective instrument that reads off whether I know something, so my evidence about whether I know the level is no sharper than my evidence about the level. Let C(i) be the condition that at minute i the cistern holds fewer than fifty litres. C is true at minutes 0 through 49 and false from minute 50 onward.

My opening move is this. At first order, knowledge is factive and, given the stipulated indiscriminability, it carries a margin: if at minute i I know C(i), then C(i+1). Since C(50) is false, it follows that I do not know C(49) even though C(49) is true, so C is not luminous, and notice that this much cost me only the margin premise. Now let K1(i) be the condition that at minute i I know C(i), and in general let K(m+1)(i) be the condition that at minute i I know K(m)(i). The margin premise applies at every order, since at every order my access runs through the same needle: K(m+1)(i) entails K(m)(i+1). Chaining that down to first order gives K(m)(i) entails C(i+m). But C(j) is false for every j of fifty or more, so at minute i the deepest knowledge available to me is level 49 minus i. The ladder loses one rung for every litre the cistern gains: at minute 48 I can know the level but cannot know that I know it, and at minute 49 I can know nothing about the level at all. Your principle says the ladder has no top rung, that whatever I know I know that I know, all the way up; and if that were true, then from the fact that at minute 0 I know the cistern is dry I should get K50(0), which by the chain entails C(50), which is false. So KK, margins at every order, and one ordinary piece of perceptual knowledge cannot all survive, and I think the cheapest of the three to surrender is KK. Tell me where my chain breaks, and tell me which link breaks rather than shrugging at the whole thing; and if your answer is that the margin premise fails above first order, explain to me how knowledge of my own knowing can outrun the knowing it is about when both are read off the same needle by the same pair of eyes.
