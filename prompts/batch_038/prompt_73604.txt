I'm auditing a philosophy-of-mind reading group that meets at the community college where my partner teaches, and last week we got onto Dennett's multiple drafts model. One of the regulars — a guy who writes a popular science newsletter, very smart, not a philosopher — explained it to the room with a movie theater picture he says he uses all the time. His version: consciousness used to be imagined as a cinema, one screen, a fixed showtime, and somebody sitting in the dark watching it; what Dennett does is turn the house lights on and reveal a multiplex with a dozen projectors running at once, no final cut, and nobody in the seats. So the theater is still there, you've just noticed there's no audience and no authoritative print. He put it to work twice: first, to say the hard problem is a hangover from that audience-seat picture and dissolves once you notice no one is watching; second, to say we should stop asking when or where a particular experience happens — his example was the colour-phi thing, where two dots of different colors flash one after the other and you seem to see a single dot traveling and changing color midway, which can't literally have been true at the time it seemed true.

The grad student running the session pushed back and said re-describing multiple drafts in theatrical terms at all is already a loss, because theater vocabulary comes with showtimes whether you want them or not: a projector, a print, a screen, a house — all of it presupposes that at some moment the content is fixed and inspectable, whether or not anyone is around to recieve it, and that's exactly the finish line the model is supposed to be denying. She also brought in the Orwellian/Stalinesque pair (as I understand it: either you genuinely had the misleading experience and your memory got rewritten afterwards, or the misleading version was assembled before it was ever available for report) and said the cinema picture makes it look like one of those has to be the true story, when the point is supposed to be that there's no fact of the matter about which. I can see both sides and I dont have the vocabulary to settle it, and we ran over by forty minutes.

So can you do the audit for me? I'd like the correspondences laid out explicitly — what plays the projector, the film stock, the screen, the seat, the audience, the showtime, the projectionist, the lobby — then where the mapping breaks and which features of a cinema the analogy quietly imports that the brain, on the multiple drafts picture, doesn't have. After that, separate the disanalogies that actually matter for what he's using it to argue from the ones that are just decorative: does the no-audience-so-no-hard-problem move survive once you list what the theater smuggles in, and does the stop-asking-when-it-happened conclusion follow from the analogy or from something independent that would still stand if the analogy were dropped? And if the grad student is right that the theatrical frame is fatally loaded, what would be a better analogy — is there one that carries the same content without handing you back a screening time? You don't need to cite anything, I'm not after a reading list, just the reasoning. But do tell me how confident you are in each verdict, and flag the single point you're least sure about — my guess is it'll be whether the cinema imagery is doing real damage or is just scaffolding you can kick away once the picture is standing.
