I spent thirty-seven years in a national measurement laboratory, most of them concerned with calibration hierarchies, uncertainty budgets, and interlaboratory comparisons, and I retired four years ago. Since retiring I have been reading philosophy — seriously, but with the handicap that I reach for instruments before I reach for arguments. I have become absorbed by Ian Hacking's work on the looping effects of classification, and I should like you to translate it into the vocabulary I actually think in, and then to report how much of it survives the journey.

My vocabulary is this: measurands and true values, traceability chains, open-loop as against closed-loop measurement, feedback and the back-action of a probe upon the system probed, drift, hysteresis, operating points and attractors, system identification under the constraint that the identification procedure perturbs the system being identified, and the distinction between error that can be budgeted and error that cannot. I am not fluent in the social-theoretical idiom in which Hacking is usually read, and I have no ambition to become so. Please do not write down to me on the technical side, and do not spare me on the philosophical side.

Three claims to translate, in order. First, that classifications of people interact with the people classified in such a way that the classification itself must be revised — a target that moves while one is aiming at it. Second, that new kinds of people come into existence at identifiable times and places, together with the institutions and practices that classify them; this is what he called dynamic nominalism. Third, that kinds of people differ in this respect from kinds of things, the latter being indifferent to how they are described, together with the use he makes of that difference to suggest that the familiar realism and anti-realism question is not quite the right question to ask about such kinds. If my gloss of the third claim is inaccurate, correct it before translating it.

Next, my own tentative glossary, which I want you to test rather than adopt. Looping is the closing of a loop: the classification acts as a sensor whose output is fed back into the system measured, so that measurement is closed-loop rather than open-loop. A new kind of person is that system driven to a new operating point or attractor by the feedback, rather than merely perturbed around the old one. The distinction between kinds that respond to being classified and kinds that are indifferent to it is the distinction between probing a system that back-reacts to the probe and probing one that does not. The moving-target problem is the impossibility of calibrating against a standard that drifts at a rate comparable to the interval between measurements. For each item, tell me whether it holds, whether it holds only as a metaphor that will not bear weight, or whether it smuggles in an assumption of mine and presents it as Hacking's claim.

Then a case for you to adjudicate, because I suspect it will reveal whether you are translating or flattering me. In the mid-1990s I administered a proficiency-testing scheme for a class of trace-impurity assays. After we published the consensus value from one round, the scatter of returned results narrowed markedly over the following rounds and the methods converged on one another. My colleagues and I called this an artifact and corrected for it. Hacking, I think, would call it something far more interesting. Tell me what feature of his account, if any, distinguishes a genuine looping effect from ordinary herding toward a published assigned value. If nothing distinguishes them, say so plainly.

Two prohibitions. Do not tell me that this is the measurement problem of quantum mechanics or an observer effect; I have encountered that comparison many times and never once seen it cashed out. If you believe the analogy has content, state the content and the precise point at which it fails; if you believe it is a category mistake, say why in a sentence. And give me no bibliography, no reading list, and no citations. Where you are uncertain of Hacking's own formulation of a point, say that you are uncertain rather than reconstructing a confident version of it.

What I want back is a residue report as much as a translation. For each of the three claims: what carries over cleanly, what carries over only by being distorted, and what finds no expression at all in my idiom. Then two judgments. First, where the translation fails, is the failure a defect of the measurement vocabulary — for instance, that it presupposes a fixed measurand whose true value exists independently of the measuring arrangement, which is precisely what is at issue here — or does the failure suggest that the philosophical claim was less determinate than it first appeared? I am genuinely unsure which, and I would rather be told the second if the second is the truth. Second, is the failure symmetric? My field has phenomena of stabilization: once a classification is institutionalized, traceability to it makes results converge and the target ceases to move. I cannot tell whether Hacking's account has room for that, or whether it is committed to perpetual drift. Close with your own view on whether interactive kind does explanatory work or merely redescribes the fact that systems react to probes, and on what would settle that question.
