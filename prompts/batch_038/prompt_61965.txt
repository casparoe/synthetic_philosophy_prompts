I found a beat-up copy of a book about the history of peace plans in my library's free pile, and the chapter on Kant's essay Perpetual Peace reads kind of confident but I think it's sloppy. Can't tell which. Here's how it sums up his whole argument in one paragraph:

"Kant's starting point is that war is natural to human beings and peace isn't, so peace has to be manufactured rather than found. The only way to manufacture it is one world government with an army of its own that no single state can resist — anything looser just leaves the state of war between nations exactly where it was. On top of that you need republics, because in a republic the people who vote for the war are the same people who pay for it, which is why democracies never fight each other. Finally, universal hospitality: nobody arriving at a foreign border can lawfully be turned away. Kant admitted the project would never be finished, but said we have to act as if it could be."

Two things bug me. I've heard it claimed Kant actually wanted something more like a voluntary club of independent countries and was worried a single world ruler would end in despotism, which would make that first part backwards. And my cousin who does a politics degree insists "republic" and "democracy" were not the same word for Kant, but she wouldn't say why and I couldn't follow her.

So: how much of that paragraph would a Kant scholar actually sign off on? Go through it claim by claim and sort it into roughly right, compressed-but-misleading, and just wrong, and say what Kant's own reason was where the book gets it wrong. Then the part I care about most — if the world-government line really is the opposite of what he argued, does the rest of the case still stand on its own, or does pulling that thread take the whole thing down with it? Also tell me if the hospitality bit is doing something sneaky with a word, because "turned away at a border" sounds like it means something more specific in Kant than it does in that sentence.
