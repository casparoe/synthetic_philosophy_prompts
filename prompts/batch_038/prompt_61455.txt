I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine — long features, not news. For the better part of last year I hung around a survival-research group, people in the old Society for Psychical Research line who want hard evidence that consciousness continues past death, and while doing it I fell down a Sidgwick hole. Henry Sidgwick: one of the founders of the SPR and its first president back in 1882, moral philosopher by trade, and a man who thought the traditional arguments for immortality couldn't carry any weight and wanted something empirical instead. What sticks with me is that he never got it, and that he understood better than most why it was so hard. Two problems, roughly. First, any apparent hit has to survive fraud, cold reading, sensory leakage and cryptomnesia before it's even interesting. Second, and worse, even a hit that survives all of that might show only a genuine supernormal faculty — telepathy out of living minds, or straight clairvoyance — rather than a dead person, because information can turn up without anybody turning up with it. The cross-correspondence business that people after him pursued was an attempt to plug that second hole by scattering fragments to separated automatists so that no one living head holds the whole, and its that hole I care about most.

Could you write me a short story that dramatizes this? Not a séance in gaslight and velvet curtains — I want it dry and procedural, like a lab slowly realising its own protocol is a trap. Here's the case I've got rattling around; change names and details freely, but keep the structure:

A retired statistician, dying of pancreatic cancer, designs what he calls a posthumous protocol. He splits six pieces of private information — a calibration constant, a street name, a date, an inscription, a nickname nobody has used in forty years, the contents of a sealed envelope — across five people who have never met and have no way of contacting each other, each fragment meaningless alone, and he keeps the key that makes them cohere in his own head. His reasoning: if a medium later produces the assembled whole, no single living mind can have supplied it. He dies. Eight months later a medium in another country produces five of the six, and the sixth in a form that only makes sense once you already have the other five.

What I want the story to do:

- have the investigator want it to be true, badly, and be good at their job anyway;
- let the audit go wrong in a specific, believable way — something about who actually knew what, and when, and the difference between never having been told and having forgotten being told;
- show the second round, the fix, the fragments split among automatists kept strictly seperate from one another so they genuinely can't have colluded, and let it work;
- then land the Sidgwickian gut-punch: what a protocol like this can ever deliver is data, not a person. No memory of anyone, no motive, no character, no way to tell whether someone is speaking or whether the medium just has a standing faculty for pulling documents out of nowhere.

Format: exactly five numbered scenes, nothing before scene 1 and nothing after scene 5 — no prologue, no epilogue, no author's note explaining the ideas. Give each scene a two-or-three-word header, keep each one to roughly 150–250 words so the whole thing lands under about 1,200, and let the philosophy come out of what people say and do rather than a narrator telling me about it.
