ok so for my formal epistemology seminar we each have to post our "doxastic profile" before the workshop so the others can attack it, and honestly I'm already worried mine doesnt hang together. Here's what I'm committed to, roughly in the order it came to me:

(1) I'm a Stalnakerian about belief. To believe is to rule out possible worlds, so a belief state is just a set of live worlds.

(2) I want the norms of rationality to be exact, not approximate. Rational credences satisfy the probability axioms, full stop. I don't want to build "well, finite minds can't manage it" into the norm itself — that just lowers the bar and then you can't criticize anyone.

(3) I accept the consequence of (1)+(2): every logical truth gets credence 1, and if my live-worlds set is nonempty then I'm committed to every logical consequence of everything I believe. Fine by me, that's what the ideal is for.

(4) But also — last week our prof put a CNF formula on the board, maybe 300 clauses, and asked whether it was satisfiable. I had literally no idea. My honest credence was like .55 that it was. And I don't think I was being irrational. I think .55 was the correct attitude for someone in my epistemic position, not a lamentable failure to live up to an ideal.

(5) Working through a proof teaches you something. When I finally saw the derivation, my credence in the theorem went from .55 to 1, and that felt like genuine learning rather than like noticing I'd been confused the whole time.

(6) I don't want to solve this with impossible worlds or with fragmented belief states. Both look like cheating to me — if you let worlds be inconsistent, or if you let me have a bunch of separate credence functions that never talk to each other, you've thrown away the thing that made the framework explanatory in the first place.

(7) My own proposal, which is the part I actually want to defend in the paper: keep the probability axioms exactly as they are, but treat deduction as a kind of evidence. A valid proof of P is evidence for P, and I update on it the way I'd update on anything else. I'm calling it logical learning.

Here's the mechanism I had in mind for (7), since "treat the proof as evidence" on its own is too vague. I thought you restrict closure to short consequences: an agent only has to be closed under what follows in at most n steps from what they believe, for some fixed n, say 5 or 10. Then my .55 on the 300-clause formula is consistent with the axioms, because that formula is a long way off, and I still get omniscience about the easy stuff.

Can you go through this and tell me which of these actually pull against each other? Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them all as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give and roughly what that costs me. My gut says that updating on a proof you already knew was valid does nothing whatsoever, but I can't tell whether that gut feeling is right or whether I'm confusing two different things.
