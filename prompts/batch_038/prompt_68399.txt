I have no academic training in history or in psychology — I left school at seventeen and spent thirty-one years as a newspaper copy editor — but for roughly the past six years I have read on my own about how people explain events after the fact, and in particular about hindsight bias and what is now usually called the narrative fallacy. I am writing to you because I have recently been given a job that puts those ideas under real pressure, and I have a limited amount of attention to spend.

In January I was appointed chair of a volunteer project at the historical society in my town. We are building a permanent exhibit about the 1998 flood, which killed eleven people and destroyed most of the buildings along River Street. It opens in eighteen months. There are four of us, we can manage perhaps ten hours a week between us once we set aside insurance, panel fabrication, and donor correspondence, and there is no money for consultants or for hired researchers. That works out to something like a thousand hours of usable attention, and nearly all of it will go to three things: interviewing survivors and former town officials, working through the newspaper morgue from 1994 to 2001, and writing the text that ends up on the walls.

My difficulty is this. Almost everyone we interview tells us the flood was obviously coming: that the levee was known to be inadequate, that the warnings after 1993 should have settled the question, that the council's decision to keep permitting construction on the low ground was reckless. Some of the same people said nearly the opposite at the time, and I have the clippings that show it. At the same time, I feel the pull — as a former editor, more strongly than most — to give the exhibit a clean shape: rising danger, warning ignored, catastrophe, reckoning. That is what makes a visitor stop and read. My fear is that a well-built narrative exhibit is a device for manufacturing hindsight bias in the people who walk through it, and that we would be operating the device while congratulating ourselves on our candor.

I can list a good many distinct problems in this territory, and I suspect I cannot afford more than two or three of them. They include: the way a known outcome makes the past feel more determined than it was; the reconstruction of memory in light of how things turned out, which affects our interviews more than anything else we do; the fact that the people who agree to be interviewed are not a sample of the people who were there; the difference between what was foreseeable in 1997 and what was actually foreseen, and our inability to recover the second from the first; the temptation to treat a coherent causal story as stronger evidence than it is; the possibility that a warning-ignored framing quietly assumes the warning was actionable by people who had no money and no authority; the risk of reading our own documents retrospectively, knowing how they end; and, deepest of all, the question whether an exhibit with a beginning, a middle, and an end can avoid the narrative fallacy at all, or can only choose which distortions to accept.

Given a thousand hours, four volunteers with no technical training, and one room, which two or three of these deserve the bulk of our effort, and which should I deliberately set aside — either because they are unfixable at our scale, or because they are serious problems for professional historians writing monographs but not for a town exhibit that a visitor spends twenty-five minutes in? Please defend an ordering rather than walk me through the field. I want to know what you are ranking by: how much each problem would actually change what a visitor takes home, how tractable it is with the tools we have (interviews, clippings, wall text, photographs, and the physical sequence of a room), and whether fixing it costs us the human interest that brings people through the door in the first place. Where those considerations point in different directions, tell me which one you would sacrifice and why. Finally, tell me what would move items up or down your list: what further facts about our situation, if I supplied them, would turn one of the problems I should set aside into the one that matters most?

I am not asking for suggested readings, references, or a bibliography; I can find my way to those myself, and I would rather have your judgment about where the effort belongs. Please write your answer in American English.
