I am putting together a case-by-case catalogue of Augustine's rulings on lying for a seminar paper, and I have reached a case that the verdicts I have collected do not appear to settle. Here is the track record as I have it:

- No lie is ever permissible, whatever the consequences. Lies are not equally grave, however, and he ranks them in severity: lying about religious teaching stands at the top; then lies that harm someone and help no one; then lies that harm one person in order to benefit another; then lies told for the sheer pleasure of deceiving; then lies told merely to be agreeable in conversation; and then three grades that harm no one — a lie that protects someone's property, a lie that saves someone from death, and, least grave of all, a lie that shields someone from sexual violation. Even the bottom rung is still a sin.
- Remaining silent is not lying, and neither is refusing to answer, nor announcing that one will not answer. Concealing the truth and asserting a falsehood are different acts.
- The midwives in Exodus who misled Pharaoh about the Hebrew women were rewarded, but not for the deception: the reward was for their mercy, and the lie was tolerated in them rather than approved.
- Rahab, who hid the spies and sent their pursuers on a false trail, is commended for faith and hospitality; the misleading itself is not commended.
- A person who says something false while believing it to be true does not lie. A person who says something true while believing it to be false does.
- Christ's feigning to go further on the road to Emmaus is not a lie, because nothing was asserted and no one was meant to be deceived; the gesture signified something figuratively.
- Against a sect that taught it lawful to lie, and even to swear falsely, in order to protect its members from persecution, he holds firm: conceal, refuse, endure — but do not assert a falsehood, not under oath and not to save the Church's own people from torture.
- A parable, a figure of speech, or a story told for the sake of what it signifies is not a lie, since no one is expected to take it as an assertion; but a falsehood told only to be pleasant company still is one, low on the scale.

The case I cannot resolve. A believer is sheltering a catechumen from a magistrate's men. He is served with a written question — is the fugitive in your house? — which must be signed and returned, so that silence and refusal are both unavailable, and a refusal would itself trigger a search. He writes a sentence that is true and that he believes to be true: that no one is in his house whom he is bound to hand over. He knows exactly how the magistrate will read it, and he chooses the wording for that reason.

Predict his ruling — lie or no lie, and if a lie, where in the ranking it falls. If you think one of the verdicts above is close enough to settle the matter, say which and why; otherwise proceed as follows. State which principle of lying his verdicts jointly support: one keyed to the actual truth value of what is said, one keyed to what the speaker believes about it, one keyed to the intention to cause a false belief, or one keyed to a duty of speech owed to the hearer. Lay out at least two principles that fit all eight verdicts and nevertheless come apart on the written sentence, say which you would bet on and why, and note explicitly what turns on whether the object to be assessed is the meaning the writer intends or the meaning a reasonable reader would take from the page. Then name a further case that would discriminate between your two candidate principles — for instance a purely nonverbal feint, such as leaving a lamp burning and a cloak on a chair so that pursuers conclude the man is at home, which asserts nothing and yet reliably installs a false belief. Finally, say whether the verdict changes if the misleading reading is one the writer intends or one the magistrate supplies on his own to an otherwise innocent sentence.

Please answer in American English, in continuous prose rather than bullet points, with no bibliography, references, or citations of any kind — I want the reasoning, and I want you to be candid about where the earlier verdicts genuinely underdetermine the new case instead of smoothing the difficulty away.
