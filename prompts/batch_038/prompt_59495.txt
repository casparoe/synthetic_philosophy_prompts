I'm a backend engineer, about ten years in, and my only philosophy background is podcasts and the occasional 2am wikipedia spiral, so forgive me if I describe this clumsily. At work I've been wiring up a zero-knowledge proof system for authentication. The plain-English version of what it does: I can convince you I know a secret without ever telling you the secret, and after enough rounds you're rationally forced to believe me — but you walk away with nothing you could hand to someone else. If you write the whole exchange down, the transcript is worthless to a third party, because a clever forger could have produced that exact transcript without knowing anything. So the evidence is completely binding for the person who was in the room and structurally useless for everybody else. That's not really a fact about crypto to me, it's a fact about testimony and knowledge — it's like the gap between being shown something and being told something, except it turns out to be neither.

I want to turn that into a short story, and I'd rather it not be about computers at all. Here's the seed I've been chewing on: a low-tech society, no machines, no electricity — a small order that claims to be the custodian of some secret (a true name, the location of something, the answer to a prophecy, your pick, but the content should never be revealed to the reader, because the content doesn't matter). An official arrives from the center to determine whether the order actually holds it or is running a long con. The verification happens as a physical ritual with ordinary objects — doors, jars, coins, shuffled cards, a courtyard, whatever you like — and the story should make the ritual concrete enough that a reader can follow why repeating it over and over drives the chance of bluffing down to nothing. By the end the official is convinced, genuinely and correctly. Then the second half: he has to go home and justify himself, he produces a full written account of every round, and someone back at the capital — maybe a junior clerk, maybe a rival — patiently demonstrates that his account could have been written by anybody, so officially he has no evidence at all. I'd love for the story to end with the reader sitting in that clerk's chair rather than the official's, i.e. structurally unable to inherit the conviction even though every step was shown.

Constraints, roughly: 1200 to 1800 words, third person, and please no modern crypto vocabulary anywhere — no "protocol," "verifier," "prover," "soundness," nothing that sounds like a paper. Don't have the characters explain the philosophy in dialogue and don't land it with a closing narration paragraph that spells out the moral; the last scene should just leave the official holding a true belief he can't transfer, and let that be uncomfortable. I also don't want the cheap twist where the monks were frauds after all — the whole point is that he's right. Some beat in the middle where the custodian says something small and almost offhand that makes the asymmetry visible (he knows, the official believes, and neither of them can pass it along) would be great, but keep it to a line or two. Give it a title if you want, and if there's a spot where the ritual logic doesn't quite work, just tell me after the story where you had to fudge, since I'll notice and it'll bug me.
