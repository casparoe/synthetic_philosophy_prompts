I am assembling a working paper on why certain long-standing moral norms collapse, and I need a disciplined comparative judgment rather than a survey of the literature. The anchor is Appiah's *The Honor Code: How Moral Revolutions Happen* (2010), whose central claim is that practices such as dueling end not when people are persuaded that they are wrong but when the honor code governing them is reconfigured, so that the practice becomes shameful rather than merely impermissible. Rival accounts assign the causal work to explicit moral argument and conscience-raising, to shifts in material and strategic interest, or to statutory prohibition and enforcement.

Please work through the following five cases:

1. `dueling` — the disappearance of the duel among British gentlemen and officers over the nineteenth century.
2. `footbinding` — the collapse of footbinding in China between the reform societies of the late Qing and the first decades of the twentieth century.
3. `slave_trade` — the British movement against the Atlantic slave trade and against slavery in the colonies.
4. `honor_killing` — the still-contested norm against so-called honor killings in Pakistan.
5. `marital_exemption` — the abolition, across many jurisdictions in the late twentieth century, of the marital exemption in the law of rape.

The fifth case is deliberately not one of Appiah's, and I want it treated on exactly the same footing as the others.

**First task: allocate causal shares.** For each case, distribute exactly 100 points across five mechanisms, using integers, with no mechanism exceeding 60 points in any case:

- `argument`: uptake of explicit moral reasoning — pamphlet, sermon, treatise, testimony, the conversion of individual consciences by reasons addressed to them.
- `honor`: reconfiguration of an honor world — redefinition of who counts as a person of honor and what such a person does, including the admission of previously excluded people into the honor world whose opinion then matters.
- `interest`: change in the material, fiscal, strategic, or occupational payoffs to practitioners and their patrons.
- `law`: prohibition, prosecution, and the institutional coercion that makes the practice costly or unavailable.
- `substitution`: displacement by a rival practice, technology, or institution that rendered the old one functionally redundant — new venues for status competition, new forms of credit, new household arrangements — without anyone arguing the old one out of existence.

**Second task: score endogeneity.** I want to introduce a distinction I have not seen formulated in this literature, and I need you to apply it rather than critique its novelty. Call a moral revolution *endogenous* to the extent that the pressure came from inside the community whose members performed the practice — its own elites, its own honor code, its own arguments — and *exogenous* to the extent that it came from outside judgment, sanction, or example: foreign powers, colonizers, transnational movements, metropolitan courts legislating for a community that did not ask them to. Score each case on a continuous 0.0–1.0 scale, where 1.0 is wholly endogenous. Be attentive to the awkward possibility that a community can internalize outside contempt and thereby convert an exogenous shock into an endogenous motive; say in your note whether that is what you think happened anywhere.

**Third task: rank and predict.** Rank the five cases by how much total support they lend the honor thesis; rank them by argument-sensitivity; and rank them by snapback risk, which I define as the probability that the practice returns in some recognizable form among the descendants of the community that abandoned it within a century. Finally, state your subjective correlation between endogeneity and durability across these five cases — my own hunch, which I want you to test rather than flatter, is that exogenously driven collapses snap back and endogenous ones hold.
