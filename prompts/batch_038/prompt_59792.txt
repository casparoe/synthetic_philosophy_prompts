and here's the student's answer, unedited apart from fixing the handwriting:

> Hume's argument in Part I starts from his general account of how belief should track evidence: a wise man proportions belief to the evidence, and all our evidence for matters of fact comes from experience — including the evidence of testimony, which we trust only because experience has shown that people are mostly honest and mostly get things right. Evidence comes in degrees, and where experience has been completely uniform it amounts to a full proof rather than a mere probability. Hume is clearly not a sceptic about testimony as such, since he thinks almost all our knowledge of history rests on it.
>
> He then defines a miracle as a violation of a law of nature, and says the laws of nature are established by a firm and unalterable experience. So there is a complete proof against any miracle whatsoever. Testimony in favour of a miracle is a proof on the other side, and proofs must be weighed against each other, the weaker subtracted from the stronger. Hence the maxim: no testimony can establish a miracle unless the falsehood of the testimony would be more miraculous than the miracle it reports. This maxim looks like it leaves a door open, but it can never in fact be satisfied, because the proof against a miracle is complete by definition. So the argument shows not only that a miracle cannot be rationally believed on testimony, but that miracles cannot actually happen — and Part II then adds further independent support for that same conclusion: that people love wonder and surprise, that miracle stories flourish among ignorant and barbarous nations and recede as societies civilise, and that the miracle claims of rival religions destroy each other.
>
> The critics who call this question-begging are right, though not usually for the reasons they give. The circularity is not in Hume's appeal to experience, it is in the definition. Once you define a miracle as a violation of the laws of nature, and the laws as whatever experience has found uniform, no possible evidence could count in favour of a miracle, so the conclusion is already sitting inside the premises. Hume seems to half-see this, which is why he dresses it up as a probabilistic argument: his maxim is just a Bayes' theorem calculation written out in prose, and anybody who thinks the probability calculus could come out the other way here hasn't done the arithmetic.
