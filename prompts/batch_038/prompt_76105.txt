so i'm teaching a two-week unit on independence results in a phil-of-math seminar — twelve students, four of whom have ever had a proof course, the rest are philosophy majors who've heard the name Cantor. we end thursday on the axiom of choice and i want to close by reading a poem aloud, not a summary.

the thing i keep circling and want the poem to actually be about is that old set-theorists' joke — the axiom of choice seems obviously true, the well-ordering principle seems obviously false, and nobody can tell about Zorn's lemma — even though it's the same statement three times. so: a sestina. use these six end-words: socks, order, chain, piece, measure, rule. (socks for Russell's infinite pairs, where there's no way to say which one you take; order for well-ordering the reals; chain for Zorn; piece for the ball you cut up and get two balls back; measure for the sets that don't have one; rule for the fact that no step of the choosing can ever be written down.) the whole trick of a sestina is that the same six words come back in a fixed rotation, which is exactly the structure i want — the reader's gut feeling about each word should flip depending on which stanza it lands in and what it's wearing that time. by the envoi all six get crammed into three lines and the disguises should collapse; that's the punchline, that it was one thing the whole time.

it needs to be accurate enough that the four proof-course kids don't wince. in particular: the doubling is a three-dimensional business (with rigid motions in the plane you genuinely can't do it), the pieces aren't measurable, and countable choice isn't full choice — a poem that blurs those three will read as mush to exactly the people i need to reach. and please don't let it land on either "so mathematics is arbitrary after all" or "so it's certain after all." we didn't get there in seminar and the poem shouldn't get there either.

one thing i cant work out: a sestina's rotation is itself a rule, which is arguably the opposite of the point. i don't know whether to fight that or lean into it — you decide, but do it deliberately, and the envoi is probably where it has to be dealt with.

then after the poem, a few lines of notes for me, seperate from the verse: where you took liberties with the math or leaned on a reading a set theorist would dispute; which of the six end-words you think is carrying the least weight and what you'd swap it for and why; and what would change your mind about the poem — if someone in my seminar pushed back on a specific line, which pushback would actually make you rewrite it rather than defend it. if you're not sure about a claim you put in the poem, flag it instead of smoothing it over.
