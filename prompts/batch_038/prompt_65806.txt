I teach a once-a-month evening seminar for adults at a public library in a mid-sized city whose economy used to run on a single manufacturer and now runs on a regional hospital and a scattering of remote-work professionals; the enrollment is a mix of retired machinists, several schoolteachers, two nurses, and one graduate student in sociology. This term's theme is the moral treatment of success — why some communities cut down whoever rises above the rest and others turn the riser into a civic ritual. We have spent two sessions on the American Dream and two on the Scandinavian idea usually called the law of Jante, the set of injunctions under which the inhabitants of Aksel Sandemose's fictional town live. The sessions have gone badly, and not because the group disagrees: two regulars in particular — a Danish-born engineer and a man who grew up two blocks from the library and ran his own body shop for thirty years — agree on every case we put to them and disagree on every explanation of it.

My diagnosis is that they are describing two different objects of resentment, and I want to test the diagnosis on a clean example before I take it back to the room. Call the first standing-resentment: objection to someone's occupying a higher rank than you in a shared order, where the grievance is the inequality itself. Call the second claim-resentment: objection to someone's asserting, or being disposed to assert, that he occupies a higher rank — an objection that survives the concession that his rank really is higher, and survives in people who would not want that rank for themselves. The Jante injunctions, as I read them, are framed as prohibitions on thinking rather than on being: what is forbidden is the self-conception, not the achievement, and a neighbor who is plainly cleverer is tolerated so long as he does not treat his cleverness as a standing entitlement to be heard first. The American Dream looks like the mirror image, because it not only permits that self-conception but makes it a precondition of the achievement — you are supposed to believe you can. I am no longer sure that contrast holds, though, since the Dream seems to police claims too, only along a different axis: it tolerates the claim when it is cashed out as effort plus luck and stays formally open to everyone else, and it turns hostile when the claim is cashed out as birth, credential, or taste. One of my regulars raised a third possibility, invoking Veblen's remark in *The Theory of the Leisure Class* (1899) that reputability rests on invidious comparison: on that view, cutting down the tall poppy is not a rejection of the ranking at all but the same ranking game played from below, which would make claim-resentment a species of standing-resentment rather than a rival to it.

What I want from you is a single worked example — invented is fine, and probably better, so long as it is the sort of thing that could plausibly happen in a town like mine — that discriminates among these readings. Concretely: give me one person and one community, and two closely matched episodes separated by some years, in which the person's actual standing is unchanged between them but the community's response flips. Build the example so that the flip cannot be explained by standing-resentment alone, and say explicitly which feature of the second episode is doing the discriminating work. Then tell me whether the Veblenian reading can absorb the case anyway — whether a defender of invidious comparison can redescribe the flip without giving up the claim that all of this is one ranking game — and, if he can, what further detail would separate the two accounts, because my group will immediately want to know how the distinction could ever fail. Please do not send me citations, a reading list, or a survey of the empirical literature on the tall poppy syndrome; I have the reading, and what I need is the construction and the argument. One caution on register: the example will be read aloud to people who are not philosophers, so keep the vocabulary plain and let the case carry the weight rather than the terminology.

Please organize your reply under these headings, in this order:

- **Bottom line** — three or four sentences saying whether the example genuinely discriminates and whether the Veblenian redescription succeeds.
- **The example** — roughly 450–600 words, narrating the two episodes.
- **The discriminating feature** — about 200 words, naming the single respect in which the episodes differ and why standing-resentment cannot account for the flip.
- **The Veblenian reply** — about 250 words, giving the strongest redescription available to the invidious-comparison reading.
- **What would settle it** — about 150 words, describing the additional detail or third episode that would come apart on the two accounts.
- **A note for the room** — about 100 words, offering one sentence each in which I could put the distinction to the engineer and to the body-shop owner such that both would recognize their own view in it.
