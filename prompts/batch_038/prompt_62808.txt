Situation. I sit on my university's animal care and use committee. A neuroscience group came back with a revised protocol where the mice go under deep anesthesia, tissue is harvested, and they never regain consciousness. So on our usual harm-benefit worksheet there's basically nothing to enter on the harm side — no pain, no distress, nothing the animal is around to experience. Two members still want to reject it or force changes, and when I pressed them their reasons turned out to be Kantian-ish: the problem isn't the mice, it's what doing this repeatedly does to the grad students and techs performing it, plus something about treating a dependent creature as raw material. A third member says that's just a badly-worded harm claim.

Here's the bit I keep coming back to, and its the part I want the memo to actually engage instead of papering over: if the objectors are right about what's going on, then the party being damaged isn't in front of us. The people whose characters are supposedly at risk aren't research subjects, they haven't asked to be protected, and the damage is diffuse and long-run rather than something you can put in a column and weigh. Our remit is the animals. So a sound version of their objection seems to show our committee is the wrong body to be hearing it — which isn't the same as showing the objection is wrong. There's also a separate question of whether the habituation claim is just an empirical prediction that could turn out false (maybe people compartmentalize fine, maybe the absence of any visible suffering weakens the habituation story rather than strengthening it), and what's left of the objection if it is false. Kant's own example of owing something to an animal that has served a household for years might matter here, or might be doing different work than the objectors think.
