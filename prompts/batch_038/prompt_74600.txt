I am finishing a novel set in Copenhagen between 1848 and 1855, and its epigraphs are all invented rather than quoted — each attributed to some fictitious sheet, pamphlet, or sermon of the period. For the section dealing with the daily newspapers I need one more: a villanelle purportedly clipped from an unnamed Copenhagen daily, on the subject of the press and "the public" as Kierkegaard understood them. I want the form to carry the argument instead of the diction announcing it.

So: nineteen lines, two rhyme-sounds, and the two refrains returning in their usual places through line seventeen — but the refrains should be second-hand sentences, the kind said by everyone and owned by no one, so that each recurrence enacts a little more of the migration of a claim out of a speaker's mouth and into the tally. Work into the poem the distinction between an actual assembly or a generation, which is present, embodied, and can be called to answer for what it does, and the public, which is a phantom brought into being only by the press's counting of absent readers; the leveling should be felt in what happens to the lines rather than named. Then let the form break: the closing couplet should abandon both refrains, rhyme, and admit the first-person singular, which must not have appeared anywhere earlier in the poem, so that the last line is the only sentence in it for which somebody could be held responsible.

Two further constraints. Nothing in the poem should name the philosopher or his books, and nothing should be attributed to him as a quotation; the press should figure not as a mere bad influence on opinion but as the very medium in which the individual dissolves into readers. And the rendering must be accurate enough that a specialist would recognize both the concept of the public and the concept of leveling, and would also notice where the poem gets the relationship between them right.
