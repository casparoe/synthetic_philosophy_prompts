And here is the answer I've been given:

> In the Treatise on Probability (1921) Keynes rejects two things that were then standard. He rejects the frequency theory, on which a probability just is a long-run ratio in a series of events, and he rejects the assumption that every probability can be given a number. For Keynes a probability is a logical relation between a body of evidence — the premises you actually have — and a conclusion. It is objective in the sense that the relation either holds or it doesn't, whatever anyone happens to feel about it, but it is always relative to a state of knowledge, so the same conclusion can be probable for you and not for me if we know different things. Crucially, he thinks many such relations can't be expressed numerically at all, and worse, that some pairs of probabilities aren't even comparable: there isn't always a fact of the matter about which of two conclusions is better supported. He makes the point with the question of whether to take an umbrella when the barometer is high but the clouds are black, and suggests that in such cases it can be perfectly rational to let caprice settle it rather than trying to weigh evidence that has no common measure.
>
> On top of this he introduces the weight of an argument, which is a second dimension. Weight is about how much relevant evidence you have, as opposed to how favourable the evidence you have is. You can have a small amount of evidence pointing strongly one way, or a large amount pointing mildly. In practice this means weight and probability move together, and Keynes's advice is to keep gathering evidence: a conclusion resting on a thin evidence base is a low-probability conclusion even when the reasoning from that base is sound, and the way to fix it is more data. This is also why he is so suspicious of large-scale economic forecasting. The evidence base for a claim about the economy twenty years out is so thin that the probability attaching to it has to be low, no matter how elegant the model. (The distinction Keynes draws between measurable risk and unmeasurable uncertainty is the one Frank Knight had already put forward in Risk, Uncertainty and Profit, also 1921, and which Keynes took over.)
>
> Is this subjectivism? I think it is, or at least that Keynes is a forerunner of the subjectivism that ended up winning. His probabilities live in states of knowledge rather than in the world: they are facts about how confident a reasonable person should be given what they know, and they change whenever the evidence changes. That is the subjectivist picture. Ramsey, who was Keynes's own student at Cambridge, made the same view explicit a few years later by identifying a degree of belief with the odds a person would accept, which is essentially the modern Bayesian position. So the Treatise and later Bayesian orthodoxy are on the same side of the map, and the real difference between Keynes and Ramsey is that Ramsey had the mathematics and Keynes didn't.
>
> The later work is where the uncertainty notion does its heavy lifting. In the chapter of the General Theory on the state of long-term expectation, Keynes argues that investment decisions depend on prospective yields spread over years or decades, and that for many of the questions that matter — what the rate of interest will be in ten years, whether there will be a new war, what invention will come next — there is no basis for a calculation. His point is not that we are ignorant of the numbers. It is that there are no numbers to be ignorant of, because nothing yet determines what the answer will be. This is unlike roulette, where the odds are fixed and calculable and the only thing missing is knowledge. Because calculation is impossible, markets fall back on convention: people assume the present state of affairs will continue unless they get specific news to the contrary, and they watch what everyone else expects rather than the underlying facts. When convention breaks down, what fills the gap is not reason but a spontaneous urge to act rather than not act — animal spirits.
>
> This is also where Keynes leaves the Treatise behind. If the future is genuinely undetermined, then probability relations between evidence and conclusion can't do any work in these cases, so the later Keynes has no theory of probability left to apply. Decisions get made by instinct and convention, and Keynes's conclusion is that this is rational — he thinks acting on a spontaneous urge rather than on a calculation is exactly what keeps an economy going, and that we should not expect more of investors than that. Uncertainty, for Keynes, means that in the important cases reason runs out and instinct takes over, and economics ought to stop pretending otherwise.
