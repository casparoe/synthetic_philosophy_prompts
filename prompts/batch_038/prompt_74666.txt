I am in the middle of my fourth novel, which runs on two interleaved strands. The first is Lisbon on the morning of 1 November 1755: an Oratorian priest with a French copy of the *Théodicée* (1710) on his shelf is about to walk down to the quays and watch the Tagus withdraw before the wave returns. The second is the present day, in which the chairman of a Geneva foundation, asked at a dinner party about a vaccine trial in which eleven people died so that a programme could continue, replies without irony that we live in the best of all possible worlds. That sentence is the hinge between the two strands, and I have come to see that I keep putting it into mouths where it cannot possibly mean the same thing — my priest is answering Bayle, my chairman is answering a journalist, and my heroine, who loses her younger brother in the lower town in 1755, needs to attack something definite rather than a caricature. Since I cannot have her demolish a position no serious person held, and I equally cannot let my chairman sound as though he has read Voltaire's *Candide* (1759) and drawn the wrong lesson from it, I need the sentence itself taken apart.

So: give me a numbered menu of the distinct theses that this one sentence could be expressing, running from the near-platitude to the radically strong, and for each say what would have to be true for it to hold, whether Leibniz was in fact committed to it, and whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or simply false. I want the load-bearing pieces attended to rather than glossed: what is doing the work in "possible", given that compossibility restricts which worlds are genuinely on offer to a creator; what the comparison class is, and whether the claim is that this world is better than every alternative an omnipotent being could have actualised or only that no actualisable alternative beats it; whether "best" means the greatest total of good, the greatest balance of good over evil, or something closer to the greatest variety of effects obtained at the simplest cost in laws; whether the doctrine presupposes that there is a maximum at all, or whether the objection that the series of possible worlds ascends without end — so that there is no best world and the slogan cannot be true as stated — is one Leibniz can answer; whether it entails that each particular evil was required, or only that the sum is unsurpassable, which is a very different thing for a woman standing in the ruins of her street; whether it is a metaphysical claim, a claim about divine choice under moral necessity, or a piece of practical advice about how to feel. Then separate out the modern secular deployments my chairman could be making — stoic resignation, faith in historical progress, a determinist's shrug that nothing could have been otherwise, the platitude that everything happens for a reason — and say which of these are continuous with Leibniz's own doctrine and which are corruptions of it, however sincerely meant.

Finally, tell me where the argumentative pressure sits. Which reading, if granted, actually licenses the use my characters make of the sentence — that is, entitles a man to say it to someone who has just been bereaved without offering an insult? And which reading should my heroine attack, meaning the one that is respectable enough to be worth a scene and vulnerable enough that the scene can turn? Name the single strongest objection to the most philosophically defensible reading and say plainly whether any reading survives it. Lead with your bottom line: your first sentence should identify the reading my two men are relying on when the sentence functions as a conversation-stopper, and only then unfold the menu and the reasoning. No preamble, no restatement of my question, and no bibliography — I want the distinctions, not the scholarship. Please write in British English.
