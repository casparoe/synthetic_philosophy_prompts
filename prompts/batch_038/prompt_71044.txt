At a philosophy-of-religion talk last month, the speaker argued that the problem of evil rests on a false ontology, and offered the familiar comparison: evil stands to good as darkness stands to light, or cold to heat -- not a thing in its own right, but a privation, an absence where some fullness ought to be. He drew two conclusions from it. First, God cannot be charged with creating evil, since evil is not among the things there are to create. Second, the evidential argument from apparently pointless suffering repeats the same error, because a complaint of that kind has to identify some positive feature of the world that ought not to be there, and the advocate for sufferers can point only to absences. Please audit the comparison. Set out the mapping term by term -- what plays the light, what plays its source, what plays the obstruction that casts the shadow, what plays the turning away that lets the shadow fall -- and then say where the mapping breaks. Distinguish the disanalogies that are merely decorative from the ones that actually bear on his argument: does the image quietly import a norm about how things ought to be, a thesis that absences are causally inert, or a conception of good as a conserved quantity? Finally, tell me whether either conclusion survives once those imports are on the table, and if the comparison misleads, propose a better one for a defender of the privation view along with an honest statement of what that replacement could be made to carry.
