ok so I'm a second year anthro major and I'm TA-ing the weekly lab section for intro archaeology — 18 first-years, 75 minutes, a room with movable tables, a projector, and literally no budget. This week we're meant to do inference from material culture and I really dont want to just re-explain the reading back at them.

Design a hands-on activity that makes them feel, rather than hear, that a static pile of objects massively underdetermines the behaviour that produced it. Three things I want them to run into, in this order, with nobody saying the words until the end: (1) the same assemblage can be the trace of several completely different sequences of events and they will confidently pick one; (2) stuff keeps moving after it's deposited — later disturbances cut through and mix things, so what looks like one event is really two or three; (3) what people tell you they did and what they actually threw away dont match.

The department has a teaching collection but the curator wont let 18 first-years loose on it, so assume all material is modern and cheap: my own flat's recycling, the staffroom bin, whatever students have in their bags. I can print handouts and I can spend a couple of days assembling and documenting a deposit beforehand.

Two mechanics I want built in — tell me if you think either is a bad idea. First, I hold a written ground truth of exactly how the assemblage came to be and only reveal it at the very end. Second, something quietly alters the assemblage between the round where they record it and the round where they interpret it — a staged disturbance standing in for a later pit or a flood or whatever — and they find out afterwards that the thing they just spent twenty minutes carefully plotting had already been messed with. I kind of want them to feel cheated for about ten seconds before they get why, but tell me how to run it so it lands as a lesson and not as me screwing them over.

Give me: the materials list I can actually scrounge, a minute-by-minute timeline for 75, exactly what they write down and on what sheet, how the reveal works, and a debrief that maps each moment onto the real concepts — equifinality, formation processes and that Pompeii-snapshot assumption, the middle-range reasoning business, and Rathje's Tucson refuse sorting where what was in the bins diverged from what households reported. Then: what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it. In particular what do I do if one group nails the ground truth in five minutes and the rest of the session deflates, and what do I do if someone's genuinely annoyed about the tampering. And be frank about which part of the real problem the activity cant reproduce, that I'll just have to supply out loud.
