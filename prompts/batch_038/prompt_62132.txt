I am trying to pin down what I actually believe about decision problems with imperfect recall, using variants of Piccione and Rubinstein's absent-minded driver as probes, and I would like help extracting the principle implicit in my verdicts. Base case: a driver faces two visually indistinguishable exits. Exiting at the first pays 0, exiting at the second pays 4, driving past both pays 1. He cannot tell the exits apart and knows he cannot. My gut reactions to variants:

1. At an exit, splitting credence evenly between the two, exiting is worth 2 and continuing worth 2.5, so he should continue. I hold this firmly. I equally firmly think it was rational of him to adopt at the outset the plan continue-then-exit, worth 4. Both verdicts survive reflection, even though the first prescribes an act that, applied at both exits, guarantees 1.

2. If he can commit in advance to a mixed strategy, I think he should: exiting with probability 1/3 at each exit yields 4/3, better than the 1 that consistent continuation delivers. But I do not think the figure 1/3 speaks to him at the exit itself — there it is just noise, and I would not criticize him for continuing.

3. Replace the single driver with two: D1 decides at the first exit and is replaced by D2 at the second, neither told which stage he is at, both paid on the same schedule. The informational structure is identical, yet here I think each should simply continue, and I feel no pull at all toward randomizing. Asked what the difference tracks, I say that in this version there is no one positioned to adopt a behavioral strategy — but I cannot see why the bare fact of personal identity should alter what is rational at a moment.

4. Suppose a billboard is visible from the first exit and never from the second, and that he notices it with probability 1/2. On failing to notice, his credence that he is at the second exit rises to 2/3 and exiting becomes locally optimal; on noticing, continuing is obviously right. Here my local verdicts coincide with what I would have chosen beforehand, and the paradox evaporates. That makes me suspect the difficulty is about self-locating information rather than about plans as such.

5. With three indistinguishable exits (0 for exiting at the first, 0 at the second, 6 at the third, 1 for passing all), exiting is worth 2 and continuing looks worth far more, so again: continue. But now, unlike in case 2, I feel no temptation to randomize in advance, even though randomizing beats consistent continuation here as well. I cannot explain that shift and suspect one of the two reactions is simply mistaken.

6. The same structure drives the halfer/thirder dispute about the coin-flip-and-amnesia case. There I am a thirder and I use those centered credences directly for betting, yet nothing in that case tempts me to set aside act-level reasoning for planning purposes — which is exactly what I wanted to do in case 2.

My tentative principle: at a decision point, act on credences conditional on your total evidence there, including its centered content; a prior plan retains authority only where it remains executable by the very agent who adopted it. Randomization is a means of implementing a plan, never an independent reason.

Please state one or two candidate principles precisely enough that I can test them against cases, indicating for each clause which of the six verdicts it exists to capture and which it cannot capture. I want an honest list of the verdicts each principle forces me to give up, and I would rather have a simple principle that overturns case 3 or case 5 than an elaborate one that saves everything. Then apply your recommended principle to three situations I have not listed: a driver who can pay a small cost at the start to make the exits distinguishable and declines; a driver who does not know how many exits there are, only that there are at least two; and a version in which the stages are the same person but the payoff for exiting at the first goes to his earlier self and the payoff for exiting at the second to his later self. Finally, say what would change your mind about which principle is correct here — which argument or consideration would push you to the other candidate, and at which step in your own reasoning you are most likely to have gone wrong.
