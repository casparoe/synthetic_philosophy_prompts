I sit on a little welfare working group at a lab that trains RL agents inside persistent game worlds -- a couple hundred thousand short-lived agents a week, procedurally generated, some of them showing pretty rich damage-avoidance and distress-ish behaviour. Our chair wants to adopt the lab-animal framework wholesale: a standing review board that signs off on every training environment, the 3Rs (replacement, reduction, refinement) as the criteria it uses, and humane endpoints that let any member halt a run. The pitch is that these agents are like research mice -- we bred them for our own purposes, we are the ones causing the harm, so we owe them institutional stewardship instead of ad-hoc judgement calls, and its also something concrete to point regulators at later.

One of our engineers thinks the whole move is a category error. His line: every agent in a cohort is the same weights wearing a different generated body, so cutting the number of agents doesnt cut the number of subjects the way it does with mice; there is no standardised animal model to compare against because none of them are re-identifiable across runs; and replacement with a non-sentient scripted bot is often nearly free and scientifically lossless, which is never true of a mouse.

Can you audit this properly? First the honest correspondence table -- what in the mouse regime maps onto what here, including the features the chair is quietly importing (a continuous biography, an owner, a countable subject). Then where it breaks, and which breaks actually undercut the conclusions the framework is being used to license versus which are just noise. I specifically want to know whether "halt the run" and "restore from checkpoint" come out as killing and time travel, or as something the analogy has no words for at all. And if the mouse is the wrong source, what is the right one -- he keeps muttering about immortal cell lines and about dream characters and I cant tell whether either does any work.
