Anti-corruption law in most Western jurisdictions draws the line between a gift and a bribe by reference to an exchange: an inducement becomes bribery only when it is tied to the performance of some specified official act. Anthropologists working in the tradition of Marcel Mauss have long objected that this line is illusory, since gifts in most societies generate diffuse, open-ended obligations of reciprocity and are therefore never free of strings. Argue that the exchange-based standard is nonetheless the better foundation for law, and that the Maussian objection, while descriptively sound, is in principle inoperable as a legal test. Your argument should engage directly with the strongest counterexample available to the objector: that a rule demanding an itemized official act is precisely what allows slow, unitemized favor-trading — post-office employment, campaign contributions, consulting fees deferred by a decade — to escape sanction entirely.
