I am co-teaching a seminar on metaphysics and the Gothic novel, and for our session on parthood I want to assign Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (1818) alongside the special composition question. I need a critical reading that will hold up in a room containing both metaphysicians and literary scholars, so please be as rigorous about the novel as you are about the philosophy.

Here is the material I want the reading to engage. The special composition question asks under what conditions some things compose a further thing. The three answers I plan to put on the table are unrestricted composition, on which any plurality whatsoever has a sum; mereological nihilism, on which nothing ever composes anything and there are only simples; and Peter van Inwagen's answer in Material Beings (1990), on which composition occurs exactly when the activities of the parts constitute a life. That last view has the striking consequence that there are no artifacts: no chairs and no tables, only particles arranged in the pattern a chair would have, related in the way the bees of a swarm are related rather than the way the organs of an organism are.

Against that background, I want you to work through the following, in this order:

1. Victor's assembly of the creature and its animation. Before the animation, do the assembled parts compose anything? The answer differs on each of the three views, and the novel seems to describe the frame as already complete before life is conferred on it. Does Shelley treat the moment of animation as an ontological event, a coming-into-existence, or merely as a causal one? Is the text committed either way, and if it is uncommitted, is that evasive or is it doing something?

2. The artifact problem. The creature is paradigmatically a made thing, yet on van Inwagen's view made things do not exist while living things do, so the creature is a case in which being manufactured and being alive come apart rather than coinciding. Does the novel exploit that gap, or does it simply never notice it?

3. The Orkney scene, in which Victor tears the nearly finished female to pieces as the creature watches from the window. Is that a murder, a dismantling, or something the novel deliberately leaves indeterminate? Does completeness matter, or only animation? And is there an asymmetry the text marks between that act and Victor's own death at the end, with the creature mourning over the body?

4. A proposal of my own, which I want tested rather than flattered. Call it recognition-dependence: the thought that what makes these parts compose a person is not their internal organization but their being addressed by another person and answering in kind, which is why the creature's time observing the cottagers, the books he finds, and above all his demand for a companion read as conditions of his existence and not merely of his happiness. Does the novel support that reading? Is it a metaphysical claim or only an ethical one wearing metaphysical clothing? And what would it cost, given that it seems to make composition dependent on minds other than the one composed?

Finally, please tell me where the reading breaks down: where Shelley resists being used this way, where a metaphysician would say the novel is being merely loose, and where a literary critic would say the ontological framing is tone-deaf. I have a suspicion that asking what the creature is, rather than who he is, may repeat Victor's own failure, and I would like to know whether you think that objection defeats the whole project or can be absorbed into it.

About 1,600 words, with section headings. No bibliography and no reading list, please. Do not invent quotations from the novel; work from paraphrase and scene description, and flag any detail of the plot you are not certain of rather than smoothing over it. If a scene differs between the 1818 and 1831 texts, say so and proceed with the version you know better.
