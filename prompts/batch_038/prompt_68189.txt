Week five of the grad seminar I'm co-teaching with someone from the policy school — cross-listed, half the room has never heard of an aspiration level — and the proverb 'the best is the enemy of the good' came up three separate times in one ninety-minute session, doing three completely different jobs, and nobody, including me, noticed at the time that they weren't the same claim. First use: my co-teacher, who is an economist, deployed it about performance league tables. His point was that when you rank institutions on noisy estimates and then pick the one at the top, you have systematically selected for the largest positive error, so the top-ranked program tends to underperform the tenth-ranked one over the following few years. Second use: a student invoked it to defend satisficing against the charge that it's irrational, and she meant the strong version — that stopping at 'good enough' can be the rationally right thing to do even when you know a better option is sitting right there within reach, not merely a forgiveable concession to the fact that we can't compute the optimum. Third use was mine and I'm now not convinced it was coherent: I used it while we were discussing aspiration levels to suggest that people who lower their standards to match their circumstances have adapted sensibly, and someone shot back that if 'good enough' is set by whatever deprivation you've been conditioned to tolerate, the proverb is just a nice-sounding name for sour grapes.

So I want the readings teased apart properly, as a menu rather than a single gloss. For each candidate reading of the proverb, state the precise claim it expresses — what it quantifies over, whether 'the best' is even assumed to exist, whether we're comparing outcomes or outcomes-net-of-process-costs, whose standards set 'good enough' and at what time — and then give it a verdict: platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and controversial, or false or incoherent. The thing I most want pinned down is which of these readings is just cost-inclusive maximizing in a folk costume, i.e. where 'pursuing the best' silently bundles in the search, comparison and deliberation costs so that the proverb turns out to say nothing at all against maximizing; and then whether any reading escapes that collapse and genuinely licenses a non-maximizing choice rule. Also tell me which single reading the ordinary person repeating the proverb probably has in their head, since I suspect it isn't any of the interesting ones. Then do the mapping: which reading, if any, actually licenses the league-table use (and does that use need a claim about the world or only a claim about our estimates of it?), which one licenses the student's strong claim about rationality, and whether any reading survives the adaptive-preferences objection to my own use. If the honest answer is that my use had no coherent reading behind it, just say so; I'd much rather have that than a rescue.

Practical stuff. I'll be reading this on a plane, so no bibliography, no citations, no 'for further reading' tail — analysis only. Numbered menu with a one-line verdict on each reading, then a short section on the three uses, call it 1100–1400 words total. Assume the reader is comfortable with expected-utility maximization and has heard the phrase bounded rationality but hasn't read Simon, and assume they've never seen an auction-theory result, so the winner's-curse point has to be spelled out in prose rather than gestured at with a term. And be scrupulous about where the proverb simply runs out: part of what I want from this is a clean statement of which of those three uses it is silent on, rather than every use being forced into some reading.
