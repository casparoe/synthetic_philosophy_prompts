**Nozick's animals, and why his sliding scale might not slide**

1. Setup. In the chapter on moral side constraints Nozick considers a hybrid position he labels utilitarianism for animals, Kantianism for people: maximize the total happiness of all living beings, but keep stringent side constraints on what may be done to human beings. He isn't simply endorsing it, he's testing whether it holds together. Two problems usually get raised. First, the wild. Animals hurt each other constantly, so a maximizing principle covering animals seems to obligate us to intervene in nature, which Nozick doesn't want — as best I remember he suggests animals in the wild are living in accordance with their nature, so the principle has to govern how we treat them rather than making us the police of the food chain. Second, the line. What's special about humans? Nozick's own basis for side constraints is the capacity to shape your life in accordance with some overall plan you've chosen, and that capacity obviously comes in degrees, so he floats a sliding scale of moral standing instead of a sharp boundary.

2. My claim. The sliding scale doesn't just wobble, it reverses, once you combine it with something else in the book. Nozick distinguishes boundary crossings that are flatly forbidden from crossings that are permitted provided you compensate the person whose boundary you crossed. Which side a given crossing falls on isn't settled by how grand the boundary sounds in the abstract; it turns on whether a transfer can make up for the crossing.

3. Run the sliding scale through that. A weaker right, on this picture, ought to be a right that's easier to cross with compensation. So the chicken's claim against confinement is supposed to be softer than a person's claim against being locked in a room. But the things we actually do to chickens — lifetime confinement, slaughter at six weeks — are precisely the crossings where no compensation is available. There's no transfer that hands the bird a life it didn't get to have, and after slaughter there isn't even a recipient left to pay. So the scale has nowhere to bite: in the domains where animals have their biggest interests, the crossings are uncompensable, and on Nozick's own logic uncompensable crossings are the ones that end up forbidden outright.

4. The objection I know is coming. This proves too much. Murder can't be compensated either, so if non-compensability is what makes a crossing forbidden, the sliding scale never does any work anywhere and I've shown all rights to be absolute — which is absurd, because taking somebody's truck for an hour is clearly not on the level of killing them. My answer is that compensation doesn't have to restore the identical thing that was taken, it has to leave the person, by their own ranking, better off than they otherwise would have been. The truck owner can be left better off. A dead hen can't. I'm honestly not sure that's a real distinction or just a restatement of what I wanted to prove, and this is the section I'm least happy with.

5. A smaller second point. The maximizing half of the hybrid is aimed at happiness. But Nozick's experience machine argument elsewhere in the book is an argument that experience isn't the whole of what matters — we want to actually do things, to be a certain way, and to be in contact with what's real. If that's right, a hen in a perfectly pleasant simulated coop is missing something, and maximizing total animal happiness is grading animals on the wrong currency. The hybrid imports the hedonism the rest of the book argues against.

6. Where that leaves it. If both points land, the hybrid gets squeezed from both ends: the utilitarian half measures the wrong thing, and the deontological half, once moral standing is allowed to come in degrees, ends up hardening animal rights rather than softening them. Nozick wanted a halfway house. What he gets, I think, is a view that keeps sliding toward the thing he was trying to avoid — not toward cruelty, but toward animals having rights you can't trade off at all.

I don't want a bibliography or citations, I can't chase those down, just your judgment on whether my memory's off.

3. Tell me whether section 5 is pulling any weight or if I should cut it and spend the space tightening the compensation argument instead.
