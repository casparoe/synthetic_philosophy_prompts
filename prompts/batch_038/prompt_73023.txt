I left school at sixteen and have never taken a philosophy course. For twenty-two years I have worked as a study coordinator running blinded comparison panels — taste tests, paired-preference trials, that kind of thing — where my entire job consists of keeping information from travelling between the people in the room: who knows the allocation, what the labels say, whether the moderator has met the panellists before, whether the samples were prepared by someone who has a stake in the outcome. I have read around the edges of my subject out of curiosity rather than duty, and one thing I keep returning to is the closing section of Turing's 1950 paper on computing machinery and intelligence, where he takes up extrasensory perception as an objection to his imitation game.

My recollection of that section, which you should correct wherever it is wrong or overconfident, is this. Turing lists telepathy, clairvoyance, precognition and psychokinesis among the objections, and treats them not as a joke but as the objection he finds hardest to dismiss, partly because he thinks the statistical evidence for at least telepathy is stronger than he expected. His suggested remedy, as I remember it, is to alter the conditions of the game — to house the competitors in a room shielded against such influence so that the test is run fairly. And he draws a further consequence: if minds can be acted on at a distance, the assumption that the interrogator, the man and the machine are three sealed-off systems may not hold for men any more than for machines.

Please restate that whole worry in the vocabulary I actually work in — blinding and its failure modes, allocation concealment, unblinding, contamination and carryover, information leakage between sites, protocol deviations and amendments, confounding, chain of custody, pre-specified analysis, and the standing distinction my field draws between a result that is invalid because the procedure broke down and a hypothesis that is false. Do the translation as faithfully as you can before you start criticising it, as a coordinator would write it up: what exactly, on this reading, is the defect in the imitation game's protocol, whose blinding is failing, and what would count as a sufficient amendment.

Then give me the residue report, item by item, under three headings: claims of Turing's that translate cleanly into my protocol language; claims that translate only with distortion, where you must say precisely what gets distorted in the transfer; and claims that have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all. I want the third category to do real work rather than being a shrug. In particular, tell me whether his remark about minds not being sealed off is a statement about the test instrument or a statement about the world, and whether my vocabulary — which treats any leakage as a defect of the setup to be engineered away, never as a permanent feature of the objects under study — is capable of holding that distinction. Say also whether a shielded room, on my reading, is a protocol amendment or a quiet change in the question being asked.

Two things I want you to watch for in your own answer. First, do not smuggle in the assumptions of my discipline and then present them as findings of the translation; if a step in your rendering only works because I already assume that a broken blind invalidates rather than complicates a result, flag it as your assumption and not his. Second, be explicit about confidence. Mark each item in the residue report as settled, tentative, or a guess, and identify which single item you would most expect a specialist in the paper to relocate. Finish by telling me what would change your mind: what reading of that section, or what argument about the imitation game, would move an item from the third category into the second, or would convince you that the whole worry is nothing more than a fairness objection my field would dispose of in a paragraph. And state plainly where your reasoning is most likely to have failed, including any place where you are reconstructing Turing's text from an imperfect memory of it rather than from what he actually wrote.
