So a friend of mine teaches a first-year seminar on how we know anything about the deep past — mostly freshmen, twenty-five of them, a mix of true-crime-podcast people and kids just filling a humanities requirement — and she got me a guest slot when her co-lecturer bailed. Seventy-five minutes, whiteboard only, no slides. The topic she's assigned me is "when does a legend become history," and the obvious version of that session is the greatest-hits tour: Troy, Arthur, the Exodus, three verdicts, done. I don't want to do that, partly because I'd be adjudicating things I'm not qualified to adjudicate and partly because a verdict isn't a transferable idea. The one narrow thing I actually want them to walk out with is this: an arguement from silence only has force once you've estimated how much trace that kind of event was likely to leave in the first place, and how likely we were to recover what it left. A mass movement of people through a small, closely administered, heavily excavated desert is one sort of claim; one warlord winning a skirmish in a post-imperial backwater with no chancery, no stone, and no surviving contemporary historian is a completely different sort. The sources are silent in both cases and the silence is not worth the same amount. In my notes I've been calling this the "detectability budget," which is my own coinage and not a real term — tell me honestly if it's actively misleading rather than merely informal, because I'd rather drop it than plant a bad picture in twenty-five heads.

What I need from you is the plan, not the lecture. Give me the sequence: which ideas have to be in place before the weighting move even makes sense to someone who has never thought about sources at all (I suspect they need to see a text broken down claim by claim before they can weigh anything, and I suspect they need genre and purpose before they can break it down, but I don't know where the real load-bearing joints are), and what depends on what, with the reason at each step. Then the carrying example: pick the one case that does the work, and tell me what it has to have and what it has to lack for the idea to come through cleanly — I've been assuming Arthur, since the gap between the battle everyone's heard of and the ninth-century battle-list that first names him is so blatant, but maybe Troy is better because Hisarlik actually gives them a destruction layer and a Hittite archive with a place-name and a ruler's name that look suspiciously like the Greek ones, so they get to see detectability pay off rather than only fail. And then say what I do with the other two, and in what order, if they belong in the session at all — the Exodus is the one I'm most nervous about, since half the room will have some stake in it and I don't want the last twenty minutes to be about someone's childhood, though the Egyptian frontier record and the stele naming Israel as a people by around 1200 BCE make it the sharpest possible illustration of high-trace/low-recovery versus low-trace/high-stakes.

Then the misconception map, and I want it in the order they'll actually arrive, not the order of severity. The one I'm sure of is the flat "no evidence means it didn't happen." The one I'm scared I'll create myself is the over-correction right after it — "so you can never actually rule anything out, it's all just a matter of faith" — which is the exact opposite failure and much stickier. I'd also guess "archaeology will settle it, we just haven't dug the right site yet," and "the story is true or false as a whole" rather than piece by piece, and probably a fifth one you can see coming that I can't. For each, tell me where in the sequence to head it off and roughly what I say or draw at that moment, and flag any that my setup will actively invite so I can change the setup. Tell me also what to openly refuse at this level — there's a whole pile of technical machinery behind this (dating methods, source criticism, oral-tradition theory, the politics of who gets to excavate) and I'd rather announce that I'm skipping it than blur it — and end with one exit question, ideally about a case they haven't seen in the session, that separates "understood the weighting move" from "learned the slogan." One last thing, and it's selfish: I'd like to lift the spine of this into a column afterwards, so if the teaching order doesn't work as an order for prose, tell me where it breaks and what to swap.
