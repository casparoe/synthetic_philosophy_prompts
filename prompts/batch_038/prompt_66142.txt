Been circling Good's theorem lately — the 1967 result that free information is never expected to make you worse off, on the grounds that deciding after you learn is an average of maxima while deciding now is a maximum of averages — and I want to pressure-test it against a real refusal of information rather than a toy case. The Macondo well looks like the cleanest one available. As I have it: April 2010, BP is bleeding something like a million dollars a day on a rig that's behind schedule, they opt not to run a cement bond log even though the tool and the people to run it were on board, and then when the negative pressure test throws readings that don't look right, the crew talks itself into a benign story (something about a bladder effect in the annulus, as I understand it) and goes ahead and displaces the mud. Eleven dead, months of blowout.

What I want is the analysis running both directions, and I'd rather you commit to verdicts than hand me a menu of possible readings.

First, do the mapping before the evaluating. Who is the agent in this decision problem — BP the corporation, the two well site leaders on the rig, or the whole BP/Transocean/Haliburton tangle? My suspicion is that the theorem says different things at different levels and that the level you pick is doing most of the work, so pick one explicitly and then say what changes if you'd picked another. State the options, the states, and what counts as the evidence question E, and be honest about where that formalization is doing violence to the actual situation.

Second, go through the assumptions one at a time instead of in a lump. The theorem needs (a) learning Ek to make no difference to the utility of any act in any state, and (b) your later self to conditionalize and maximize. For each, say whether Deepwater Horizon breaks it, and if so what the break consists in concretely — not just that it happened.

Third, here's the bit I'm least sure I'm framing correctly. There's an intuition I keep running into, call it deniability as an asset: in a liability regime, documented knowledge can upgrade ordinary negligence into something much worse and can trigger duties you wouldn't otherwise have, so an organization might genuinely be better off not knowing. Is that a real counterexample — i.e. the utility of the act really does depend on Ek, so assumption (a) fails — or is it just smuggling a cost term back in and dressing it up as an exception? Straight answer, not both-sides.

Fourth: the negative pressure test is information they already had and misread. Is that simply assumption (b) failing in the wild, or is there something structurally interesting in the gap between failing to acquire evidence and failing to conditionalize on evidence you've got? Does the theorem have anything to say about the second failure at all, or is it outside its scope entirely?

And then the payoff question. Does this episode make Good's theorem look confirmed, anomalous, or irrelevant? My worry is that it's unfalsifiable in applied settings — any actual refusal of information can be redescribed after the fact as a preference for ignorance, which turns the theorem into a tautology about idealized agents rather than a claim anyone could have used to predict BP's behavior. If you think that worry lands, say so plainly. Same goes the other way: if you think the whole exercise is misframed — the theorem was never meant to apply to organizations, or I've got the sequence of events wrong, or a different blowout would test it better — tell me that up front rather than politely working around it.
