For three years I have run the shared-services group at a wholesale food distributor — roughly 2,400 employees, eleven depots, no economists on staff, and my own background is operations rather than anything academic. A large part of my job is deciding how scarce internal resources get handed out: refrigerated dock slots and trailer parking at the depots, the yard trucks and forklifts several departments share, the hours of a small platform engineering team that everyone wants, and more recently cold-storage capacity that we now bill back to two of our own retail divisions. Almost all of it is still allocated by committee queue and an annual budget, and the complaints never change. The teams that shout loudest get the slots. Nobody admits how much slack they are holding. And the internal chargeback rates finance built have changed nothing, because no department can actually refuse to pay them, so the numbers in the system tell us nothing about who values what.

I have been reading the socialist calculation debate on my own time: Mises's claim that without genuine prices for the means of production there is no common measure that lets you compare competing uses at all, Hayek's claim that the knowledge that would decide the question is local, tacit and constantly changing and never arrives at the center in a form that can be used, and Lange's reply that a planning board could simply set prices as parameters by rule and revise them when it saw surpluses and queues forming. It strikes me that this is an argument about my job in disguise. My group is a small planning board, and the choice between running a resource by committee, by internal price, or by letting teams trade among themselves is the choice those three were arguing about.

Please write me a decision procedure I can run on any one internal resource and get a defensible answer out. It should be a sequence of gates: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each, where the procedure stops at keep this on rules and a queue, where it says set a price and revise it against observed excess demand or slack, where it says hand the resource over and let the teams trade, and where the question is one of values rather than facts and has to go to a person. Keep the Mises test and the Hayek test separate — I suspect I have been confusing a resource I cannot compare across uses with a resource I cannot gather information about, and I want the procedure to sort my four complaints above into whichever failure each one actually is. Then tell me which parts of the original arguments you are keeping and which you are dropping to make this runnable, and what is lost by dropping them; and tell me where the analogy between a firm and an economy breaks down, since I have read that companies exist precisely because using markets costs something, and that an organization my size can be supervised directly in ways a national economy cannot.

Constraints: it has to be runnable by me and one analyst, neither of us trained in economics, in about half a day per resource; we revisit quarterly and ad hoc when a new shared resource appears; the output goes to a COO who signs off on it and to a finance business partner who will attack any figure we cannot justify, so each gate needs to say what evidence satisfies it. Mark every step that is really a judgment call wearing procedure's clothes.

Finally, run it on three cases: refrigerated dock slots and trailer parking at our two busiest depots; the platform engineering team's hours; and the cold-storage capacity we bill to our own retail divisions. I want at least one of these to come out against my preferences — I have spent two years building the chargeback system and I am prepared for the procedure to recommend tearing part of it out. Close with what the procedure cannot decide for me at all. No reading lists or citations; I need something I could hand my analyst on Monday.
