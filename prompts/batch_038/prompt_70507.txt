Our little sustainability book group just got through one of those popular collapsology bestsellers — not naming it, it's the one everybody's read — and the passage that blew up the discussion is where the author says civilizations are basically organisms: they get born, they grow, they hit maturity, they senesce, and they die, so collapse is just mortality at the scale of societies, inevitable, natural, and arguably even healthy since death makes room for new life. What I want picked apart is that he leans on it two ways at once, and I'm not sure the analogy can support either: first as comfort (stop fighting it, this is the natural order, grieve and adapt), and second as a hubris warning (any serious attempt to prevent it is like medicine trying to defeat aging). One of us is a demographer and says the analogy is carrying the entire argument, strip it out and nothing is left; another says that's unfair, all historical generalization is a stretched analogy, so what's the alternative. I've read enough Tainter to already be suspicious of life-cycle stories — his whole diminishing marginal returns thing seems to be about the economics of problem-solving, not senescence — and I keep coming back to the fact that the Maya and the western Roman empire didn't exactly die: political superstructures dissolved while populations and their descendants went on at lower levels of complexity, which sounds nothing like an organism. There's also Spengler lurking behind all of this, as far as I can tell, where cultures literally have a morphology and a fixed allotted lifespan, but I've never read him and I don't want to caricature him.

Can you take on the role of a philosopher of history who's also genuinely fluent in the archaeological and paleoclimate literature, not just a popular reader of it, and audit the organism analogy for me? Work out the correspondences carefully first — which features of organismic life the analogy is claiming to transfer (birth, growth sustained by metabolism, maturity, senescence as a programmed decline, clear individuation and boundaries, death as irreversible, and death's ecological function) — and then for each one say where the transfer fails and what the analogy quietly imports that societies don't actually have. Please be disciplined about separating the disanalogies that really bite against the two uses he's putting it to from the ones that are merely interesting; e.g. whether a society has determinate boundaries at all seems to me way more damaging to the comfort argument than the fact that organisms don't write histories of themselves, but you tell me. Also worth flagging anywhere the analogy does real explanatory work that I'd be foolish to give up, since I don't want a hit piece.

Format: brainstorm as much as you want internally, but what you hand me is exactly five numbered items in American English, each one two or three sentences, no introduction and no wrap-up paragraph. Items 1 through 4 each take a single correspondence from the organism story, say what it's supposed to map onto, and then say where it breaks and what it smuggles in. Item 5 is the verdict: does the combined conclusion — collapse is natural and resisting it is hubris — survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the analogy has to go, what would be a better one for this purpose, or is the honest answer that no analogy should be doing this kind of work at all.
