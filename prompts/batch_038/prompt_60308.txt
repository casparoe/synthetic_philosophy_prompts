I'm taking a philosophy of science course this term and we've just finished a unit on the Duhem–Quine problem and on how theories get rescued rather than refuted, and Malthus came up as the class's stock example of a prediction that failed spectacularly without, everyone agreed, being thereby refuted. In seminar a classmate defended that verdict with an analogy I keep turning over. Malthusian pressure, he said, is water behind a dam: human fertility pushes the population against the height that subsistence permits, and famine, disease, and war are the spillway that carries off the excess whenever the water reaches the crest. The Green Revolution did not drain the reservoir; it raised the dam. So the water is still pressing against concrete, and the fact that nobody has drowned is no evidence that the hydrology was wrong. He added that the Ehrlich–Simon wager over the price of a basket of metals is the same thing once more: a bet about how high the water will stand in a given decade, not about whether it rises at all.

Could you audit this analogy for me? I'd like an explicit table of correspondences, then the places where the mapping fails — including, if it does, the asymmetry that a spillway operates mechanically while Malthus's checks run through creatures who can foresee scarcity and change their behavior in response to the forecast. Sort the disanalogies into those that actually threaten the use he is making of it and those that are merely decorative. And then tell me what the analogy is quietly importing — whether water that only rises, whatever the engineering, is doing any work in making the theory look unrefutable rather than right — and whether the right-but-early defense survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If a better analogy exists for the relationship between a long-run prediction, its apparent failure, and the auxiliary hypotheses that keep it alive, say what it is and where it too gives out.
