ok so i teach a history-of-science seminar and we've hit the awkward week: kepler. everyone knows the three laws and the ellipse, nobody in my class knows that the same man cast horoscopes for pay his whole career, wrote a treatise arguing astrology has a firmer foundation than either its fans or its enemies admit, and thought the earth was a kind of animal with a soul that reacts to the angles the planets make with each other as seen from down here. i want to open the session by handing them a poem and letting them argue about it.

what i want: 24-30 lines, in his voice, blank verse (unrhymed pentameter, and it can be loose about the pentameter as long as it feels like a line of verse and not chopped prose). one formal constraint that matters: pick a single line and let it come back twice, once early and once near the end, same words both times, but the second time it has to mean something completely different — that doubling is the whole point, because it's how i want them to feel the difference between the astrology he despised and the astrology he kept.

the argument of the poem should move like his own. start with what he threw overboard: the twelve signs and the houses, which he regarded as human bookkeeping with no natural force behind it, the nativity readings for dukes and generals, the stock predictions he was contractually obliged to write and privately gagged at. and let the turn be the moment he says what he's actually defending and why: not the signs but the angles. the aspects are geometry — the circle being cut into halves, thirds, quarters, fifths, sixths by figures you can genuinely construct, and only those count, because a division that can't be constructed can't be known, and what can't be known can't move anything. then the mechanism: some formative faculty in the earth and in living things, stirred by those angles the way weather is stirred, the moon dragging the tides about, seeds and souls attending to the sky the way an animal attends to a stimulus it didn't choose. he's the third party stepping between the physician who says the whole thing is superstition and the court astrologer who says all of it is literally true, and the poem should be aware that both sides hate him for it.

tone: no modern sneer, and no rescue mission either — dont turn him into a skeptical astronomer who merely tolerated the horoscopes for the paycheck. he's a devout man who thinks god geometrized and that the human soul is a small harmonic instrument attuned to the same ratios the planets trace. hold both things at once: the guy who proved mars's orbit can't be a circle, and the guy who wrote a duke's nativity for money and meant at least some of it.

end on something that stays open rather than resolving, so my students can fight about whether he was inconsistent or just more careful than we give him credit for. don't cite or name his books, and please no latin titles dropped in for flavor — the ideas should carry it, not the bibliography.
