I spent thirty-eight years as an analytical chemist and have come to philosophy late, with a working prejudice in favour of apparatus. That prejudice suggests to me that Bertrand's chord problem is standardly over-read: that its three answers — 1/3, 1/2, and 1/√2 — are not one principle misapplied three times, but the well-defined outputs of three different randomising devices, so that the apparent contradiction confuses a distribution over chords with a distribution over chord-producing procedures.

Please construct the strongest argument you can for this position: that the principle of indifference is legitimately applied only over outcomes some specified procedure of finite resolution can distinguish; that under this restriction each of the three classical results is correct relative to its own device and none of them conflicts with the others; and that the paradox therefore does not refute indifference but exposes the error of positing chords apart from any means of generating them.

Set out your premises explicitly, say which of them a defender of symmetry-based methods for fixing a unique prior would grant and which they would reject, and concede honestly whether your restriction retains any content at all in the case where the device is unknown to us.
