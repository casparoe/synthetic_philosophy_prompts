Im a senior in a philosophy elective on rationality and we do anonymous peer grading, I got this one and honestly I don't feel confident enough to call it. The question was worth 15 points, 5 for each part. Can you grade it and write the point-by-point comments I should hand back — what's solid, what's shaky, what's actually wrong — plus the score for each part and a short overall note? I just want to not give credit for something false or dock them for something that's fine.

**The question we were given:**

Yara prefers concert ticket A to ticket B, B to ticket C, and C to ticket A. A scalper makes her three offers in a row, one at a time: first, swap A for B and he'll give her $1; second, swap B for C plus $1; third, swap C for A plus $1. She takes all three. She ends up holding A again, with $3 less.

(a) Explain why each offer looks like a good deal to her at the moment it's made, and what the setup is supposed to prove about her preferences.
(b) Now suppose Yara knows the whole sequence of offers in advance. Does that change whether she ends up $3 down? Explain.
(c) One reply to the whole argument says it doesn't really depend on her preferences at all, but on assumptions about how an agent handles choices spread out over time. Say what that reply comes to and whether you buy it.

**Their answer:**

(a) Each time, she's getting a ticket she likes better *and* a dollar on top, so by her own lights no single trade is a mistake. That's the point: you can't catch her doing anything wrong at any step and still she walks away worse off with the exact same ticket she started with. The loss is certain, not a gamble that went badly, so you can't blame luck. This is supposed to show that preferring A to B, B to C, and C to A isn't a legitimate taste, it's a defect, because a preference ordering that lets someone drain you for sure can't be rational. It's basically the same argument as a Dutch book: incoherent mental states guarantee you'll be exploited, and that's how we know they're irrational.

(b) No, knowing in advance doesn't save her. When the first offer comes she's still getting something better plus a dollar, and if she turns it down she's turning down free money. She'd have to be able to change her mind about A vs B just because of what happens later, which would be its own kind of inconsistency. Foresight doesn't create new reasons.

(c) The reply is that the argument only bites on agents who are short-sighted or forget what they were doing, and maybe real people usually are, so it shows something about psychology rather than about the preferences themselves. I partly buy it, since the scalper has to be the one running the sequence. But Yara's ordering is still intransitive either way, and if she somehow avoids the pump that just means she was never really pumped — you'd have to conclude her choices reveal transitive preferences after all. So the reply doesn't rescue the ordering, it only rescues her wallet.
