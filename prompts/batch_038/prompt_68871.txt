I'm a curator at a municipal history museum, and every autumn we relabel: gallery titles, wall panels, exhibition names, and the date ranges printed on the floor plan. The complaints run both ways. Some visitors and a couple of local academics tell us that terms like "the Middle Ages," "the Renaissance," and "the Scientific Revolution" are retrospective inventions that flatter one corner of the world and quietly write everyone else out; donors and school groups tell us that if we stop using familiar period names, nobody will know what a gallery is about before they walk in. Our head of interpretation has to sign off on every label, and she has asked for something we can apply consistently instead of arguing case by case.

Write a decision procedure my team can run on any proposed period label before it goes on a wall: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a passing answer at each, which failures mean the label has to be rewritten rather than merely caveated with a footnote, and where the procedure must hand the decision back to a curator because the question is about what we want the gallery to do rather than about what the past was like. Ground it in what the arguments about periodization actually say — that these names were coined after the fact by people with a position to take, that many carry evaluations inside them, that a term can fit one region or one domain and not another, that the start and end dates are almost never where the change was — rather than in generic advice about being inclusive. It has to be something two of us can apply in under an hour per label with no specialist historiography at hand, it should tell us what to write on the wall when a label only partly survives, and it should be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure.

Then run it on three cases: "The Scientific Revolution, 1543–1687" as the title of our instrument gallery; a "Medieval World" gallery whose cases cover Islamic Spain, the Mali Empire, and Ming China; and the phrase "the end of the Middle Ages" in a wall panel about the arrival of printing in Europe. Assume at least one fails outright and that I won't like losing it: say which one, why the tests catch it, and what we should put in its place that still tells a visitor what the room contains.

And if you think the request is confused — that no ordered sequence of tests can decide between a familiar label and a defensible one, and the honest answer is just to lay out what each choice costs — say so plainly rather than handing me a flowchart that pretends to settle it.
