I teach an upper-level seminar on Rousseau's political thought, and I want to close the week on the Letter to d'Alembert on the Theatre with Chekhov's The Seagull. Write the reading I would hand my students, and argue against it wherever it strains.

State first, in your own words, four claims from the Letter that the play can be made to test: that pity felt in the theatre is discharged there and so relieves the spectator of real beneficence; that the stage trades in amour-propre, corrupting the performer who lives on applause and the spectator who sits in judgment of him; that spectacle belongs to idle cities and sits ill among people whose days are given over to work and weather; and that a free community should instead hold open-air festivals in which the people are a spectacle to one another rather than spectators of paid professionals.

Then take each claim to the play scene by scene: Treplev's drama performed by the lake to an audience of five from his own household; Arkadina's dread of coming second; Trigorin's inability to experience anything except as material; Nina's ruin and her refusal to renounce the vocation; and a suicide that happens offstage and is concealed by the doctor so that the household can go on playing lotto. Rousseau holds that comedy is obliged to render the honest man ridiculous, and Chekhov subtitles this play a comedy. Is Treplev his Alceste, and does that subtitle convict Chekhov or Rousseau?

My thesis, which I want pressed as hard as it is defended: the estate is Rousseau's festival gone wrong — everyone at once spectacle and spectator, with paralysis rather than civic joy as the result — but Nina is the case Rousseau has no room for, since she finds in the stage a discipline of endurance that no festival could supply. Say plainly where the play refuses this framing, and end with what Chekhov contributes to the debate about spectatorship that Rousseau's argument and its standard liberal rebuttals both miss. Paraphrase throughout; no quotations, no bibliography.
