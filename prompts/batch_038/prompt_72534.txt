I sit on the design committee for a citizens' assembly our county will convene each year to decide how the road-maintenance budget is spent. Members are to be drawn by lot from the voter registration list, stratified so that the assembly matches the county by age, income, and neighbourhood. We are deadlocked over two rules, and I suspect our chair is conflating them.

The first rule is mine: anyone summoned should be free to decline, with the vacancy filled by a fresh draw from the same stratum. My reasoning is that people who do not want to be there will simply stop showing up, and an assembly of no-shows decides nothing. The chair objects that this turns the assembly into a club of the eager and ruins the mirror.

The second rule is his: anyone who has held elected office or worked for the county is excluded, so that insiders cannot dominate. In the same breath he wants to require that at least a third of members hold a college degree, because otherwise, he says, the professional staff who brief the assembly will steamroll it. When I pointed out that these two rules pull in opposite directions, he replied that one is a question of legitimacy and the other a question of competence, and that these are separate matters.

Is he right that they are separate? And should I go on defending my own rule, given that it produces exactly the effect he says it does?
