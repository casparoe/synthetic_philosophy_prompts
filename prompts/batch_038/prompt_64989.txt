I sit on a three-person mediation panel a state health agency set up, and we file at the end of the month on how a hospital system and a small rural clinic network should split the value of a joint telehealth venture. If they don't agree the venture dies: our economist puts the hospital's fallback (an in-house version it can run alone) at $2M and the network's fallback (a federal grant it already holds) at $1M. Together the venture is worth $12M, but the frontier is bent, because the network can't usefully absorb more than about $6M -- it can't hire and stand up sites any faster than that -- so the most it could ever get out of this deal is $6M, while the hospital's best case is $11M (leaving the network exactly its fallback). That's enough bend to make the two standard rules disagree. Maximizing the Nash product of gains-over-fallback gives $6.5M/$5.5M. Kalai-Smorodinsky, which equalizes the ratio of each side's gain to the distance from its fallback to its best conceivable outcome in this deal, gives about $7.8M/$4.2M. So $1.3M swings to the hospital purely because the network is the party that can't use more money.

My co-panelist wants KS and she has a point I can't answer. The network circulated a non-binding letter of intent last week with a second health system that would lift its absorptive capacity to $9M; under KS that's worth it roughly a million dollars, and under Nash it's worth literally nothing. She says thats the monotonicity axiom -- a party whose real options grow shouldn't be left standing still -- and that the Nash rule gives it up. I worry the other way: under KS a party improves its position by announcing a bigger aspiration it hasn't committed to, not by changing anything about the actual bargain.

Draft the recommendation we file. It needs the split for this case, the standing rule the panel should adopt for future cases, a decision on the letter of intent, and a straight statement of the strongest case for KS that she'd recognize as her own argument rather than a strawman.
