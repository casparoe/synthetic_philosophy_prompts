I sit on a seven-member advisory panel that rules on proposed transplants of Nordic institutions into other democracies. Our terms of reference are narrow: we are to advise only on transplants that will still be operating as designed ten years after adoption, and we do not opine on whether a proposal is desirable in principle. Our minutes record the verdict, the vote margin, and any conditions attached — but never the reasoning, because the members declined to write opinions. I am now trying to reconstruct, for a methods appendix, what rule the panel has actually been applying.

The panel codes prospective recipient countries by profile rather than by name, since several are composites constructed for the exercise. Treat the profiles below as stipulated data; do not attempt to identify the countries, and do not add features to them beyond what is listed.

- **A** — population 6 million; one dominant language; stranger-trust in the top band of the surveys we use; confidence in public agencies high; revenue administration in the top decile, with near-universal third-party reporting of wages and interest; collective agreements covering about 15 percent of employees, with bargaining conducted firm by firm; tax-to-GDP close to the Nordic average.
- **B** — population 85 million; several large regional and linguistic groups; stranger-trust in the bottom band; confidence in public agencies low; revenue administration mid-range, with a substantial cash and informal sector; collective coverage about 60 percent at sector level; tax-to-GDP well below the Nordic average.
- **C** — population 45 million; heterogeneous; stranger-trust high, with dense membership associations; confidence in public agencies low; revenue administration mid-range; collective coverage about 70 percent at sector level; tax-to-GDP low.
- **E** — population 10 million; homogeneous; stranger-trust high; confidence in public agencies high; revenue administration top decile; collective coverage about 75 percent at sector level; tax-to-GDP at the Nordic average.

The five decided proposals so far:

1. A universal child allowance — paid automatically from pre-populated tax records, with no application, no means test, no eligibility interview, and no verification of household circumstances — transplanted into **B**. Rejected, 7–0.
2. The same allowance transplanted into **A**. Approved, 7–0.
3. A Nordic-style labor-market package — weak statutory protection against individual dismissal, high income replacement, an individual legal entitlement to retraining, delivered through sector-wide collective agreements — transplanted into **A**. Rejected, 5–2.
4. The same package transplanted into **E**. Approved subject to a ten-year sunset with a mandatory review at the midpoint, 6–1.
5. A Nordic school-governance model — no routine inspection of schools, no test-based evaluation of teachers, budgets devolved to municipalities and head teachers, entry to teaching restricted to a research-based graduate degree — transplanted into **C**. Rejected, 4–3.

The proposal now before us is the school-governance model from item 5 again, this time for **F**:

- **F** — population 12 million; homogeneous; stranger-trust in the bottom band; confidence in public agencies high, with a competitively recruited and well-paid civil service; revenue administration top decile, with near-universal third-party reporting; collective coverage about 70 percent at sector level; tax-to-GDP at the Nordic average; teachers recruited from general bachelor's programs and licensed by a national examination rather than by a graduate degree; and school funding follows the pupil, so parents choose among schools and schools compete for enrollment.

Please give me the following:

1. Enumerate every candidate rule that is consistent with all five precedents — including rules keyed to generalized trust among citizens, to confidence in public agencies, to administrative capacity, to fiscal capacity, to scale and homogeneity, to the presence of companion institutions, and to anything else the record supports. For each, state whether it can only be doing necessary-condition work or whether the record licenses something stronger, and say which precedents would have come out differently had the panel been applying it as a sufficient condition. Flag any precedent that resists every rule you list.
2. Predict the verdict on **F**, with a predicted margin and your confidence. Say which of your candidate rules drive the prediction and which are silent.
3. Identify the closest analog among the five precedents and state precisely which features of the recipient differ, and whether each difference cuts toward approval or rejection.
4. Say honestly whether the record genuinely underdetermines the verdict. If it does, say what principle the panel would be adopting by ruling either way — in particular, what it would be committing itself to about whether the trust these institutions run on is a stock that must already be present or a flow the institutions themselves can generate.
5. Explain how you read the sunset condition attached in item 4, and whether it is evidence about the panel's rule or merely about its politics.
6. Name the single precedent that does the most work in your reading, and say how your prediction for **F** would change if that precedent had gone the other way.
7. Propose the next transplant proposal we should schedule in order to separate the rules that survive your analysis, specifying the institution and the recipient profile it would need.

Argue from the record and the stipulated profiles alone. Do not cite studies, name countries, or recommend sources; if you need a fact that the profiles do not supply, say so explicitly and mark it as an assumption rather than filling the gap silently.
