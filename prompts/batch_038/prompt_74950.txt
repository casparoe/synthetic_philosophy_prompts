For the past year I have been writing the grading standard that our warehouse uses to decide whether a returned garment goes back on the shelf as new or is resold as used. The work exposed a difficulty I cannot resolve on my own, and I would like to know what principle, if any, underlies the verdicts I keep giving. I will state them in the order they struck me, and I should warn you that at least two of them look incompatible.

1. A jacket tried on indoors for two minutes, tags attached, no odor, no marks: not used. I have no hesitation, and I do not think my confidence reflects unusual expertise.
2. A jacket worn daily for a fortnight, frayed cuffs, smell of smoke: used. Equally unhesitating.
3. A jacket worn twice outdoors, no visible marks anywhere, no odor, packaging thrown away: neither verdict comes naturally. I resist saying it is used and I resist saying it is not. What I want to insist on is that this is not a matter of needing more evidence. Brighter lighting, a longer inspection, a fabric analysis, testimony from the customer — none would settle it, because none would tell me anything about the jacket I do not already know. My ignorance here does not look like a gap in my information.
4. Take any two jackets alike in every respect except that one carries a single faint additional scuff on an inside seam. I cannot believe that one is used and the other is not. Yet starting from case 1 and adding scuffs one at a time I arrive at case 2. So I appear committed both to there being a last not-used jacket and to there not being one.
5. Asked to point at the first used jacket, I would nominate a stretch of roughly forty consecutive items and say that each is as good a candidate as any other. I do not think any further fact — about how the garment was worn, about anyone's intentions, about the words we use, about anything — would narrow the field to one. And yet I do not think all forty are used, or that none of them is.
6. Of case 3 I accept, trivially, that either the jacket is used or it is not. But I also want to assert something I suspect is stronger, and I cannot tell whether it is: that there is a particular way things are with this jacket, and that one of the two holds of it. Tell me whether this second claim adds anything to the first, or whether I am restating a logical form with emphasis.
7. I am unwilling to say of case 3 that it is definitely not used. I am equally unwilling to say that it is definitely a case of the intermediate kind. When asked whether it is definitely definitely not used, the question seems to lose its grip rather than receive an answer, and the same at every further level; the levels do not appear to terminate anywhere.
8. Contrast our written rule that any item whose original packaging has been opened counts as used. That rule has a sharp boundary and I am entirely comfortable with it, precisely because we made it. But I am uncomfortable with the thought that the ordinary notion has a sharp boundary, and I cannot explain why the two should differ — if anything, the stipulated rule seems to borrow whatever point it has from the ordinary one.
9. Wagers bring out something I cannot reconcile. For each adjacent pair in the series I would take even money on the claim that if the first is used, so is the second; each conditional looks safe to me taken singly. But I would not back the conjunction of all two hundred at any odds, because I am certain the conjunction is false. Separately: I will wager either way on case 1 and either way on case 2, but on case 3 I will take neither side at any price — and when I try to say why, I produce two explanations pointing in opposite directions. One is that there is no fact there for me to be right about. The other is that there is such a fact but that I am barred from knowing it, on the grounds that if I could know this jacket to be used I would have to be able to know the same of every jacket indistinguishable from it, and so of the next, which I plainly cannot do.

What I would like from you:

- State the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that would generate these verdicts, clause by clause, identifying which numbered case motivates each clause. State them precisely enough that I could tell whether some new case falls under them.
- For each candidate, say which of my verdicts it cannot deliver and which single verdict I ought to abandon in order to adopt it. Where a candidate saves all nine, say what the cost is, and whether that cost is one of complexity, of what the world must contain, or of leaving something unexplained.
- I expect that a principle accommodating all nine will be baroque and that a simple one will overturn some. Tell me which trade you would make on my behalf and why. I do not want the options presented as equally respectable.
- Diagnose 4 and 9 specifically: are they genuinely contradictory or only apparently so, and if only apparently, which inference am I making that I am not entitled to make?
- Say what your preferred candidate implies for situations I have not described. Two inspectors who disagree about a jacket inside the forty-item stretch: can both be right, and does it matter that neither could ever tell? A garment worn hard but leaving no trace, where the relevant respects come apart and no single ordering captures them all? And the stock toy examples philosophers reach for with problems of this shape — does your candidate deliver the same verdicts there, and if it does, why does my warehouse feel like the harder case?
- Tell me whether any of the candidates is merely notational: a redescription of my predicament in a technical vocabulary that leaves everything exactly as puzzling as before. If one of them is, say so rather than offering it as a solution.

You are welcome to attach names to the positions as you go, but names are not the deliverable; principles are.
