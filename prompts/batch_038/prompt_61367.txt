--- DRAFT ---

The textbook story runs in one direction. Ruth Benedict's wartime study of Japan gave the contrast between guilt cultures and shame cultures its widest currency, and E. R. Dodds applied the same labels to the Greeks, telling a story in which archaic Homeric shame gives way over the centuries to the guilt of the philosophers and the Christians. Dodds warned that his labels invited misconception, and the warning was deserved: the scheme treats shame as essentially a matter of having an audience and guilt as essentially a matter of conscience, which makes the first look like a social mechanism of control and the second like a moral achievement. It is that asymmetry, rather than the historical sequence, that later work dismantled.

Bernard Williams's objection is the important one. Shame need not involve a real audience; the spectator can be internalized — an idealized other whose view of me I have taken over — and when it is, shame is not less mature than guilt but more ambitious, concerning my standing as a whole rather than this or that act, and so able to register losses for which guilt has no vocabulary. The psychological literature appears to point the other way, at the level of the individual. In the tradition of June Tangney's work, shame-proneness is associated with withdrawal, with anger turned outward, and with poorer adjustment, while guilt-proneness — the disposition to construe a transgression as something one did rather than something one is — is associated with apology, reparation, and less aggression. If guilt is the healthier disposition in persons, it is tempting to conclude that a guilt culture is the healthier culture and that the question of choice is thereby settled.

I think that inference is a category mistake, and I want to substitute a different variable. Call it the audit problem. An emotion can serve as a culture's default instrument of moral regulation only insofar as third parties can cheaply detect both that it has occurred and that it has been discharged. Shame passes this test readily: its object is a public standing, its signs are legible, and its resolution — apology, recompense, a ceremony of reinstatement — can be witnessed and recorded. Guilt fails it: its object is private, its signs are suppressed, and its discharge is frequently indistinguishable from ordinary behavior. The implication is uncomfortable. Institutions that must govern at scale will recruit shame even while their official ideology praises guilt, because shame is the emotion they can see. What we call the historical shift from shame to guilt then looks less like moral insight than like the arrival of new techniques for making the inner legible: the confessional, the pastoral interview, the parole hearing, the written apology, the case file, the permanent record. Guilt became preachable once it became auditable.

That reframes the question of choice. A culture does not select its dominant moral emotion by adopting values; it selects it indirectly, by changing what it costs to audit an inner state. This is not passivity — altering audit costs is itself a political act, and often a drastic one — but it is invisible from inside the moralizing register, where the choice presents itself as a decision about what to praise. Two cases sit at the edge of the account. John Braithwaite's distinction between reintegrative and disintegrative shaming suggests that the operative variable may be reintegration rather than publicity: shaming that ends in readmission works, shaming that ends in exclusion does not, and neither has much to do with guilt. And anonymous mass shaming online has publicity with no possibility of reinstatement, which is part of what makes it feel like punishment without limit.

I am left with a worry I cannot resolve from inside the draft: I may be sliding between shame as an emotion and shaming as a practice. The audit problem is obviously a problem for practices; whether it is a problem for an emotion type is another matter, and if it is only the former, my thesis is considerably less interesting than it sounds.
