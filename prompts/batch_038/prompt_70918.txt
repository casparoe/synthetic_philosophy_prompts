Retired from a national lab two years back after 37 years of magnetic measurements — mostly B-H loops on soft ferrites — and I've fallen into philosophy, which I never had time for. Lately I've been chewing on the money pump argument against intransitive preferences, and something clicked for me. I'd like to know whether I'm on the right track or just talking nonsense.

Here's the click: a money pump is just a hysteresis loop. You walk the agent around A preferred to B, B to C, C back to A, and at the end she's holding the same option she started with minus a few cents per step. That's the area enclosed by the loop, energy dissipated per cycle, the integral of H dB. Same geometry, different substrate.

If that's right, two things follow, and I want someone to tell me where the reasoning breaks. First, whether intransitivity is irrational starts to look like a measurement question instead of a logical one: you don't have to eliminate the loop, you just anneal it down. Every material has some coercivity; a soft enough one is worthless as a permanent magnet but perfectly fine sitting in a transformer. So how small does the loop area have to get before the pump stops mattering, and has anybody actually put units on it? Dollars per cycle divided by what? What plays the role of the applied field when the utilities are supposed to be merely ordinal?

Second, and this is the part I want to press: if there's no field-like quantity to integrate against, then the pump argument must be smuggling in cardinal utility behind the scenes, and it collapses into a claim about measurable intensities that nobody really believes anyway. Isn't that decisive? I keep expecting to find someone making this objection and I haven't, which makes me suspect I'm missing something — but I can't see what.
