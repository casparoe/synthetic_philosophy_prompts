I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine and I'm drafting a piece on archives, so I need someone to help me figure out what I actually believe about when a record has to survive. Gut verdicts, in no particular order:

- A paper keeping its whole archive searchable, including a 1994 story about a man who was arrested and never charged: bad. He should be able to get it delisted.
- The same paper refusing to delist a 1994 story about a councilman who took bribes: good. That one stays forever.
- A site selling removal of your mugshot for $300: obviously bad. A genealogy database charging for access to the same mugshot 70 years later, when everyone in it is dead: fine, and I don't feel guilty saying so.
- A town renaming a street that honored a slave trader: good. Scrubbing the paperwork showing who it was named after and why: bad — even though what changes on the ground is identical.
- A catalogued, climate-controlled oral history recording nobody has played in 40 years: I'd say the memory isn't there. The same story retold at a family table, drifting a bit each generation: I'd say the memory is there. Fidelity points the other way.
- Destroying the last print of a film made to incite a massacre: bad. Destroying a victim's recorded testimony of abuse to spare her family: also bad, but for a seperate reason I can't articulate. That's the pair I keep circling.

My tentative rule: a record may be suppressed when what it records no longer bears on anyone's standing today, and when the suppression is announced rather than done quietly. Does that survive the list? If not, give me the replacement — one principle with clauses, each clause tied to the case that motivates it — and then run it on ones I haven't thought of: a platform deleting a dead teenager's account at his parents' request; a model trained on everything that can't unlearn one person; a truth commission that burns its transcripts to protect witnesses; a state that keeps the file but classifies it for 75 years. I've read both camps on this (Margalit's duty to remember radical evil, Rieff's case that memory turned into an industry that manufactures grievance) and neither one settles my list. Tell me straight which of my verdicts your principle can't save — if it comes down to it I'd rather lose the mugshot one than the renaming one.
