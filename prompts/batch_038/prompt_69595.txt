I run a reading group on the typogenetics material in Gödel, Escher, Bach and on von Neumann's construction for self-reproducing automata, and I have assembled an argument that now strikes me as proving far too much. I want a diagnosis, not reassurance.

First, so the premises cannot drift, I will stipulate the machinery. Call a system a *strand system* if it has: (i) lowercase strings over a four-letter alphabet, which are inert descriptions; (ii) an expression step taking a lowercase string to an uppercase string read in non-overlapping triplets, each triplet selecting one of finitely many operators; (iii) operators that rewrite lowercase strings by finitely many rules, including rules that duplicate a string; (iv) at least one *self-reading* operator, one that can bind to and rewrite the very lowercase string encoding it. Such a system is a *replicator* when some configuration yields, from a lowercase string s, a new configuration containing a copy of s together with the operators needed to copy s again — Hofstadter's picture with the codon table left open, plus von Neumann's requirement that the description be used once as instruction and once as data. Call a replicator *certified* if it also contains a verification procedure V which, applied to a parent string and a putative copy, terminates in a certificate of identity or of difference.

The argument:

(1) A replicator must carry within its own resources a description sufficient to determine what it makes; otherwise the offspring is not produced by the parent but merely happens to coincide with it.
(2) If the replicator is certified, that description must include a description of V, since a certificate is worthless unless it covers the certifier: a V that has itself drifted would certify drifted copies as faithful.
(3) So a certified replicator contains a self-description from which it can establish claims about its own copying behavior, including claims of the form "this output is identical to me."
(4) Any system whose rules can count positions in strings, match triplets, and branch on matches can encode enough arithmetic to represent its own syntax; a strand system of the stipulated kind can.
(5) By diagonalization, such a system, if consistent, contains a sentence asserting its own unprovability that is true but unprovable within it, and it cannot prove its own consistency.
(6) The self-referential sentence available to a certified replicator is precisely a claim about copying failure — "this strand is not faithfully reproduced" — and by (5) the replicator can establish neither it nor its negation without ceasing to be consistent.
(7) Therefore a certified replicator cannot certify its own fidelity: there is always a true claim about its replication that its verification procedure cannot establish.
(8) Uncertified error is uncorrected error, since correction presupposes certification.
(9) Hence, by induction over generations, no lineage of certified replicators maintains fidelity; drift accumulates without bound, and mutation-driven evolution is a logical necessity rather than a contingent feature of chemistry.
(10) But this refutes things I am sure of. A routine that copies a file and recomputes a checksum over the copy does what (9) declares impossible, and a polymerase with proofreading and mismatch repair is standardly described as achieving per-base error rates low enough that a genome is passed on essentially intact, generation after generation. Worse, (7) seems to entail that nothing can ever verify any of its own outputs.

Two things I will not surrender: diagonalization, in whatever form it actually has, and (1), since without it there is no von Neumann replicator to discuss. I would rather lose (2), (6), or (8).

What I want from you:

(a) Identify the step at which the argument breaks, and say precisely what is conflated there.
(b) Explain why that step looked innocuous — which feature of the DNA–Gödel analogy actively invites it, rather than merely permitting it.
(c) Price the alternatives: for each of (2), (3), (6), (8), and (9), state what an account of self-replication loses if that premise is dropped, and which drop is cheapest overall.
(d) Say whether the over-reach in (10) is a symptom of the same flaw or an independent second one; and whether appealing to an external verifier — a checksum computed by a distinct subsystem, repair proteins distinct from the polymerase — is a genuine escape or merely relocates the problem one level up, with a regress to show for it.
(e) Finally, once the argument is repaired, what survives of the isomorphism? If the content of typogenetics is not that replicators inherit the incompleteness of the formal systems they resemble, say what the isomorphism is in fact tracking, and whether the strongest true claim in this neighborhood has any bite for biology or is only a redescription of the obvious fact that copying is fallible.
