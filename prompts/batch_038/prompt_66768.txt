I write about science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a piece about what kind of knowing a language model actually does. A source of mine keeps telling me I've got the wrong toolkit and that I should be looking at Nyāya epistemology instead of the usual post-Gettier stuff.

So: play the role of a professional philosopher who works on both classical Indian epistemology and AI, and think carefully before you answer — start with a few lines of brainstorming about which pramāṇas are even serious candidates, then commit to numbers.

Take the four Nyāya pramāṇas — perception, inference, comparison (upamāna), and testimony (śabda) — and give each a score out of 10 for how well it describes what's going on when a model puts out a confident factual claim about the world. Then rank them best-fit to worst-fit, two or three sentences each on why it lands where it does. I want you to weigh the Naiyāyikas' own standards here rather than doing loose word-matching: what they require for an invariable connection between a reason and what it's supposed to prove, and what they require of a speaker before their words can generate knowledge at all. On those terms, would they grant a model any real knowledge, or is the whole comparison a category error?

And if you think there's a fifth thing models do that none of the four covers, say what it is and whether the Mīmāṃsaka extras — postulation (arthāpatti), non-apprehension of an absence — would handle it better.

No citations or reading lists please, just your judgement and the reasoning behind each score.
