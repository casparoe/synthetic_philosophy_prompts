Two friends of mine, Nadia and Omar, had a prolonged argument last month which they have now asked me to help settle. I have no stake in it and, honestly, could not follow much of it at the time, so I am reporting the exchange as neutrally as I can.

The occasion was Nadia's habit of watching, every year on the anniversary of her father's death, a documentary about the hospice where he spent his final weeks. It is not a fiction: her father appears in it, and the death it depicts is his. She has now watched it six times. She describes the ninety minutes as the worst ninety minutes of her year, says she dreads pressing play, and says she will press play again next year and would be alarmed about herself if she did not.

(1) Omar's opening move was that at least one of her descriptions must be false. Nobody, he said, chooses what she sincerely believes to be the worst ninety minutes of her year. So either she exaggerates when she calls them the worst, or she is not in fact getting what she says she gets from them, or the grief she feels there is not the same kind of bad thing that grief is in ordinary life. He favored the third: sorrow felt in a chair, on cue, in response to images, is a milder species than sorrow that arrives unbidden, and that is precisely why she can welcome it.

(2) Nadia rejected this as a slur on the experience. The feeling, she said, is the same feeling; she weeps in the same way and is tired in the same way afterward. What differs is not the feeling but the fact that nothing is required of her. Ordinary grief arrives with obligations and with people depending on her, whereas these ninety minutes are the only stretch in the year in which she gives her father her undivided attention. She is not choosing the grief; she is choosing the attention, and the grief is what the attention costs.

(3) Omar replied that this concedes his real point. If the feeling is identical, and identical feelings are equally bad, then she has merely described paying a genuine cost for a genuine benefit — which is unremarkable. The puzzle, he said, only looks like a puzzle if one assumes that people always act in order to feel better, an assumption nobody need make. On his account the puzzle is therefore a philosopher's fabrication, and Nadia's case does nothing to revive it.

(4) Nadia answered that the cost is not genuine, and that this is what he keeps walking past. She would take a pill that removed a headache without a second thought, she said, and would refuse a pill that removed the grief of the viewing — and she would refuse it even if the pill left the attention entirely intact and merely took the ache out of it. There are unpleasant feelings one would decline to be relieved of, and once that is admitted, the word unpleasant has stopped doing the work the puzzle needs from it.

(5) Omar's response was that she was equivocating. Refusing the pill shows only that she wants the feeling for the sake of what it brings, which is an instrumental reason; instrumental reasons do not make a feeling pleasant. On her logic a dental drill would count as pleasant whenever it prevents something worse.

(6) Nadia's last move was that the two cases come apart exactly here. She would gladly accept a painless drill, because what she wants from the dentist is the tooth; she would refuse a griefless memorial, because what she wants is that feeling and not only its effects.

Please work through the exchange move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each of the six moves, give a rating from clearly mistaken to clearly right with a sentence or two of justification, and say specifically where a move succeeds and where it only appears to. Then tell me who is more right overall and what each of the two is overlooking that neither raised.

Two further requests. First, a number of the familiar ways of dissolving this problem look unavailable in Nadia's case: the film is not fiction, her father really is in it, and she certainly believes the events occurred, so nothing can turn on her awareness that she is not in the presence of the thing she grieves. Please state explicitly which of the standard routes out of the problem are blocked by these particulars and which remain open to one or the other of them. Second, as you go, please distinguish what you take to be settled among people who work on these questions from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position to me as though it were the consensus.
