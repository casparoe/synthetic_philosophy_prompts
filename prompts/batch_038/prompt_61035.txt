I'm drafting a long essay that pairs Ibsen's *An Enemy of the People* with Arendt's two essays on lying and truth in politics: her distinction between factual truth and rational truth, her insistence that matters of fact are politically intolerable precisely because they cannot be argued with and can only be lied about, and her later claim about the Pentagon Papers that the modern political lie no longer hides a reality so much as installs a fabricated substitute, with the deceivers eventually taken in by their own work. I want a critical reading of the play through that apparatus, not a catalogue of matching scenes.

Three things I can't settle. First, the town does not merely suppress Stockmann's report. It rebuilds its account of him — a crank, an ingrate who would ruin the community that made him, a man itching to rule — and the newspaper, the meeting, and his brother all converge on that replacement story. Is that organized lying in Arendt's sense, or is she simply the wrong instrument here, since there is no regime, no official secrecy, and no party whose image is being managed: only a self-governing town discovering that a fact it cannot afford must not be allowed to exist? I want your view on whether that structural difference — a truth with no constituency, hidden from no one in particular and costly to everyone, including the man who discloses it — is something Arendt's framework can absorb or something that breaks it.

Second, Stockmann's own drift. By the fourth act he has converted a matter of fact into a denunciation of the majority, and by the end he resolves to stay and stand alone, which the play appears to reward. Arendt would read that as a flight from plurality and a truth-teller's will to rule. Make the strongest case the play offers against her, and say plainly whether you think it wins.

Third, does her material on self-deception — written about policy intellectuals rather than about a man who is simply right — have anything to say about a truth-teller whose certainty has hardened into a character trait?

Assume I know the play and the essays well: no plot summaries, no quotation, no pointing me toward secondary literature. Close by telling me what the play knows about the politics of truth that Arendt's arguments, taken alone, miss.
