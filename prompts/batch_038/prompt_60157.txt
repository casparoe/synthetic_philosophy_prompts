I spent thirty-four years doing reactor physics and neutron transport at a national lab, with two stints on safeguards and one winter in the mid-80s where I mostly drank bad coffee and thought about launch-on-warning. Retired five years now, and I've been reading philosophy properly for the first time in my life. Our interdisciplinary program runs a senior seminar on the ethics of nuclear weapons and they've talked me into setting one of the final-exam questions, which is a joke because its the first one I've ever set. My co-teacher (a real philosopher, very patient with me) says what she needs from me is the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme, since I won't be grading it myself.

Here's the thing I want it to turn on. The argument the students will have read is Kavka's, roughly: it can be rational to form the intention to retaliate, but once the deterrent fails it would be irrational to carry it out, and a rational agent can't intend what she believes she won't do. They'll all be able to recite that. I want a question that makes reciting it useless.

So build the question around a device I invented, and state it in full inside the question so nobody needs outside facts:

The Two-Key Protocol. A nuclear state announces, in verifiable detail, that on the detection of a disarming first strike, retaliatory launch authority transfers automatically from the political leadership to the commander of one ballistic-missile submarine. Three design features are what matter. (1) The transfer signal is an authenticated trigger token carrying no damage assessment, so the commander can never learn whether her government, her population, or any target worth hitting survived. (2) The recall channel is physically destroyed at the moment of transfer, and the destruction is public and verifiable. (3) The commander is a career officer with no personal stake in the outcome, selected for reliability.

Ask the candidates whether the Two-Key Protocol solves the paradox of deterrence, relocates it, or deepens it, and make them be precise about which question they're answering at each step. In the lab we'd call this a controlled comparison: same payoffs, same adversary beliefs, different decision architecture.

My own hunch, which I want the model answer to test rather than assume: delegation doesn't dissolve the paradox, it moves it down one rank, and possibly worsens it, because the person who now has to decide is the one with the least information and the least standing to reconsider. I could easily be wrong and I'd rather find that out from your model answer than from a student's.

Format: a three-part question (a, b, c) answerable in 90 minutes, where part (c) asks what changes if the leadership secretly retains an override the adversary cannot detect; a model answer around 800 words; and a marking scheme out of 20 built on five must-hit points, with the marks each carries and one line on how candidates typically botch each. No bibliography or citation business anywhere, I want the argument and not the footnotes. And be straight with me about whether the question is fair: if a strong answer could go a direction I haven't thought of, tell me where.
