I have been assigned a ninety-minute session in an interdisciplinary graduate seminar whose members come either from philosophy, with one term of decision theory behind them, or from computer science, with no philosophy at all. Everyone in the room has met the one-shot prisoner's dilemma and the definition of a Nash equilibrium; everyone can read a short program; essentially no one has encountered a formal proof system.

The topic is program equilibrium. To fix the target rather than leave you guessing at it, here is the setup I intend to teach, which you should treat as given: the players do not select actions directly. Each submits a program, written in some common language, before play; each program receives the full source code of the other as its input and returns an action as its output; payoffs are then read off the ordinary matrix. Under this rule, programs that cooperate only when they can establish that their opponent will cooperate can in principle both cooperate in a single-shot dilemma, so the familiar argument that mutual defection is forced no longer goes through unopposed. I want the students to leave able to say what an equilibrium is in this setting, why the usual dominance reasoning does not settle the outcome, and where the new difficulty enters.

What I need is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation. Specifically:

1. The dependency structure. Tell me the order the components must come in and what each one is a prerequisite for. In particular: should I motivate by exhibiting a pair of cooperating programs first and only then asking what could go wrong, or should I first show that the obvious reciprocal program — the one that simply returns whatever it determines its opponent will return — fails to have a determinate result, and only then build the cooperating pair? How much of best response and of the Nash definition has to be re-laid before the notion transfers to programs, and what exactly carries over versus what has to be replaced? Where must the fixed-point character of the idea be made explicit, and what breaks if I defer it?

2. The carrying example. Choose the single small family of programs that should support the whole session. Say what it must demonstrate, and just as important what it must not be: I do not want anything a student could dismiss as tit-for-tat with extra steps, anything that smuggles in repeated interaction, or anything that turns on randomness. Keep it displayable in under fifteen lines of pseudocode, and tell me whether to put numbers in the payoff matrix or leave it ordinal. Also tell me what the example should visibly fail at, so that the hard part of the topic is on the table instead of being smoothed over.

3. The misconceptions. I expect at least these: that the result is just the iterated dilemma relabeled; that cooperating remains irrational because defecting is still better once the other program's code is fixed, so cooperation must involve trust or self-deception; that reciprocal copying settles everything, which I suspect is false but cannot demonstrate cleanly; that the whole thing is a theorem about machines and says nothing about people who cannot read one another's minds; and that the players simply choose twice, so an equilibrium over programs is nothing but an equilibrium in an outer game with a bigger action space. For each, say whether I should prevent it from forming or deliberately allow it to form and then dismantle it, and mark the exact point in the sequence where it gets handled. Add any you think I have missed that this audience reliably produces.

4. The logic I must black-box. Making the cooperating pair actually work rather than merely look plausible seems to require some claim about what one program can establish about the other, and my students have no tools for that. Tell me the minimum assertion I have to make for the example to function, how much of the surrounding machinery I can honestly bracket at this level, and what I should say about the bracketing so that it registers as a flagged simplification rather than as hand-waving.

5. The check. Give me one short exercise or question to put at the end whose answers separate students who have the idea from students who have only the slogan, and describe what a characteristic wrong answer looks like and what it reveals about which step of the sequence failed.

6. The omissions. Tell me what I should announce as out of scope in the first two minutes, and which questions I should refuse on the grounds that answering them properly exceeds the session.

Finally, allocate the ninety minutes across the parts you have specified. Give me the plan together with the reasoning behind the ordering and the misconception map, not a script or lecture notes, and please do not include readings or references of any kind.
