I am auditing a graduate seminar in decision theory, and last week's session was devoted to the two-envelope problem. To fix notation, here is the argument exactly as the instructor wrote it on the board, in six steps: (1) two envelopes are prepared, one containing twice as much money as the other, and you take one at random; (2) let X denote the amount in the envelope you are holding; (3) the other envelope therefore contains either 2X or X/2; (4) each of those two possibilities has probability 1/2; (5) so the expected amount in the other envelope is (1/2)(2X) + (1/2)(X/2) = 1.25X, which exceeds X; (6) therefore you should switch — and since the identical reasoning applies to the envelope you did not take, you should switch back, and so on without end. The seminar spent ninety minutes producing "fixes," and the economists and the philosophers ended up defending different ones; I now have to write the handout that says which fix is actually a fix. I have rewritten the six proposals below in neutral terms and deliberately stripped out any attribution, because I want them judged on content rather than pedigree. Please do not supply a literature review, author names, or references, and do not treat familiarity as a mark of quality.

The candidates are these. **A (inadmissible prior):** the problem is not a well-posed decision problem until a prior distribution over the smaller amount n is specified; the only priors under which step 4 holds exactly for every possible X are priors with no finite mean, and no proper distribution spread evenly over all positive amounts exists, so with any honest prior step 4 fails in the tail and step 5 never goes through generally. **B (equivocation):** the symbol X in step 5 does two different jobs — in the "2X" branch it denotes the amount in your envelope given that you hold the smaller one, and in the "X/2" branch it denotes that amount given that you hold the larger one — so the two terms are conditioned on different events and averaging them produces an expression that is not the expectation of anything. **C (look first):** the arithmetic is not the problem, the timing is; the decision an agent actually faces is taken after the envelope is opened, and with any finite-mean prior an observed amount is evidence about which envelope you hold, so above a threshold fixed by the prior switching is bad and below it switching is good, while if you refuse to look there is no asymmetry to exploit and switching is simply indifferent. **D (risk, not expectation):** even granting the 1.25X figure, expected value is the wrong criterion; switching leaves the payoff distribution's center where it is but fattens its spread, and any agent whose utility for money is concave — which is to say anyone with finite wealth, finite patience, and amounts large enough to matter — sees the alleged gain vanish, so the 1.25X is a decoy manufactured by assuming linear utility without limit. **E (preparation weighting — a friend's own proposal, offered by an engineer in the class who is not a philosopher):** once you are holding the envelope its amount is a fixed number n, and the two candidates for the other envelope are not two outcomes of one preparation but two different preparations — one in which the preparer placed n in your envelope as the smaller member of the pair, another in which he placed it as the larger; step 4's coin flip silently assumes those two preparations are equally likely at every n, which is a claim about the preparer's procedure and not about the toss that decided which envelope you reached for, and once a single actual procedure assigning weights to the pairs is required, the branches have unequal weight for large n and the 1/2–1/2 split is withdrawn. **F (deflation):** nothing in steps 1 through 5 is false and no contradiction is generated; the recommendation to switch is issued from the agent's ex ante information state and the recommendation to switch back from a later one, and two recommendations held at different points in an information history cannot conflict, so the oscillation exists only if you insist on comparing them.

Please evaluate and rank the six candidates by how much of the original inference each one actually invalidates, and tell me which single one belongs in the handout.
