I have published three novels and am roughly two hundred pages into a fourth, set in a provincial city under a long military curfew. My narrator is the clerk of a municipal registry office — she stamps papers, transcribes names, copies dates — and the spine of the book is her habit of issuing documents for people who do not exist and, more damagingly, of failing to issue the documents that would betray those who do. The complication is that the narration itself practises a systematic withholding: for eleven chapters the reader is not told what is in the satchel she carries across the bridge each morning, and is given, instead, a great deal of meticulous, entirely truthful detail about the weather, the queue at the office, the state of her mother's hands. A friend of mine who reads Aquinas for pleasure insists I am confusing two traditions and that what I want is a theory of mental reservation, which Augustine would not have given me. My editor, meanwhile, keeps writing "too coy" in the margin. So I have decided to settle the question properly before I finish the book, and I would like you to do the settling in the only vocabulary I am genuinely fluent in.

Here is the task. Take Augustine's account of lying — the argument in his treatise on lying and the later, much harder-lined book he wrote against the sect who claimed one could profess a false creed in order to survive persecution, together with the apparatus around them: the criterion of the double heart, by which a liar is one who has one thing in mind and puts another into words, so that a person may say something false without lying and may say something true while lying; the ranking of kinds of lie by descending gravity, down to the least culpable sort told to shield somebody from harm; his treatment of jokes as a class of utterance that is not lying at all because the manner of delivery announces that no deceit is intended; and above all the distinction on which his whole later argument rests, that one may conceal the truth but may never assert a falsehood — and restate all of it in the working vocabulary of fiction: withholding and disclosure, subtext, free indirect style, the unreliable narrator as against the unreliable character, dramatic irony, foreshadowing and payoff, the fair-play convention that governs what a narrative may hide from its reader and what it may not, the difference between a first-person voice that misrepresents and a third-person camera that merely declines to look. I read both treatises in translation some years ago and retain the shape of them rather than the letter, so please do not assume I will catch an error; equally, I know my craft cold and will notice instantly if you are using a term of art loosely. Work through the correspondence deliberately and one pair at a time, and do not let your sense of how novelists like to talk about themselves quietly settle the question in my favour — I want the Augustinian side stated as strongly as it can be, even where it is unflattering to me, and especially where it implies that my narrator's eleven chapters of careful truth-telling are no different in kind from the clerk's forged stamps.

Then give me the residue, which is the part I actually want. Divide it three ways: claims that translate cleanly, so that the craft term and the Augustinian term are doing the same work; claims that translate only under distortion, where you must bend something to make it fit and can say exactly what bent; and claims with no expression at all in the vocabulary of narrative craft, where I simply have no word for what he is talking about. I suspect the last category will be crowded and that its contents will concern the speaker rather than the utterance — that craft talk has nothing corresponding to the state of a person's heart, no way of asking whether the one who withholds is at peace with what he knows, and treats omission as a technique with a cost in readerly trust but no cost in the person who deploys it. If that is right, I want you to say plainly which side the defect sits on: whether the residue shows that Augustine's framework is missing something a novelist needs — some account of what an addresser commits to beyond what he asserts, some notion of an audience's entitlement to be led into error without being told a falsehood — or whether it shows that the craft vocabulary I have been using all my working life is missing something, and that the margin note reading "too coy" was tracking a real moral fact rather than a pacing one. Two housekeeping points: I will supply every citation myself, so no bibliography and no list of secondary sources, and I would much rather have your careful paraphrase than a quotation you are not certain of — please put nothing in quotation marks that you have not verified, since a false Augustine in my acknowledgements would be, on his account, a rather serious joke.
