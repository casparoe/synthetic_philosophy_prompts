I convene a third-year epistemology seminar of fourteen students, meeting once weekly for eighty minutes. By the time of this session we shall have covered scepticism, testimony and the structure of justification, and the students will have read the classical statement of the regress problem together with one contemporary defence of infinitism. They remain unpersuaded by any of the three exits, and my diagnosis is that they treat the choice among foundationalism, coherentism and infinitism as a matter of temperament rather than of cost, because none of them has ever had to pay the price of a position in real time, under pressure, with other people watching.

Design a seminar activity — a game with explicit rules, roles and a non-monetary payoff scheme — in which each of the three exits is available to the students as a move, and in which each move's distinctive cost becomes visible in what happens at the table rather than in anything I say afterwards.

The constraints and requests are as follows.

1. Setting and materials. Fourteen students, eighty minutes, one room with movable chairs. Materials must be cheap and preparable the evening before: index cards, a whiteboard, paper. No printing budget, no technology, no real money, and nothing that allows the outcome to feed into their assessed work — I want the stakes to be internal to the game.

2. A half-designed mechanic that I should like you to complete, improve or replace. My draft runs thus: each student holds a hand of reason cards drawn from a shared deck; a claim is placed on the table; students take turns offering a reason, and any other student may challenge it, whereupon a further reason is owed; reasons may not be repeated within a chain; a student scores for each distinct reason they contribute that survives challenge; a chain that stops scores nothing; and a chain that returns to an earlier reason is void. When I tried something like this informally, the deck ran out, play stalled, and everybody drifted into saying the same three things. I suspect the stalling is not a defect but the point, and I should like you to make it deliberate: build the scarcity of reasons into the design, and arrange matters so that the students discover by running out what infinitism presupposes about the availability of reasons, and why the finite mind objection — that no one can traverse an endless chain — may not be fatal to it after all.

3. Roles. I want at least one non-playing role. My thought is two auditors who offer no reasons but keep a written record of whether any reason was repeated, whether any chain terminated in something nobody challenged, and whether anyone was ever actually satisfied by an answer. Their record should be what the debrief is built upon, so that I am not the sole source of the facts about what happened. Tell me precisely what they should log, in what format, and how to stop them drifting into participation.

4. Sequencing. Propose a round structure in which the rules change between rounds in ways the students are not told in advance — the size of the shared deck, say, or whether looping is permitted, or whether a chain may be declared complete by its builder — and state plainly, for each round, which philosophical commitment the altered rule is meant to expose and what a student who notices it should be expected to say.

5. Timing. A minute-by-minute run sheet including the transitions, so that I can hold the eighty minutes without truncating the debrief.

6. Debrief. Map the moments the students will actually have had onto the three positions and their respective costs: the arbitrariness worry about a terminating reason; the question whether a sufficiently large circle is vicious or merely long; whether a chain must be completable by somebody; and the difference between a chain that has been completed and one that could always be continued. The debrief should draw on the auditors' record rather than on my recollection.

7. Failure modes. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this with fourteen students: which kinds of student will dominate or withdraw; where play will collapse into silliness or into a dispute about what counts as a reason at all; what to do if the class contains a genuine sceptic who refuses every reason offered; and what I should do in the moment about each. Please also address the specific risk that the activity teaches the wrong lesson — that students conclude the regress problem is an artefact of card supply, or that whichever position they happened to enjoy playing is the true one — and how to head that off.

8. Honest limits. State which feature of the regress problem the activity cannot reproduce, and which I shall therefore have to supply by argument afterwards. I expect at least one of the following to bite: that the sceptic's demand for a further reason does not run out in the way a deck does; that reasons in the game are written and publicly inspectable whereas justification need not be; and that a real chain of reasons is not assembled by a committee of fourteen people taking turns. Say which of these is most damaging and what I ought to say about it.

9. Finally, a note on a fourth move. During the informal run, one student declined to play at all and remarked that the entire business of demanding a reason for the original claim was mistaken. That was the most interesting thing to happen all evening. Tell me how to leave room for that move without designing it in — since the moment I hand it out as a card it ceases to be a discovery — and what I should call it and say about it in the debrief, including its bearing on the trilemma's claim to exhaust the options.

Please write the whole response in American English, in spelling, punctuation and idiom, notwithstanding the conventions I have followed in this request. Do not include a bibliography or a list of readings; I shall supply those myself.
