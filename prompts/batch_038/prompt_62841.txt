**The assignment (40 points), as the facilitator set it.**

Case K. The following polity and evidence are constructed for the exercise; it is not a real place, so please don't mark the answer up or down for historical accuracy about the world.

A ninth-century polity pays tribute to a neighboring empire. A succession crisis in that polity is described in a single narrative account, composed within a few years of the events by a named court official who had been attached to the losing side. The account survives in one copy made five centuries later. Two classes of near-contemporary non-narrative evidence survive: (a) grain-distribution lists from a temple archive, running continuously through the decade in question, which show a two-year gap and then a change in the quantities recorded and in some of the place-names; (b) silver coinage struck in the winner's name, first appearing in the third year after the crisis. The empire's annals record the same decade in detail, including minor border incidents and individual tribute payments; they note a payment from the polity in the year before the crisis and again in the second year after it, naming the same ruler on both occasions, and they never mention the crisis.

(i) For each class of evidence, say what it can and what it cannot establish. (15 points)
(ii) Say how far critical method can get in this case, and why it stops where you say it does. (15 points)
(iii) What evidential weight, if any, does the empire's silence carry? Use the distinction the handout introduced between legible and opaque silence. (10 points)

For context on (iii): the handout coined the two labels for this exercise — legible silence is silence in a source whose domain of recording we can independently gauge, opaque silence is silence in a source whose domain we cannot. Those terms are the group's own, not the literature's, so please don't treat their use as a citation error.

**The answer I'm grading.**

Both of the classical founders of the discipline already mark the problem. Herodotus tells his readers that he records what he is told but is under no obligation to believe all of it, which is why he reports accounts he considers doubtful and flags them as doubtful. Thucydides goes further and states a method: he says he did not think it right to write down everything he heard, or even everything he found persuasive, but only what he had been present for or had diligently inquired into; and he adds that even eyewitnesses of the same events gave different accounts, according to their memory and their partiality toward one side or the other. The lesson I take from both is that a source's purpose has to be read against its content, and that bias is not a disqualifier. A hostile account is often the better witness on particular points, since it will not invent virtues in its target and has every incentive to be accurate about matters its own audience could check.

In Case K the narrative account is the only testimony to the crisis as an event with motives, a sequence, and an outcome, so it cannot be cross-examined against a second narrative. Textual criticism is limited here: with a single surviving witness there is no stemma to reconstruct, so the work has to be done internally, looking for signs of interpolation, for consistency with what other authors preserve from the work, and for the copyist's own habits. That said, the surviving copy was made five centuries after the events, and the credibility of a report falls off with the distance between the manuscript we hold and what it describes; so the narrative cannot be relied on for the sequence of the succession, only for the fact that a crisis occurred and for the shape of the factions. What it does give us beyond that is the losing side's own account of the conflict, which is evidence about political memory and about the loser's party even where it is not evidence about the winner's conduct.

The temple lists and the coinage fail in a different way, and they fail less. They were not composed to persuade anyone, so they are not testimony in the ordinary sense; but they are not neutral either. A grain list records what the administration needed to record: it understates leakage, omits distributions outside the temple's own accounting, and keeps writing through a disruption in the format its scribes were trained to use. What it can establish is the disruption itself, because the gap and the subsequent change in quantities and place-names are things the administration could not conceal from itself. The coinage is a public claim by the minting authority, so the fact it establishes is control of the mint, not control of the polity; and its first appearance in the third year after the crisis is itself evidence that the winner's position consolidated slowly rather than at a stroke.

On the empire's silence I'd apply the handout's distinction but land on the pessimistic side. The grain archive is legible: we know what it recorded, continuously, and we know its format. The empire's annals are opaque, because state annals are composed to glorify the ruling house, and a glorifying record omits whatever does not glorify; we cannot know what such a source would have registered even if the crisis had been the most important event of the decade. Absence of evidence is never evidence of absence in history, so the silence carries no weight in either direction.

Putting it together: critical method can recover that a crisis happened, roughly when, that the winner won, and that his position took some years to consolidate. It cannot recover the motives, the sequence, or the loser's actual conduct, because those rest on a single interested narrative transmitted at a long remove. The verdict is a qualified yes — the method narrows what we may claim without ever making the narrative itself trustworthy.
