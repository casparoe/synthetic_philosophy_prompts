I have spent years reading commitment-and-bargaining material in the Schelling idiom, and I have accumulated a set of case verdicts that I cannot get into a single frame. The standard treatments tend to stop exactly where my difficulties begin, so I would like you to do the construction work rather than summarize anyone. Below are ten cases with my verdicts, followed by a tentative principle of my own that I am fairly sure is broken, and then what I want you to produce.

The cases, numbered so you can refer back to them.

1. A state announces in advance, in statute, that it will never negotiate with or pay ransom to anyone who takes one of its citizens. When the first citizen is taken and his family begs, the state holds. Verdict: adopting the policy is clearly right, and holding to it is right too — though I hold the second claim with noticeably less confidence than the first, and I cannot say why my confidence differs, since both seem to depend on the same facts.

2. A man who once paid a blackmailer resolves, silently, never to pay again. Nobody knows of the resolve; it can only be discovered by the blackmailer's later experience of him. Verdict: worth having, but I do not think it is a commitment in the strategic sense at all — at most a disposition. I suspect I would say something quite different about the same disposition if it were common knowledge, and I do not know whether that difference is a difference in kind or only in degree.

3. Before bargaining opens, a union's members vote publicly to strike if the offer falls below a stated number, and the vote is minuted and reported. Verdict: good, and better than an equivalent private instruction to the leadership.

4. Before bargaining opens, the same union votes publicly to accept any offer above a stated number, and says so. Verdict: bad — I would call it a capitulation wearing the costume of a commitment. Yet structurally this is case 3 with the inequality reversed: equally observable, equally credible, equally a change in the other side's incentives. I have no account of why one is a weapon and the other a surrender.

5. Two drivers, one lane, head-on. One rips out his steering wheel and throws it through the open window in full view of the other. Verdict: in a one-off this is simply the correct move and it wins. But if both drivers have time to do it, both do it, and I cannot bring myself to say that either was irrational. The collision does not look to me like anybody's mistake, which is itself a problem for how I am evaluating commitments.

6. At forty, healthy, I sign a directive: if I reach advanced dementia, no antibiotics for pneumonia, no tube feeding. At seventy-eight I am in exactly that state — placid, unaware I ever signed anything, enjoying my food and my visitors, expressing no wish to die. Verdict: the directive should not be honored. Set this beside case 1: there the person the policy kills wants it broken and I say hold; here the person the directive would kill is not even in a position to want it broken and I say break.

7. The head of a weak state cultivates a reputation for erratic and disproportionate retaliation so that stronger states take his threats seriously. Verdict: permitted, and I think praiseworthy. The same behavior by a strong state toward weaker ones: not permitted. I can find no principle that yields this asymmetry except one that makes it a matter of the odds, and the odds are not what I am reacting to.

8. A frontier lab announces that it will stop training at a defined capability threshold unless an external panel clears continuation, and hard-codes a tripwire that shuts the cluster down. Verdict: good, and better than the bare promise. But if the lab's competitors are unconstrained and will reach the same threshold regardless, the announcement looks less like a way of slowing the race than a way of losing it tidily — and the lab often cannot know which world it is in at the moment it commits.

9. A woman announces to everyone who will listen that she will never speak to her brother again, whatever he does. He later apologizes sincerely. Verdict: she should speak to him — and beyond that, I think the announcement was improper when she made it. Yet the policy in case 1 is also stated over an unbounded future, and I think that one was proper. The difference must have something to do with the range of situations the commitment reaches across, but I cannot say what.

10. I decide in advance not to read work messages after eight in the evening, and I install a filter that enforces it. No one else's behavior depends on it; it is purely me against me. Verdict: obviously good. But notice that when I call this a commitment I am using the word for something with none of the machinery that matters in cases 1 through 8, and I suspect the shared name has been corrupting my thinking.

My tentative principle, which I believe is wrong in both directions. A commitment is rational to make if and only if (a) it changes what at least one other agent does, and (b) at the moment you must act on it, you would not wish you had made a different one. Clause (b) was meant to catch case 5 when both drivers commit, and case 8 in the bad world. But (b) also condemns every deterrent that ever had to fire — that is, it condemns precisely the commitments worth making — and it condemns case 1 exactly when case 1 is doing its job. Meanwhile (a) condemns case 10 and probably case 2, which I do not want condemned.

What I would like back:

- A principle, or a small number of candidate principles, stated precisely enough that someone else could apply it to a case I have not seen and reach the verdict I would want. If it requires conditions, name them; if it must be indexed to what other agents are able to do, say exactly what it is indexed to and at what point in time that index is evaluated.
- For each clause, the case from my list that earns it. I want to see which of my verdicts is load-bearing for which piece of the principle, so that I can tell what I would lose by giving a verdict up.
- Your principle's verdicts on the three places I flagged uncertainty: the gap in my confidence between adopting and honoring the policy in case 1; whether case 2 is a commitment at all; and the strong/weak asymmetry in case 7.
- At least one case of your own that my list does not contain, in which your principle delivers a verdict that goes against the spirit in which you built it — the case you would least like to have to defend. Then defend it, or concede it.
- A frank accounting of which of my ten verdicts your principle cannot keep. If you must choose between a principle that saves all ten by acquiring epicycles and one that overturns two and remains statable in a few sentences, tell me which you chose and why, and name the two casualties.
- A direct answer to this question, which I cannot settle: is there any condition under which committing second is better than committing first? Cases 5 and 8 push me toward the view that commitment is a race and races are won by moving early, but I suspect that is too crude, and I want to know when the second mover holds the advantage and what feature of the situation confers it.

I am not after a survey of the literature and I do not want a reading list — I have done that part. I want the principle, the fit, and the casualties.
