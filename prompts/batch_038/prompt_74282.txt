Berlin separates negative liberty — the absence of actual interference by others — from the republican conception that Pettit formulates as non-domination: freedom from another's arbitrary will, whether or not that will is ever exercised. Consider a third measure, stipulated here rather than drawn from the literature, which we may call insured liberty: an agent is insuredly free with respect to a given choice just in case no other agent could interfere with that choice without thereby incurring costs she has strong independent reason to avoid. Compare insured liberty with each of its two rivals, working through two cases that pull in opposite directions: a slave whose master's interests happen to coincide with the slave's own, and a citizen whose security against a powerful neighbour rests wholly on that neighbour's private calculations rather than on anything the citizen herself can call upon. Does the first case reveal insured liberty as a refinement of negative liberty, or as a disguised account of non-domination? Does the second reveal it as non-domination stripped of everything republicans care about, given that the costs deterring interference are borne by the would-be interferer instead of being owed to the protected agent? Conclude by saying whether insured liberty is best read as a sharpening of one rival, as a deflationary reduction of the other to expected non-interference, or as a genuinely third concept — and what, if anything, it can do with the republican notion of arbitrariness.
