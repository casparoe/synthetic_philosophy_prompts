I want a disciplined case analysis of the 1998 collapse of Long-Term Capital Management read through Machiavelli's account of fortuna and virtù, with particular attention to the twenty-fifth chapter of The Prince.

So that the analysis does not depend on your recalling the finance, here are the facts I will treat as settled. The fund was founded in February 1994 by John Meriwether, formerly vice chairman of Salomon Brothers, and among its principals were Myron Scholes and Robert Merton, who were awarded the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1997 for their work on the pricing of derivatives. Its business was convergence arbitrage: buy the less liquid side of a spread and sell the more liquid side, then hold until the two move back together. Because the individual spreads were narrow and the trades were regarded as close to riskless, meaningful returns required enormous leverage; at the end of 1997 the fund carried roughly thirty dollars of debt for every dollar of capital, with substantially more notional exposure sitting in derivatives. In August 1998 Russia devalued the ruble and defaulted on its own domestic ruble-denominated debt, which set off a general flight toward liquidity and quality; spreads in almost every market the fund held moved apart rather than together, and because other institutions held similar positions the unwinding reinforced itself. LTCM lost about 44 percent of its value in August alone. On 23 September, after a competing bid to buy the partners out and recapitalize the fund fell through, the Federal Reserve Bank of New York convened fourteen banks and brokerage firms that between them put up $3.625 billion in exchange for 90 percent of the fund, leaving the partners to run it under an oversight committee, without bonuses and on reduced salaries, until the positions could be sold off in an orderly way. The Fed committed no public money: the creditors who stood to lose most from a disorderly liquidation financed the rescue themselves.

With that in hand, do the following.

First, make the mapping explicit and refuse to strain it. Who is the prince here — the partnership, Meriwether, or the fund as an institution with a will of its own? What is the principality, and what does acquisition by one's own arms mean for an enterprise whose arms are borrowed from counterparties rather than raised from subjects of its own? Where does virtù sit: in the models, in the traders' nerve, in the reputation that let the fund raise money on terms nobody else could obtain? And where does fortuna begin? Attend especially to whether the Russian default counts as fortuna at all, given that it was a deliberate political act by another sovereign, and say what Machiavelli's vocabulary does with the fact that one agent's calculated decision is, from inside another's ledger, indistinguishable from the weather.

Second, take the dykes seriously as engineering rather than as aphorism. In the chapter I mean, Machiavelli compares fortune to a river that in flood destroys the plains and carries everything before it, and argues that in calm weather men can raise dikes and embankments so that the next rise does less damage or can be channeled; he adds that fortune governs roughly half of what we do and leaves the other half, or close to it, to us; and he observes that fortune displays its power where virtù has not been prepared to resist it. LTCM's variance estimates, its diversification across many markets, its stress tests, and its market-neutral construction were exactly such works, raised over several calm and very profitable years. Distinguish as sharply as you can between the dike that fails because nobody built one and the dike that fails because it was built to a standard the flood exceeded, and say whether Machiavelli's framework has the resources to tell those two cases apart after the fact. Then say which of them this was, and what would have to be true for the answer to be the other.

Third — and this is the reason I am asking — test the fortuna/virtù distinction for falsifiability. It looks as though any outcome can be absorbed: success is read as virtù well deployed, failure as a shortfall in virtù that fortune merely exposed, and the observation that fortune prevails where resistance was unprepared converts every defeat into evidence of precisely the deficiency that was supposed to be the question at issue. If you judge the distinction empty in this sense, say so plainly, and then explain what work it is doing instead — exhortation, the self-presentation of an out-of-office secretary angling to be useful again, the training of a reader's judgment rather than the prediction of his outcomes. If you think a non-empty version can be recovered, reconstruct it, and specify what that version would have committed the partners to believing before August 1998: what they would have had to be able to say about their own preparations such that the subsequent collapse would count against them rather than merely befall them.

Fourth, examine one feature of the case that I suspect the theory has no category for, and test the suspicion rather than indulge it: the flood was partly manufactured by the dikes. The trades were crowded precisely because they had been shown to work and because similar models were in use across the Street; the flight to liquidity was amplified by everyone adjusting at once; and the authority that finally decided the fund's fate was a public institution with no principality in the matter, passing judgment on an enterprise it neither owned nor had chartered. Whether virtù of this reflexive kind produces the fortuna that eventually destroys it is a question Machiavelli's own cases do not seem to pose — the founders who rise by their own arms, Borgia, whose ruin he attributes to an extraordinary and extreme malignity of fortune, and the mercenaries and auxiliaries of the twelfth and thirteenth chapters, which come closest to describing a power that is simultaneously yours and someone else's. Tell me whether the consortium of fourteen creditors — armed, willing, and arrived to save you — is best read as auxiliary force, and whether the rescue leaves Machiavelli's prince intact, indebted, or effectively deposed.

Finally, be disciplined about silence. I would rather you identify three places where Machiavelli simply has nothing to say about LTCM than receive a reading in which he anticipated everything. Where the theory is mute, mark it as mute, and say what a modern account would have to supply in its place.
