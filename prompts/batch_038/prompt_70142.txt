I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm putting together a piece around von Neumann's short book on brains and machines — the one he was preparing for the Yale lecture series and that came out after his death in '58. To get my bearings I've been leaning on a long appreciation of it that ran in a rival publication a few years back, written by a proper historian of computing whose stuff I usually trust. Trouble is, I've started to suspect I'm using it as a crib for claims I can't actually defend in print. Here's the key paragraph, retyped from memory so the wording isn't exact but the substance is:

"The Computer and the Brain is von Neumann sitting down with the question of whether the human brain is a computer, and answering yes. His argument is straightforward: because a neuron either fires or it doesn't, the nervous system is built out of on-off switches, exactly as a machine is built out of valves and transistors, so the brain is a digital computer and the difference between it and an artificial one is only engineering. What engineering, though: the components are fantastically slow, so the brain makes up for it by running enormous numbers of them at once rather than one thing after another, and it does all this on a trickle of power no machine of the day could match. Von Neumann's deepest point was about logic. He argued that the brain does not run on the two-valued logic we inherited from Aristotle, and that mathematics as we practice it is just one code among others that history happened to hand us — a code the nervous system doesn't speak. Which lets him predict that a machine built on the brain's plan would end up thinking in the brain's logic too, and it's why the neural-network models he sketches in the closing pages are, for him, the obvious road to machine intelligence."

Can you audit this for me?
