I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and I am working on a piece about the Condorcet jury theorem's sudden afterlife in machine learning: several labs now sample many candidate answers from a single model and take the majority verdict, and their publicity material treats this as the eighteenth-century result ported to silicon. My editor would like a sidebar that sorts readers by what they actually believe about majority accuracy rather than by which slogan they would reach for, so could you write me a decision tree for that purpose? Please begin from the shared premise that the theorem holds under its textbook assumptions, and make each branching node one assumption a reader might be prepared to surrender — independence of votes, individual competence above a coin flip, a binary choice, sincere rather than strategic voting, a fixed electorate — with every node phrased as a question about a concrete case, such as a panel of models trained on the same corpus, a town whose residents have all read the same editorial, a three-option referendum, or a juror voting against their own judgement to avoid a hung panel, so that nobody can tell where a branch leads before answering it. Set out first the space of answer-combinations your questions permit, then place the named positions in it: naive wisdom-of-crowds optimism, epistemic proceduralism, epistocracy, the deliberative line that trades independence for competence, correlated-error deflationism, and any leaf you think belongs there but nobody occupies, each with one sentence on its most awkward commitment and a guess, for the empty corners, at whether the absence reflects incoherence, unfashionability or simple neglect. Finally, flag any pair of leaves your questions cannot genuinely separate, and state where in the tree you would put the machine-learning case with which I opened. American English, please, and assume a reader who is numerate but has never seen the theorem proved.
