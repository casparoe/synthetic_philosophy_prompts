You're a philosopher of action who also actually reads the intertemporal-choice economics — someone as comfortable with Davidson on akrasia and with Holton's stuff on resolutions as you are with quasi-hyperbolic β-δ discounting and the naive/sophisticated procrastination models. Please think hard about this rather than pattern-matching, and before you give me any principle, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming out loud: list the candidate dimensions that could possibly be doing the work in my cases below (judgment vs. desire, resolution vs. mere wish, whether the temptation was foreseeable when the resolution was made, the agent's own later endorsement or shame, the shape of the discount function, cold vs. hot states, whether the act was intentional) and say, for each, which of my cases it looks like it separates and which it doesn't.

Here's the thing I'm after. I want to know if there's one principle that draws the line between genuine weakness of will and mere change of mind, precise enough to decide cases I haven't thought of yet. I've been going back and fourth on this for a while, so go by my gut verdicts:

1. In January I resolve to run three times a week and I write it down. In March, on a rainy Tuesday, I'm a bit tired and I stay in. I haven't changed my mind about running — I still think three times a week is what I should be doing, and I feel bad about it. Weakness of will, clearly.

2. Same setup, but by June I've stopped thinking fitness matters much. I've genuinely come to prefer a leisurely life. When I skip a run I don't feel like I'm betraying anything. Not weakness of will — I changed, and the change is mine.

3. Ten years ago I decided I wanted to be an academic. Now I work in industry, I don't regret the switch, and I don't think the 22-year-old me was deluded either. Not weakness of will.

4. Two years ago I decided I wanted to be an academic. Last month, in the middle of a rough semester, I signed a contract with a consulting firm. When I look at my old dissertation notes now I feel ashamed. Weakness of will — even though from the outside this looks exactly like case 3.

5. Monday morning I resolve not to open email until noon so I can write. At 11:50 I cave. Weakness of will, trivially.

6. I resolve to write all weekend. Saturday night some people invite me out, I go, nothing comes of it, Sunday is a wash. Weakness of will.

7. Identical to 6, except that at that party I meet the person I end up collaborating with for the next decade and the work we do is the best of my life. I don't want to call that weakness of will at all. But I can't see what's different between 6 and 7 from where I was standing on Saturday night.

8. $50 now versus $60 in a week: I take the $50 now and I don't think I should be too hard on myself, money now is money now. $50 in a year versus $60 in a year and a week: taking the $50 there strikes me as just stupid, no excuse available. Both are the same kind of preference reversal and if my discount function is hyperbolic they come out of the same mechanism, so whatever principle I have can't be grounded only in the shape of my discounting. I want you to say explicitly whether this pair is really inconsistent or whether there's a principled asymmetry (magnitude? the year-ahead choice being made cold? the fact that the near one is partly about liquidity?).

9. My partner resolves every January to stop drinking, means it every time, and by March she's drinking again saying "one glass won't hurt." Weakness of will obviously. My cousin announced once that he'd quit sugar, never wrote anything down, made no plans, and ate cake at a wedding three months later. I would not call him weak-willed — he had a wish, not a resolution. So the line seems to run through intention and not through judgment.

10. Ulysses stuff. I put my phone in a timed lockbox at 9am so I can work. At 3pm I calmly and sincerely want it out because I need to answer my mother — I don't think my 9am self gets to overrule me there. But if the calm 3pm self sincerely wants a drink and I've locked the bottle away, I do think the 9am self gets to overrule him. Both are my later, lucid, considered desires being blocked by an earlier commitment, and I can't say what makes them different.

11. For five years I've said "I'll quit tomorrow" every morning, without feeling torn when I say it. I'd still call that weakness of will, even though there's no single act of caving. But someone who never resolves anything and just smokes for fifty years I wouldn't call weak-willed at all, just a smoker. So the label seems to require the resolutions, which makes 11 sit badly with 9.
