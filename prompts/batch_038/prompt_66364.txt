I'm a philosophy PhD student working on the ethics of information hazards, and there's an institutional practice I can't quite locate philosophically. In software security research, the most influential statement of the coordinated-disclosure norm is deadline-based: Google's Project Zero, whose policy several other research groups have copied, gives a vendor ninety days to ship a patch and then publishes full technical details of the flaw whether or not anything has been fixed; if the vendor does patch in time, publication waits a further thirty days to let the fix propagate, and if the team has evidence the bug is already being exploited in the wild, the ninety days collapses to a week. The published rationale for the deadline almost always includes some version of the thought that a serious flaw is going to be found by someone else in any case, so withholding it buys delay rather than prevention, whereas a fixed and credible publication date buys pressure on the vendor to patch before the attacker's advantage is public. Meanwhile researchers working on enhanced pathogen protocols and on frontier model weights, who face what looks like a formally identical problem, converge on the opposite conclusion: certain information is simply not to be published, whatever the timeline.

Could you reconstruct the theory the deadline practice presupposes? I'm interested in particular in whether it locates the harm in the fact that information is public or in the asymmetry between who knows what and when — the thirty-day uptake window and the seven-day rule both seem to say that once the attackers know, or once defenders can be protected, publication as such costs nothing; in whether it treats individual discoverers as interchangeable, so that one researcher's restraint has no effect on the probability that the information ends up out there; and in what standing it grants the researcher to weigh harms to users and vendors she has no relationship with. Then separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with: could someone who denied the interchangeability of discoverers still defend a deadline rule, and on what grounds? Finally, say what the practice would have to look like if we took seriously the assumption on the biology side that each act of disclosure carries non-redundant marginal harm, and whether the divergence between the two fields is best explained by a different theory of harm or simply by different empirical estimates of how many independent discoverers there are and how fast the information travels.

Please be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely contested among people who work on disclosure ethics and risk; I don't want a contested position handed to me as though it were the consensus. And write it as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long letter — no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
