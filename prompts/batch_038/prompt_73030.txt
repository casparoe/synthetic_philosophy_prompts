I have recently taken over a recurring column that explains ideas at the intersection of cognitive science and artificial intelligence to a general readership, and one analogy keeps surfacing in everything I read: thinking is a matter of talking to yourself in a language only you can hear. It appears as the friendly gloss on the language-of-thought hypothesis, as the intuition that there must be an inner voice for there to be a thinker, and — most usefully for my purposes — as the shared premise of two arguments that point in opposite directions. Before I commit to it in print, I would like a proper audit.

Please begin by separating two things the analogy can be asked to do: serving as a heuristic gloss on a substantive hypothesis about the format of thought, and serving as a substantive claim in its own right — that thinking consists in the manipulation of sentence-like representations with a combinatorial syntax and a compositional semantics, roughly as Fodor described it, with inference explained by causal operations sensitive to the structure of those representations. State explicitly which of the two you are auditing at each stage.

Then:

- **The mapping.** Lay out the correspondences, ideally as a table. What plays the role of the private language, of individual sentences, of the vocabulary, and of the grammar? What, if anything, occupies the role of speaker and of hearer? Where the analogy leaves a blank rather than supplying a correspondent, say so, and note whether the blank is doing work for whoever deploys the analogy.
- **Where the mapping breaks.** Sort the disanalogies into two piles: those that are harmless for the intended use (the private language makes no sound, has no public conventions, and need not be for communicating anything) and those that are load-bearing. I would particularly like these addressed: whether a system whose states are distributed and context-sensitive can lack discrete, independently reusable constituents and still be compositional, productive, and systematic; how much weight inner speech can bear once introspection research suggests it is sparser and more fragmentary than people assume, and once we take seriously those who report no inner speech at all yet plan, navigate, and reason normally; and what the analogy smuggles in about determinate content and about the need for an audience — including whether Fodor's own way of avoiding an inner reader commits him to something the analogy does not obviously deliver.
- **What the analogy can carry unaided.** Distinguish what follows from the correspondence itself from what has to be borrowed from independent argument or evidence about real systems.
- **The two conclusions.** In the correspondence I receive, the analogy is used to license both of the following: (i) any genuinely thinking system must have a language-like code in which it thinks, so systems without one are at best simulating thought; and (ii) any system that manipulates sentence-like structures thereby thinks, so the relevant systems already do. Assess whether each survives the disanalogies, and say whether the fact that today's large models are trained on natural-language text rescues or undermines (i).
- **A replacement.** If the analogy fails for these purposes, what should stand in for it? One suggestion I have come across is that thinking resembles consulting a map rather than composing sentences. Say what such an analogy would preserve, what it would lose — especially with respect to inference and negation — and whether it is genuinely better or merely differently biased.

Please be explicit about your confidence at each verdict. Where the question is contested in the literature rather than settled, say so and indicate which side you take to be better supported and why; where your audit depends on a particular reading of the hypothesis that a defender might reject, flag that dependence rather than resolving it silently. Finally, close by saying what would change your mind: what finding, example, or argument would lead you to reverse your verdict on either of the two conclusions above, and at which step your reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong.
