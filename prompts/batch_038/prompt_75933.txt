Here is the half-formed idea, which I want you to develop and discipline rather than simply endorse. The 'strategy-stealing assumption' does load-bearing work in some arguments about advanced AI systems — roughly, that a single agent which attains decisive strategic advantage can, without loss to itself, appropriate and implement whatever strategies other agents would have pursued, including cooperative ones, because being more capable is never a disadvantage and because it can model everyone else. That usage borrows its name from a classical argument in combinatorial game theory, and I cannot find anywhere that checks whether the structural conditions making the game-theoretic argument valid actually obtain in the settings where the assumption is deployed. As I understand it, the games argument requires symmetry of roles, monotonicity (an extra stone on the board is never a handicap), the impossibility of a draw, and the absence of forced moves that damage the player obliged to make them; it accordingly fails to deliver a first-player win in tic-tac-toe, cannot be applied to chess at all because of zugzwang-type positions, changes its conclusion to a guaranteed draw when the rules are amended to permit passing, and is blocked outright in Go once compensation points make the starting position asymmetric. My suspicion is that the governance-side usage silently assumes monotonicity plus something like a pass option, and that a range of realistic scenarios violate them: advantage that arrives bundled with hard deadlines, forced public commitments, enlarged attack surface, or the obligation to keep existing infrastructure and constituencies functioning, so that the stronger party would dearly like to be able to pass. A second and related suspicion is that the combinatorial argument is famously non-constructive — it proves a win exists without exhibiting the winning strategy — and that the governance analogue may inherit this defect in a more damaging form, namely that stealing a strategy may be possible in principle while being infeasible given the information, time, and computational resources actually available.
