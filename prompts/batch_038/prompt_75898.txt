I run a tiny evaluation team at a grant-making foundation — four of us, no economists, and I did one welfare economics module about fifteen years ago, which is roughly as much theory as is in the building. We get round about twelve to fifteen funding proposals a quarter that come with some rule for adding up welfare effects across the people they'd affect, and the rules are all over the shop: straight sums of expected wellbeing, sums with prioritarian weights that discount gains to the already-better-off, a maximin-flavoured "only count the worst-off group" rule, one that treats a household as a single unit, one that does the calculation before a lottery is resolved and another that does it after. Trustees have started asking whether we have any principled way of saying one of these is defensible and another isn't, rather than just going with whichever produces the biggest number, and I've been told Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is definately the thing that settles it. So far my colleague's summary to the board has been "the theorem proves you must add utilities, therefore equal-weight sums are the only legitimate rule and everything else is arbitrary" — which I suspect is wrong, or at least right only under assumptions none of our proposals actually check, and I don't want that going out again.

What I want is a reusable screening procedure I can hand to a grants officer with no maths beyond arithmetic and have them run on a proposal in under an hour, before the quarterly meeting. For each step: the question to ask, what in the document counts as a sufficient answer, and what the step does when the answer is missing or unclear (pass, fail, escalate). It should start from what the theorem actually requires — the conditions on the individual and on the social evaluation, the part about uncertainty and expected utility, and whatever symmetry or anonymity-type condition you need to get equal weights rather than merely some weights — and turn each of those into a question a non-specialist can put to a piece of paper: where did these utilities come from, are they comparable across people or only within a person, does the proposal's own method actually meet the conditions, or is it using a different rule and calling it Harsanyi. I want the procedure to be explicit about which conditions our proposals most often break, and about what survives when one breaks, because "the theorem doesn't apply" shouldn't automatically mean "the proposal is nonsense" — there should be a step that separates a rule that's merely unsupported by the theorem from one that's positively at odds with something the theorem does deliver. Mark clearly where the procedure stops being about facts and hands the decision back to a human because it's a values question (how much weight to what, whether to make interpersonal comparisons at all), and be candid about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume.

Then run it on three cases that would be entirely normal for us. (a) A health proposal summing expected QALYs across beneficiaries at equal weight, using a standard instrument, where the numbers are elicited within each person and then just compared as though they were on one scale. (b) An education proposal applying concave prioritarian weights, so a unit of gain to a worse-off child counts for more, justified by saying this follows from the same theorem. (c) A proposal for an intervention allocated by lottery over scarce places, where the applicants have computed the sum of expected utilities both before the draw and after the outcome is known, the two rankings disagree, and their note says to pick whichever reading you prefer. One of these should come out badly for a proposal the trustees are keen on, so don't soften it — I'd rather have the awkward verdict now than in the boardroom. Finish with a short list of what the procedure can't do: the aggregation questions it can't reach at all, the failure modes where it'll hand back a confident but wrong verdict, and the cases where you'd want an actual welfare economist in the room instead of a checklist.
