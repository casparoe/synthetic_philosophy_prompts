Setup: Tomas works in an office. His brother Marek drives lorries.

(a) [4 marks] A member of the group thinks the following argument is fatal to counterpart theory.

1. On counterpart theory, "Tomas could have driven lorries" is true if and only if, in some world other than ours, some counterpart of Tomas drives lorries.
2. Any counterpart of Tomas is a different person from Tomas, existing in a different world.
3. So on counterpart theory nothing is true of Tomas himself at any other world, and strictly speaking it isn't Tomas who could have driven lorries.
4. But "Tomas could have driven lorries" says of Tomas himself that he could have driven them.
5. So on counterpart theory, ordinary claims of the form "x could have been F" are either false or not really about x.
6. Therefore counterpart theory isn't a rival account of what such claims mean — it's a denial that they ever come out true.

And here is the answer that came back:

---

(a) The argument is valid but unsound, and it goes wrong at (4). This is Kripke's objection: he makes the point with a politician called Humphrey who loses an election and is then consoled with the thought that he might have won, and Humphrey objects that he could not care less whether somebody else, however similar to him, would have won in another world. Lewis's reply is that (4) isn't a neutral fact about English, it's the rival theory's main claim wearing a disguise. Whether a person is ever literally present in more than one world is exactly what is in dispute, so you cannot settle it by insisting on what "of Tomas himself" has to mean. On counterpart theory the sentence is true, and it is made true by there being a lorry driver in another world who is Tomas's counterpart, and the counterpart theorist says that is all "could have" ever amounted to.

There is also a slide between (5) and (6). Even granting (3) and (4), it doesn't follow that counterpart theory makes these claims false. The theory affirms them; it just offers truth-makers the objector doesn't like. And (6) is supposed to be a reductio to an error theory, which is a completely different complaint from "your analysis doesn't capture what we meant." So at best the argument shows the analysis is unfaithful to our intuitions about the sentence, not that the theory is committed to saying nothing we say about possibility is ever true. That would be a much stranger position to hold, since the theory is offered as an account of true modal discourse.

(b) What they disagree about is an individual's relation to the other worlds. The transworld identity theorist says Tomas is there, whole and entire, in lots of worlds, and thinks this is unproblematic because worlds aren't distant places you spot through a telescope — they're stipulated, so there's no puzzle about picking Tomas out again in another one. Counterpart theory says individuals are world-bound: Tomas exists in exactly one world and is represented elsewhere.

The interesting part is what does the representing. Everyone assumes it's plain qualitative similarity, but that can't be the whole of Lewis's story, because then his view would be in no better shape than a picture that identifies Tomas across worlds by description. What attaches Tomas to the right individual over there is a primitive thisness which doesn't itself cross between worlds but is handed on, so to speak, to whatever stands in the counterpart relation to him. So counterpart theory is haecceitism kept but disciplined: Lewis's quarrel with the other picture isn't that it posits bare hooks, it's that it lets them travel. That's also what lets him say Tomas might have been wildly unlike himself in some respects — the hook does the work, not the resemblance.

What they agree on, and what people think they're disputing: both take de re modal claims to be made true by how things are at other worlds, and neither thinks we have some special faculty for detecting those worlds. The fight is over the truth-making relation, not over whether there are truth-makers out there at all.

(c) First consequence: counterpart theory gives up the necessity of identity. Because one individual in another world can be a counterpart of both Tomas and Marek, the theory allows that two people who are actually distinct could have been one and the same person. Transworld identity with the ordinary logic of identity forbids this — if Tomas isn't Marek then necessarily he isn't. I'd call that a benefit, since it lets counterpart theory handle fusions and origins that the rival has to strain at, though it does sound strange said out loud.

Second consequence: the counterpart relation is a matter of degree, so counterpart theory has to accept that a lot of de re modal claims are indeterminate at the margins, whereas transworld identity has no such problem — either it is Tomas over there or it isn't. That looks like a cost, but arguably the indeterminacy is in the subject matter rather than in the theory, since nobody can say exactly how much a person could have differed and stayed a person.
