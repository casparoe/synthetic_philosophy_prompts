I have spent something like fifteen years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and over that period I have watched the phrase *costly signalling* do an extraordinary amount of work: explaining monastic vows, effective-altruism pledges, startup equity, why people post their failed predictions. I would like to check my priors against the underlying biology, because I suspect the online usage has drifted a long way from it and I do not know how far.

A chapter of a trade book I recently read, on the evolution of honesty and deception, summarizes the theory as follows:

"In the mid-1970s an Israeli ornithologist, Amotz Zahavi, made a proposal that almost everyone considered absurd: the peacock's tail is an advertisement precisely because it is a liability. Biologists dismissed it for more than a decade, John Maynard Smith among them, until Alan Grafen converted it into a game-theoretic model in 1990 and showed that Zahavi had been right all along. The reasoning is now standard. Anyone can claim to be high quality; only someone who genuinely is high quality can afford to demonstrate it. So the cost of the display is not incidental to its reliability, it is the reliability mechanism: the signal must be genuinely wasteful, and it must hurt a poor signaler more than a rich one. In the equilibrium Grafen derived, every signaler pays for its display, including the best one; if the top individual could signal for free, the whole structure would collapse. The same logic scales up to us without modification, which is why an expensive wedding, a vow of poverty, and a founder's three unpaid years are honest signals of exactly what they appear to signal. Anyone who maintains that these could be faked has simply not understood the handicap principle."

Please audit this for me.

1. Go through it essentially sentence by sentence and sort the claims into what a theoretical biologist working on animal communication would accept without qualification, what is a legitimate simplification for a general audience, and what is wrong. I would much rather have the sorting than an overall verdict on the paragraph's quality.

2. Two sentences I cannot evaluate myself. The first is the claim that every signaler pays, including the best one, and that the structure collapses if the top individual signals for free: is that a consequence of the model, or is the author reporting a feature the model does not have? The second is the pairing of "genuinely wasteful" with "must hurt a poor signaler more than a rich one." These strike me as asserting different things, and I cannot tell whether the difference matters, or whether one is quietly doing the work the other is credited with.

3. Is the handicap principle a claim that cost *is* the mechanism of honesty, or one proposed mechanism among several? If there are others, what separates them, and how does a biologist sort a trait that is mechanically impossible to fake — the depth of a bullfrog's croak, say — against a display that is merely expensive to produce? I have the impression this sorting is less clean than popular treatments suggest, and I would like to know how settled it is. Relatedly: the model treats the cost of signalling and the benefit of being believed as quantities that can be added together in one currency. Is that assumption doing real work in the result, and what happens to the conclusion if it fails?

4. Does the human conclusion survive the correction of the biology? Once the overstated parts are stripped out, is there still a valid inference from peacocks to weddings and vows of poverty, or does that inference need premises the paragraph never states? I am inclined to think that much of what these forums call signalling is something else entirely — a trait that cannot be faked rather than one that is expensive to fake — and I want to know whether that instinct tracks a real distinction or is just my preference for a tidier story.

5. One case from my own world, to test the machinery. In the communities I read, one of the highest-status moves available is publishing a detailed, self-critical post-mortem of your own badly wrong forecast. It costs standing, and someone who is genuinely miscalibrated cannot keep doing it indefinitely. Is that a handicap in the technical sense, an index, a convention backed by social punishment, or none of these? I note that here the cost is paid in standing and the benefit is also received in standing, which may or may not be the arrangement the models have in mind.

Please tell me how confident you are in your main claims, and single out the one point in your answer where you think you are most likely to be wrong or to be overreading what the models actually show. Write in American English.
