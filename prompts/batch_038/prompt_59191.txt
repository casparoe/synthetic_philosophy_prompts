You are a philosopher of medicine with a serious working knowledge of clinical epidemiology — someone who has thought hard about the naturalism/normativism debate over disease and who is equally at home with the practicalities of diagnostic threshold-setting. Please think carefully and resist the urge to referee this by splitting the difference evenly; some moves below are better than others, and I want to know which.

Context. I sit on a hospital working group drafting internal language for our employee health screening program. The group was deciding whether to describe people who fall above a newly lowered blood-pressure threshold as "having hypertension" or merely as "having elevated cardiovascular risk," and the drafting conversation turned into a philosophy argument between two members: Iris, a nephrologist who takes a broadly naturalist line, and Deshawn, a bioethicist with a normativist sympathy. I was taking notes and did not take a side. Here is the exchange as I recorded it (the figures Deshawn cites in the first move come from our briefing handout; please treat them as stipulated rather than going off to check them).

(1) Deshawn: When the national guideline panel lowered the hypertension threshold from 140/90 to 130/80, something on the order of thirty million American adults became patients overnight. Not one of their bodies changed by a single molecule. So "disease" cannot be the name of a biological kind. It names a decision we make, and the naturalist's talk about species-typical function is just that decision wearing a lab coat.

(2) Iris: You're confusing the ruler with the thing being measured. Vascular damage accumulates continuously as pressure rises, so any threshold has to sit somewhere on a continuum; moving the tick mark neither creates the continuum nor alters any fact about it. We do the same thing with the poverty line and with "tall." Choosing a cutoff is conventional, but nobody concludes from that conventionality that height is a value-laden property. I can grant your entire description and deny your conclusion.

(3) Deshawn: The height analogy fails, and it fails for a reason that matters here. For height there is a fact about what is typical that has nothing whatever to do with what we want. For blood pressure, the cutoff moved in one specific direction: toward more diagnosis, more medication, and a larger market for the drug classes studied by the societies whose members staffed the panel. And the cutoff was not chosen by biology. It was chosen by weighing the expected benefit of treating against the cost, the side effects, and the evidence — which is thin precisely in the 130-to-160 range. That weighing is a values calculation, not a measurement.

(4) Iris: Who benefits from a definition is a separate question from whether the definition is biological, and moving from the first to the second is a genetic fallacy. Notice also that the trade-off you just described concerns treatment, not disease. Whether a given person's vascular function is statistically subnormal for a member of their reference class is an empirical question answerable without consulting anyone's preferences. We then decide, separately, whether to treat. Two questions — and your argument only works by collapsing them into one.

(5) Deshawn: Fine, then run your empirical question honestly and see what it presupposes. A reference class is itself a selection: age, sex, sometimes race. And your criterion is contribution to the goals of survival and reproduction — goals that nobody in this room is actually treating anyone for. Our own member Dr. Okonjo is eighty-two with a systolic of 135. She is not failing at reproduction, and the marginal survival benefit of lowering her pressure is small at best. Whether her state counts as a disease depends entirely on which goal and which class you index to, and indexing is normative all the way down.

(6) Iris: That is exactly what the reference-class machinery is there to handle. "Normal" is always normal-for-a-class, and the classes are fixed by biology, not by policy: age and sex track real, documented differences in functional levels. As for survival and reproduction, that phrase is a way of stating statistically what an organ system is *for* across the species — it is not a claim about what any individual patient should want or be treated for. If you delete it, you lose the only thing that distinguishes a subnormal heart from a merely unusual one.

(7) Deshawn: Then apply it to the cases we were actually arguing about this afternoon. The six-millimeter papillary thyroid cancer that will never grow. The low-grade prostate lesion in a man of ninety. The endurance athlete with a resting pulse of 38. All three are statistically atypical internal states of the sort your criterion is supposed to pick out, and your theory has to call one of them a disease and the others not. What does the sorting work? Not function. It's whether we judge the state to be bad for the person. That's the value judgment you claimed to have left outside the ground floor.

(8) Iris: The athlete is easy, and you know it: the criterion is functional *ability*, not performance under one set of conditions, and a conditioned heart has greater capacity, not less. I'll grant the incidental tumor is harder, and I'll grant further that "lesion present" is not automatically "disease" if there is no functional derangement and no expected harm. But that is a concession about how much of the naturalist story is doing the work — not a concession that values enter at the foundation.

Neither budged, and the chair asked me to write up where they actually disagree rather than who won. Please do the following.

First, before rendering any verdict, spend a short paragraph reconstructing each position in its strongest form — what a sophisticated naturalist and a sophisticated normativist would say about this exchange if they had read the other's transcript.

Then adjudicate move by move. Rate each of (1) through (8) on a five-point scale running from clearly mistaken through a wash to clearly right, and justify each rating. Where a single move does two things — as several here do, bundling a strong point with a weak one — split it and rate the parts separately. For each move, name the premise that is actually doing the load-bearing work and say whether it survives the reply that follows it.

Then answer four sub-questions directly, and say who is right on each: (a) can a change of threshold literally change who is diseased, or only who is labeled; (b) is the selection of a reference class a normative act, and if so, does that infect the criterion or only its application; (c) does the fact that thresholds are chosen by weighing benefits against harms and costs show that *disease* is value-laden, or only that *treatment* is; and (d) do overdiagnosis cases like the indolent thyroid lesion refute the naturalist account, or merely force a refinement of it — and if a refinement, what does the need for that refinement reveal?

Then tell me what the whole exchange misses. I specifically want to know whether the naturalism-versus-normativism binary is even the right frame for what Iris and Deshawn are doing, whether there are intermediate positions that dissolve parts of their dispute, whether the two of them are equivocating on "disease" between an internal-state sense and a social-role sense, and whether either has misstated the position they are attacking in a way that makes their own case look stronger than it is.

Finally, return to the drafting question: given your adjudication, which formulation should our screening document use for people above the lower threshold — "hypertension" or "elevated cardiovascular risk" — and does the answer depend on settling any part of the philosophical dispute, or is it independent of it?

No citations, bibliography, or reading list, please, and no need to prove you know the literature; if you invoke a named theorist, do so only where the position really is theirs. I want your own reasoning, and I would rather you call a move confused than diplomatically hedge.
