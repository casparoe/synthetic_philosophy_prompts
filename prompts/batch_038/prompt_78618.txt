Fifteen proposals a year land on my desk for a university press series in comparative economic history, and most of them promise to answer some large contrastive question: why mechanized industry emerged in Britain rather than in the Yangzi delta, why modern science took the form it did in Europe and not in China, why the West rose and the rest did not. My readers' reports on these are uselessly divided — one calls the question profound, the other calls it malformed and unanswerable as asked — and I have no way to adjudicate before committing two specialists and a year to a manuscript. I need something I can run myself, in about an hour, without specialist knowledge of the period.

Write me a procedure for testing whether a contrastive historical question is well posed, and whether a given manuscript could in principle answer it rather than merely discuss it. Give me the questions to ask in order and what would count as a sufficient answer to each: how the foil gets fixed and who gets to fix it; what the explanandum has to be described as for the contrast to be determinate at all; what it takes for a candidate cause to explain the difference between the two sides rather than just the fact on one side; and how to handle causes present on both sides, causes sufficient but not necessary, causes that would equally have produced the foil, and cases where the two sides are not units of the same kind. Mark clearly where the procedure terminates in a verdict I can act on, where it hands the decision to a historian's judgment, and which distinctions it may safely compress for a non-specialist and which it must not.

Then run it on three proposals: an England-versus-Yangzi-delta account of the industrial takeoff; a restatement of the Needham question; and a book from our best-selling author asking why the West rose and the rest did not, which I have already assured the marketing department we will publish. At least one of the three should produce an answer I will not like. Close by stating what the procedure cannot detect — the ways a malformed contrast can pass every step.
