**Question 3.** State Adams's thesis about indicative conditionals. Then reconstruct Lewis's argument that no indicative conditional can have truth conditions that obey it. What exactly is the "triviality" Lewis ends up with, and what does he conclude about indicative conditionals?

**Student's answer:**

Adams's thesis says the probability of an indicative conditional "if A, B" equals the conditional probability of B on A: P(A→B) = P(B|A), whenever P(A) > 0. Adams treats this as a claim about truth conditions — the proposition expressed by "if A, B" is supposed to have probability P(B|A) — and Lewis's objection is that no proposition can have that property.

The argument works on the four-cell partition A∧B, A∧¬B, ¬A∧B, ¬A∧¬B. Suppose P(A∧B) and P(A∧¬B) are both non-zero, so both cells are live. Now apply the thesis to the probability function conditioned on B: P(A→B | B) = P(B | A∧B) = 1. Apply it to the function conditioned on ¬B: P(A→B | ¬B) = P(B | A∧¬B) = 0. Total probability then gives P(A→B) = P(A→B|B)P(B) + P(A→B|¬B)P(¬B) = P(B). Together with the thesis that's P(B|A) = P(B), so B comes out independent of A. Worth stressing there's no extra premise hiding anywhere: this is just the probability calculus applied to one credence function, so what Lewis has shown is that no probability function at all can satisfy Adams's thesis.

That's the triviality — the conditional is probabilistically equivalent to its own consequent, i.e. its probability has collapsed to either 0 or 1, which is absurd for something we can rationally be unsure about. The result also kills the material-conditional reading, since P(A⊃B) = P(¬A) + P(A∧B), which coincides with P(B|A) only by accident. Lewis concludes the indicative conditional doesn't express a proposition after all, which is roughly where Adams was heading anyway, except Lewis shows it's forced on you rather than a matter of taste.
