I'm a second-year philosophy MA student and, kind of by accident, I'm the student member of my university's animal research oversight committee (we call it the AWC — basically our IACUC). I'm also in a seminar this term on animal minds and welfare science, so I'm getting the theory and the paperwork at the same time and its a real knot. Next Tuesday we're reviewing a protocol from the aquaculture group that I think most of the committee will wave through on jurisdictional grounds, and I want to circulate something written the night before so I'm not just the person making noises in the meeting.

The protocol: a 14-week growth trial on Pacific whiteleg shrimp postlarvae, 3,840 animals total, run as 48 recirculating tanks of 80, testing a plant-based replacement for fishmeal in the feed. Every animal is killed at the terminal endpoint by plunging the tank basket into an ice slurry, which the PI describes as the standard method at that scale and as producing rapid torpor rather than distress. There's also a linked side study on 160 shrimp whose whole purpose is to find out whether that method is actually distressing: animals are assigned to ice slurry, to slow chilling of the tank water, or to an overdose of a chemical anaesthetic, and they're video-scored during induction for escape and avoidance behaviour. So the study that's supposed to settle whether the killing method hurts is the part that inflicts the most candidate-harm, which nobody in our pre-meeting packet addresses. Our policy defines a covered animal as a live non-human vertebrate (including independently feeding larval forms) plus cephalopods — it's modelled directly on the main EU research-animals directive, and the PI cites that scope at length to argue shrimp simply aren't our business. But the university's new research integrity statement says humane treatment applies to any animal used in research, and the state aquaculture grant funding the trial requires us to certify humane treatment of "all research animals" without defining the term. Second wrinkle: our policy exempts a protocol from full committee review if no single treatment group exceeds 200 animals, the stated rationale being that harm is assessed group by group. The PI has arranged things so that no tank and no feed arm exceeds 200, and argues that even if the committee takes jurisdiction voluntarily, the protocol is still exempt and needs only a veterinary sign-off. Our chair suspects this is gaming the rule but couldn't say why in the meeting.

Then there's the argument I keep hearing in seminar, which I expect to be pushed on. One classmate says the committee should do expected-value reasoning: put some probability on shrimp being sentient (he floats 0.2), multiply by number of animals and severity, and compare against alternatives — which he thinks makes it easy, because 3,840 animals at 0.2 comes out worse than a fish protocol we approved last month with 60 animals. Another says that's exactly the wrong frame: you can't put a credible number on it, the animals can't consent, and treating moral status as a discount factor is itself the objectionable move, so the committee should apply a precautionary rule — if there's a non-negligible chance of sentience, treat the species as sentient and stop counting. I don't fully buy either, and I'd like the memo to actually take a position on which frame a committee can legitimately use, rather than listing them.

Can you draft the memo I'd circulate — aimed at the committee, 800–1,000 words, in a tone I could read out loud without sounding like I'm lecturing scientists? It needs: a clear recommendation among (a) decline jurisdiction and refer to veterinary staff, (b) take jurisdiction and approve with conditions, (c) take jurisdiction and return for revision; if you go with conditions, make them concrete and operational (who signs off, what the induction method should be for each arm, whether the side study runs before or concurrently with the growth trial, what the humane endpoint rule is, and how animals should be counted — per tank, per feed arm, or per protocol, with a reason); a serious and honest treatment of the PI's strongest case, which is not a dumb one, since the trial is meant to cut fishmeal use and it seems self-defeating to block aquaculture welfare research precisely because the welfare methods aren't yet settled; your resolution of the threshold-rule question; and a paragraph saying what, if anything, the committee should do about the policy gap going forward. Please don't give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to studies — I can't check them and I'm not saying anything in that room I haven't verified. Reason from the case as I've described it. If you refer to how other jurisdictions actually handle decapods, keep it to a sentence and mark it clearly as something I should double-check before saying it out loud. End with a single final line in exactly this form: Verdict: DECLINE JURISDICTION / APPROVE WITH CONDITIONS / RETURN FOR REVISION (choose one, and put nothing after it).
