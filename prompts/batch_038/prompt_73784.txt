For about eighteen months I have worked as an annotation lead on the post-training side of a lab that builds large language models, and my job is mostly to keep a preference-collection pipeline running. I would like to describe that pipeline as concretely as I can, because I suspect the interface is doing philosophy without saying so, and I want someone to tell me which philosophy. Raters are shown a prompt and two candidate responses and must click one of them. There is no option meaning both are acceptable, no option meaning neither is, and no option meaning they are on a par in a way that resists comparison; nor is there anywhere to record how much better the chosen response was. The click stream is then fit with a Bradley–Terry-style logistic model, which converts win rates between pairs into differences between two real numbers, and that single number per response becomes the reward signal for policy optimization. Where raters disagree, the disagreement is not preserved anywhere: it is pooled as label noise and averaged out, so the trained reward model expresses one ordering rather than a distribution of orderings. Our written guidelines tell raters to answer not as themselves but as a stand-in for a general audience, explicitly setting aside their own politics, religious commitments, and personal tastes. Downstream, a second layer has the model critique and revise its own drafts against a written list of principles, and the preferences generated that way are substituted for human labels on the sensitive categories. Meanwhile the finished product is marketed as something that adapts itself to each individual user's aims and style. Two internal details may matter. When we once piloted a tie button, the extra responses could not be absorbed by the fitting procedure and the pilot was dropped; and when we once collected a rough intensity rating alongside each choice, it was discarded because variance across raters was too high to be usable.

With that description in hand: reconstruct the theory of value this practice presupposes, and be scrupulous about separating what it strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably. Does clicking one of two responses and nothing else commit the lab to the view that betterness is a single scalar magnitude, or only to the weaker claim that local orderings exist? Does fitting the Bradley–Terry model commit it to completeness and transitivity in the underlying relation, or does it merely enforce those properties as an artifact of the estimator, given that the tie-button pilot failed for mathematical rather than metaphysical reasons? Does pooling disagreement as noise commit it to the thesis that disagreement about value is always somebody's error, converging under good conditions on one answer? Does instructing raters to speak for a general audience commit it to the agent-neutrality of reasons? And does the whole apparatus locate value in outputs rather than in dispositions, in what a system says on this occasion rather than in any stable character it has? For each commitment, say which rival positions it forecloses or renders literally inexpressible at the interface, and attach the standard labels from the metaethics literature where such labels exist; where I have described something that does not map onto any view you know, say so rather than forcing a label.

Then turn to coherence and to replacement. Is a scalar, trade-off-permitting reward signal consistent with a principle list that appears to function as a set of lexical constraints rather than as one more term to be weighed? Is training toward an impartial average consistent with selling personalization to each user? Does substituting model-generated preferences for human ones make sense only under a view on which the normative facts are independent of human responses, and is that view compatible with the way the same lab talks publicly about human values and about respecting disagreement among them? I am especially interested in how much of the practice is philosophically underdetermined: my suspicion is that the ranking interface is metaphysically humble, that almost any theory of value able to generate local orderings would license it, and that the substantive commitments live in the aggregation step and in the substitution step rather than in the clicking. Argue for or against that suspicion. Finally, take one commitment, overturn it, and tell me what the pipeline would have to become: if par and incomparability were real, or if values were genuinely plural and not reducible to one ordering, or if disagreement were a permanent feature of the target rather than noise, what would the interface have to collect, what would the reward model have to output, what would the optimizer have to do with a set of permissible options instead of a maximum, and who would then make the choice the scalar used to make for us? Please aim for roughly a thousand to fifteen hundred words, organized in a few clearly labeled sections.
