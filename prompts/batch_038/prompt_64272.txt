You are a philosopher specializing in social epistemology, with a research interest in how the conceptual apparatus of epistemic injustice applies (or fails to apply) to automated institutional systems. Think carefully before answering, and work from the content of the concepts themselves rather than from lists of sources or citations.

**Context.** I sit as a patient representative on the ethics advisory group of a public hospital network. Two years ago the network replaced nurse-led intake interviews with an automated pipeline: patients record a spoken account of their symptoms at a kiosk or over the phone, a speech-recognition model transcribes the recording, a second model converts the transcript into a structured summary plus a triage score, and clinicians see only the summary and the score. A community organization obtained an internal audit through a records request. Controlling for presenting complaint and for comorbidities, recordings delivered in one stigmatized working-class dialect of the region showed markedly higher transcription error, produced shorter and vaguer summaries, and received lower triage scores. The audit also noted that repeat callers from the affected neighborhoods had, over successive visits, changed how they described their symptoms.

At last month's meeting, two members argued at length about how the finding should be characterized. I am reporting the exchange as neutrally as I can; I did not take a side at the time and I would rather not signal which of them I found more persuasive, because I want an adjudication of the arguments as they were actually made.

**(1)** Miriam said this is a clear case of testimonial injustice: an entire class of patients is systematically awarded less credibility than it is due, and the harm is exactly the one the concept was built to name — they are wronged in their capacity as givers of knowledge, not merely as recipients of care.

**(2)** Tom replied that the concept is being used loosely. Testimonial injustice, as it is standardly defined, requires a hearer whose perception of the speaker's credibility is distorted by an identity prejudice. A recognition model has no perceptions and holds no prejudices; it has error rates that vary by accent. If every statistical disparity in accuracy counts as testimonial injustice, the term collapses into a synonym for "unfair system," and we lose the specific phenomenon it was introduced to pick out.

**(3)** Miriam said the model is not the only hearer in the chain. Clinicians read the summary and act on it, and the prejudices of the wider society are embedded both in the training data and in the readers who treat a garbled, heavily hedged summary as evidence of an unclear or unreliable patient. On her account the machine is a conduit for a human credibility judgment, not a substitute for one.

**(4)** Tom granted that there is a human hearer at the end of the chain, but said this proves too much for Miriam's purposes. If the wrong is done by clinicians discounting patients they find hard to follow, then it is the old wrong, one that predates the software and was present in the nurse-led interviews it replaced — merely laundered through new machinery. The honest conclusion, he said, is that nothing of normative significance changed, which is not what the community organization was claiming.

**(5)** Miriam said two things did change. First, the pipeline standardizes the discount across every intake and removes the recording from view, so a patient can no longer contest a judgment whose basis she cannot inspect. Second — and this she considered the deeper point — patients have begun truncating their own accounts, describing symptoms in the flat, standardized vocabulary the system handles well and omitting what they expect will not be understood. There is a term for that, she said, something like testimonial smothering: a speaker cuts down her own testimony because she has judged the audience unable to receive it.

**(6)** Tom said he accepted that self-truncation is a real and serious harm, but denied that it is hermeneutical injustice, which Miriam had also invoked. Hermeneutical injustice requires a collective gap in interpretive resources, produced by the marginalization of a group from the practices that generate shared meanings, so that an experience cannot be made intelligible at all — the canonical illustration being that there was no name for sexual harassment until there was. Here, he argued, nothing is missing: everyone possesses the concepts chest pain, numbness, racing heart, and the patients used them competently before the kiosk existed. What they lack is not conceptual resources but fidelity in one channel. If degraded articulation fidelity counts as hermeneutical injustice, the concept stops discriminating and can no longer tell us anything.

**(7)** Miriam replied that the audit itself shows the gap to be collective and institutional rather than individual: the intake schema contains no fields for the ways these patients actually characterize their symptoms, the community had no part in designing the schema, and the resulting summaries cannot express what was said to them. Hermeneutical marginalization, she said, concerns who participates in shaping the shared pool of meanings, and on that question the answer here is not in doubt.

**(8)** Tom said that if these patients possess perfectly good concepts among themselves and the institution simply refuses to accommodate them, then the wrong is not a lack of resources but the imposition of the institution's resources upon them. That may well be an injustice — and a political one, concerning exclusion from design — but filing it under hermeneutical injustice obscures the mechanism rather than revealing it, and it lets the network off with a story about a gap in meaning rather than a story about a decision they made.

Neither would concede. Please act as referee for this exchange.
