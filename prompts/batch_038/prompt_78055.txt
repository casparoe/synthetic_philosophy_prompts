I have spent three years running a freight and warehousing depot in a West African port city for a company whose head office is in Rotterdam, and in January they added the local compliance function to my responsibilities. Until now that function consisted of a laminated copy of the group code of conduct kept inside a cupboard, and the code was written in Europe by people who have never cleared a container through this port. My predecessor in the operational role was a local woman who held the job for fifteen years; she has retired, and I am increasingly aware that I cannot do what she did, and that if the head office ever examined how she did it, they would regard it as a scandal. I have no training in philosophy or law — I did a business diploma and went to work — but I have been keeping a private list of the things I have actually done and seen, together with my honest verdict on each, because I wanted to find out whether my judgements add up to anything. They may not. Here they are.

First: a port official expects cash before he will release a container, and the regulations say the container must be cleared within forty-eight hours; it had been sitting for nine days, holding antimalarials. I paid, and I would pay again, and I do not believe I did anything wrong. Second: the same official, the same sum, asking to move a container of used tyres ahead of a queue of food shipments, where the tyres ought properly to have been inspected and would not have been. I refused and would refuse again; that, to me, is the real thing. Third: my predecessor attended every wedding, naming ceremony and funeral in the district for fifteen years, never without an envelope, took nothing for herself, and was by a wide margin the most effective person ever to hold the job; I regard her conduct as not merely permissible but as the reason she was good at it, and yet under the new code I would be obliged to dismiss her. Fourth: a forwarding agent sent me two cases of wine at the new year, worth rather more than my weekly wage here; I drank them, did not declare them, and am certain they have not shifted how I treat him, and I think that was acceptable. Fifth: the same agent offered me the equivalent value in cash, in an envelope, in my own office, and I was genuinely angry — so the difference between the wine and the cash is the thing I most want explained, since in money terms there is no difference at all. Sixth: our depot manager hired his brother as shift supervisor; the brother was plainly the strongest of eleven applicants, the relationship was declared in writing before the interviews began, and the manager sat out the panel; I think that was entirely proper, whereas my Dutch colleagues, when I mentioned it in passing, thought it the most obvious instance of wrongdoing in the whole list, which rather unsettled me. Seventh: a mining concern builds a village a school, after which the village stops complaining about its boreholes running brown; our own company pays a similar sum into a national education fund administered in the capital, receives a tax receipt and a paragraph in the annual report, and is answerable to nobody in the district — and I think the second arrangement is worse than the first, though I cannot defend that. Eighth: we retain a lobbying firm in the capital, two of whose three partners are former ministers, and it has been extremely effective for us; I do not think this is wrongdoing, though I have begun to suspect that the only reason it is not is that it is legal and invoiced, while a competitor's payment of a retainer to a minister's nephew for visibly no work at all is called corrupt by everyone, myself included. Ninth, and the one that keeps me awake: my aunt paid my school fees when I was fourteen, on the plain understanding that I would look after her in her old age, which I have done and will continue to do; if that is a benefit conferred now against a return expected later, and if the mine's school is corrupt on those grounds, I cannot see why my aunt's fees escape.

So I would like to know whether there is a principle, or a small number of candidate principles, that generates these verdicts rather than merely describing them. Please state it as precisely as you can, and tell me which of my cases motivates each clause of it — I want to see what work each part is doing, and I want to be told plainly if some clause exists only to rescue a judgement I ought to give up. Then apply it to a case I have not listed: the port workers' association has asked every operator on the quay to contribute to its Eid fund, which is redistributed to the families of dockers injured at the quayside, and the association's chairman also sits on the licensing board that decides how many operators may work here. Finally, and this is where I most need to be argued with rather than reassured: my colleagues in Rotterdam are confident that their code states a neutral standard and that local practice here is a set of exceptions to it, while my staff are equally confident that the code is simply European custom with a logo on it. I do not know how to settle that without begging the question on one side or the other, and I should like you to say whether it can be settled at all, and if so what the principle above implies about which of the two is provincial. Please write to me in British English, in continuous prose as one would write a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables — and please do not send me a list of authors or books to consult, since I have neither the time nor, at present, the internet connection to chase them.
