I am assembling a graduate seminar module on the philosophical anatomy of peace settlements, and I want a worked case study rather than a summary of the relevant literature. The case is the Treaty of Versailles of June 1919; the instrument of analysis is Kant's 1795 essay on perpetual peace. What interests me is not the usual gesture toward the essay as a precursor of the United Nations, but whether its actual provisions — including the six preliminary articles, which are almost always skipped in favor of the three definitive ones — have real diagnostic purchase on this particular settlement, and what the settlement's collapse between 1919 and 1939 shows about the essay as a piece of political theory.

So that the analysis does not stall on historical detail, here are the features of the episode I take to be salient; correct me if any of them is wrong or misleadingly stated. The Covenant of the League of Nations was incorporated as Part I of the treaty itself. Germany was excluded from the negotiations and signed under protest. Article 231 assigned responsibility to Germany and her allies for all the loss and damage suffered by the Allied and Associated Powers, and it supplied the legal basis for the reparations claim, whose total was fixed two years later at a sum widely regarded at the time as unpayable. The disarmament provisions were unilateral in fact, opening with a declaration that German disarmament was to be the prelude to a general limitation of the armaments of all nations — a general limitation that never arrived — while capping the German army at a volunteer force of one hundred thousand, abolishing conscription, and demilitarizing and occupying the Rhineland for fifteen years. Territorial settlement was uneven in its use of self-determination: plebiscites in Schleswig, Upper Silesia, and East Prussia, but none in Alsace-Lorraine, none in the Saar until 1935, and a prohibition on union between Germany and German-speaking Austria that could be lifted only with the consent of the League Council. The former German and Ottoman colonies were redistributed to the victors as mandates held as a trust on behalf of the international community. The United States Senate refused ratification, so the principal architect of the League never joined it. A leading British Treasury official attached to the delegation resigned and published, within six months, a widely read attack arguing that the treaty's economic terms were unworkable and would produce a further war. Meanwhile, in 1919, Allied troops were still on the ground in the Russian Civil War — in the north, around Odessa, and in the far east — and the German polity that signed the treaty adopted its republican constitution only after signature.

With those facts fixed, please deliver the following.

First, run the preliminary articles against the settlement one at a time, and for each give a verdict: which feature of Versailles violates it, which feature satisfies it, and where the article simply fails to bite on anything the peacemakers did. Two deserve special treatment. Against the first article — that no peace treaty is valid which tacitly reserves matter for a future war — decide whether Versailles counts as a peace treaty at all on Kant's own diagnostic, and whether the resigned official's economic argument is an instance of that test being applied or a different kind of objection entirely. Against the third and fourth articles — the abolition of standing armies and the prohibition on contracting national debt in connection with external state affairs — assess my working hypothesis: that the disarmament asymmetry, and not the reparations burden, is the more Kantian explanation of why the settlement failed. Argue for or against it, and say what different remedies the two diagnoses imply and whether those remedies conflict.

Second, take the definitive articles. On the first: whether Germany was a republic in Kant's sense at the moment of signature, given the timing of its constitution, and note explicitly that Kant's republicanism is a matter of representation and the separation of powers rather than of democracy, which he treats with suspicion — so do not read a democratic peace thesis back into the essay. On the second: whether the League was in fact the voluntary, non-coercive federation of free states that the essay prescribes, or something Kant would not have recognized; and whether the interval from 1919 to 1939 vindicates his refusal of a world republic on grounds that it would end in despotism, or instead constitutes evidence that a league without coercive power cannot do the work he assigns to it. On the third: what cosmopolitan right, limited to the conditions of universal hospitality, makes of the mandate system, and whether the essay's condemnation of the conduct of commercial states abroad has anything to say about a trust administered by the victors in the name of civilization.

Third, address two second-order pieces of the essay. Does Kant have any resources for making sense of a clause that assigns moral guilt for a war, given that his proposal is prospective and grounded in right rather than retributive? And do the peacemakers' maxims survive the transcendental formula of public right offered in the essay's appendix — the test by which a maxim affecting the rights of others is unjust if it cannot be publicly avowed without defeating its own purpose — considering both the closed character of the negotiation and the fact that the treaty's most damaging criticism came from a participant who published it?

Fourth, run the analysis in the opposite direction: identify the places where the episode embarrasses the theory rather than being explained by it. I would expect at least the guarantee of perpetual peace that the first supplement locates in nature's mechanism and in the spirit of commerce, tested against a Europe whose commercial interdependence in 1914 was unprecedented; the fifth preliminary article's prohibition on forcibly interfering in another state's constitution, tested against interventions then underway in a country whose government had been constituted by revolution and was unrecognized by the interveners; and the worry that the essay's own concession that the ideal is to be approached only asymptotically renders it unfalsifiable. Say plainly where the text is silent and where a reading is being supplied by charity.

Fifth, be constructive. Specify three amendments a Versailles drafted under the essay's articles would have contained, and for each say whether it was politically available in 1919 given French security fears, British electoral pressure to exact payment, and American reluctance to assume European obligations. If it was not available, say whether that is a limit of the theory or a limit of the actors.

Close with a single defended verdict: is Versailles a confirmation of the essay, in that Kant's framework names precisely the features that undid the settlement; a refutation, in that his own prescription was implemented and failed; or neither, because the League was not his federation and the case is beside the point. State what evidence would move you off that verdict. Please do not append a bibliography or reading list, and quote Kant sparingly and only where the wording itself matters, without attributing quotations to a particular translation. Aim for roughly eighteen hundred to twenty-five hundred words.
