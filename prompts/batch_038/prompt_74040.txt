I spent thirty-five years in experimental condensed-matter physics and have come to philosophy seriously only since retiring. In Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach there is the image of the careenium: a fluid-filled skull in which the symbols of a mind are whirlpools — his simmballs — that merge, split and spawn one another, and whose interactions, once there are enough of them, begin to drive the motion of the fluid itself. On that picture the symbols end up pushing the substrate around. My reading group takes it to settle the case against reductionism; I am less sure, because it looks to me rather like an effective-field-theory argument, in which a collective variable genuinely constrains the microscopic degrees of freedom without being anything over and above them.

Please audit the analogy for me. Set out the correspondences explicitly — fluid and vorticity against neurons and their firing patterns, the merging and spawning of vortices against the combination and association of concepts, and whatever is meant to play the part of the equations of motion in the mental case. Then separate the disanalogies that actually threaten the intended conclusion from those that are merely untidy: vortices are patterns in a continuous medium with a well-understood coarse-graining, and it is far from obvious that neural activity has an analogue of either. Above all, tell me whether the downward causation survives — do the whirlpools have causal purchase that a complete account at the level of the fluid would miss, or are they a convenient redescription of it? If the picture fails, say what would serve better in its place. I should like a committed answer: take a side on whether symbol-level causation is doing genuine work or is dispensable, and argue for that judgement, rather than laying out the options and leaving the choice to me.
