I am a senior at a small high school that runs an annual philosophy essay prize, and this year I am one of the readers handling the first round. Each reader scores the submissions assigned to them on a ten-point scale and writes comments that go back to the author, who then gets one week to revise. I have been given the submission below. I have read enough Kierkegaard to follow the argument, but I do not want to score this on my impressions alone, since the comments have to be specific enough for the author to act on. Please act as the reader.

The scoring bands the prize uses are: 9–10, first-class and near publishable; 7–8, strong, with at most minor slips; 5–6, competent but containing a substantive error or a real gap; 3–4, only partly addresses the question; 1–2, does not address it.

What I need from you:

1. One score out of ten on those bands.
2. Numbered comments keyed to the paragraphs of the submission. Each comment should name the particular claim it concerns, say whether it holds up, and, where it does not, say what the correct account is. Where the submission is right, say so briefly and say why, so the author knows what to keep in revision.
3. One sentence at the end identifying the single largest problem in the answer, if you think there is one.

Judge what is on the page. Assume that neither I nor the author has the text to hand right now, so please do not send me off to check passages, and do not include a bibliography or a list of further reading. If a claim is flatly mistaken rather than debatable, say so plainly instead of calling it one reading among others; if a claim is genuinely contestable, say what the strongest defense of it would be and whether the author supplies it.

The question set for the prize was:

In A Literary Review (1846), Kierkegaard argues that the press brought into existence a category that had not existed before, namely "the public." (a) Explain what he means by that claim. (b) Explain how it relates to his account of "leveling." (c) On his own premises, would a regulation requiring every published online comment to carry its author's real name advance or frustrate what he wants? Answer all three parts.

The submission:

Paragraph 1. Kierkegaard's A Literary Review responds to a novel by the Danish writer Thomasine Gyllembourg, also titled Two Ages. It is one of the works he issued under a pseudonym, and that matters for how we read it: like his other aesthetic writings, it sets out a point of view that is not straightforwardly his own, so its complaints about the press should be treated as a position being tried on rather than as settled doctrine. Even so, the target is clear enough.

Paragraph 2. The review contrasts two ages. The age of revolution was passionate: people took sides, acted, and were prepared to live with the consequences of what they had done. The present age is reflective and passionless; it replaces decision with understanding, deliberation, and second thoughts about second thoughts. Out of this passionlessness comes leveling, which is the process Kierkegaard most wants to stop, because it wears away every distinction, of rank, of authority, and of the difference between what matters and what does not, and a society without distinctions cannot produce greatness. Underneath the leveling he identifies envy: those who cannot do anything resent those who can, and the age turns into a society of spectators. He knew this from experience, having just been attacked by The Corsair, a satirical paper that made his appearance, his trousers, and his habits on his daily walks into a running joke for the whole city.

Paragraph 3. The engine of leveling, on this account, is the press. Kierkegaard's central claim is that the daily newspaper created something that had not existed before: "the public." Earlier ages had peoples, generations, classes, and crowds, concrete bodies of actual persons who were present at what happened and could be held to account for it. A public is an abstraction instead: it is composed of nobody in particular, and no one who belongs to it is ever present at the thing the public is said to want. The press makes this possible by carrying reports of everything to everybody, and by turning every serious matter into chatter, into a summary, a comment on the comment, and a take. The harm, accordingly, is informational: what reaches the reader is flattened, secondhand, and frequently just wrong, so the public is systematically misinformed and its opinions are worthless.

Paragraph 4. That diagnosis decides the policy question. Kierkegaard objects to a public made of anonymous voices addressing anonymous readers, because anonymity is what lets a person speak without standing behind what he says. A rule requiring every online comment to carry its author's real name would remove exactly that feature: the commentators would become identifiable individuals again, and the public would stop being an abstraction and become what a crowd once was, a collection of actual people who can be asked what they meant. Kierkegaard would not want to go further, since he is no reformer and does not believe that laws can make anyone good, but on his own premises he would have to welcome a requirement that no one address everyone unless he is prepared to sign for it.
