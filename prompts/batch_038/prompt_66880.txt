Just had a three-hour argument with a mate about state apologies and my head's spinning, so I want to slow right down and get the spec sorted before we go another round.

His line: an official apology for a historical wrong only counts if it's costed — if the apologising state actually gives something up — and if that state isn't still carrying on with some version of the wrong today. Anything less is cheap speech, a press release with feelings. I half agree, but I kept coming up with cases that don't fit cleanly either way, and I don't want to trade examples any more until I know what we're even holding these things to.

So: write me the desiderata for a theory of the adequate state apology — the list of things any account has to be able to deliver before I'd take it seriously. The machinery, not the history of who said what. The purpose is fixed, and it matters: I want it good enough that a small committee could actually use it as a checklist when drafting an apology, or when deciding not to issue one. So it needs to be operational rather than taxonomic.

At minimum it has to cope with the following — add more if I've missed the important ones:

- an apology offered by a government that didn't commit the wrong, and was arguably founded by opposing it
- victims who are dead, victims who can't be located, victims who flatly refuse the apology, and descendants who say it isnt theirs to accept
- me: the citizen who thinks the wrong wasn't a wrong at all. Is an apology issued in my name binding on me, and does it need my authorisation to be mine?
- apologies for apologies — the second one after the first got walked back, or came out conditional, or was disowned by the next goverment. Does your list nest, or does it quietly assume one-shot apologies?
- the gap between regretting that something happened and accepting responsibility for it, and whether the exact wording carries any weight or is just legal theatre
- whether an apology can be part of a deal that closes off further claims, and what that does to the wrong if it can
- what happens when the same state issues dozens of these over the years: does the practice devalue, and is "sincerity" even a property a corporate agent can have?

For each requirement, give the case or consideration behind it, and tag it as non-negotiable or as something only one camp insists on. What I most want: which pairs of these can't be jointly satisfied. If the checklist turns out to be impossible to fill, that's a result and I'd much rather see it stated plainly than smoothed over. And tell me which single requirement does the most work in knocking out the popular accounts.

Finish with a verdict on my mate's costed-apology condition — genuine requirement, a disguised restatement of another one, or a prejudice he's brought to the table?

Format, please, in this order:

- **The brief** (~100 words): what the checklist is for and what would count as clearing it
- **The requirements** (~800 words): numbered, each with its motivating case and its negotiable / non-negotiable tag
- **Prejudices, not requirements** (~200 words): the things people demand here that shouldn't make the list
- **Pairs that can't both hold** (~250 words)
- **The heavyweight** (~150 words): the one requirement that excludes the most
- **Where that leaves the costed-apology view** (~150 words)

British English throughout. And you don't need to name any real apologies, any governments or any philosophers — I'm not after citations, and I'd rather you didn't guess at them.
