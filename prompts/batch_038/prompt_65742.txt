Our reading group on translation and understanding spent last week on Hofstadter's old complaint about machine translation — the idea that these systems track surface patterns and never get at what a text is about. (The book with the little Renaissance French poem by Marot that he gets dozens of different people to render into English, one after another.) I tried to turn the complaint into an actual argument for my response paper and it blew up into something that proves way too much, and now I cant tell whether the charge is empty or whether I'm the one who broke it. Here's what I've got:

1. Call a translation process *shallow* if it produces target texts by tracking regularities in surface forms rather than by grasping what the source text is about.
2. A charge of shallowness is a real charge only if it could show up in the outputs. If some process's translations were indistinguishable from the best human translator's across every text it was ever asked to handle, then calling it shallow is just a way of saying you dislike its insides.
3. For any finite set of translation tasks you can build a system — call it the Phrasebook — that stores the ideal target text for each task and returns it on demand. It parses nothing, represents nothing, understands nothing; it is a lookup table with a very good index.
4. The Phrasebook's outputs are indistinguishable from the best human translator's across every text it is ever asked to handle.
5. So by (2), saying the Phrasebook is shallow isn't a real criticism; nothing is missing from its performance.
6. Real statistical and neural MT systems differ from the Phrasebook only in degree, not in kind. They extrapolate somewhat past their training cases, but the extrapolation is still pattern-tracking, and there's no principled boundary between "extrapolates a bit further" and "grasps the content."
7. So the emptiness carries over: the shallowness charge against actual MT systems is not a real criticism either.
8. Except the charge clearly does bite somewhere. You can have a system and a human translator produce equivalent renderings of every text in some batch, then hand both of them something new — an ambiguous pronoun, a joke that turns on knowing who the author is writing to — and they come apart. Being able to say where they'll come apart seems to be the whole content of Hofstadter's complaint.
9. So (1) through (8) can't all stand.

Which step do you drop, why did it look innocent on the way in, and what does the position that drops it have to pay? Where my sympathies sit, in case that biases me: I'd much rather keep (6) than (8), because giving up (6) means I owe an account of the exact point where pattern-tracking turns into grasping, and giving up (8) means I've conceded that the complaint tracks nothing. But I also don't want to be pushed into dropping (2), since dropping it makes the shallowness charge look unfalsifiable, which is its own problem.

Two follow-ups I'd like answered in the same pass: does your diagnosis change if the Phrasebook is indexed by sentences rather than whole texts, so that it emits novel outputs for novel combinations of sentences it already has on file? And does it change if the "batch" in (8) is the entire literary output of a language rather than a finite sample somebody happened to test?
