ok so my friend and I got into it last night, three solid hours, about inheritance tax, and I lost — but not on the part I expected to lose on.

His opening stuff was the usual: you're taxing a dead person, it's money that already got taxed once, the person who earned it gets to say where it goes. I had answers for those, or I thought I did. Then he said something I couldn't touch. He said the money is the least of what rich parents pass on. Take 100% of the estate tomorrow and his kids still land on their feet, because what actually travels is the bedtime reading, the know-who-to-call, the school, the assumption that asking for a raise is a normal thing to do. So the tax is a gesture — it lets the state look like it's doing something about equal opportunity while the thing that actually generates unequal opportunity goes completely untouched. And he wasn't being smug, he was making it as a reason the whole debate is aimed at the wrong target.

I want to run that past students before I run it past him again.

I TA a 28-person intro ethics discussion section, 75 minutes, next Thursday, and we're on distributive justice. Design me a game they play in class that walks them straight into this without me explaining anything beforehand.

Here's roughly what I'm picturing — push back wherever my design is dumb:

- each student is a family line, not an individual, and the line runs three or four generations inside the 75 minutes
- every generation does a real effort task, something where working harder genuinely yields more and I can score it in under two minutes. not trivia, nobody should be able to coast on being quick
- earnings convert to tokens, and whatever tokens you're holding when your generation dies pass to the next generation of your line
- plus a second channel: something heritable that changes your exchange rate between effort and tokens, and that channel is not taxable. it has to be possible to work exactly as hard as someone else and finish ahead purely because of what you were born holding. the cards or slips for this should read like compliments, not like advantages — if students clock it as the rigged channel in the first two minutes the whole thing is dead
- the class votes the transfer rate every generation and the vote has to be able to move. I want the currently-broke students to catch themselves softening on a high rate once their own line starts climbing.

The thing I most want them to hit, and it has to come out of the play and not out of my mouth: that even at a punishing rate the finishing order barely budges, and that the students holding the good cards at the end sincerely — not cynically — believe they outworked the room. I want the merit illusion manufactured from the inside.

No real money, but give the stakes some teeth and make them outlast the session: a visible status thing the winning line keeps for the rest of term, or whatever you think works better.

Constraints: no lecture up front, and "inheritance tax," "estate tax," and "equal opportunity" are all banned until the debrief — the rule sheet uses neutral names. One page of rules I can photocopy, written so a student can play from it without hearing a single word of explanation. Materials a normal classroom has: index cards, poker chips, dice, whiteboard. Minute-by-minute timeline, including how long the dying-and-inheriting transition eats, because thats where I assume it'll bog down.

Then the debrief: map the moments they just lived onto the concepts, including what the votes did and why; tell me straight which parts of my friend's argument the game actually earns and which ones it quietly assumes away (I suspect it assumes the non-taxable channel is a card someone deals you, when in real life it's also a thing parents choose and could be pushed to choose differently); what predictably goes wrong the first time I run it — the hoarder, the kid who's bad at the task and gets needled, the line that dies out in generation two and then has nothing to do for twenty minutes, whatever else you see coming, and what I do about each; and be honest about the biggest thing 75 minutes of poker chips cannot reproduce, the piece I'm going to have to supply just by talking.

And please don't pad it with statistics or study results. I don't need numbers and I don't want to be defending any you made up.
