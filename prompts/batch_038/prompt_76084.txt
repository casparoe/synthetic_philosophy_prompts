I am preparing the closing session of a graduate seminar on the philosophy of death, which this term I am running jointly with a colleague from the literature department, and we have assigned Tolstoy's novella The Death of Ivan Ilyich as the text against which to test the Epicurean position. The session has become contentious because one student has circulated a short paper arguing that the novella is, despite appearances, an Epicurean work: that what Ivan Ilyich dreads through his months of illness is not being dead but the process of dying and the emptiness of the life preceding it, both of which Epicurus happily concedes can be bad, and that the closing movement, in which his terror simply goes out of him when he feels pity for his son, dramatizes the claim that death itself is nothing to us. A second student objects that the reading survives only by ignoring Tolstoy's own religious frame, since Ivan's release is purchased with a premise Epicurus would reject: the discovery that his life has been wrong and that pity can be worth more to him than continued existence. A third thinks both are missing the point and that the book is really an argument for the deprivation account, its horror lying in how much Ivan stands to lose. I should like to settle, so far as it can be settled, which of these the text actually supports.

What I want is criticism, not summary. Begin by stating the Epicurean case precisely enough to be testable: the no-subject argument (harm requires someone harmed and a time at which they are harmed, and death supplies neither), the symmetry argument that posthumous nonexistence mirrors the prenatal nonexistence nobody mourns, the resulting timing problem, and the distinction Epicurus needs between being dead and dying. Then read the novella against those claims scene by scene, and be concrete: the opening chapter in the law courts, where Ivan's colleagues register his death chiefly as an occasion for promotions and transfers and one of them leaves early for a card game; the syllogism Ivan recalls from his student days in logic — Caius is a man, men are mortal, therefore Caius is mortal — which he had always granted for Caius in the abstract and could never make apply to himself; the fall while he was hanging curtains in the new apartment, and the triviality of the cause; the household's settled pretense that he is merely ill, and Gerasim, the peasant servant, who alone says plainly what is happening and who relieves him by holding his legs up; the sensation of being forced into a narrow black sack; the three days of screaming; and the final moment of pity. For each, say what work it does for or against the Epicurean claims, and in particular address whether the first chapter — narrated entirely from the standpoint of the survivors, which is the only standpoint the Epicurean says is available on death — helps the reading or quietly undermines it. Be honest about the direction of fit: Tolstoy wrote the book to make a moral and religious point, and if the Epicurean reading depends on bracketing that point, say so and say what remains. Please do not quote the novella or the philosophical texts (my students are working from several translations, and I would rather you describe scenes and reconstruct arguments in your own words), and do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; argue from the material itself. End by saying what the novella contributes to this debate that argument alone could not — my own hunch is that it concerns the difference between assenting to a proposition and being able to think with it, but I want that hunch tested rather than indulged.
