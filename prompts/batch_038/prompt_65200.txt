I am a third-year philosophy student in a metaethics seminar, and for my term paper I tried to reconstruct what I took to be the cleanest version of the Frege–Geach objection to expressivism. A classmate of mine who works on probabilistic semantics read it over and said that it does not merely refute expressivism but proves far too much, and that unless I can find the defect I will be committed to consequences about conditionals and probability talk that neither of us is willing to accept. I have gone back through it several times and still cannot locate the slip; every step looks defensible to me in isolation. Here is the argument as I gave it to him:

(1) Expressivism holds that the meaning of a moral sentence such as "Lying is wrong" is given by the non-cognitive attitude — disapproval — that asserting it expresses. (2) An attitude is expressed only by an utterance in which the speaker actually manifests it, which is to say only in assertoric use. (3) In the moral modus ponens "If lying is wrong, then getting your little brother to lie is wrong; lying is wrong; so getting your little brother to lie is wrong," the first occurrence of "lying is wrong" is unasserted, since nobody who utters the whole argument thereby disapproves of lying. (4) So by (1) and (2) that embedded occurrence either has no meaning at all or has some meaning other than the one it carries when asserted. (5) If the embedded occurrence had a different meaning from the unembedded one, "wrong" would shift sense between premises and conclusion, and the argument would commit a fallacy of four terms. (6) That argument is valid; its validity is the datum the objection starts from, and it is the premise I am least willing to surrender. (7) Therefore expressivism is false.

My classmate's move was to generalize. He said that nothing in steps (1) through (7) turns on moral subject matter or on non-cognitive attitudes in particular, but only on the claim that meaning is fixed by use rather than by truth conditions, so the same reasoning must show that any such account renders embedding unintelligible. He offered two cases he thought decisive: a subjectivist reading of probability statements, on which "There is a 30 percent chance of rain" expresses a partial credence rather than describing anything, and Adams's thesis about conditionals, on which "If it rains, the match will be cancelled" is not truth-apt at all and is accepted in virtue of a sufficiently high conditional credence. If my argument is sound, then "If there is a 30 percent chance of rain, you should bring an umbrella; there is a 30 percent chance of rain; so you should bring an umbrella" is a four-term fallacy, and likewise every ordinary modus ponens with an indicative conditional, since on Adams's view the conditional in the first premise never contributes a truth-condition-bearing content of its own. His conclusion is that my argument demolishes probabilistic semantics and the theory of conditionals along with expressivism, which is absurd, so one of my steps must be bad — but he would not say which. So: which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want the diagnosis to explain why the faulty step looked respectable when I wrote it down, not merely to flag it, and I want the competing exits priced rather than listed, since I suspect that telling me the expressivist can supply a compositional semantics for attitude contents, that she can go hybrid and let a descriptive component do the logical work, or that she can follow Adams and redefine what validity means for moral inference are three very different bills, and I have no sense of which is cheapest. Please answer as exactly five numbered items, each two or three sentences at most, with no preamble and no closing summary.
