I serve on a county bird records committee. As well as vetting sightings, we occasionally comment on proposed changes to the regional checklist, and one long-standing member has a stock formula for dismissing any proposed split: if they can interbreed, they are the same species. Last month he deployed it against a submission arguing that a Palearctic leaf-warbler complex should be divided in two. The proposers cited a narrow hybrid zone, marked and consistent differences in song, and genetic divergence implying long separation; he replied that hybrids are still produced where the two forms meet, that the split was therefore fanciful, and the submission was withdrawn without further discussion.

I want to answer properly rather than intuitively, and my suspicion is that his one-liner is doing several distinct jobs at once, only some of them defensible. Could you set out, as an explicit menu, the different precise claims it could be making? I would like you to distinguish at least the following dimensions:

- whether it is offered as a claim about what the word "species" means, or as an empirical criterion for deciding which populations to group together;
- what "can interbreed" is supposed to amount to — does interbreed in the wild, would interbreed if brought into contact, produces offspring at all, or produces fertile offspring that go on to reproduce;
- whether the intended conditional runs in one direction only or is meant as a biconditional, noting that what he actually relies on seems to be the contrapositive (if they cannot interbreed, they are different species), and that the two directions fare very differently;
- whether compatibility of this kind is even transitive, and what the ring-complex cases do to any reading that treats "can interbreed with" as picking out an equivalence class;
- whether the claim is tacitly restricted to sexually reproducing organisms, and what, if anything, it then says about the remainder of the tree of life or about fossil material.

For each reading, tell me whether it is trivially true by stipulation, substantive and defensible, or false, and say briefly why. Then tell me which reading, if any, would actually license his conclusion from the evidence described — a narrow hybrid zone, song differences, deep genetic divergence — and what such a zone does and does not show under each. Please also identify the auxiliary premises about rates of gene flow that the slogan would need before it could bear any weight in a checklist decision, and note where it quietly assumes there is a single correct answer to the question, and how the assessment changes if species are treated as individual historical entities rather than as classes.

A list I could circulate before the next meeting would be ideal. No references or bibliography, please; I will supply my own. Please write in British English.
