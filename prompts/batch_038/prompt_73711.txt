I serve on a seven-member civic commission in a mid-sized American city. Within four months we must recommend whether a public middle school keeps its current name. The namesake was a physician who founded the city's first charity hospital in the 1890s and later sat on the school board; our archival subcommittee has documented that he wrote and enforced the policy assigning Black students to a separate, badly underfunded building — and that contemporaries, including two fellow physicians and the city's Black newspaper, said so publicly and called it unjust at the time. Whenever those findings come up, the working group's chair reaches for the same sentence: "You can't judge people of the past by today's standards." He then draws two conclusions from it, sometimes in one breath: (a) the namesake did nothing morally wrong, and (b) the commission should not recommend a renaming.

I want the sentence taken apart rather than answered rhetorically. Please lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses it could be expressing. For each entry:

- state the claim with its quantifiers and indices explicit — whose standards (the prevailing views of his society, the best-justified views actually available and articulated in his milieu, the views he as an individual could reasonably have been expected to reach, our own current views), standards for what (the wrongness of acts, the culpability of agents, our entitlement to appraise at all, the permissibility of an institution acting on the appraisal now), and applied to whom and at which time;
- classify it as a platitude, substantive and defensible, false, or question-begging in this setting, with a sentence or two of argument;
- say whether it supports (a), (b), both, or neither.

Please make sure the menu covers readings that turn on the fact that "his time" was not of one mind, since that is exactly what our research found, as well as any reading on which the slogan is really about the limits of our own epistemic position rather than about his. Where a reading is defensible but still fails to reach the chair's conclusion, show the missing step.

Argue from the concepts, not the literature: no bibliography, no reading list, no attributions to named philosophers, and nothing here requires looking anything up. Technical vocabulary is welcome where it earns its place.

End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" naming which of the chair's two uses fails first — "(a)", "(b)", "both together", or "neither" if some one reading genuinely delivers both conclusions.
