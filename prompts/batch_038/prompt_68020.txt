I lead a reliability engineering team at a mid-sized software company, and for the last four months a group of six of us — mostly engineers, none with any formal training in philosophy — have been reading Machiavelli's *The Prince*. The discussion of *fortuna* and *virtù* has produced an impasse that has now survived three sessions, and I would like a diagnosis of the impasse itself rather than a resolution of the underlying question.

The two readings in our group run roughly as follows.

Reading A takes Machiavelli at his word as a theorist of agency. On this view, the claim that fortune arbitrates one half of our actions and leaves us the other half, or a little less, is a substantive division of labour: roughly half of any outcome is outside your control, and the remainder responds systematically to preparation and nerve. The comparison of fortune to a river in flood supports this — the flood cannot be prevented, but defences and barriers built during fair weather channel the water so that it does less damage when it comes. *Virtù* is therefore a competence, closer to engineering discipline than to daring: you reduce the impact of shocks you cannot foresee by investing in slack, redundancy and speed of response while conditions permit it.

Reading B says that the same passage, read carefully, claims almost the opposite. The dykes do not stop the river; they merely make the destruction somewhat less total, which is a claim about limiting downside, not about producing success. And the argument that follows — that a man succeeds while his manner of proceeding accords with the spirit of the times and is ruined when the times change, because he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to — appears to remove agency from the half that fortune supposedly left us. Julius II prospered by impetuosity because the circumstances happened to suit impetuosity; had they required caution he would have been destroyed, since he could not have changed his disposition. On this reading, *virtù* is not a skill that improves your prospects but a fixed temperament that happens, or fails, to fit an environment you cannot predict or read in advance — a bet, not a competence.

Both sides cite the same sentences. What makes me suspect something other than an ordinary interpretive disagreement is this: when we tried to pin each reading down as a claim with truth conditions, we found that both are compatible with every sentence in the chapter, and that the real divergence seems to be about variance rather than about expectation. Reading A treats *virtù* as raising the average outcome of a course of action; Reading B treats it as leaving the average roughly where it was and merely truncating the worst outcomes, while the average is set by the match between a fixed character and shifting circumstances. Those are separable claims, yet nobody in the group argues about them as such.

So: what kind of disagreement is this? A genuine exegetical dispute about what Machiavelli believed; a verbal one about the word *virtù*, which our translations render interchangeably as skill, prowess, energy and ability; two groups answering different questions — one about the text, one about whether preparation can control outcomes in high-variance environments — under a single heading; or a dispute that is ill-posed because a metaphor is being asked to settle an argument it was never meant to carry? Please ground the diagnosis in what each side actually says, and address specifically whether the "one half of our actions" remark can bear any weight at all, or is rhetorical.

Then tell me what would count as progress, given your diagnosis: a distinction we have failed to draw, a reading of the river passage that discriminates between the two claims about average versus worst-case outcomes, an internal-consistency test against the rest of the chapter, or something else. If your conclusion is that no progress should be expected, say so and explain why. Finally, I would like to know whether the two readings actually imply different advice about how to act — different recommendations about when to prepare, when to commit and when to accept ruin — or whether they collapse into the same practical guidance, in which case our argument has been idle.

Two housekeeping requests. Please write in British English. I have no philosophy background, so gloss any technical term on first use, and do not give me a bibliography, a reading list or citations to secondary scholarship — I cannot check any of them, and I would rather have your reasoning laid out in full.
