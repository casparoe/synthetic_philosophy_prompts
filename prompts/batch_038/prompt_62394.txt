I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and I am reporting a long feature on the timing of conscious experience. The centerpiece is the color phi phenomenon: the effect that Kolers and von Grünau documented in the mid-1970s after Nelson Goodman asked what a color change would do to apparent motion. Two differently colored dots are flashed in succession a few degrees apart, and at certain spacings and intervals subjects report not two flashes but a single dot traveling across the gap that changes color abruptly at the midpoint — before any light from the second dot could have reached the eye. In my reporting I keep running into two camps that have spent three decades talking past each other. The first takes Dennett's line: there is no moment at which the content of experience is fixed, so the Orwellian story (the dot genuinely looked red for the first leg, and the record is edited afterward) and the Stalinesque story (the color change was staged in advance and only the finished version is ever presented) are two ways of describing the same neurophysiology, differing only about whether a given locus counts as pre-experiential or post-experiential — and subjects could never tell the difference, which shows the question was malformed, a hangover from the Cartesian theater. The second camp is roughly the global-workspace people: they agree there is no inner audience, but they insist that conscious access has a determinate, neurally identifiable onset — a discrete ignition, a broadcast with a beginning — and therefore that whether the color change was fixed before that ignition or revised after it is an empirical question with a real answer.

What I want from you is the document those two camps should have produced and never have: the joint statement of a good-faith adversarial collaboration between a committed multiple-drafts theorist and a committed ignition theorist. Please build it in four parts. First, a numbered section of claims both can sign, including claims about the color phi data itself — what the reports are, what the stimulus timing is, what nobody thinks happens at the retina. Second, a disagreements table in which each remaining disagreement is stated twice, once in wording the multiple-drafts side would accept as a fair description of its own position and once in wording the ignition side would accept, so that I can see whether the two formulations are actually contradicting each other or merely sliding past one another. Third, the cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one side, and for each, the experiment, argument, or case both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction recorded in advance. I suspect at least four candidates, and I would like you to evaluate each rather than simply adopt my list: whether a secondary discrimination task or probe inserted in the window between the two flashes could reveal content that the subject's eventual report contradicts; whether the cortical-stimulation results associated with Libet, in which a sensation reportedly requires on the order of half a second of sustained activity yet is dated by the subject to the stimulus onset, are real evidence for retroactive revision or an artifact of what subjects are being asked to judge; whether an ignition-style neural signature can be time-locked precisely enough to arbitrate an interval of a hundred or two hundred milliseconds; and whether a verbal report should be treated as a readout of experience or as a reconstruction of it, and what observation could tell those apart. Fourth, for each camp, what it would count as evidence against its own view.

Throughout, separate the disagreements that are substantive from the ones that turn out to be about words — I specifically want to know whether the phrase the content of experience at a given time has a determinate referent at all, and if the two sides are using it differently, say so plainly and then tell me what residue is left once the merely verbal portion is stripped out. Two practical constraints. Do not give me citations, a bibliography, or suggested further reading; I will verify names, dates, and attributions myself, and a list of sources I cannot check is worse than none. And do not let either camp's favorite assumptions masquerade as shared ground: if a claim only looks neutral because it is phrased in one side's vocabulary, flag it in the table rather than filing it under agreement. My editor has allotted me a sidebar of a few hundred words, but the statement you draft should be as long as it needs to be; I would rather cut it myself than have it arrive pre-diluted.
