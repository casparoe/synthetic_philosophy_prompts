Three weeks from now I'm giving a talk at a state-and-religion colloquium in Germany and I need to know what's about to hit me in the discussion. The room is basically Staatskirchenrecht people: German public lawyers and political theorists who work on religion law as their day job, two French scholars who do laïcité, one church historian, and as far as I can tell not a single Americanist. I've been gently warned not to lean on US case law, and honestly I don't want to — those objections would be a different talk.

Here's the thesis, roughly. My claim is that the variable that actually matters in church-state arrangements isn't recognition and isn't even funding, it's whether the state acts as the registrar of religious belonging. I use a three-part test: does the state keep a record of who belongs; does it enforce the money consequences of belonging and of leaving; does it decide which groups count as religious at all. The principle underneath is that exiting a religious community shouldn't cost you any more than exiting any other voluntary association, and that a state which maintains the membership roll has turned belief into a fiscal status — which I think is a distinct wrong, not reducible to symbolic establishment or to subsidy.

On that test my ranking is deliberately annoying. Germany comes out worst: the church tax is a surcharge on income tax, affiliation sits in a federal database, employers get excerpts of it and are obliged to withhold, and leaving means making a formal declaration to a state office — after which some churches will refuse you a wedding or a funeral. England comes out comparatively benign despite having an actual established church with bishops in the upper house and the monarch as its governor, because there's no state membership roll and no revenue line. France fails a third way: no establishment, no church tax, but the state still sorts groups into religions and sectes and still pays clergy over in Alsace-Moselle. And the US lands closest to legitimate, though not for the reasons Americans usually give.

I want a prep sheet, not a defense of me. Give me the eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the argument if I fumble it, and phrased the way they'd phrase them — world-view neutrality that has to be positive rather than indifferent, subsidiarity and the churches as carriers of welfare, negative religious freedom, the reading of the Weimar settlement as a deliberate peace after a century of conflict, all that vocabulary. Split out where the French two will go at me differently from the Germans, because I expect they will. For each objection: a reply I can actually deliver in about two minutes, what I should concede out loud before I try to answer, and a note on where the reply is softest — including where my three-part test is just smuggling in a voluntarist picture of what a religious community is.

Then tell me which two of them I should pre-empt inside the talk rather than save for Q&A, and which single reply has to land for the whole ranking to survive. And give me the question I'm hoping nobody asks: I have a guess it's the one about whether the American charitable deduction plus tax exemption just relocates the entanglement into the tax code and condemns my own case, but tell me if there's something worse sitting in that room.
