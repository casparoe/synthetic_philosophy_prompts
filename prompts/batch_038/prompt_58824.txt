I am the sailing master of a wooden pilot cutter, and in three weeks I am giving the address at her decommissioning. I would like you to write it. Everything below is true of the vessel and of the occasion, and everyone in the room already knows it; none of it needs explaining to them, which is the difficulty.

The ship was launched in 1921 at a small yard on this coast, worked as a pilot cutter until 1966, and has belonged to the Harbour Trust since then as a sail-training vessel. Under the Trust's rolling repair programme, every plank, frame, fastening, deck beam, mast and spar has been replaced at least once, the engine twice. The last piece of her 1921 timber to come out was the stem, in 2011. She now contains no original material whatsoever: not wood, not iron. Her bell was recast in 1968 after the original cracked; her figurehead was carved in 1966 by a local man. What she does still have is her name, her official number — unchanged since the year she was built — her repair ledgers, and a sailing season in every year but two.

The Trust kept everything it removed. Roughly thirteen hundred numbered pieces of 1921 oak spent forty years in a barn behind the sail loft. In 2019 the regional maritime museum, forty miles inland, acquired the collection, and for six years it has been reassembling those pieces into a complete hull in a covered dry dock. About one piece in eleven had to be cut new — rot, and some that walked off in the eighties — and all the fastenings are new, because the old iron was gone. The hull sits on a steel cradle and will never float. It opens to the public next spring, captioned as Constancy, 1921. The cracked original bell is in a case at its bow. Their publicity refers to our vessel as the sailing reconstruction; my crew refer to theirs as the barn.

Both hulls have now applied for the official number. The museum says the number belongs where the ship is; the crew say it belongs where the service was. The registrar has written to me to say the striking entry cannot be made without a single vessel named against a single number, that the board is deadlocked, that under the Trust deed the recommendation of the sailing master stands, and that the entry will be read out at the ceremony. So I have to say it aloud, to both camps, on the same afternoon.

Two things about me. I signed on in 1979 at nineteen and I am retiring the same day as the ship, so this address is doing double duty and I would rather it did not. And in the winters of 1985 and 1986 I was paid to catalogue the barn, piece by piece, so I have handled the museum's hull with my own hands and I know what that oak smells like; two of the conservators sitting in the second row learned to sail on our deck. Finally, in a radio interview in 1998 I said, offhand, that the planks are just her hair and fingernails — they grow back. The museum's curator has quoted that line at me in three funding applications.

What the speech must do:

1. Six minutes, roughly a thousand words, written to be read aloud outdoors in wind. Short sentences. No headings, no lists, no stage directions beyond two or three marked pauses.
2. Do not use the phrase Ship of Theseus, and do not use any philosophical vocabulary in front of this room. Someone who knows the puzzle should be able to hear that I have thought it through and taken a position; nobody else should notice that a puzzle is present at all.
3. Address both hulls, and neither may be called a copy, a replica, a reconstruction or a fake, nor described as the real one in a way that demotes the other. Find other words. There should be a moment in which I describe what it is actually like to stand in each of them — one dry, still and complete; one moving, tarred and incomplete — and the description should be doing argumentative work rather than decorating.
4. End by making the decision: state which hull the official number belongs to and hand the entry to the harbour master. The reason must be a reason about what the number records, not about how anyone feels, and it must be one the losing camp can repeat to their own people afterwards without sounding as though they were told they are wrong about the facts. That will require the speech to show that the two camps have been answering different questions, and that only one of those questions was the registrar's.
5. Take account of the fact that she has been sold. The buyer means to keep her sailing, under a new name, in another country. So the continuity I have been trading on for forty-six years does not end at the ceremony, but it ends today as ours and as Constancy. The speech cannot lean on "she will go on sailing" without saying so.
6. Do not pretend the decision is costless. The number cannot be in two places, and people I like and respect are about to get less than they came for. Say that plainly. Do not convert it into a shrug or into a benediction over both hulls that turns out to mean nothing.
7. One genuine laugh, at my own expense, from the barn winters. Nothing at either camp's expense.
8. Deal with my 1998 line. I may sharpen it or I may take it back, but I cannot leave it in the room unaddressed, with the curator sitting twenty feet away.
9. Avoid the customary send-offs — fair winds, following seas, she sails on in our hearts. The crew would find them insulting, and so would I. Warmth must come from particularity, and where a particular person is needed, describe them (the builder's granddaughter, who is eighty-one and has her father's photograph of the launch) rather than inventing names for anyone in that audience. The ship may be named; people may not.
10. Put nothing in my mouth that I could not defend to men and women who have been aboard both hulls.

I have deliberately not told you which way I mean to decide. Decide, argue it, and make the case in my voice; I will then tell you whether I am able to say it in public.
