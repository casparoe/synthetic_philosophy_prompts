For thirty-eight years I worked in a physical chemistry laboratory — molecular spectroscopy, for the most part — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of science with more time than rigour. I have just finished the penultimate chapter of a recent trade-press book on the philosophy of physics, which is devoted to a position called structural realism. The chapter summarises the view as follows:

"Structural realism says that science cannot tell us what the world is made of, only how its parts are related; and since relations are all there is to be known, structural realists hold that the world is nothing but a web of relations, the objects at the nodes being at best convenient placeholders. The case that put the view on the map, in the hands of the philosopher John Worrall, was Fresnel's theory of light: Fresnel accounted for reflection and refraction by treating light as a transverse wave in a rigid luminiferous ether, and when Maxwell showed light to be an electromagnetic phenomenon the ether was duly discarded, yet every one of Fresnel's equations passed intact into the new theory. It is precisely because mathematical structure is what survives revolutions of this kind that structural realism is immune to the pessimistic meta-induction — the history of abandoned entities no longer counts against our best theories. Critics have objected that this leaves nothing: strip away every claim about what the entities are like and all that remains is the assertion that some objects or other stand in some relations or other such that our equations come out right, and any sufficiently large collection of objects can be made to do that; Worrall has no answer. In effect the view concedes the anti-realist's central point — that theories are instruments for organising phenomena — while reserving the word 'true' for the mathematics. The practical lesson is therefore plain: trust the equations of a well-confirmed theory, distrust its pictures of the unobservable world, and no scientific revolution will ever catch you out."

I find this persuasive, and I should like to know whether I ought to. Please work through it sentence by sentence and tell me, for each, what a philosopher of science working on this debate would accept as it stands, what is a defensible simplification, and what is simply mistaken — including whether the chapter is describing one coherent position or two that its own proponents keep apart. Then tell me whether the closing "practical lesson" follows from any part of the summary that survives correction, or whether it is doing independent work.

Two further questions, if I may. First, the chapter nowhere discusses the case I should have expected a physicist to press: Lorentz's ether theory and Einstein's special relativity are commonly said to be empirically equivalent, so that giving up the ether cost nothing. Is that claim accurate as stated, and would it help or hurt the structural realist's argument rather than merely illustrate it?

Second, I have a candidate example of my own from my working life. When dielectric response was recast from the classical picture of bound electrons oscillating on springs into a quantum-mechanical account, the mechanical picture disappeared, but the Kramers–Kronig relations and the oscillator sum rules carried over without alteration. I had assumed this was a fresh instance of Worrall's Fresnel case, and rather a good one. A colleague suggests it proves nothing, on the grounds that those relations follow from causality and linearity rather than from any particular theory of matter, so that any future theory of matter would have to reproduce them whether or not it tracked the world's structure. Is he right? And if he is, what does that imply about which retentions across theory change can serve as evidence for structural realism at all, as opposed to retentions that were never at risk of being lost?
