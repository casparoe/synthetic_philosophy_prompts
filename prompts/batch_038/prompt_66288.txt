I teach a philosophy-of-law seminar, and on the first day I post my own position statement on drug policy so the students have something concrete to shoot at. Before I put this year's version up, I want a cold-eyed audit of whether it actually coheres. Here it is, roughly as I'd write it:

(1) I'm a harm principle person: the state may coercively interfere with a competent adult's conduct only to prevent harm to other people. That the conduct is bad for the person doing it is never, by itself, a sufficient reason.

(2) So personal possession and use shouldn't be a crime for any drug, heroin and meth included. Decriminalization across the board is the default, and the burden sits on anyone who wants to recriminalize.

(3) Cannabis should go further and be legalized outright — licensed production, taxed retail, quality control. My reasons are mostly about what prohibition itself does: the black market's violence, adulterated and unpredictably dosed product, and enforcement that lands hardest on the least powerful.

(4) Methamphetamine should not be legalized on that model. Not because users wreck their own lives — per (1) I don't think that counts. But because of what happens around them: child neglect, domestic violence, houses that can't be lived in, the load on emergency services, the way production and distribution hollow out small towns. Those are harms to others, so the principle does license interference there.

(5) Addiction substantially undercuts the voluntariness of use. Somebody in the grip of severe opioid use disorder isn't choosing in the sense that grounds desert, so blaming and punishing users is wrong, and those "they chose to shoot up" arguements against funding treatment are worthless.

(6) I reject soft paternalism in every form. No nudging, no default-setting, no sin taxes, no mandatory counseling, no graphic warnings, where the justification is the agent's own good. If you can't say whose harm you're preventing, you can't do it.

(7) Portugal is what I hold up in class. Decriminalization bolted onto a health-led response: police confiscate and refer, an interdiscplinary commission hears the case, it can fine you or impose other administrative measures, and with dependent users it pushes hard toward treatment, suspending the proceedings if the person accepts it. I think that package beats both prohibition and a laissez-faire legal market.

(8) A student asked me about safe supply — pharmaceutical-grade opioids prescribed to people with severe use disorder — and I said obviously yes, that's just medicine.

(9) I also tell them that the empirical evidence on what legalization does is thin and contested, and that when the stakes are large and hard to reverse, uncertainty ought to tilt us toward the less expansionary option.

So: which of these pull against each other, and how badly? I want the tensions ranked by severity, not a flat list where everything gets flagged as equally suspicious — and I specifically don't want the pairs that only look bad on the surface treated the same as the ones that are actually load-bearing problems. For each real one, tell me which commitment has to be revised or abandoned and what the revision costs me downstream. Where a tension can be made to vanish just by re-describing the justification for one of the commitments, say so, and say whether that re-description is genuinely available to me or whether it's a cheat I'd be caught doing in front of a room full of smart undergrads. Then end with the single change that buys the most consistency for the least loss. I'm not looking for a survey of drug policy or a defense of the status quo — just the audit of this set, tracing what commits me to what.
