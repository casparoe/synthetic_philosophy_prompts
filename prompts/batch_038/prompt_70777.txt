1. "Some of the cookies are gone" — plain assertion, speaker knows exactly how many. Implicature: yes, that not all of them are. (Tagged G.)
2. "If some of the cookies are gone, we'll have to bake more tomorrow." Implicature: no — he says the not-all inference just doesn't show up in this position.
3. "You may have tea or coffee." Yes, and he counts two: that either one is permitted, and that having both isn't.
4. "Either the oven was left on or the wiring's shot" — said by someone visibly unsure which. Yes: that the speaker doesn't know which. (His note: if she knew, she'd have said which — that's the whole inference.)
5. "He's either rich or wealthy." No implicature, and he marks the utterance itself as infelictous.
6. "She's French but she's generous." Yes, though tagged differently from the rest: he says it comes from the word itself rather than the situation, and that the sentence would be true even if there were nothing surprising about the combination.
7. "If you eat your vegetables you can have ice cream." Yes: no ice cream otherwise.
8. A: "Wanna grab a movie?" B: "I've got a 6am shift." Yes: no. (Tagged P.)
9. A: "Is there any milk left?" B: "The store closed at nine." Yes: no milk. He notes there's no scale word involved anywhere, and tags it P.
10. "John stopped eating meat." No — he says that's a presupposition and goes in a seperate column entirely.
11. "Some of the students passed, in fact all of them did." No.

He also has a standing policy he repeats constantly: he won't code an inference as an implicature unless the speaker could tack on something like "and I don't mean to suggest anything beyond that" without contradicting themselves.
