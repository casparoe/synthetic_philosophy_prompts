I am a retired physical chemist — forty-one years of reaction kinetics, and in my earlier work a fair amount of statistical mechanics — and I have come to philosophy late, which is to say that I read with a pencil in hand and a standing suspicion of loose analogies. For two years I have belonged to a fortnightly discussion group of retirees in my town. At last Thursday's meeting two of its members, a retired actuary I shall call Ellen and a retired professor of economics I shall call Victor, fell into a sustained argument about the Kelly criterion and about the body of work that goes by the name ergodicity economics. I took notes as they went and reproduce the exchange as faithfully as I can. Let me be clear that I am reporting and not advocating: by the end of the evening each of them had persuaded me twice, and I have no settled view of my own to defend.

The case Ellen put on the table was this. You are offered a gamble you may play as often as you like, sequentially, wagering each time some fixed fraction of whatever wealth you then hold. A fair coin is tossed each round: on heads your wealth is multiplied by 1.6, on tails by 0.55. She asked the group to consider first the all-in version, in which you stake your entire fortune every round.

1. **Ellen.** The expected value of your wealth rises by 7.5 per cent per round — the arithmetic mean of 1.6 and 0.55 is 1.075 — so anyone who takes expected value as his criterion ought to play, and to keep playing, and to stake everything. But the geometric mean multiplier is the square root of 0.88, about 0.938, so the wealth you will actually hold shrinks by roughly 6 per cent per round. After a hundred rounds the expectation is somewhere near fourteen hundred times your stake, yet only about one history in seven finishes above its starting point, and the median history has lost more than 99 per cent of its value. A criterion that recommends this is not merely bold. It answers a question about an ensemble of lives you will never lead, when you are fated to lead exactly one. The growth rate of wealth along a single trajectory is the quantity that describes what will happen to you, and maximising it requires no assumption whatever about anybody's tastes.

2. **Victor.** It requires precisely one: that your utility of wealth is logarithmic. Maximising the expected logarithm of terminal wealth and maximising the long-run growth rate are the same exercise. The growth-rate criterion is therefore not a rival to expected utility theory but a special case of it, and a familiar one. As for the lives you will never lead, expected utility theory has never promised that its optimum would coincide with the median outcome, and a criterion is not refuted by the skewness of the distribution over which it optimises.

3. **Ellen.** Calling it a special case conceals the work being done. Expected utility is written down over lotteries resolved at a single moment and then carried across to sequences; but when payoffs compound, that extension licenses a policy — stake everything, forever — that deteriorates the longer it is followed, in the exact sense that the proportion of histories ending well tends to zero while the expectation tends to infinity. Growth-rate maximisation has a property that policy lacks: it is additive in time, so whatever maximises it across a hundred rounds maximises it across any stretch within them. That is a structural advantage, and it is available without naming a utility function.

4. **Victor.** The multiplicative structure is an assumption about the world, not a discovery about time. Suppose instead that each round pays a fixed sum: you win 600 dollars or lose 450, the amounts independent of your holdings. Your wealth then performs an additive random walk, the time average and the ensemble average of its increments coincide, and the expectation criterion behaves perfectly well. What you have shown is that concavity matters when stakes are proportional to wealth, which expected utility theory with any concave utility has maintained for seventy years. Second, under proportional betting you cannot actually go bankrupt: your wealth remains strictly positive forever. There is no ruin in your example, only a distribution with a very long right tail. Ruin is rhetoric.

5. **Ellen.** I accept the first half of that, and it does not assist you. Additive dynamics are the exception; the gambles that matter — investment, the choice of a research programme, a population in a variable environment — are multiplicative, and it is exactly there that your criterion fails. On the second half: a fortune reduced to a thousandth of itself and still falling is ruin for every purpose that ever motivated the word, and I cannot see what the distinction between that and the ledger reaching zero is worth. There is a further point. Any criterion less concave than the logarithm will have you stake more than the growth-optimal fraction. In this example the optimum is a stake of roughly 28 per cent, and once you pass about 56 per cent the typical history shrinks outright; so the whole region between the growth optimum and the all-in policy your own criterion endorses is one in which the typical fate is worse than it need be, or worse than nothing. The logarithm is not one taste among many. It is a floor.

6. **Victor.** Your floor is a first-order condition. The figure you have just quoted is what falls out of maximising the expected logarithm — it is the Kelly stake — and it is derived from a utility function, so your claim to be preference-free has been killed by your own arithmetic. Beyond that, the world you describe is one in which the agent commands no other resources: he cannot earn, borrow, or decline to play on, and he must convert his whole fortune into the gamble. Give him wages, or a finite horizon with the option to stop, or credit, and the paradox evaporates. Strip all of that away and what remains is the advice not to bet one's entire fortune on the toss of a coin — advice no expected utility theorist ever disputed. Most seriously: you are selecting your decision criterion by asking which statistic of the outcome distribution happens to be typical. That is not an argument; it is a preference dressed as a measurement.

I take it that the shape of this disagreement outgrows the arithmetic. It concerns whether a normative criterion can be read off the dynamics of a situation rather than off the preferences of an agent — a question I should once have called a matter of physics and now recognise as not one.

Would you go through the exchange move by move and tell me who is right at each step? I should like a rating for each of the six moves, from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with your reasons, and then answers to four narrower questions:

(a) Is Ellen's claim in move 5 — that any criterion less concave than the logarithm condemns the agent to a worse typical fate — in fact true for the gamble she is using? Please test it against the numbers rather than against the slogan, and say whether her figures of 28 and 56 per cent are right.

(b) Does Victor's observation in move 4, that proportional betting can never reach zero, dispose of the ruin objection, or is it a verbal victory?

(c) Is Victor right in move 6 that the growth-rate criterion is nothing but expected utility with logarithmic utility and therefore adds no normative content? Note that Ellen and Victor appear to agree on the algebra and disagree on what it shows; tell me whether there is anything substantive left over after the re-description, or whether the entire dispute is about which of two equivalent formalisms one calls fundamental.

(d) My own difficulty, and the reason I am asking rather than settling this at the next meeting: the only place I previously encountered the word *ergodic* was in statistical mechanics, where it names a hypothesis about a measure-preserving dynamical system — that a single trajectory visits regions of phase space in proportion to their measure, so that time averages equal ensemble averages for the observables one cares about. Is the use made of the term in this economic literature the same notion, a legitimate generalisation of it, or an equivocation that borrows authority from physics? I should value a candid answer even if it proves unflattering to both parties.

Finally: which of the two is more right overall, and what is the consideration that neither of them raised?
