My mother died in November, and in the months since I have returned, with something of a physicist's embarrassment, to Gödel's rotating universe, which I last studied properly as an undergraduate. I mean to read a poem at the anniversary gathering in the spring, and I have settled on a pantoum, because the form's recursion seems to me the only honest way to handle the subject: the second and fourth lines of each quatrain reappear as the first and third of the next, and the final stanza folds the opening lines back into itself, so that the poem returns to its own past precisely as a closed timelike curve does.

Please write it in British English, eight to ten quatrains, and keep the physics accurate rather than merely atmospheric. Gödel's solution is homogeneous, singularity-free, and filled with swirling dust together with a negative cosmological constant; it rotates with no privileged centre, and its light cones tip ever further over as one moves outwards, until beyond a certain critical radius a future-directed worldline curves back to the very event it began from without ever locally exceeding the speed of light. Above all, the poem must not become a consolation fantasy about visiting the dead. Gödel's own conclusion was bleaker and more interesting: a spacetime admitting such curves simply lacks any objective lapse of time, so that passage is not part of the furniture of the world — and the model purchases this at the cost of not being our universe, since it does not expand.

Let the grief enter sideways, through the machinery of the form rather than by declaration: the mourner's half-formed sense that the past is not annihilated but merely not now, and the recognition that even a completed loop would restore nothing, since the traveller arrives as an intruder in a moment that has already happened and cannot be altered. The closing stanza should complete the circuit without resolving it, and the diction should stay plain enough for a room of relatives, none of whom has met general relativity.
