My officemate and I argued until two in the morning about whether Solomonoff induction dissolves the problem of induction, and he came away convinced that it does in six lines. I want a diagnosis of the chain, not a defence of either of us.

Here is his argument, as fairly as I can render it. (1) For any two universal reference machines, the Kolmogorov complexity of a hypothesis differs by at most an additive constant, namely the length of the interpreter that translates between them; this constant depends on the machines but not on the hypothesis. (2) The prior weights a hypothesis by two to the minus its complexity, so an additive offset in the exponent is a multiplicative factor in the weight, and a factor that is common to every hypothesis cancels in the normalisation and therefore in every posterior and every prediction. So the choice of reference machine is inert, and Solomonoff induction is one uniquely defined method rather than a family indexed by a formalism. (3) The universal semimeasure dominates every enumerable semimeasure multiplicatively, which is standardly glossed as: if any computable predictor succeeds on a sequence, this method succeeds on it, and no computable method beats it by more than a constant. (4) A method that succeeds whenever success is possible is not one inductive policy among others but the correct one, since any rival is either equivalent to it or provably worse. (5) Provable success whenever success is possible is a demonstration of the reliability of induction rather than a presupposition of it, so Hume's problem is answered, not restated. (6) Hence a rational agent should adopt this prior, and once adopted, its predictions are fixed by its evidence alone, with nothing left over for simplicity intuitions, taste, or interpretive judgement to settle.

I cannot live with (5) or (6), and (4) makes me uneasy, but (1) and (3) are theorems I would rather not surrender, since without them there is no interesting framework left to argue about. When I press him on (2) he says I am confusing engineering with foundations: the prior is uncomputable, certainly, but uncomputability tells us only that we cannot implement the method, not that the method is wrong or indeterminate.

So: where does the chain break? Name the step, identify the specific confusion that makes it look unobjectionable, and say what is lost if I reject it. I would like three things priced. First, if I give up (2), does the framework collapse into an arbitrary choice of coding scheme, or is there a weaker invariance claim that survives, and if so, what exactly is invariant and what is not? Second, does (3) really license (4), or does (4) require an assumption about the world that (3) only earns conditionally, and what kind of sequences does the guarantee fail to cover? Third, is (5) circular, and if it is, at which precise point is the empirical assumption smuggled in rather than proved? Also tell me whether his response about uncomputability works, or whether the gap between the method that is provably optimal and the methods a finite agent can actually run does philosophical damage that he is entitled to dismiss.
