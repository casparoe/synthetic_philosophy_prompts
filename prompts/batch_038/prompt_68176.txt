Novelist here, and I've been stuck in the same research hole for about two years, so this is going to come out sideways — bear with me. The book is set around a nineteenth-century surveying expedition that went badly. I'll leave it unnamed on purpose, not out of secrecy but because the particulars genuinely don't matter and I don't want you chasing them. The surviving record is small and closed: a ship's log with a gap in the middle, maybe forty letters, and three memoirs written by survivors decades after the fact, each of them plainly adjusting the story to suit himself. That's everything. There will never be any more of it.

Here's what's got me. There are two flatly incompatible accounts of the last weeks of the trip. In one, the man in charge loses his nerve and orders something that gets people killed; in the other he makes a cold, correct calculation that only looks like cowardice from where we're sitting. Both accounts cite the same documents. Every scrap of evidence the first one leans on, the second one also handles — not dismisses, handles, and often handles it better, because it reads the same letter as meaning something slightly different. And when I went looking for a tiebreaker, a stray harbour record, a diary fragment, anything, everything I turned up got absorbed by both camps within a paragraph or two. I've come around to thinking no discovery could break the tie now, because whatever surfaced would be read through whichever account you already hold. But the thing that really bugs me isn't the tie. It's what happens after it. The people who study this expedition haven't ground to a halt or politely agreed to disagree — they've sorted themselves into two seperate camps, each going on producing careful, footnote-dense, utterly convincing work, and each account quietly repairs itself whenever something awkward turns up. The repairs are always individually reasonable, which is exactly why nobody counts them as a cost.

I see the same shape in my own manuscript, which is possibly why it's under my skin. There's a scene where I left a character's motive deliberately unset, and my beta readers split clean down the middle: half are certain he knew what he was doing, half are certain he didn't, and both halves point at the same sentences as proof. I've revised it four ways and every version gets read straight back into whichever camp the reader was already in, which is starting to feel less like a craft problem and more like the same trap with different furniture.

So — is there a name for this? Not the big one. I know there's a general idea floating around that evidence can fail to settle a theory, and I know there's talk of paradigms and of observation being soaked through with theory, and everyone I've described this to says "that's just the Rashomon effect," which I don't think it is, since there are no witnesses left to disagree — only a fixed pile of paper and two schemes for reading it. I want the narrower thing: one closed body of evidence, two complete readings that each account for all of it, no possible future datum that could discriminate between them, and a community that splits and then thrives on both halves. Tell me which established concepts come nearest, and for each one exactly where it stops fitting my case — I'd much rather have the seams pointed out than be handed a label and told it's covered. And if nothing actually picks it out, say so plainly and coin me one: define it, give me the conditions a situation has to meet to count as an instance, and give me one case that looks like it but isn't. I half suspect my own expedition doesn't qualify, and I'd genuinely like to find out.
