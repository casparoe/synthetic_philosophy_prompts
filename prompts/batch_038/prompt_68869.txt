I lead a triage team of four analysts inside a national competition authority. Each quarter we receive roughly forty referrals — leniency applications, whistleblower reports, findings from market studies, and, in growing numbers, alerts from automated screens that flag suspicious pricing patterns — and each referral gets about two working days of attention before it goes to a senior case manager with a recommendation either to open a full investigation or to close the file. Opening an investigation costs the firms months of scrutiny and us a great deal of goodwill, so the recommendation is not a formality. Our lawyers handle the statutory tests downstream; my team's job is the upstream and conceptual one, namely to work out whether, on the facts as we understand them, the firms in question acted together at all. I want you to write me a reusable decision procedure for exactly that question, drawn from the philosophical literature on shared intention and joint agency but usable by people who have never read any of it and will never read any of it.

The procedure should be a sequence of questions an analyst asks in order, each with a statement of what would count as a sufficient answer, each with an explicit exit where the answer is a flat no, and each marked where the question is really one of judgement or of institutional values rather than of fact and so has to be handed to a person. I have dipped into the literature myself and found Bratman on meshing sub-plans and mutual responsiveness, Searle on we-intentions, Gilbert on joint commitment, and the work of List and Pettit on group agents; my problem is that they do not agree, and they would return different verdicts on identical facts, so the procedure must say which account it is operationalising, what the others would say differently, and what we lose by that choice. Please also tell me which of the familiar distinctions have to survive the simplification and which can safely be dropped, and give me a short list of things that look like acting together but are not — common knowledge of one another's incentives, accurate mutual prediction, convergent interests, communication that never matures into a joint plan, one firm's unilateral decision that the others merely accommodate — with, for each, a discriminating probe: what evidence we would have to find for it to be genuine joint action rather than the look-alike. Mark the questions the procedure cannot settle and must escalate, which I expect will include whether a joint commitment can exist with no communication whatsoever, whether an algorithm can be a party to a shared intention at all, and whether a firm's internal decision procedures make the firm itself something with intentions of its own. I do not want a bibliography or a reading list; name a theorist only where a name would help my team ask a sharper question.

Then run the procedure, step by step, on three referrals, showing at each case which step did the decisive work: two fuel retailers in a small market town who have moved their pump prices within hours of each other for fourteen months, with no contact, no messages and no meetings that anyone has been able to find, flagged by the screen; three banks' traders who sat in the same industry chat group for months using a shared vocabulary of euphemisms and who each now insist they were merely exchanging market colour and set their own numbers; and five supermarkets subscribing to the same third-party algorithmic repricing service and contributing to a common data pool, with no evidence that any manager ever spoke to a competitor about pricing and with software documentation that describes matching rivals' prices as a feature. Say plainly which of the three the procedure will classify in a way my director will dislike — she is convinced the third is a cartel and I suspect the procedure will not let me call it one — and either defend the classification or explain why the procedure is genuinely torn. Finish by stating what the procedure cannot do: where it will systematically produce false positives, where it will let real coordination through, and why we should nonetheless run it. Please write the whole response in British English, as two or three pages of procedure plus a one-page triage card that an analyst can carry into the case meeting.
