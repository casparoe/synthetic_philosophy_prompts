I sit on the staff recognition panel at a big acute trust in the North West — I'm a matron, not a philosopher, though I've started a philosophy-of-medicine evening class this term and the two keep colliding, so bear with me. Every autumn we run an 'Above and Beyond' awards scheme and this year's shortlist is a bit of a nightmare. There's a healthcare assistant on a frailty ward who came in solid through a norovirus outbreak for something like three weeks, with no annual leave left and a six-month-old at home, and two of my colleagues want to give her the top prize because she 'went the extra mile'. There's a consultant surgeon who quietly paid out of his own pocket to send a patient abroad for a procedure we couldn't fund, which is lovely but is probably a governance problem and definately treats one patient differently from everyone else on his list. And there's a porter who gave a kidney to a complete stranger — non-directed donation, all above board, and he's back at work now.

What I actually want isn't a verdict on who wins; it's the short memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting, recommending what the panel does about the scheme and about these three nominations, with a time frame attached (ceremony's in November, nominations reopen in the spring). I'd like it to use the supererogation material properly — the idea that some acts are good but not required, the old Urmson worry that a moral vocabulary built only out of duty, prohibition and indifference has no room for saints and heroes, and the awkward questions about when an act even counts: does it have to cost the agent something, does it count if it means neglecting some other duty, does it count if the agent didn't think she was doing anything special at the time. But here's the bit I go round in circles on and I'd like the memo to actually take a view: a colleague has proposed what she calls the no-ratchet rule — that we may only honour these people if the citation avoids any suggestion that this is what everyone should aspire to, on the grounds that public praise for the extraordinary quietly drags the expected baseline upwards and turns last year's heroism into this year's minimum. You can watch it happening with the HCA already, since her ward manager has started naming her at handover as what we should all be like. Is that rule even coherent? Can you praise someone for going beyond duty without thereby making it owed, or without converting the praise into a soft requirement on everyone watching — and if you can't, does the panel have to stop giving awards altogether, or is there a form of recognition that genuinely doesn't ratchet?

So: give me a bottom line I could table on Thursday. Which of the three to honour and on roughly what wording; whether the porter's donation should trigger a change to our leave policy for altruistic donors, and whether that kind of institutional facilitation alters the moral status of donating at all (I have a hunch it does and I can't articulate why); and what next year's criteria should look like so that a reviewer can apply them without a philosophy degree. And please give the losing side its proper due — there's a real argument that a public sector employer handing out certificates to people for working unpaid through a staffing crisis is precisely the wrong message, and that the whole scheme is a way of buying goodwill with praise instead of rostering. I half believe that myself, so don't strawman it, tell me where it's right and where it overreaches.
