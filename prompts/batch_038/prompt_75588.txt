I cover welfare and work policy for a general-interest magazine, and I've got a piece due next week on the government's new push — tougher fraud checks plus expanded work requirements for people on out-of-work benefits. Before I file it I want to know what I actually think, because my notebook has turned into a list of commitments that I suspect dont all fit together. Here's the list, as honestly as I can put it:

(1) The deserving/undeserving distinction is a Victorian hangover, and any welfare system that sorts people by moral character is doing something wrong, not just something inefficient. (2) Stigma is the main mechanism by which that sorting actually happens, so the design goal should be to make claiming as un-humiliating and as little identity-forming as possible. (3) That said, I do think reciprocity is a legitimate part of the justification for the welfare state — someone getting something indefinitely while giving nothing back, when they could, is a real fairness problem and not just a talking point, and solidarity depends on most people feeling the deal is mutual. (4) So I'm fine with requiring genuine job search as a condition of an out-of-work benefit. (5) I'm not fine with workfare — making people do unpaid work placements as a condition of payment. (6) I'm also against the verification apparatus: data-matching across government departments, bank statement checks, home visits, the whole surveillance stack. Claimants should be believed by default. (7) Empirically, my understanding from the fraud-and-error estimates is that overpayments attributable to fraud are a small slice of total benefit spending, and that a good chunk of the overpayment total is official error rather than claimant wrongdoing. (8) I think sanctions as they currently operate are too harsh and should be softened. (9) And whatever else happens, nobody should be able to lose the means to eat and keep a roof over their head — there's a floor that policy can't go below.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Rank the real tensions by severity — I don't want a list where everything is equally bad, because then I can't act on it. For each one, say what has to give and what it costs to give it up. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, because I have a feeling there's one load-bearing item in there and I can't work out which one it is. A couple of specific things I'd like you to be careful about: some of my items are empirical claims I've picked up from reporting rather than principles, so if the problem is that I'm using a number to do work it can't do, say that plainly. And please separate what's actually settled among people who study this from what's genuinely contested — if, say, the gap between (4) and (5) is a live disagreement between reasonable people rather than a mistake on my part, tell me that instead of smoothing it over as though there's a consensus. Don't assume any of the nine is fixed in advance; I honestly don't know which one I'd drop.
