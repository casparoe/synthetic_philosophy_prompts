Thirty-five years as a field ecologist — mostly counting things in muddy water — and I only started reading philosophy for real about two years ago, so forgive the naieve framing. Last Thursday our local civics group had a speaker pitch something I'd never heard argued in person: a basic civics test for voters, with ballots weighted one to three by score, questions published months in advance, and the weighting applying only to ballot measures and not to candidate races. A woman two rows ahead of me killed it with "one person, one vote," the room nodded, and we moved on to the coffee. I dont think I'm on the speaker's side, but I also don't think that slogan did any work at all, and I couldn't have said why on the spot.

So: pull the slogan apart. What precise claims can "one person, one vote" be making — one about each counted ballot carrying the same arithmetic weight, one about every adult getting a ballot at all, one about your having an equal chance of swinging the outcome, one about citizens having equal influence on what government actually does once elected, one about equal standing or respect rather than about counting — and for each, is it a platitude, a substantive and arguable claim, or simply false? Then tell me which of them, if any, would actually rule out the speaker's weighted-ballot scheme rather than just sounding like it does.

Two things I dug up that you should fold in, since you may not have them to hand. First, when US courts invoked the phrase in the 1960s they were worried about legislative districts with wildly unequal populations; in Wesberry v. Sanders the Court glossed it as meaning that "the weight and worth of the citizens' votes as nearly as is practicable must be the same" — note the hedge, and note that the whole issue was apportionment between districts, not anyone's competence to vote. Second, the slogan seems to go back to the 1880s, and a country where it circulated freely kept plural voting — extra votes for university graduates and for owners of business property — for some elections until 1969. So people apparently chanted it while running a system that flatly violated the most obvious reading. What does that tell us about which reading the phrase really carries, and about whether the woman could have meant that one?

Answer as exactly five numbered items, one candidate reading per item, one or two sentences each: state the reading precisely, give a verdict (platitude / substantive / false), and say whether it would sink the weighted-ballot proposal. No introduction, no wrap-up paragraph, and don't pad the items. If you think one of the five is the reading the woman was actually invoking, mark that item — it's the one I want to press her on next time I see her.
