Reading Maimonides, Guide for the Perplexed, part 3 chapter 32 (Friedlander translation) and this argument is doing my head in. Here's the stretch I keep circling:

> On considering the Divine acts, or the processes of Nature, we get an insight into the prudence and wisdom of God as displayed in the creation of animals, with the gradual development of the movements of their limbs and the relative positions of the latter ... In a similar manner did God provide for each individual animal of the class of mammalia. When such an animal is born it is extremely tender, and cannot be fed with dry food. Therefore breasts were provided which yield milk, and the young can be fed with moist food which corresponds to the condition of the limbs of the animal, until the latter have gradually become dry and hard.
>
> Many precepts in our Law are the result of a similar course adopted by the same Supreme Being. It is, namely, impossible to go suddenly from one extreme to the other: it is therefore according to the nature of man impossible for him suddenly to discontinue everything to which he has been accustomed ... But the custom which was in those days general among all men, and the general mode of worship in which the Israelites were brought up, consisted in sacrificing animals in those temples which contained certain images, to bow down to those images ... It was in accordance with the wisdom and plan of God, as displayed in the whole Creation, that He did not command us to give up and to discontinue all these manners of service; for to obey such a commandment it would have been contrary to the nature of man, who generally cleaves to that to which he is used; it would in those days have made the same impression as a prophet would make at present if he called us to the service of God and told us in His name, that we should not pray to Him, not fast, not seek His help in time of trouble; that we should serve Him in thought, and not by any action. For this reason God allowed these kinds of service to continue; He transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings, and of things imaginary and unreal, and commanded us to serve Him in the same manner ... By this Divine plan it was effected that the traces of idolatry were blotted out, and the truly great principle of our faith, the Existence and Unity of God, was firmly established; this result was thus obtained without deterring or confusing the minds of the people by the abolition of the service to which they were accustomed and which alone was familiar to them.

The usual one-line gloss is that the sacrificial cult was a concession to the ancient mindset, and the first half of that does seem to fit. But two things in the passage fight with it and I cant tell if I'm overreading or if the gloss is just lazy.

First, the framing analogy isn't about concession at all, it's about development. A newborn mammal gets milk not because milk is a watered-down version of the real diet, but because milk is exactly right for that stage and the animal is built to grow out of it. If the cult is the milk, that pushes toward "meant to be outgrown." Yet the prophet-at-present bit pushes hard the other way: he says if a prophet showed up today and told us to serve God in thought alone, no prayer, no fasting, no asking for help in trouble, we'd bounce right off it. That's us — supposedly the grown up audience, well past the stage where the concession was needed — and he's saying we still couldn't handle disembodied worship. So is "the nature of man" in this passage a claim about human beings at all times, or a claim about that particular generation? He seems to lean on the same phrase for both, and the two readings give you completely different doctrines about whether the sacrifices are load-bearing forever or temporary scaffolding.

Second, look at the verbs. "God allowed these kinds of service to continue" and then, in the same breath, "commanded us to serve Him in the same manner." Allowing and commanding arent the same act. If it's permission, the whole cult is a second-best thing merely tolerated; if it's a command it carries positive value — but then the only value he actually identifies is negative, i.e. blotting out the traces of idolatry and stopping the people from being confused by abolition. Nothing in the rites themselves gets credited. Can a commandment be obligatory in the full sense while its entire justification is "this kept them from losing the plot"?

So what I want is the structure of the argument laid out: is the claim that the purpose of sacrifice is spent, that it's still being served, or that I've missed a third option where both are true without contradiction? And how much does the milk analogy actually constrain the answer — does it license "outgrown," or is it only doing the weaker job of showing that God's wisdom works through staged processes? Last bit I'm fuzzy on: "He transferred to His service that which had formerly served as a worship of created beings." Transferred how, exactly? What has to stay fixed in a rite for it to keep working when the object it's aimed at is swapped out, and what does that tell you about whether the form was ever the point?

Don't bother sending me to other chapters or listing commentaries, I've got the book — I just want this passage's logic untangled.
