I have been arguing about the socialist calculation debate on forums for something like fifteen years, and I have come to think that the stock formulations have stopped doing any analytical work. Mises says a rational allocation of the means of production is impossible without market prices for them; Lange says a planning board can simulate prices by tâtonnement and treat them as parameters for managers; Hayek then shifts the ground from can-it-be-computed to can-the-knowledge-be-had, and the argument dissolves into two sides talking past each other. I want to test a reformulation on you, and I want a number, not a survey.

The reformulation treats the price system as a lossy compression scheme. A fantastically high-dimensional, continuously shifting distribution of individual valuations and local scarcities gets encoded into a comparatively thin vector of money prices, and the decoder is the body of routines, habits, and heuristics that economic actors have evolved for reading that vector. On this picture the question the debate was circling is a rate–distortion question: is the thin vector adequate, or is there irrecoverable distortion? Lange's reply is then a claim that an engineered code can match the distortion of an evolved one.

Proposition P, stated so that it has a truth value: in a closed economy of roughly 100 million adults and on the order of ten million distinct produced goods and services, a central allocation system with (i) ubiquitous real-time observability of transactions, (ii) some well-specified mechanism for eliciting consumer valuations, and (iii) compute and modeling resources plausibly available in 2060 — run for at least three decades, and responsible for its own capital-goods allocation — would come within 5 percent of a competitive market economy with identical endowments and technology on a consumption-equivalent welfare measure, while sustaining a comparable rate of genuinely new product introduction.

Give me your credence in P. Then decompose it. At minimum I want separate numbers, each with one or two lines of justification, for: (a) that the residual obstacle is epistemic in Hayek's sense — tacit, local, non-articulable — rather than merely arithmetic; (b) that a designed code could in principle be at least as good as an evolved one, i.e. that the adequacy of prices is a fact about the information structure of economies rather than a historical accident; (c) that the discovery of new goods and processes has a computational analogue that does not itself require population-level selection under profit and loss; (d) that the political problem of who audits and revises the planner's objective function is decisive independently of (a) through (c); and (e) that the historical record — Soviet planning, and the fact that the largest private firms allocate internally by administration rather than by internal prices — carries real evidential weight on P rather than serving as atmospheric background.

Then show me how those terms combine. Say whether you are treating them as roughly independent and multiplicative, or as overlapping constraints on a common bottleneck, and where your final number lands. Tell me which single term P is most sensitive to, and what observation, experiment, or natural case would move you furthest. Finally: a friend of mine, a professional macroeconomist, sits at about 0.8 on P while I sit at about 0.2, and we have been unable to locate the crux. Tell me which of the five terms most likely contains our disagreement, and what you would ask each of us to establish that.

No reading list, no bibliography, and no retreating to it-depends-on-what-you-mean-by-socialism: I know the literature well enough. What I want is your arithmetic, with its weak points marked.
