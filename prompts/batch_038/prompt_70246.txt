You're a political philosopher who's spent years on self-determination and territorial rights, and I want you to do the unglamorous part before any of the theories. Context: I've been asked to write an internal memo for a democracy-monitoring outfit that has to decide whether to endorse some version of a right to secede in its public statements and its country reporting. So the bar isn't elegance, it's whether a theory can be turned into something my employers could actually say out loud about a live crisis without it collapsing on contact.

What I want first is the spec. Write out the desiderata — what any account of when a group may take a slice of an existing state's territory and set up on its own has to deliver to be adequate for that purpose. For each requirement, give me the case or the consideration that motivates it, and mark whether it's genuinely non-negotiable or whether it's something one camp insists on and dresses up as a requirement (plebiscitarians will demand one set of things, just-cause and remedial theorists another, and I suspect a lot of what gets listed as a desideratum is a partisan preference in costume).

Use these as your test cases but don't stop there: the Scottish vote Westminster agreed to in 2014, where the parent state handed over the legal power to hold it; the Catalan one in October 2017, which the Spanish constitutional court struck down and suspended before it happened, held anyway at roughly 43% turnout with police physically closing polling stations, followed by a declaration of independence that lasted about as long as it takes to say; Quebec in 1995, where the No side won by a few tenths of a point on a question whose wording everyone argued about, and then Ottawa went to the Supreme Court and afterwards legislated about what counts as a clear question and a clear majority; and a consensual dissolution where nobody much objects, like the velvet split. A good spec should tell me in advance what the theory has to say about each of those, and about the cases where they disagree with each other.

Also insist that the distinctions I care about not get collapsed: between having a right to leave and having a right to take the land with you; between external self-determination and the internal kind; between a remedy for ongoing injustice and a standing permission any sufficiently cohesive group can invoke whenever it likes; and between the claim of the seceding group and the claim of the minority inside it that doesn't want to go. Call that last one the recursion problem: if a province can peel off, why can't a city inside the new state peel off from it, and why does the peeling stop at any particular level rather than going all the way down to the individual, which is just an exit right with no territory left to exit with.

Now the part I actually care about most. Tell me whether some subset of your list is jointly unsatisfiable. My suspicion is that demanding a determinate answer to who exactly counts as the demos, demanding neutrality about contested historical claims on both sides, and demanding that a successful secession be final and stable so the thing doesn't just restart can't all be had at once — the demos question has to be settled by someone, and whoever settles it isn't neutral, and any settlement leaves a group inside the new border with a complaint that looks exactly like the original one. But I might be wrong, so argue it rather than assume it, and if the set turns out to be consistent after all, say why and show me the model. If it is impossible, that's the finding, and I want to know which requirement gets dropped and what specifically we lose by droping it.

Last thing: tell me which single requirement does the most work knocking out candidate theories, the one that if you take seriously rules out the most otherwise attractive views.

Take your time and brainstorm out loud first — a few lines of you sorting which demands are real from which are shibboleths, before you commit to the list. Then write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs that run into each other. If you feel like ending with a couple of lines on how the main families of view you know measure up, fine, but don't name-drop authors or give me references — I can't check them and I don't want a bibliography. Definately more interested in the criteria than in the scorecard.
