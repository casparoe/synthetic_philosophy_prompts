I sit on the board of a forty-unit housing co-op in Ghent, and last month's general meeting ended with two members — I'll call them Owen and Nadia — in a quarrel I've been unable to resolve, partly because I was chairing and felt obliged to stay out of it. The background: for about nine years a resident named Dario has repaired bicycles out of the storage room in our basement, at first as a favour and later for money. He is by a wide margin the best mechanic anyone around here has access to; the nearest shop is a forty-minute tram ride away and currently has a two-week queue. Last autumn he raised his price for a standard tune-up from €15 to €35, began declining the small free jobs he used to do, and started servicing bicycles belonging to people who don't live in the building. His take is now several times what most members clear in a month from their side work, and the basement room — common property, heated and lit at the co-op's expense — is effectively his private workshop. Nadia moved that the co-op cap in-building service fees at cost plus ten percent and require anyone running such a service to keep a fixed number of hours per month free for members. Owen spoke against the motion, and what followed was the most substantive argument I've heard at one of our meetings in years. Here is roughly how it went:

(1) Nadia: the arrangement is unjust, because it converts a shared room and a shared membership into a private income stream for one person, and because the resulting gap in what people have is starting to show up in how they treat one another — Dario has begun to address members as clients.
(2) Owen: every euro he has was handed to him voluntarily by people who could have taken the tram instead. If the starting position was legitimate and every step was consented to, the end state can't be illegitimate; to say otherwise is to say that the shape of holdings at the finish line counts for more than what people actually did along the way.
(3) Nadia: the starting position wasn't legitimate in the way your argument needs it to be. The room is jointly owned; the co-op guaranteed a €2,000 loan for his tools in 2019; and he had nine years of goodwill accumulated inside a closed community. He isn't a stranger who wandered in with a skill — he's someone whose position we collectively built, and the price rise only became possible once none of us had a realistic alternative to him.
(4) Owen: even granting that some of the origins are murky, that's an argument for settling an old debt, not for a standing rule that caps what he may charge in perpetuity. A rule like yours has to be enforced against fresh acts of consent every single month, against members who want to pay him €35 and are perfectly content to. A principle that requires continuously forbidding free exchanges between adults who both want them isn't a principle of justice; it's a permanent intervention dressed up as one.
(5) Nadia: we intervene continuously already. You can't play music after eleven, you can't smoke on your balcony, you can't keep a dog in a studio. Nobody here calls that tyranny. And your objection proves far too much — if no rule may restrain a voluntary transfer, the co-op can't have rules at all, in which case your point isn't an argument, it's your conclusion stated twice.
(6) Owen: those are restrictions on things that harm other people. Capping what a person earns from a skill he has harms nobody, unless you count "the community now looks less equal" as a harm, which is just another way of saying you dislike the result. And notice what happened a moment ago: when I conceded for argument's sake that the room and the loan might make the history messy, you didn't say the cap would then be justified as repayment of that debt. You still wanted it because of the gap it would close. So your objection was never about how holdings came to be. It's about the shape of the distribution — which is exactly the kind of principle you insist you're not relying on.
(7) Nadia: that's a misdescription of my concession. I allowed that the history was messy; I didn't allow that history is the only thing that could ever matter. A rule can rest on several considerations at once without collapsing into the view that nothing but the end state counts.
(8) Owen: then the mixture is available to me as well. If history plus consequences can justify your cap, consequences plus consent can justify leaving Dario alone, and neither of us has an argument left — we just have preferences.

They then talked past each other for about ten minutes. Afterwards Owen told me he thought he'd been making Nozick's Wilt Chamberlain argument, and Nadia said she knew perfectly well what that argument was and that it had never applied to a case like ours.

Could you go through the exchange and adjudicate it move by move — for each of the eight, say whether it lands, lands only in part, or misses, and why, rather than just telling me who wins at the end? I'm especially interested in (3) and (6), which struck me as the two strongest things said all evening, and in whether (6) genuinely catches Nadia in an inconsistency or merely redescribes her position uncharitably; and I'd like to know whether (7) and (8) actually cancel each other out, as Owen claimed, or whether one of them is a better reply than the other. Then tell me who is more right overall, and what, if anything, both of them left out — including whether a dispute involving a jointly owned room, a membership nobody can cheaply exit, and a service with no available substitute is even the kind of case the argument they think they were having was built for. I chaired that meeting and I don't want my own sympathies leaking into the question, so please don't read anything into the way I've described it; judge the arguments as they were actually made.
