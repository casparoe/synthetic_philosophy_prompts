I'm putting together a session on Pascal's political fragments — the cluster on justice and force — and I want to see whether they can actually do work on a real case rather than just getting quoted at people. Here's what I take those fragments to say, so we're working from the same text: justice without force is impotent and force without justice is tyrannical, and since men could not make what is just strong they have made what is strong just; laws are obeyed because they are laws and not because they are just; whatever authority law has rests on something that must not be traced back to its origin, because tracing it destroys it, which is why the origins of established orders get covered over; and there are orders of body, mind and charity separated by infinite distances, so that force can move a body but can never produce assent in a mind, and wanting to be loved where you can only be obeyed is a usurpation, a category error.

Now run the Nuremberg trial of the major war criminals, 1945–46, through that. I already have the victors justice complaint — charter drawn up by the winners after the events, tu quoque, ex post facto — so dont spend the answer relitigating it. I want the mapping, and then what breaks.

First, keep three claims in Pascal apart that people usually smear together: the descriptive claim that positive law originates in force and usurpation; the quietist conclusion that one should therefore obey the established order; and the orders claim that force cannot reach belief at all. Say which of the three each piece of the episode bears on.

Then the features I want accounted for. Jackson insisted on building the prosecution out of the Germans' own captured documents rather than out of witnesses, because he didn't think affidavits would convince anybody — is that a man trying to work in the order of minds with tools from the order of bodies, and by Pascal's lights could it have succeeded? The acquittals: three of the men in the dock walked, nobody was convicted on the conspiracy count at all, and the admiral's ten years was expressly not assessed on his submarine warfare because an American admiral had put in writing that the US had done the same thing. Pascal's cynicism should predict a tribunal with zero acquittals, so tell me what his own machinery does with a court where the strong lose three times and say so out loud. And the Soviet judge dissenting because the verdicts were too soft and because the court declined to find the Katyn massacre proved against the Germans — where does that go in the mapping?

The one I most want pressed: the superior orders defense. Pascal is about the last philosopher you'd want the prosecution to have on its side, since he tells subjects to obey the established power precisely because its justice is a fiction. So is the tribunal's refusal of that defense a straight contradiction of the quietist claim, or does his split between outward obedience and inward esteem give him a way out? Be honest about which.

Reverse direction at the end: what does the episode show about the theory — confirmation, anomaly, or a case Pascal never anticipated? He had no concept of a legal order above the strongest, no idea that a state as such could commit a crime, and died two centuries before the charter. Finally, a short coda: the aggression amendments the ICC states agreed at Kampala in 2010, jurisdiction switched on in 2018, with the proviso that the court cannot reach nationals of states that haven't joined and that members may opt out by declaration. What does Pascal's account predict about that proviso, and is the prediction a confirmation or just the theory being unfalsifiable? Say if it is.

No bibliography, no citations, and don't invent passages — where Pascal is simply silent, mark it as silence rather than making him speak.
