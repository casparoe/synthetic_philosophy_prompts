Federal courts in the United States apply two doctrines about corporations that look like they pull in opposite directions. The first, the collective knowledge doctrine, allows a court to satisfy a statutory knowledge or intent element by pooling what several different employees each knew, so that the firm is treated as knowing what no single one of its employees knew. The second, the intracorporate conspiracy doctrine, holds that a firm generally cannot conspire with its own officers and employees, because the corporation and its agents are one legal person and conspiracy requires a plurality of conspirators — subject to an exception where an agent acts for a genuinely personal stake, and to some circuits' refusal to adopt it at all. Take the pairing as the ordinary case.

What account of the corporate mind does each doctrine presuppose? Specifically: does the first require that a firm can literally hold a belief that is distributed across members and located in none of them, or can it be defended without any such metaphysics — as an evidentiary shortcut, a deterrence device, or a way of closing off the strategy of splitting a task among mutually ignorant subordinates? And does the second require that the firm is a single agent whose parts are not agents in their own right, and if so, is that compatible with counting the parts' mental states to build the firm's? Distinguish what each doctrine strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with, and say where the practice is underdetermined — in particular, whether the plurality requirement in conspiracy law tracks a claim about how many agents there are, or a claim about the distinctive danger of concerted action, and what difference that makes. Then identify at least one theory of group agency that could license both doctrines together, and describe what would have to change in one or the other if courts abandoned it, either by treating the firm as nothing over and above a set of contracting individuals or by treating it as an irreducible agent with mental states of its own.
