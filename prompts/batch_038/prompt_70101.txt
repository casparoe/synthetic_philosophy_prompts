Two requests come before our Medical Policy Committee on Thursday, and the coverage language we are supposed to apply to them turns out to be unsatisfiable. I have served on this committee for nine years; before that I spent a long stretch reading and arguing in the rationalist blogosphere, which left me permanently distrustful of criteria that sound principled but cannot be applied twice in a row. I have been asked to circulate a written opinion in advance of the meeting, and I would like help drafting it.

Our policy on auditory prostheses says the plan pays for a device that is medically necessary to restore a member to normal function, pays to replace a malfunctioning device so as to restore the member to the level of function previously achieved, and excludes anything intended to improve function beyond the normal range. In practice 'medically necessary' has become a rubber stamp, but the other two clauses are doing real work, and both fail here.

Request A. A girl of eleven, profoundly deaf in both ears from birth, was implanted at age three under our pediatric plan. Her internal electrode array has failed. The vendor confirms in writing that nothing comparable to her original device is manufactured any longer: the current internal device is materially better at speech perception in noise, supports direct wireless streaming from a phone or a classroom soundfield system, and is conditionally compatible with the 3-tesla scanners that are now standard. The literal criterion — restore her to the function she previously had — therefore cannot be met at any price, because the target no longer exists as a product. Her family adds an argument I cannot dismiss: a child implanted for the first time this year under the same plan receives that identical better device, and we call it medically necessary. The same hardware is therapy for one child and, apparently, an upgrade for another.

Request B. A man of forty-nine with adult-onset bilateral sensorineural loss. Our audiometric thresholds were written when the technology was, and are now stricter than prevailing clinical practice; he falls short of them, though he derives little benefit from hearing aids. An implant would leave him below normal hearing yet well above his present function and above anything a hearing aid can give him.

Two colleagues have already staked out the poles. One holds that the therapy/enhancement distinction is doing no work in either case and that we should simply fund what improves hearing. The other holds that approving A commits us, as precedent, to funding every future generation of device for roughly two thousand current implant users whose hardware will fail over the next decade — the per-case increment is trivial, the aggregate is not, and precedent is the only thing we actually decide when we decide a case like this.

Please draft the opinion. It should recommend a disposition for each request — approve, deny, or approve with the conditions stated explicitly enough to be operational; confront the family's consistency argument in A head-on rather than around it; propose replacement language for the clauses about restoring previous function and about the normal range, written so that a utilization reviewer could apply it next year and again in ten years as device capability keeps improving; and state the strongest case for whichever disposition you are not recommending, in a form its proponent would recognize. Assume a reader who is intelligent but has no background in bioethics, and do not pause to rehearse the general debate about therapy versus enhancement: I know it well, and it does not decide either case. I want reasoning I can defend at the table, not sources.
