For most of the twentieth century, the standard way to dispose of Maxwell's demon was to charge it for looking. A demon that sorts molecules has to detect them, and detection was said to require, at minimum, a source of illumination out of equilibrium with the gas; the entropy spent in acquiring the information was then enough to pay for the sorting. Since the early 1980s the orthodox account has moved the bill. Measurement is now held to be, in principle, free; so is the copying of a result into a memory register. The whole vindication of the second law is located in one later step: the moment when a demon whose finite memory has filled up wipes a bit clean to make room for the next observation. Landauer's principle prices that act at kT ln 2 per bit, and the price is said to cancel exactly what the demon gained on the strength of what it learned. The same accounting underwrites the standard treatment of Szilard's one-molecule engine, and the same bound has migrated into a second setting, where discussions of energy-efficient computing cite kT ln 2 as a fundamental floor on the cost of any logically irreversible operation — a floor that real electronics overshoot by many orders of magnitude.

Begin with the demon accounting and reconstruct the theory of information it presupposes. What must information be — what kind of thing, held by whom, individuated how — for erasure to be the one step that cannot be made free? Does the resolution depend on the memory's being finite, and if so, does that turn the second law into a claim about bounded agents rather than about the world? Does it depend on the register's being isolable, given that copying is free and copies multiply, so that erasing one bit in a world full of redundant traces is not obviously a well-defined act? Does it depend on the gas's having had a definite state that the measurement merely revealed?

Then assess the practice for internal consistency. It treats a bit at once as an abstract object, since irreversibility is a property of a truth table, and as a physical thing carrying a price. It declares thinking free and locates all cost in forgetting. It glosses entropy as missing information, which sounds like a feature of a description, while pricing information as a feature of a system. Which of these are genuine tensions, and which merely apparent ones?

Throughout, distinguish what the accounting strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with, and identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where rival metaphysics of information would license the very same bookkeeping, the same equations, and the same experiments with single particles in controllable double wells that are said to have measured the bound. Say which rival views the orthodox resolution excludes, and whether it needs to exclude them.

Finally, say what would have to change — in the practice, in those experiments, and in the standing of the second law — if the presupposition were dropped: if information were not a quantity owned by a register, or if whether some process counted as an erasure depended on the coarse-graining the physicist happened to adopt. And does the computing-efficiency use of the bound presuppose the same thing as the demon resolution, or something else? What view of the relation between fundamental limits and engineering makes it meaningful to invoke a bound that actual devices exceed by orders of magnitude — is that invocation doing argumentative work, or is it decoration?
