I attended a reading-group session last month on Hume's section on miracles in the *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, and the evening collapsed into a stalemate that I now think was entirely about the meaning of a single sentence. Our discussion leader, resisting the charge that Hume makes testimony to a miracle inadmissible in advance, said that Hume is only articulating ordinary epistemic hygiene — the line about the wise man, who therefore proportions his belief to the evidence — and that declining to accept a miracle report on that basis is no different from declining to act on a rumour. A week later, in an online exchange about a recovery that was documented by physicians and had no assignable natural explanation, a correspondent quoted that same sentence back at me in support of the opposite verdict: the evidence for the recovery is direct, contemporaneous, and multiply attested, whereas the evidence against it amounts only to a generalisation about how bodies usually behave, so proportioning belief to evidence obliges us to accept it. Identical words, contradictory conclusions, and nobody on either side ever said what the words assert.

What I want is a disambiguation of that maxim, not a defence of either party. Please lay out the distinct precise theses it could be expressing, and separate the axes along which they differ: whether "belief" denotes a graded confidence or an all-or-nothing acceptance, since proportioning obviously makes sense of the first and is at least puzzling for the second; whether "proportions" means matching degree to degree, or merely varying in the same direction as, or simply not exceeding, the strength of the evidence; whose evidence is in question, and at what time — the believer's total evidence, the evidence actually before them, or the evidence available in principle; whether the rule is indifferent to the content of what is believed, or whether it permits the antecedent improbability of the reported event to be counted on the evidence's side, which is what seems to do all the work in Hume's application; and whether the sentence is descriptive, a claim about how wise people in fact form beliefs, or normative, a claim about how they ought to.

For each candidate reading, say whether it is a platitude nobody would dispute, a substantive thesis that can be argued for, or straightforwardly false — and then say which reading each of my two interlocutors needed in order for their use of the maxim to go through, and whether any single reading could support both uses. I would also like the relation between this maxim and the comparison rule Hume advances a little later in the same section, roughly that testimony establishes a miracle only if the testimony's being false would be stranger than the event it reports: does that rule follow from the proportioning maxim, restate it, or smuggle in a further premise about how the two quantities are to be compared?

Two further requests. First, tell me where you are most confident and where you are constructing readings that may correspond to nothing anyone has actually held; mark the difference rather than presenting the whole menu as equally attested. Second, say what would change your mind: which consideration, if it came out the other way, would push you to a different verdict about what the maxim claims or about whether it can be turned against miracle testimony at all — and identify the step in your own analysis that you think is most likely to be wrong. No reading list or references are needed; I am after the carving, not the scholarship.
