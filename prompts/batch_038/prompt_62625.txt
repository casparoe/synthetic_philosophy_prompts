You are a formal epistemologist who works on non-omniscient epistemic logics — awareness-based semantics, impossible worlds, fragmented and resource-bounded accounts of belief — and I would like you to do something rather specific with a set of case verdicts of mine. I am drafting a chapter on the problem of logical omniscience, the one Hintikka's *Knowledge and Belief* (1962) bequeathed to the literature: on a standard possible-worlds treatment, belief is closed under logical consequence, so anyone who believes anything thereby believes every theorem of arithmetic, which is plainly false of real agents. My difficulty is that I want to select a framework on the basis of the verdicts I actually hold, rather than the other way round, and I suspect those verdicts are not jointly consistent. So please begin with a short paragraph of brainstorming: list the candidate principles that could account for any substantial portion of the data below — closure under consequence the agent has actually derived, closure under consequence the agent could derive within some resource bound, closure restricted to sentences the agent is aware of or can represent, closure within fragments of a compartmentalized belief state, a threshold-on-credence account that fails conjunctive closure by construction, a purely dispositional account — and note for each which of my verdicts it obviously buys and which it obviously costs. Only then commit to a recommendation.

Here are the verdicts. (1) A colleague who is fluent in arithmetic and accepts the axioms of Peano arithmetic has, as far as I can tell, no belief either way about whether a given hundred-digit number is prime, even though that is a consequence of what she accepts. No belief. (2) My neighbour accepts that if the sprinkler runs the lawn gets wet, and accepts that the sprinkler is running; she has never put the two together. I would nonetheless say she believes the lawn is wet — she would be unsurprised by it and would act on it. Belief, with no inference performed. (3) A first-year student who has memorised a valid proof of a theorem he does not understand, and can recite it line by line. Here derivation is present and belief absent: I do not think he believes the theorem. So (2) and (3) together are what I cannot reconcile — belief without derivation, and derivation without belief. (4) A neighbour signed a forty-page lease, believes each clause is binding, and has not noticed that two of them conflict. She believes each clause; she does not believe their conjunction; and she certainly does not believe an arbitrary contradiction, even though one follows from what she believes. (5) A historian who believes each claim in her book and also believes the book contains at least one error. Both beliefs are real, the contradiction is not believed, and she is not on that account irrational. (6) A person who believes the morning star is bright and that the evening star is not bright, and does not know they are the same object. I refuse to ascribe to her the belief that something is both bright and not bright, but I also refuse to say her beliefs are consistent, and I cannot say what the difference between those two refusals amounts to. (7) The devout scientist: in church she believes God exists, in the laboratory she believes physics is causally complete, and she would deny the conjunction. With her I want to go further than with the leaseholder — I want to say she does not believe either proposition simpliciter, only relative to a compartment. That is flatly at odds with (4) and (5), where I happily ascribed each conjunct. (8) A logician who accepts the premises of a thirty-step valid argument, is walked through it, and then says she already believed the conclusion. I think she is wrong: she came to believe it during the proof, and did not believe it before. (9) A system that stores p and p→q in a database and returns q only after running a search over its stored sentences. I am willing to say it believes q while the query is running, and unwilling to say it believed q the night before, which strikes me as an unstable position. (10) Belief seems to require conceptual capacity: someone without the concept of a prime cannot believe anything about primes, however much they could be told.

What I want back is a principle, or at most a small set of competing principles, stated precisely enough that I could write down a semantic clause for it, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. Then tell me what your principle says about cases I have not listed: a patient under general anaesthesia, whose standing beliefs I want to say are unchanged, and whose consequences I want to say are no more closed than they were while she was awake; an agent with credence 0.9 in p and 0.9 in p→q, where the threshold account predicts belief in q and my gut is genuinely uncertain; a six-year-old who believes that if she stays quiet her father will not find out and that she was not quiet; someone who accepts a theorem on testimony without having seen the proof and has no view about its corollaries; and a research group whose members separately believe p and q respectively, where I am tempted to say the group believes p∧q even though no member does. Say plainly which of my ten verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and must overturn, and if you think (2) and (3), or (4) and (7), are simply not jointly satisfiable, say so rather than building a baroque apparatus to save both. Finally, name the one further case I should add to my list that would most sharply discriminate between the leading candidate principles, and explain why it does the discriminating. Since I will be relying on this for a chapter I have to defend, please state how confident you are in the principle you settle on, and single out the single point in your answer you are least sure of — including which of my verdicts you are least comfortable overturning and what would change your mind about it.
