I'm rebuilding the last three weeks of my third-year aesthetics seminar — the stretch on the paradox of tragedy — and I've decided to hang the whole thing on *Atonement* instead of the usual film-and-tissues examples, partly because every student has read it and partly because I've become convinced the last section is doing philosophy rather than just pulling a rug out. Here's the frame I want to test. Hume opens *Of Tragedy* with the observation that it seems "an unaccountable pleasure, which the spectators of a well-written tragedy receive from sorrow, terror, anxiety, and other passions, that are in themselves disagreeable and uneasy", and that such spectators are "pleased in proportion as they are afflicted". His own solution leans hard on eloquence: the pleasure in the writing is the dominant passion, and it seizes the mind and converts — or at least so strongly tinctures — the painful one that the pain stops being painful on the whole. Call that the conversion thesis. My problem with it, and the thing I want you to actually wrestle with rather than summarise back at me, is that it seems to predict that the better written a tragedy is, the less tragic it can be: a sufficiently gorgeous prose would convert the grief away altogether and leave us admiring sentences about a death, which is surely a failure of the thing and not its perfection. Hume can't want that, since he also insists the passions be genuinely rouzed and supported. So there's a live pressure point in the account, and I think McEwan puts his foot precisely on it, because the style of *Atonement* is doing conversion work at every level and then the final section takes the consolation away while the beauty stays.

So: read the novel through that. The obvious material is the ending, where the old Briony — seventy-seven, first person, already diagnosed with vascular dementia — tells us that Robbie died of septicaemia on the Dunkirk beaches and that Cecilia drowned months later in the flooding at Balham station, and that the reunion we were given in the third section never happened; she wrote it. Note what that does to the Humean picture. For most of the book we are being given a sorrow that the prose keeps making lovely, and then we are told the loveliness was a deliberate act of fabrication by a character who is herself a novelist trying to buy forgiveness. Two things follow that I want teased apart, and this is the distinction I've been kicking around with a colleague and haven't yet got straight. First, there's the grief we feel for Robbie and Cecilia once the reveal lands — arguably the first time in the book we feel unconverted sorrow, because it arrives as information about a lie and there's no eloquence left to enjoy. Second, there's the quite different feeling we have about Briony, and by extension about McEwan, in the moment of the reveal: something like resentment at having been manipulated into feeling better, or shame at having wanted the reunion. Is that second feeling a species of the paradox of tragedy at all? It isn't pleasure converted out of pain, and it isn't pity for a character; it's an attitude towards the act of consoling us. Tell me whether it's a genuine third category the standard debate (Hume, Fontenelle's weaker-pain line, the make-believe/quasi-emotion stuff) has no slot for, or whether a good enough account of those can absorb it.

Then do the honest bit: where does this reading strain? *Atonement* is also a novel about a child's false accusation, class, wartime nursing and the ethics of storytelling, and I don't want it flattened into an illustration of an eighteenth-century essay — so tell me which passages or strands actively resist the tragic-pleasure frame, and where I'd be distorting Hume to make him fit (his case is theatre and oratory, after all, not the modern novel with its unreliable narrator and its second-order games). I'd also like a short comparison with the film's handling of the same material: the long take along the Dunkirk beach, all Ferris wheel and singing soldiers, is the conversion thesis made visible, and the reveal is delivered as a television interview, which changes who we're angry with. Does making it visual help or hurt Hume's account? Finally, say what the novel adds to the debate that an argument wouldn't — I suspect it's the fact that we can be shown our own preference for a beautiful falsehood and then made to feel bad about having had it, which is not something a premise can do.
