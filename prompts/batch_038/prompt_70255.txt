Design a thought experiment that makes the independence condition in the Condorcet jury theorem do visible work: a panel that satisfies every other condition of the theorem and still lands below a coin flip.

**Specification.** Five members of a real-world committee (a hospital board, a regulator, a lending desk — not a trolley, not an urn) vote on a binary question that has a determinate right answer in each case. Each member, taken alone, is right at least 55% of the time. The panel decides by simple majority, and the majority is right strictly less than 50% of the time. The joint distribution over the five votes must be exchangeable, so that no member is systematically the fool and the failure cannot be pinned on anyone in particular; the identity of whoever catches a bad case rotates from case to case. Pairwise dependence must be mild: no pairwise correlation between two members' votes above 0.10, so that the usual audit — comparing observed pairwise agreement rates with what independence predicts — reports a nearly independent panel. The mechanism must be non-conspiratorial: a common upstream input plus each member's private cross-check, not collusion or mass suggestion.

**Deliverables, in order.**

1. The setting and the mechanism, in a few sentences.
2. An explicit joint distribution over the five votes, with the arithmetic shown for the individual marginal, the majority's accuracy, and one pairwise correlation.
3. Which condition of the theorem fails, and why that failure is invisible to the pairwise-agreement audit.
4. An honesty check. The dramatic version of this case needs an implausibly exact mechanism, so also compute the natural variant: the upstream signal is accurate 25% of the time, and when it is inaccurate each member independently catches the problem with probability 0.4. Report that variant's individual accuracy, its majority accuracy, and its pairwise correlation, and say what the comparison exposes about the trade-off between a striking result and a believable mechanism.
5. One or two sentences on what an aggregation rule that knew the structure of your distribution would do with these very same votes, and how a defender of the jury theorem would attack your case.

The point is not to refute the theorem but to show that its independence condition is load-bearing in a way standard correlation diagnostics do not detect. So a scenario that merely asserts the votes are correlated, without producing a distribution and checking the numbers against each clause of the specification, does not count.

Maximum 700 words.
