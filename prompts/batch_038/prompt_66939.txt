Last night I argued with a friend for the better part of four hours and we got nowhere, which is a problem, because in three weeks I am presenting a design proposal at a closed seminar that she will be attending. She runs deliberative mini-publics for a living and considers one of my central claims not merely false but professionally reckless.

The setting: a closed design seminar convened by the body that funds deliberative processes in our region, on whether the regional legislature should add a chamber filled by sortition. The room will contain four or five professional mini-public organizers, all of whom recruit by stratified civic lottery with demographic quotas and treat that as non-negotiable craft practice; a survey methodologist who thinks in frames, inclusion probabilities, and nonresponse; two political theorists, one working on descriptive representation and the mirroring account of legitimacy, the other a republican concerned with domination and arbitrary power; and one sitting member of the national parliament, twenty years' service, who agreed to come and is politely but genuinely hostile. No formal modellers. Everyone there either builds these processes, studies them normatively, or has to live with them politically. I get twenty-five minutes and then an open floor.

My thesis has three linked claims. First, the distinctive value of a lot-filled chamber is neither wisdom nor representativeness but the ex ante unpredictability of its composition: nobody outside can know in advance who will be sitting there, so nobody can pre-brief, pre-position, or build standing relationships with the members. That unpredictability is the only structural anti-capture device a non-elected body can supply, and it is precisely what elected chambers staffed by career politicians have lost. Second, therefore, the practices my profession treats as best practice each erode that value — demographic quotas make composition foreseeable, standing multi-year panels make it negotiable, member lists published months ahead make it targetable, and a permanent professional secretariat that carries over from assembly to assembly makes the *real* membership continuous even when the nominal membership rotates. My design responds with a permanent open voluntary registry that any resident may join, draws by audited randomization from that registry, a single non-renewable term of eighteen months, no demographic quotas of any kind, composition published only after the draw, and service paid at the regional median wage with travel and care costs covered. Third, the mirroring account of legitimacy is the wrong account for these bodies: authority comes from equality of the chance to serve and the anonymity of the office, not from statistical resemblance to the population. I would rather carry demographic skew openly than hide it behind strata categories that somebody had to choose, since the choice of categories is itself discretionary, contestable, and therefore lobbyable.

I intend to concede up front that a volunteer registry will skew old, educated, retired, and politically over-engaged; that my design cannot deliver descriptive representation; and that my first claim is an argument from mechanism rather than from evidence, since I have nothing empirical showing that quota-based assemblies have in fact been captured.

Prepare me for the question period. I want the eight to ten objections this room will actually raise — not the objections political theorists raise in print, and not the ones a journalist would raise — ordered by how much damage each does to the proposal if I handle it badly, with a separate note on how likely each is to come at all. Phrase each one in the objector's own register: how the methodologist would actually put it, how the member of parliament would put it, how the mirroring theorist would put it. For each, give me a reply I can deliver standing up in about two minutes without slides, plus an honest note on where that reply is soft and what follow-up would expose it. Tell me what to concede immediately and what I must refuse to concede, because there are one or two points where a concession takes the whole design down with it.

Format. Begin with a markdown table, one row per objection, with exactly these columns: rank; objection, quoted as the objector would say it; raised by; likelihood (High/Medium/Low); severity (High/Medium/Low); concession, in one line; soft spot, in one line. Then, below the table, one numbered section per row containing the two-minute reply written as speech rather than as an outline, the follow-up that would expose it, and a single sentence marking the line between what I give and what I hold. End with the one question I am hoping nobody asks, why it is the most dangerous thing in the room, and a three- or four-sentence fallback answer that does not sound like surrender.

Two constraints on the content: no citations, reading lists, or references — I want reasoning, not sources. And keep the objections genuinely distinct; if two of your rows would be raised by the same person in the same breath, merge them.
