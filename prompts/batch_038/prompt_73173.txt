So I'm giving a work-in-progress talk to my own department in about three weeks and I'd rather walk in with a Q&A prep sheet than discover my weak spots live in front of everybody.

The chapter's thesis: when you're a temporary visitor somewhere with a settled everyday convention — the local tipping rate, how the queue forms at a bus stop, whether people cross against the light when the street is completely empty — you pick up a real, defeasible moral obligation to go along with it. Not because your one crossing or your one skipped tip harms anybody; the whole point is that in the particular case nobody is made worse off and you could stipulate that. The obligation is supposed to come from two places at once. First, the convention is a small standing public good that the locals keep paying into by monitoring each other and swallowing the occasional inconvenience, and a visitor who enjoys the resulting predictability while exempting himself is free-riding on it. Second, non-compliance is expressive: declining to hold yourself to the local rule is a way of declining to treat these people as ones you need to coordinate with, which reads as contempt even when it costs them nothing. Then I have a defeater clause — the obligation lapses when the convention is itself an instrument of domination or humiliation (queueing at a segregated counter, a tipping norm that persists because employers are allowed to pay a sub-minimum wage and let the tips make up the difference), and in those cases the visitor's duty is to not comply, or at least not to comply silently.

The room will be about twelve people and almost none of them work on this. Two fairly orthodox Rawlsian political philosophers. One Scanlonian contractualist who will immediately want to know what principle nobody could reasonably reject is doing the work here and whether I've smuggled in a practice-relative version of it. A couple of metaethicists, one of whom is a cheerfully self-described error theorist and treats these sessions as sport. A visiting anthropologist who did years of fieldwork in market settings and is deeply suspicious of philosophers generalising about "what people around here do." And a law professor who writes about how customary norms get recognised in international law, who will be measuring my account against how custom actually hardens into something binding. Nobody in the room is a convention specialist, so Lewis-flavoured coordination talk is not shared background and I don't want to burn the question period explaining it.

What I want is the prep sheet, not a defence of my view and not a rewrite of the talk. Roughly eight objections I'll actually get from this room — not the ones a game theorist or a Kant specialist would raise — ordered by how much damage each would do, and each phrased the way that specific person would phrase it, so I can hear it coming. For each one: what I should concede in the first sentence, the two-minute reply, and an honest note about where that reply goes soft or quietly changes the subject. Mark separately the objections that only look devastating because they've misread me, and tell me the single sentence I could plant in the talk that kills each of those before it gets raised. And then the one question im quietly hoping nobody asks — I have a suspicion what it is, but I want your guess and your reason for it, especially if you think it's a different one than mine.

No reading list, no bibliography, no "you should engage with so-and-so's recent paper." I know the literature. I need to know where I lose the room.
