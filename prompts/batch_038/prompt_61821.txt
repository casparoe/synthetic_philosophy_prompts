Second-year philosophy of physics here, working on the hole argument for my quals paper. I built a version of the argument that I think is airtight; my adviser read it, said it was obviously broken, and then refused to say anything more than that. So I'm outsourcing. Please answer in American English.

Setup: a model of GR is a triple (M, g, T) — manifold, metric, matter fields — and the field equations are generally covariant.

(1) A theory is deterministic iff any two of its models that agree on a Cauchy surface (and agree on the laws) agree everywhere else too.
(2) General covariance: if (M, g, T) solves the equations, so does (M, d\*g, d\*T) for any diffeomorphism d of M onto itself, where d\* is the drag-along.
(3) Take a bounded open region H of M that's free of matter, and a d that's the identity on H's complement but moves things around inside H.
(4) So the two models agree on everything outside H — the entire matter distribution, and all of any Cauchy surface you care to pick that lies to the past of H.
(5) And inside H there's some point p at which g and d\*g genuinely differ — not merely in coordinates, but in the value assigned, and hence in the curvature p has.
(6) Two models that differ in what metric p has are representing different physical possibilities. If they weren't, then "p has such-and-such curvature" would have no truth value, and I don't see how the theory could then explain how we ever locate an event — the whole job of M is to supply the "here" in "the collision happened here."
(7) Hence the data before H don't fix what happens inside H, and GR is indeterministic. Einstein saw exactly this and gave up general covariance for a couple of years.
(8) Nothing in (1)–(7) used anything special about gravity. Substitute "gauge transformation" for "diffeomorphism" and you get the same result for electromagnetism written in terms of the potential; substitute a Leibniz shift and you get it for Newtonian mechanics on absolute space.
(9) And Kretschmann's point cuts the way people usually take it to cut: since any theory whatsoever can be recast in generally covariant form, (2) is available to everybody, so by (8) and (9) either nothing is deterministic or "deterministic" was never a substantive constraint on a theory.
(10) So: either determinism is a myth, or GR isn't generally covariant, or determinism isn't what (1) says it is.

Two standard escapes have been suggested to me and both leave me stuck. One says deny (6): diffeomorphic models are Leibniz-equivalent, its the same world described twice, and the hole argument is really an argument against manifold substantivalism rather than against general covariance. The other says the whole thing turns on confusing active with passive diffeomorphisms, so that (2) only ever generates coordinate redescriptions, which can't be real alternatives. The first sounds like surrendering any identity for the points of M short of what the fields do, and then I can't say what the left side of (M, g, T) is contributing. The second makes general covariance notational, which is Kretschmann's complaint anyway, and then I can't see what's supposed to distinguish GR from a theory written covariantly over a fixed background. Also, "determinism holds up to diffeomorphism" reads to me like a restatement of the problem, not a solution: what's determined is an equivalence class, and which member of it obtains at p is still wide open.

So — which step actually breaks, and why did it look so innocent when I wrote it down? Then price the exits: rejecting (5), rejecting (6), rejecting (1), and just biting the bullet on (7). What does each cost, and does the cost show up as lost ontology, lost explanation, or lost predictive content? Finally, and this is the part my adviser is most smug about, tell me whether (8) and (9) really do run in parallel across GR, electromagnetism, and the Newtonian case. If the argument dies at different steps in different theories, say which step dies where and what structural difference does the killing.
