Here was the question on the exam:

> Sartre holds that human reality is radically free and that bad faith is a standing possibility of consciousness. Explain (a) what he means by radical freedom and how anguish is supposed to reveal it, (b) what bad faith is and how the example of the café waiter illustrates it, and (c) whether it is possible to be in bad faith without being aware that one is. Then say whether you think the account is coherent, and why.

And here is the student's answer as submitted:

> Sartre's starting point is that consciousness is not a thing. A stone simply is what it is; it has its properties and it coincides with them exactly. Consciousness, which he calls the for-itself, is never identical with itself in that way, because to be aware of anything it has to stand at a distance from it, including from itself. That distance is what he means by nothingness, and it is the source of radical freedom. There is no inner nature, no set of motives, and no causal chain that settles what I will do next, because a motive only counts as a motive relative to a project I have already taken up. Freedom is also not the power to get what you want — Sartre is explicit that the prisoner is free even though he cannot walk out — it is the power to determine the meaning of the situation you find yourself in.
>
> This is why anguish and fear come apart. Fear is directed at something in the world that might harm me. Anguish is directed at my own possibilities. At the edge of a cliff I am afraid of slipping, but I am in anguish because nothing in me or in the world guarantees that I will not throw myself over, and because the resolution I made a moment ago to keep back does not compel the act that comes next. Freedom discloses itself as anguish rather than as a feeling of power.
>
> Bad faith is the attempt to flee that anguish by lying to oneself. It differs from ordinary lying in that with ordinary lying I conceal the truth from someone else, whereas in bad faith the one who conceals and the one it is concealed from are the same consciousness. Sartre's way through the obvious difficulty — that I would have to know the truth in order to hide it from myself — is to say that consciousness is not a simple unity. There are two levels: the level that carries on the project of fleeing, and the level that knows what is being fled from. This is also what separates him from Freud. Sartre rejects the Freudian unconscious because it chops the mind into separate agencies that cannot intelligibly interact, but he keeps the same basic structure; the difference is that for him the split falls inside a single consciousness rather than between two psychic systems. Because of that split we can be in bad faith without realizing it: the awareness is present, but at the other level, so it never becomes something I can simply own up to.
>
> The waiter is the standard illustration. He moves a shade too quickly and a shade too precisely, he leans in a bit too eagerly, his solicitude is overdone. He is, in fact, not a waiter at all — he is a free consciousness performing the role of waiter, and his bad faith consists in letting the performance conceal that from him. He treats being-a-waiter as though it were a property he possessed in the way the inkstand on the table possesses its properties, when it is really a part he has picked up and could set down. More generally, bad faith is a confusion between facticity and transcendence: either I treat my past, my body, and my social roles as things that fix what I am, or I deny them entirely and pretend to be nothing but possibility. The waiter is guilty of the first.
>
> I think the account is coherent, but only because of the two-level move. Without it, Sartre's own objection to Freud — that whoever hides the truth must already know it — would apply to his own description of bad faith just as much as to the censor.
