I write a long-running column on the philosophy of science and its institutions, and I'm currently drafting a piece about how much public decision-making runs on statutory standards that are vague by design — "substantial," "significant," "material," "reasonable" — and what, if anything, is actually lost when those standards get handed to an algorithm. For a sidebar I want one worked case that lets a general reader watch two rival theories of vagueness pull apart, rather than reading my summary of the debate. Please build the case to the following specification.

The two theories, as I understand them. Supervaluationism: a borderline claim is neither true nor false, because the language admits a range of equally acceptable ways of making it precise, and truth is truth under all of them. Epistemicism: every such claim is true or false, there is a sharp boundary, and our inability to say where it falls is ignorance rather than absence.

What I need is a scenario in which a public body applies one such standard to a small, finite, strictly ordered set of real cases, and in which the two theories agree on everything the body does and on everything it may say out loud — and disagree on exactly one further sentence, the one a member insists goes into the written minutes.

Specifications:

1. Setting and series. A contemporary public body of your choosing — a benefits appeal panel, a heritage listing committee, a hospital ethics board, a disaster-relief assessor — applies a vague statutory predicate to six or seven cases arranged along a single continuum. The first case must be clearly inside the standard and the last clearly outside it, the ordering must be common ground to everyone in the room, and no case may be a duplicate of another.
2. Agreement where it counts. Both theories must license the same decision on every case and the same set of sentences as assertible by the panel. A reader told only what the panel decided and what its members said aloud should be unable to tell which theory they held.
3. The single point of divergence. Exactly one sentence — the minute sentence — must come out differently: the epistemicist must be committed to calling it true, and the supervaluationist must be unable to call it true. It has to be something an ordinary committee member would say in ordinary language, and it may not contain any technical vocabulary ("precisification," "supertrue," "cutoff," "vague," "sorites").
4. A trap I want you to check rather than repeat. A colleague suggested building the case on a sentence of the form "there is a point in this series at which the classification flips." My suspicion is that both theories call that one true, for different reasons — the supervaluationist because every acceptable way of making the standard precise has such a point, even though which point it is moves between precisifications, the epistemicist because there is exactly one. If I'm right, the sentence is useless to me and the case needs a different hinge. If I'm wrong, say so and show me why.
5. Devices I'm ruling out. No heaps, no bald men, no piles of sand, no colour patches — I want something a reader could meet in a news story. No omniscient being, oracle, perfect AI, or time traveller, and no stipulation anywhere in the story about where the boundary lies or whether there is one. No context shift: the wording of the standard, the panel's expectations, and the evidence available must be fixed from the first case to the last; if the divergence only appears because someone changes the subject mid-review, the case is dead to me. No fuzzy logic, no degrees of truth, no contextualism, no relativism, no pluralism about truth — those are rival positions, and importing one is how this kind of exercise gets fudged.
6. Length. The scenario itself in one paragraph of no more than 180 words. Everything else comes after it.

What the case is for, since that fixes what counts as cheating: it has to show a reader that this is a substantive dispute with a locatable hinge, not a notational variant. A case that stipulates its verdicts, or that gets the divergence by quietly giving one theory extra machinery, fails.

Deliverable, in this order:

a. The scenario paragraph.
b. A numbered list of every sentence the case turns on — the panel's findings, what members say aloud, and the minute sentence — with the minute sentence flagged.
c. The supervaluationist pass: describe what the admissible ways of making this standard precise actually look like for this series, then give the status of each listed sentence and state what the panel may assert.
d. The epistemicist pass: the same, including how the theory explains the panel's inability to locate the boundary without surrendering the claim that there is one.
e. The check against specifications 2 and 3, sentence by sentence, showing where the two passes coincide and where they separate.
f. The diagnosis: which single feature of the case is doing the work, and then two removal tests — delete the minute sentence, and one further removal of your own choosing — with a statement of what happens to the divergence under each.
g. The counter-attack: how a supervaluationist who concedes every word of (c) would still resist the conclusion that the two theories genuinely disagree here, and whether that resistance amounts to re-reading the minute sentence, a claim about the word "true," or something else.
h. Your verdict.

On (h) I want a position, not a survey: tell me whether the disagreement the case exposes is a genuine one about the world or a merely verbal one that dissolves on a better reading of the minute sentence, tell me which of your two removal tests actually collapses the case and which leaves it standing, and give me your reasons. If you think one of my specifications cannot be met alongside the others, say which and why at the top, and don't quietly drop it. Where a step depends on a contested reading of either theory, flag it in a clause and say what would change under the rival reading.

No citations, no bibliography, no reading list — I can't chase them and neither can my readers. If a move depends on a particular philosopher's version of one of the two theories, describe the version in a clause instead of name-checking it.

Close with a JSON object summarizing the case, using exactly these fields: "setting" (string: the body and the standard it applies); "series" (array of strings, one short description per case, in order); "sentences" (array of objects, each with "id" (string), "text" (string), "role" (one of "panel finding", "spoken", "minute"), "epistemicist_status" (string), "supervaluationist_status" (string), "epistemicist_assertible" (boolean), "supervaluationist_assertible" (boolean)); "divergence_sentence_id" (string); "trap_sentence_verdict" (string: your finding on specification 4); "feature_doing_the_work" (string); "removal_tests" (array of two objects, each with "removal" (string), "divergence_survives" (boolean), "why" (string)); "supervaluationist_counter" (string); "verdict" (one of "genuine", "merely_verbal", "genuine_but_practically_inert"); "all_specs_met" (boolean); "spec_abandoned" (string or null); "contested_readings" (array of strings).
