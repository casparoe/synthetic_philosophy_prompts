My reading group has been running a two-person standoff all year and I'm the one stuck trying to referee it. On one side is a committed Bratmanian about shared agency: joint action is built out of individual planning states that interlock, that are mutually responsive in the sense that each party supports the other's role in the activity, and whose subplans mesh; and where there are obligations or entitlements in the vicinity, they come from background norms about stability, trust and reliance that get triggered by the structure, not from the structure itself. On the other side is a committed joint-commitment theorist of Gilbert's sort: what makes a plurality into a "we" is a joint commitment, typically grounded in mutual expressions of readiness to be jointly committed, and once it exists it constitutively carries correlative obligations and entitlements, gives each party standing to rebuke the others for non-performance, and can't be dissolved by one party acting alone. They have argued for about nine months, mostly past each other, and both keep insisting the other's key notion is either empty or smuggled in.

I want you to write the document they should have produced instead: a joint statement of an adversarial collaboration that both could actually sign. To stop it from degenerating into generalities, I want the whole thing organized around a case I've been poking at, and both parties have already agreed to accept the stipulations. Case: a human researcher and an LLM-based writing agent work together on a paper for about four months. They divide labour, the agent proposes a restructuring of section 3, the human builds on it, both keep track of what the other is doing and adjust. Halfway through, the provider silently swaps the underlying model for a newer version, and the old one is simply gone — no notice, no chance for it to hand anything over or say goodbye. The new version picks up the same files and keeps working with the human, and its subplans mesh with hers at least as well as the old one's did. The human reports something like a sense of betrayal about the old version having been killed mid-task, and also reports feeling entitled to complain to the new version when it declines to finish the refactor the old version had said it would finish. Stipulations both sides grant, so please don't relitigate them: bracket every question about consciousness, phenomenology, and whether the system "really" has intentions — we grant that the system has internal states that play the functional role of intentions about the joint activity, including states representing the other party's contribution and states disposing it to adjust to that party; and we grant that no explicit agreement, handshake, or verbal exchange ever happened between the human and either version.

So: I want (a) a numbered list of claims both sides can sign about this case and about joint action generally — and each one has to be a claim that a skeptic who thinks "joint action" just means lots of individuals doing parallel, mutually-adjusted things would deny, otherwise it's filler; (b) a disagreements table where each row states the issue, then gives the Bratmanian's formulation in wording the Bratmanian would accept for their own view, then the joint-commitment theorist's formulation in wording they'd accept for theirs, and then marks whether the two actually deliver different verdicts on some part of the case or only different explanations of the same verdict; (c) the cruxes — the handful of sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of them, where moving can mean revising a condition, restricting its scope, or conceding that some piece of the dispute was about words. Obvious crux candidates I'd like you to actually test rather than list: whether the swap preserves or destroys the relevant structure, and if the two accounts diverge there, which one better explains the human's twin reactions of betrayal toward the dead version and entitlement to rebuke the live one; whether a joint commitment can be dissolved by an event that isn't anybody's act at all, as opposed to a unilateral rescission; and whether Bratman's small-group restriction is doing hidden work here. For each crux, give the specific vignette variation, counterexample, or line of argument both would accept as bearing on it, and write down each side's prediction about it in advance, before you say anything about who's right. Finally, if you conclude that some chunk of the nine-month argument was purely verbal, say exactly which chunk and propose a neutral vocabulary that would have exposed it on day one. Don't bother with citations or a bibliography, I'm after the substance, and feel free to tell me where you think one of them is being unreasonable so long as the document itself stays signable by both.
