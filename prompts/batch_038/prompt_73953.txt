I am facilitating a three-evening deliberative workshop in a regional town that is about to double in size, and rather than opening with an argument I want to hand participants a dialogue set in an invented town that has the same structure of problems but none of the local names, so that nobody in the room feels they have been cast as a character. Please write that dialogue, keeping the following situation fixed rather than substituting a different one. Call the settlement a semi-arid town founded eleven years ago, now around 11,000 residents, with a rail terminus and a processing plant under construction that will bring another 4,000 within three years. Water is nominally allocated by permit, but the allocation that actually holds is informal: norms about how much a household draws, at what hour, and what it plants. Those norms were stable at nine hundred residents and are visibly failing at eleven thousand. A substantial share of the founding population arrived because they were leaving a community with an extremely dense normative order and public accountability for small deviances, so the question of whether to build enforcement capacity is not, in this town, a neutral technical question.

The dialogue is between the two authors of the competing reports the town assembly commissioned, and it takes place in the hour before the assembly votes. Margit Sørensen's report recommends a written community code covering the water-relevant behaviours; a public compliance standing for each household that is visible to neighbours and that affects the size and priority of its next-season allocation; a graduated schedule of sanctions administered by a standing office rather than by neighbours; and a norm-education programme in the schools. Her core reasoning is that informal enforcement breaks down at scale because the cost of sanctioning a neighbour falls on the sanctioner while the benefit is diffuse, and that the newcomers have no shared history with the founders, so norms cannot be transmitted by osmosis; she also insists that informality is not the neutral baseline it looks like, since it delegates enforcement to whoever holds social capital, which here has meant the same dozen families, and that a written code with appeal rights is the more egalitarian option. Elias Nkemdirim's report recommends roughly twelve bright-line prohibitions that are cheap to verify and hard to dispute; everything else referred to a rotating panel with wide discretion and a published duty to give reasons; a sunset on every rule, so that no provision survives more than four years unless it is re-adopted on evidence that it changed behaviour; and a deliberate refusal to create a permanent compliance office. His core reasoning is that the town's problems are non-stationary, so a code written now encodes this year's guesses with next decade's force; that a public compliance standing does not merely record behaviour but changes what people are willing to attempt; and that once an enforcement office exists, the code drifts toward what the office finds convenient to detect rather than what matters. Both reports should read as the work of competent people, and neither should be the one a sensible observer would obviously reject.

The dialogue should run to roughly 1,300–1,600 words and meet these constraints:

- They must respond to each other's actual moves rather than alternating set pieces, and each must land at least one point that visibly costs the other something. Each should also modify one specific subordinate claim under pressure — not their headline recommendation — and a reader should be able to say which claim moved and why.
- Please drive the exchange through a diagnostic disagreement, not only a normative one: whether what has collapsed is the strength of the norms or the shared belief that others are following them. If people still think the drawing rules are right but have stopped believing anyone else complies, then publicising compliance might do work with no sanctioning apparatus at all; if the newcomers simply no longer regard the rules as legitimate, no ledger will fix anything. Let each author argue for a different diagnosis and let each try to say what observation would settle it.
- The dialogue must reach, without being announced as a slogan, the problem that looseness is not the absence of a norm regime but a different and more demanding one — sunsets and duties to publish reasons are themselves rules needing tight enforcement, and panel discretion earns trust only where the panel is itself densely watched. Margit should be pressed just as hard on the mirror image: the credibility of her compliance standing depends on precisely the informal trust she says has decayed.
- Neither character may appeal to "studies show" or to the cross-cultural literature on tight and loose societies; I do not want the text turning into a literature summary for an audience that has not read it. If either invokes evidence, they must state the mechanism and say what would have to be true for it to operate here.
- No moderator, no epiphany, no synthesis in the last three lines. It ends with the vote still ahead of them and the substantive disagreement intact.

After the dialogue, add a short note of your own, around 200 words, addressed to me rather than to the participants. Say which of the two reports you think is better supported given the town exactly as I have described it, and why. Then tell me how confident you actually are in the empirical premises you have put in the characters' mouths — in particular the claim about sanctioning costs being concentrated while benefits are diffuse, the claim that a visible compliance standing changes what people will attempt, and the claim about rules drifting toward what is easy to detect — marking each as something you are confident of, something you are unsure of, or something you have constructed for the scene and would not want me to repeat as fact in a public workshop. Finally, single out the one point in the exchange where you are least sure you have got the argument right. Several of our participants are unusually sharp and I would rather know where the text is load-bearing on a guess.
