You are a philosopher with specialist training in ancient Greek ethics and political philosophy who has also spent a decade advising member-owned financial cooperatives. Please treat what follows as institutional work rather than an essay question, and think carefully before you draft.

The situation. I sit on the volunteer board of a community credit union — about eleven thousand member-owners in a city whose last large employer left in the 1990s. A quarter of our members have no banking relationship other than ours, and a great many of them currently borrow from storefront payday lenders at triple-digit rates. On Thursday we vote on a proposed small-dollar product: loans of $300 to $2,000, six- to twelve-month terms, advertised at a flat 18% APR, underwritten by payroll deduction where possible. Three amendments are on the table.

(a) Zero-percent version. Charge no interest at all; absorb the cost by trimming the yield we earn elsewhere, principally on the auto-loan portfolio; and restrict eligibility to what one director calls productive purposes — tools, a repaired car, licensing fees, a tenancy deposit — refusing purely consumption borrowing.

(b) Rebate version. Keep the 18% APR, but return at year-end, as a patronage dividend to borrowers who repaid as scheduled, whatever margin the product earned above its cost of funds.

(c) No amendment. Sell the product as designed and decline to frame the decision in moral vocabulary at all.

The argument. My friend teaches classics, and his position is that any attempt to settle this with Aristotle is confused. Aristotle's objection to lending at interest, on his reading, rests on the claim that money is by nature barren — a piece of pre-modern metallism we now know to be false — and worse, it rests on treating money's function as fixed by nature when Aristotle himself says that money exists not by nature but by convention and has no value except within the community that agrees to use it. If money is a convention, then a cooperative of eleven thousand people may simply re-set the convention by vote, and the usury question dissolves into a question about our bylaws.

My reply, which I failed to land at the time: the force of the objection was never that metal is sterile. It is that household management has an internal limit — the goods that make a life possible, beyond which more adds nothing, which is why Aristotle can tell the story of the king whose wish turned everything he touched to gold and who then had nothing to eat — whereas money-making has no limit at all, so the art of getting wealth can be flipped from a means into an endless end. On that reading money's function is fixed not by its metal but by the exchange it mediates and by the need it stands in for when unlike producers, a builder and a shoemaker, trade goods that cannot genuinely be measured against one another. Interest matters because it turns the measure into the thing measured. I also want to press his own conventionalism: if we really can re-set the convention, then the question is not whether we may charge interest but what terms keep money tied to need — which is live for us, not dissolved.

What I need from you. First, about 400 words of working analysis, before any drafting: identify the two or three premises on which my friend's dissolution actually depends, say plainly where he is right and where he overstates the case, and set out which amendment the Aristotelian material genuinely favors, which it only appears to favor, and which it says nothing about whatsoever. Do not predetermine the outcome — if the conventionalist objection defeats the whole project, say so and recommend (c).

Then, roughly 900 to 1,100 words: the written opinion I will circulate to the board on Wednesday. Seven volunteer directors, none of whom has read Aristotle, two of whom are retired bankers and will smell an appeal to authority immediately. Open with an explicit recommendation — one of the three options, with conditions if you think conditions are required, and the date by which staff would need notice to implement it — then give the reasons, then state the strongest case against that recommendation in its best form and answer it rather than wave it away. Please work hardest on two questions I have not been able to settle. Whether a year-end rebate alters the structure of the exchange or merely relabels an interest payment after the fact. And whether the productive-purposes restriction in (a) is faithful to Aristotle's distinction between acquisition aimed at the goods of a household and acquisition aimed at more wealth, or whether it quietly imports a modern investment/consumption distinction he does not have while driving our poorest members back to the payday lenders — which looks worse even on his own terms, since it hands them to the most extreme form of the practice he objects to.

Constraints. No bibliography, no citations, no Bekker or page numbers unless you are certain of them, and no invented quotations; paraphrase Aristotle in plain prose. Close with a paragraph of about a hundred words addressed to members rather than directors, explaining the decision with no technical vocabulary at all. And do not pad the memo with a summary of natural versus unnatural acquisition for its own sake — every philosophical claim in it should be doing work on the vote.
