For the past three years I have sat in the same graduate reading group on the structure of justification, and its membership has been unusually stable: one committed foundationalist, one coherentist, one infinitist, and me, nominally unaffiliated but functionally the referee. What puzzles me is how little our meetings resemble an argument that could be won. All three accept that the trilemma sets the menu — the chain of reasons either terminates in a belief that is justified without inferential support, closes on itself, or goes on forever — and all three insist their horn is the least costly. Each has a polished reply to the standing objection: the foundationalist to the dialectical complaint that a belief cannot function as a reason unless the subject can say what makes it one; the coherentist to the isolation objection and to the charge that circularity, however wide, cannot manufacture support; the infinitist to the charge that nobody could ever complete an infinite chain of reasoning, which they read as a confusion between what a subject must produce and what must exist. Since infinitism came back as a live option in the late 1990s, the debate has had three fully articulated positions, and in all that time I have not seen anyone move an inch, including myself.

My working hypothesis — which I want you to test rather than adopt — is that this is not one disagreement conducted badly but three different questions sharing a heading, and that the giveaway is the phrase "S has a reason for believing p." The foundationalist seems to need reasons that are had: occurrent, psychologically available, deployable by this subject now. The infinitist seems to need only reasons that are available in principle: that there always exists some further true proposition that would support the target, whether or not any finite mind could ever reach it. The coherentist, meanwhile, appears not to be talking about a relation between a subject and a proposition at all, but about a structural property of a body of content. If that is right, each party can accept everything the others say about their own object of study and still think the others have missed the point, which is exactly the pattern I see at our table. But I may be wrong that the residue is merely verbal: there is also a real dispute about what justification is for, whether its job is to be truth-conducive or to render the believer blameless, and a further dispute, which I keep circling back to, prompted by the formal work suggesting that an infinite chain of probabilistic support can converge so that the target's probability is perfectly determinate even though no link enjoys non-inferential warrant. If such chains can be modeled, infinitism stops being a thesis about a subject's capacities and becomes a thesis about what a space of propositions supports — which might relocate it from the verbal column to the formal one, or might simply reveal that it was always answering a question the other two were not asking.

So: give me the diagnosis, not a verdict. Is the impasse over the regress problem a substantive disagreement about justification, a partly verbal one turning on "reason," three projects answering three questions under one name, a hidden empirical question about what basing actually is that belongs to the psychology of reasoning rather than to epistemology, or a correctly posed question that is simply very hard? Argue for your answer by reference to what each side actually says and what each is committed to, and say explicitly where my hypothesis above gets it right and where it overreaches. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction that dissolves the appearance of conflict, a theorem, a result about how people actually form and revise beliefs, a decision to stop using one word for several things — or, if you think no progress should be expected, make that case and explain why an argument that has run this long without conversion is not thereby an argument that has gone wrong. I am not looking for a defense of any of the three positions, and I do not want a survey of the literature or suggestions for further reading; I want the structure of the disagreement itself.
