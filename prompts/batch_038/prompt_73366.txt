I am auditing a graduate seminar in political theology this term, and its organizer has built the whole unit on political violence around a single sentence drawn from Aquinas's discussion of sedition in the Secunda Secundae: "a tyrannical government is not just, because it is directed, not to the common good, but to the private good of the ruler" — and therefore, he continues, disturbing such a government is not sedition, unless the disturbance does the subjects more harm than the tyranny itself did. That sentence has become the seminar's all-purpose answer. Last week a classmate used it to shut down a question about whether a crowd that stormed a courthouse had any standing to act, and outside the seminar the same line circulates whenever anyone wants to insist that the people who rioted were on the right side of history. I am not persuaded one sentence can do all that work, but I cannot yet say why.

The complication is that another member of the seminar wants to import it into law. Her project is a model jury instruction for sedition and insurrection offenses under which a defendant may argue that the government he acted against had forfeited its claim to stand for the common good, so that his conduct was not seditious at all but defensive; a proportionality proviso, taken from Aquinas's caveat, would require the jury to weigh the harm caused by the disturbance against the harm caused by the regime. As I understand it, nothing of the kind is available in practice today: legitimacy is not a question the jury may entertain, and the nearest analogue, nullification, is not a right anyone can assert. The proposal is therefore genuinely novel, and its defenders say the Aquinas line is its foundation.

Could you set out, as a menu I can bring to the seminar, the distinct precise claims this one sentence might be making? I want the readings separated rather than blended: is it a stipulative point about what "sedition" means in Aquinas's technical vocabulary — he follows Augustine in taking a people to be "the assembly of those who are united together in fellowship recognized by law and for the common good," and treats sedition as an offense against that unity — or a substantive moral claim that resistance to tyranny is permissible, or a narrower claim about how guilt distributes between the authors of a disturbance and its followers, or a claim about what the tyrant has forfeited? For each reading, tell me whether it becomes trivial once the definitions are granted, whether it is defensible, whether it is false, and whether it is merely verbal. In particular: does Aquinas meet the objection that delivering a multitude from a tyrant is praiseworthy by redefining "the people," and if so, has he said something or only rearranged terms? And what is the proportionality proviso doing — a moral limit on resistance, a condition on the concept, or a hedge against precisely the objection my classmate refused to answer?

Finally, say which of these readings, if any, would support the proposed jury instruction and which would leave it unsupported; what premises the legal version requires that Aquinas never supplies — who identifies a tyranny, by what procedure, and whether a modern state is a "multitude" in his sense at all; and which reading my classmate was invoking when he used the line to end an argument. I am not looking for a defense of one reading and need no bibliography: just the disambiguation, with verdicts.
