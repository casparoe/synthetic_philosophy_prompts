Watched The Lion King last night with a friend — rewatch, two adults, no kids, wine invovled — and we ended up in a two hour arguement about the Circle of Life that I still haven't recovered from. His take: the whole film is a naturalistic fallacy with better music. It tells you how the savanna works, then cashes that description in as a justification for hereditary kingship, and the rain coming back the second the rightful king stands on Pride Rock is just the landscape rubber-stamping a bloodline. My take was less crisp, which is I guess why I'm asking you.

I reached for Foot's natural goodness and for Thompson, on the grounds that judging a lion by lion standards isn't an inference from is to ought at all — it's a life-form judgment. A natural-historical statement like the female bobcat has two to four cubs in the spring isn't a summary of the bobcats you've counted; it's already norm-laden, which is why a blind owl is defective rather than merely unusual. So when Mufasa tells Simba that they eat the antelope, and when they die their bodies become the grass, and the antelope eat the grass, maybe that's not a premise for a conclusion but a way of locating a creature inside a form of life, which is a completely different job. My friend's reply: congratulations, you've just done the thing you accused me of, which is dressing up a feel-good myth as philosophy and calling it a discovery.

I'm writing this up as about 1500 words for a film-and-philosophy column I do, so I need the reading to be defensible rather than a vibe. Stuff I want from you:

- Go through the bits that are actually doing normative work and tell me what each one is: the everything-the-light-touches speech from Pride Rock, the antelope/grass exchange, Scar's life's-not-fair and the hyenas kept in the shadow of the Pride Lands, Hakuna Matata, the starved barrens under Scar, the rain and the greening at the end. For each, is it an is-to-ought move, a life-form judgment, a mythic guarantee from the ancestors and the sky, or just scenery nobody is arguing from?
- Does Foot actually license what I want her to license? I know she thinks human natural goodness runs through the rational will, so the analogy to animals breaks precisely where I need it to hold — a political order isn't a life form. Is my move salvageable, or is he right?
- Related: is Scar's reign better read as a natural-defect case — overgrazed, herds gone, hyenas hungry, nothing flourishing — i.e. a Footian evaluation of a whole system rather than of a character? Does that strengthen my reading or does it show the reading doesn't need the monarchy at all?
- He uses naturalistic fallacy and the is-ought gap interchangeably, and they aren't the same objection; one is about defining the good, one is about inference. Does that distinction bite on this movie or would it just be pedantry in the column? Tell me where each would sit, if anywhere.
- The part I'm least sure about: the balance of nature isn't true, and hasn't been thought true in ecology for decades. If the description is false, does the film's ethics collapse — or does that reveal the Circle of Life isn't a description at all but the answer a father gives a cub about death, i.e. consolation? And if it's consolation, is charging it with a naturalistic fallacy a category error?
- Where does the film resist the reading? Be honest about the hyenas specifically — the circle seems to have an outside, and the story needs one.
- Finish with what the film shows that Hume and Moore and Foot between them don't, if it shows anything. Not a tie-breaker, a real contribution or nothing.

Also, neither of us has the script open and you can't look it up, so work from the scenes as we both remember them and flag any detail you'd want verified before I print it.

I'd much rather hear it from you than from an editor.
