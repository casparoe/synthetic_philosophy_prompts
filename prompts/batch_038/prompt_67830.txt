**The question (25 of the 50 marks for the midterm; students were told to write 800–1000 words):**

A three-judge panel must decide whether a defendant is liable. By a rule that all three judges accept, the defendant is liable if and only if the defendant performed the act in question (call this p) and the relevant contract is valid (call this q). The judges vote as follows. Judge 1 accepts both p and q. Judge 2 accepts p but rejects q. Judge 3 rejects p but accepts q. Every judge's own set of views is consistent, and every judge accepts the rule connecting p, q and liability.

(a) Explain what the panel should say, collectively, about liability, and why that question admits of more than one defensible answer.
(b) Identify the general structural feature of an agenda that makes this kind of situation possible, and state what is lost and what is preserved by each of the two natural procedures for deciding the case.
(c) Assess the following claim: the discursive dilemma is simply Arrow's impossibility theorem restated in propositional logic, and the impossibility results in judgment aggregation therefore tell us nothing that was not already known.

**Student response:**

[P1] The panel is unanimous about the rule connecting the three questions and no judge is individually inconsistent, yet there is no single answer that falls out of the votes. If the panel votes separately on each of the three propositions, the majority accepts p (Judges 1 and 2), the majority accepts q (Judges 1 and 3), and the majority rejects liability (Judges 2 and 3). The collective set of judgments is then {p, q, not liable}, which contradicts a rule every member of the panel accepts. If instead the panel treats the premises as prior and lets liability follow from them, then since the majority view is that both p and q hold, the panel must find the defendant liable. This is the discursive dilemma: the premise-based and the conclusion-based procedures diverge, and neither of them can present itself as simply reporting what the majority of the panel thinks.

[P2] What generates the problem is not the number of judges, or the number of propositions, but the fact that the agenda is interconnected. Its propositions stand in logical relations that all members accept, so a profile of individually consistent judgment sets can aggregate into a collectively inconsistent one. On an agenda whose propositions are logically independent of each other, proposition-wise majority voting would raise no difficulty at all: each proposition would be settled by the votes on that proposition, and there would be no accepted constraint for the outcome to run up against. The dilemma is therefore a feature of agendas with entailment relations built into them, together with the further requirement — which we should not simply assume — that the group's judgments form a consistent set.

[P3] Each procedure buys something and pays for it. The premise-based procedure keeps the panel's judgment set consistent, at least in the standard case where the connecting rule is accepted by everyone, and it takes seriously the idea that a court decides for reasons: its verdict on liability is explained by the collective view of the premises. What it sacrifices is the majority's view about the very question the panel exists to answer, since two of the three judges think there is no liability and the panel finds liability anyway. The conclusion-based procedure delivers the outcome the majority wants but can no longer give a collective account of why, because the panel says there is no liability while the majority view of the premises, taken premise by premise, entails the opposite. And if the panel is expected to endorse all three propositions at once, proposition-wise majority voting simply hands it an inconsistent set. So the choice between procedures is not cosmetic.

[P4] It is worth being precise about which procedure offends against the independence condition that judgment-aggregation theorists impose. An aggregation rule satisfies independence when the collective judgment on each proposition depends only on the individual judgments on that same proposition. On that description the premise-based procedure is the independent one: it takes the collective view of p from the votes on p and the collective view of q from the votes on q, and then merely draws out a consequence that everyone on the panel already accepts, which is a deduction rather than a further act of aggregation. The conclusion-based procedure is where independence fails, because the collective verdict on liability is fixed by the votes on liability alone, so the majority view the panel has formed about p and about q is overridden by a vote on a different proposition.

[P5] One further consideration is often thought to settle which procedure the panel should adopt. Suppose each judge is more likely than not to be right about p and more likely than not to be right about q, and that the judges' views on these questions are independent of one another. The Condorcet jury theorem then tells us that the majority view on each premise converges on the truth as the panel grows. Since liability is nothing over and above the conjunction of the two premises, the majority view on liability must converge on the truth as well, and premise-based and conclusion-based voting will coincide in the limit. On this way of thinking the discursive dilemma is an artifact of small collegial courts, a curiosity of three-member panels that disappears once the group is large enough, rather than a structural feature of group judgment.

[P6] Turning to (c), I think the claim is substantially right, although it needs care. Any profile of strict preference orderings over three alternatives can be written as a profile of judgment sets over an agenda of six propositions — a is preferred to b, b to a, a to c, c to a, b to c, c to b — with transitivity imposed as a constraint, so that each rational ordering corresponds to a complete and consistent judgment set. Preference aggregation is thus a special case of judgment aggregation, and the impossibility theorem of List and Pettit is the general result of which Arrow's theorem is an instance. It follows that the discursive dilemma cannot be a genuinely new problem: the doctrinal paradox is the same pathology that produces cyclical majorities, dressed up in the notation of propositional logic, and the impossibility result adds nothing that Arrow did not already establish. At most, the propositional setting makes the underlying mechanism more vivid than the preference setting does.

[P7] So the answer to (a) is that the panel has no collective verdict that the votes alone determine. Whether it should find liability depends on what the panel is for. A court that is meant to decide for reasons, and to publish those reasons as binding on the parties, has to be able to say why it decided as it did, and of the two procedures only the premise-based one lets it do that. A body whose sole task is to settle an outcome — a committee choosing between options, say — has no comparable need and may reasonably aggregate on the conclusion. What the dilemma shows on either reading is that a group's judgments are not fixed by its members' judgments in the straightforward way we assume for individuals: one and the same profile of individual views can be organised into different collective verdicts, and the choice between them has to be made for reasons that are not themselves votes.
