ok, background first. I'm three years into a novel about a country that doesn't exist — call it Vessyn — which lost a civil war in the 1870s and has been telling itself a very particular story about that loss ever since. It's not meant to be a Confederate knockoff or a Masada riff; I want to build the mythology from first principles, which means I need to know what the machinery actually has to *do* before I can fake it convincingly on the page.

So: write me the desiderata. What would a general theory of how a military defeat turns into a sacred national myth have to deliver in order to count as adequate? I want the requirements list — the phenomena it has to explain, the cases it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the findings from history and psychology it cant contradict — and behind each one, the case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than just something nice to have. Then tell me which of the requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are really just the hobby-horses of a particular camp: the functionalists who think myth exists to glue the group together, the top-down people who think elites manufacture it, the grief-and-mourning crowd, the ones who treat it as basically a story that spreads because stories spread. Be honest about which demands are prejudices dressed up as criteria.

The part I care about most: flag any pair or subset of the desiderata that look jointly unsatisfiable. If the list itself turns out to be a result — i.e. no theory can meet all of it, and the interesting question is which one you drop — say so plainly. And say which single requirement does the most work knocking out candidates.

Hard cases my book has to survive, use these as stress tests:

- why some defeats get mythologized and functionally identical ones don't; nobody sings about the losing side of most civil wars
- the myth almost always fastens onto a battle the loser was within touching distance of winning, and almost never onto the actual decisive disaster
- there's nearly always a traitor figure, someone internal who supposedly handed over the loss, and a good theory owes me an account of why that role is structural rather than incidental
- the cause gets aesthetically purified: the concrete political program people fought for gets sanded off and replaced by manners, landscape, and a way of life
- the diaspora keeps the myth hotter and longer than the homeland does, sometimes for generations after the homeland has cooled on it
- the version that survives is usually not the version the losing leadership wanted, and often not the one the veterans told

Then, briefly — a paragraph, no more — how the main families of account stack up against the list. No names, no dates, and absolutely no bibliography or further reading; I'm not going to read it and you'd just be guessing anyway. What I need is the spec for the mechanism.
