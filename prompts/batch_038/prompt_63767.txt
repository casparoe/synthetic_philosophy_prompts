I am preparing a seminar presentation on counterfactual theories of causation, and I have become convinced that the standard teaching examples — the two rocks arriving at the window, the assassin with a backup shooter in the wings, the neuron that would have fired — are constructed precisely so as to avoid the kind of case that historical explanation actually confronts. I want to work one real case through carefully, and I would like you to do the metaphysics rather than the history, which I know well enough. The case is the summer of 1914. On the morning of 28 June a bomb thrown at Archduke Franz Ferdinand's car in Sarajevo fails to kill him; later that day, having insisted on visiting the wounded from the morning's attack, he is driven along a route his aides have not fully corrected, the car takes a wrong turn and stalls while reversing, and a nineteen-year-old conspirator who happens to be standing on that corner shoots the Archduke and his wife. What follows over the next five weeks is the ultimatum to Serbia of 23 July, the Austrian declaration of war on the 28th, Russian mobilization on the 30th, the German declarations and the march through Belgium, and British entry on 4 August. Historians remain split on the deeper structure: one camp holds that Berlin had already settled on a preventive war and used Sarajevo as a pretext; the other holds that no government in July 1914 wanted a general war, and that rigid alliance commitments together with mobilization timetables that could not be halted once started produced one mechanically.

So: analyze this episode through David Lewis's counterfactual analysis of causation and the modal realist semantics that underwrites it. Start by stating the analysis in its later form — causation as the ancestral of counterfactual dependence between distinct events, so that c causes e either if e depends counterfactually on c or if there is a chain of such dependences — and note that dependence is itself a matter of which worlds are closest, under an ordering that first avoids big and widespread violations of law, then maximizes the spatio-temporal region of perfect match of particular fact, then avoids even small local violations, with the consequence that the closest world in which the shot is never fired is one that diverges from ours by a small local miracle shortly before the car reaches the corner. Then work through the case. In the closest such worlds, does the general war still come? The two historiographical camps give opposite answers, and I want you to be explicit about whether their disagreement is a disagreement about the particular facts of our world or a disagreement about the closeness ordering — specifically, about how much weight the match-of-particular-fact clause gives to the details of German and Russian war planning. Suppose the answer is that war comes anyway, a year or two later, over some other Balkan incident. Was the assassination then a cause of the war at all, or only a cause of the war's coming when and how it did, and of its having the particular shape it had? Is that a defect of the analysis or one of its achievements — does it correctly demote the trigger and promote the machinery, or does it license an absurdity, since no historian of either camp denies that Princip caused the war? Say which structure this is: early preemption, late preemption, symmetric overdetermination, or something the taxonomy does not name. The obvious suggestion is that the alliance system and the mobilization timetables form a preempting backup and the Archduke's survival the preempted alternative. Press the difficulty that suggestion runs into: do the chain strategy and the later quasi-dependence revision get any purchase when the preempting backup is not a discrete event but a standing structural condition? Lewis relates events; the historian's talk of structural causes does not obviously name events, and I want to know whether that is a category mismatch the theory cannot repair or whether structural claims can be recast as claims about the absence of the intervening events that would have arrested the machinery.

Finally, the modal realist part, which is what I most want tested. Does the full-blooded ontology do any work here that an abstractionist semantics would not? Plenitude guarantees a concrete world for every way the July Crisis might have gone, and historians trade in such worlds constantly; but does plenitude help settle which of them are closest, or is closeness — vague, context-sensitive, weighted by pragmatic factors that shift with the question the historian is asking — exactly the place where concrete worlds buy nothing? A friend of mine in the history department thinks this whole exercise is a category mistake, that historians' causal claims were never in the business of tracking counterfactual dependence and that Lewis is answering a question nobody asked; give me the best Lewisian reply to her if there is one, and tell me honestly if there isn't. Then say what the framework does and does not deliver for the counterfactuals that actually matter to the historiography: whether a more cautious chancellor, a slower Russian mobilization, or a Serbian acceptance of the ultimatum would have changed the outcome. Where is the analysis silent, and where does it issue verdicts so sensitive to the closeness ordering as to be unusable? Close with a plain assessment — is July 1914 a confirmation of Lewis's account, an anomaly that the preemption literature should have absorbed, or a case the framework never anticipated — and mark clearly the point at which the theory runs out rather than stretching it to cover the episode. I am not asking for citations or further reading; I want the mapping itself.
