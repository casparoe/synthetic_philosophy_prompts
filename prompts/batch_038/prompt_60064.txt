I am a credentialed actuary working in life and catastrophe modelling: my daily tools are survival functions, hazard rates, competing-risk decompositions, credibility weighting, tail dependence, risk margins, embedded value, and reserve adequacy testing, and I am fluent in the professional literature on discounting conventions and on the value of a statistical life. My philosophy is limited to one undergraduate course. I want a philosophical position translated into the vocabulary I actually work in, and then audited.

The position is strong longtermism, which I will state as three theses so you are not free to pick the friendliest version. First, the value of what we do now depends substantially on its effects on the very long-run future, because that future is vastly large in the number of lives it may contain and in how well or badly those lives may go. Second, what we morally ought to do is determined by which available actions have the greatest expected influence on that long-run future, with no pure time preference applied merely because a benefit or harm falls later. Third, it follows that reducing the probability of events that would permanently foreclose or drastically degrade that future is among the most important priorities of the present generation, even when the annual probability of such events is small and even when the people who would benefit do not yet exist and never will unless we act differently.

A colleague who has read some of this literature claims he can render the whole thing in my own terms, and his rendering is this: longtermism is the proposal to set the discount rate to zero, to price existential risk as an absorbing state with hazard rate h in a competing-risks model, and to conclude that the actuarially optimal allocation of prevention spending is governed by the tail of the distribution rather than by its mean, since a small h multiplied by an unbounded loss dominates. I suspect he has quietly replaced the position with one my discipline would endorse on its own grounds, and I want that suspicion tested rather than confirmed.

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion. Items 1 through 3 must each translate one of my three theses, naming what maps onto a construct I recognise, what has to be distorted to fit, and what I cannot express at all. Item 4 must rule on my colleague's rendering: whether it is a translation of longtermism or a substitution of actuarial premises for the position's own, and where the difference shows. Item 5 must say whether the residue is a defect of my framework, which may simply lack the constructs needed to talk about the moral weight of people who do not exist, or a sign that the original position was less determinate than its confidence suggested, and what you would need to see to settle the question.
