My mother is retiring this month after twenty-two years as our country's minister for European affairs, and I am giving the speech at her farewell dinner in Brussels. The room is mixed by design, and its thirds do not mix easily: about a third are national parliamentarians from back home, several of whom built careers on the claim that the Union is undemocratic; a third are Commission and Council officials who find that claim insulting; and the rest are family and old friends from her constituency, most of whom could not describe how a law actually gets made here.

She has asked me not to settle the democratic-deficit question by either easy route — not by insisting that Brussels answers to voters the way a national government does, and not by conceding that because it does not, it is undemocratic. The speech should be built instead on the distinction she used to argue for at the kitchen table: that a body like the Union is legitimated more the way a constitution is than the way a government is. Its function is to bind everyone, including whoever wins the next election, so the test is not "whom can we throw out?" but "what would it take to change the rules, and who gets a say in that?" On this view the accountability question is not silenced but redirected — toward amendment, protocol, opt-out and exit rather than toward elections.

Please write the speech, five or six minutes. It must make that distinction intelligible to the third of the room that has never encountered it, without making the parliamentarians who have asked the electoral question in good faith for decades feel like fools: grant them that theirs is exactly the right question to ask of a government, then show why the Union is not one. Work in a single small, concrete application of the distinction from her career — invent the details freely, but do not present any invented episode as real EU legislation or a real negotiation. And end as a tribute to her rather than as a lecture: the argument should be the way of honouring her, not something delivered at her colleagues.
