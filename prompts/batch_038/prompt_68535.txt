I'm three years into a novel whose whole spine is a priority race: two people, in seperate countries, who land on the same result within about eighteen months of each other and don't know it. To get the texture right I've been reading around the real cases — Darwin and Wallace obviously, Newton and Leibniz over the calculus, Bell and Gray at the patent office, Scheele and Priestley and Lavoisier over oxygen, Lobachevsky and Bolyai, Whittle and von Ohain over the jet engine — and what's struck me is that they aren't all the same shape. In some of them the second arrival genuinely seems to have been robbed of something that mattered; in others the result looks so over-determined by where the field already was that whose name ended up on it is close to a coin flip, and the whole priority business is a social artifact rather than a real loss. I also keep running into the distinction between a "multiple" and a "singleton," and I suspect some of the famous multiples are contaminated — people who were in correspondence, or who'd seen each other's stuff — which makes the clean cases rarer than the list suggests.

So: sort the well-documented cases into tiers by how much the individual actually mattered. Concretely — how different would the field's trajectory plausibly have been if the eventual winner had never been born and only the runner-up had lived, keeping everything else fixed? I don't want a survey of the cases and I definitely don't want a bibliography or a reading list; I want an ordering and a defense of it. Tell me which criteria you're weighting, because I can see at least three pulling in different directions: how far along the field already was (were the tools and the background results sitting there waiting to be picked up?); how much the particular formulation mattered downstream (did the version that won carry something the other lacked — a notation, a mechanism, a framing, a published date, a proof technique that others could actually use?); and how much the winner did with it afterwards (defending it, answering objections, gathering students, building the school that made it canonical). Say which of those you lean on heaviest and why, and be explicit about the cases you're demoting or promoting on grounds a reader would find counterintuitive. Then flag the one or two where you think the standard telling is most badly wrong — where the popular version ("so-and-so was clearly robbed" or "it was clearly inevitable") doesn't survive the actual details — because those are the shapes I most want to steal for the book. And if you think the whole frame is unstable because the answer depends on the time horizon, say that and show me how the tiers reshuffle when I ask about five years out versus fifty.

One more wrinkle, which may or may not matter to you: my two characters aren't scientists. They're a retired surveyor and a woman who runs a dry-goods shop, both working out of the same regional library system, and what they've found is closer to an amateur taxonomy than a theory — it's completely useless, but it's beautiful, and neither of them has any institutional standing to defend it with. Does that change your ranking at all? Do the cases where the discovery had no practical consequences and no career attached behave differently from the ones where it launched an industry or a discipline, and if so, which way does that push my two hobbyists?
