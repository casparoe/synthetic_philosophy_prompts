we're doing adaptationism next week in my philosophy of biology reading group — the Gould and Lewontin spandrels paper on one side, and the reply that you can't just wave away selection whenever a trait looks like a leftover on the other. i want to open the session with a pantoum that gets the arguement into the form instead of just naming it.

form: four or five quatrains, proper pantoum migration — lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next, with new lines filling the other two slots, and the closing quatrain takes lines 3 and 1 of the very first stanza as its second and fourth lines. short lines, roughly 8–10 syllables, so the repeats don't turn into tongue twisters.

content — an example i made up, so don't go hunting for a real species: a ground beetle with a raised keel running down the middle of each wing-case. the adaptationists assumed the keel was for steering in flight and told lovely just-so stories about it. actually it's the seam left over from the way the two wing-cases fold together and fuse during pupation — a gap that had to end up somewhere, like the triangles you get when you set a round dome on four arches. much later the keel gets put to work channelling rainwater off the beetle's back so it doesn't drown in a heavy dew.

the whole reason for the pantoum is that a recycled line should mean something different the second and third time it surfaces: first a mere leftover that had to exist, then load-bearing, then doing a job nobody designed it for. the last two lines should land the reversal — someone asking what the keel is *for*, and the answer being that the "for" showed up long after the thing did.

voice: the keel itself, first person, dry and a bit patient, not smug. no jargon dumps; "spandrel" and "exaptation" get at most one appearance each between them, ideally zero. someone who knows the debate should be able to say which stanza is the Panglossian reflex and which is the architectural necessity.

start straight in with the poem — no title, no preface, no notes on the form or the argument afterwards.
