I want to know what principle, if any, governs my attributions of causal power. When I ask what truly causes what — and, in particular, how much efficacy is left to created things once every event is owing to God — my considered judgments come apart in ways I cannot articulate. They are set out below.

1. A sharp knife divides a loaf cleanly. I have no hesitation in saying that the knife cuts the bread: its edge is a real feature of the world and it makes a difference. A blunt knife would not have cut.

2. Fire applied to cotton produces flame. Here I side with the theologians against the philosophers: fire does not burn; God creates the burning when fire meets cotton. Nothing in heat as such entails flame, and God could have preserved the cotton unchanged.

3. A drug given in a measured dose lowers a fever. I want to say that the drug acts, that it has a power, and that medicine is a genuine discipline rather than a catalogue of divinely arranged coincidences. Yet if I have stripped fire of its efficacy, I cannot say why the drug keeps its own. The obvious reply — that we know how the drug works and not how fire works — seems to make the world's causal structure depend on our ignorance.

4. I raise my arm, unbidden. God creates the motion and I acquire it; the acquisition is real enough that this is my act and I am answerable for it.

5. A stronger man seizes my arm and uses it to strike a third person. God creates precisely the same motion, on my account, and yet this is not my act and I bear nothing for it. The difference has to lie in me rather than in what God does, and I cannot say what in me it lies in, since on my own view everything in me is created too.

6. A man raises his arm because a knife is held against his ribs. This falls between the two: his act, and yet not one I would credit or charge him with in the ordinary way. I find this verdict strange, since what he contributes looks as thin as what I contribute in the first case.

7. Suppose an attendant created will, of a kind the tradition grants, intends that a given bundle of cotton catch, and God creates the flame at that moment. I attribute the burning wholly to God and treat the created will as no cause at all. But its role is indistinguishable from the role fire plays in case 2: an antecedent that God answers with flame. Why should the presence of a will in the antecedent change my attribution of the consequent?

8. Contact of fire with cotton has never been observed to fail, and I predict burning with complete confidence. Still I would not say that I know fire burns; habit gives me expectation and not insight.

9. When God preserves a prophet in fire unharmed, my instinct is to say that the miracle reveals what was always so, namely that fire never burned anything. But a miracle ought to show something new rather than advertise something old, and I am not at ease with this verdict.

10. A dog strains at a leash toward food: genuine action, the dog doing something. A sunflower tracks the sun across the day: mere mechanism, nothing that the sunflower does. I cannot say what separates these, and I suspect that if pressed I would have to withdraw one of them.

11. A machine of silicon and metal adds two numbers correctly on every occasion. I would say without hesitation that it computes. If I have denied efficacy to fire, consistency seems to require that I deny it to silicon as well, and I do not want to.

12. A lodestone draws iron filings toward itself. Here I want to say that the magnet truly attracts, and I am embarrassed by this, since it suggests that what moves me in case 1 is not reason but the fact that geometry looks like a real feature of a thing while heat does not.

Attempting to state my rule for myself, I come up with something like this: I attribute a real power to a created thing when what it would do in given circumstances is settled by that thing's own nature, when there is a principled answer to the counterfactual question that comes from the thing itself rather than from a pattern laid over it from outside. But fire seems to be exactly such a case, and the sunflower too, and I deny efficacy to both; so the formulation cannot be right as it stands.

Please do the following.

(a) Set out one or two candidate principles, stated precisely enough that a new case could be sorted by applying them mechanically, and identify for each clause the case above that motivates it. If no single principle sorts all twelve, say where the sorting has to be done by two principles working together and how they are to be ordered.

(b) Give each candidate's verdicts on four cases I have not listed: whether a person's settled character truly causes what she characteristically does; whether petitionary prayer has any efficacy, and if so of what kind; whether God's own customary manner of acting is a cause of anything or only a description of how he acts; and whether I may claim to know that fire will burn cotton tomorrow, given case 8.

(c) Tell me plainly which of my twelve verdicts each candidate cannot keep. Where a principle preserves all of them only at the cost of extra machinery, weigh that cost against the cost of overturning one or two verdicts, and say which trade you would take and why.

(d) Say what the resulting view should be called. Does it remain occasionalism, does it become a doctrine on which God and creatures cooperate in every effect, or does it collapse into the position of the philosophers that I took myself to be rejecting in case 2?

(e) Finally, say whether any of these verdicts are merely verbal — artifacts of how my language is built rather than views about the world — and whether the whole enterprise of sorting true causes from mere occasions would be better abandoned than repaired.
