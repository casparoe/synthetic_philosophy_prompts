Write a short story of about twelve hundred words that dramatizes Bradley's regress as it bears to the unity of the proposition: the difficulty that a proposition's constituents — an object, a property, and whatever is supposed to tie them together — seem to demand a further tie, and then another, forever, so that a heap of components never becomes a single thing capable of being true or false.

Set it in an archive, bureau, or workshop where sentences are physically assembled from stored parts by officials whose entire job is attaching what attaches. Invent whatever machinery, ranks, and internal regulations you need, but make them concrete enough that a reader can follow one particular sentence down the assembly line and see precisely where the work refuses to terminate. Two constraints. First, the story must not use the words proposition, relation, regress, or unity at any point; the philosophical content has to be carried entirely by the machinery and by the characters arguing about it. Second, it must give real force to at least two rival accounts of how assembly could ever succeed — for example, that the last tie is a different kind of thing from the ones before it; that the parts on the shelf are inert but are already at work once deployed, so there is nothing left to attach; or that the whole procedure rests on a mistake and finished sentences never needed assembling at all — and then commit. The ending must decide: one account wins or definitively fails, in a way that changes what the protagonist does next, rather than leaving the reader with a shrug.

After the story, add a note of no more than three hundred words stating which position your ending endorses, why you chose it over the rival to which you gave the better arguments, and where you knowingly took liberties — in particular, whether you ran together the regress for relations between objects and the regress for the unity of a proposition, and what that conflation costs the story philosophically.
