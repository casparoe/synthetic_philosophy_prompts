**The question as set (700 words maximum):**

> State Lewis's Principal Principle as precisely as you can. Explain what work the admissibility restriction does, and give an example of information that would be inadmissible. Then explain why a Humean supervenience thesis about chance appears to make trouble for the Principle, and say what you think Lewis should do in response.

**The student's answer:**

> Lewis's Principal Principle is a constraint on how an agent's degrees of belief must relate to her beliefs about chances. In the form he gives it: if X is the proposition that the chance, at time t, of A is x, and G is any proposition admissible at t, then a rational agent's conditional credence in A on X and G together is x. The principle is not a claim about frequencies, and it is not a theorem of the probability calculus; Lewis treats it as very nearly analytic, since it is what we mean in saying that there is such a thing as objective chance at all. A principle that failed to tie chance to credence in roughly this way would leave the word doing no work.
>
> The restriction to admissible G does real labor. It excludes information that looks ahead to how the chance process actually turns out, because such information may properly move your credence away from the chance. If I know that the chance of heads on this toss is 0.5 but I also know that the coin has in fact landed heads, my credence in heads should be 1 and not 0.5; the second piece of information is inadmissible. Lewis offers no fully general account of admissibility. He gives sufficient conditions — a proposition wholly about matters of fact at or before t is admissible, and so is a hypothesis about what the chances are — and concedes that the notion is not completely characterized. This is a soft spot in the paper, since admissibility is what separates the cases where deference is required from those where it is not.
>
> A related consequence falls out of the calculus. Where an agent is uncertain among several chance hypotheses, repeated application of the Principle makes her unconditional credence in A a weighted average of the candidate chances, the weights being her credences in the hypotheses. Deference to chance thus composes with ordinary Bayesian uncertainty rather than competing with it.
>
> The difficulty comes from Lewis's own metaphysics of chance. He holds that chances supervene on the entire Humean mosaic of particular matters of fact: what the chance of A is at t depends on the whole distribution of events across space and time, and so on how things turn out in the future as well as on what has already happened. A chance hypothesis is therefore not a proposition about the past; its truth is partly fixed by what is yet to come. Now any sensible chance hypothesis assigns nonzero chance to futures in which the mosaic comes out such that the best systematization of it yields a different chance for A — call these the undermining futures. The Principle then tells me to give those futures a credence equal to their chance, which is positive. But an undermining future, if it obtains, entails that my chance hypothesis X was false. So the Principle appears to require that my conditional credence in the negation of X on X be positive, which is flatly inconsistent with the probability calculus. Lewis's constraint on credence, combined with his Humean account of what chances are, generates an incoherent demand.
>
> Lewis's way out is to revise the Principle so that the agent conditionalizes not on the chance hypothesis alone but on that hypothesis conjoined with the complete theory of chance — the whole truth about how chances depend on the mosaic. Because the complete theory entails every true chance hypothesis, no undermining future is compatible with it and the incoherence disappears; the revised principle is consistent with conditionalization, and Humean supervenience survives intact. Lewis makes this amendment in the same paper in which he first states the Principal Principle, so there is no earlier and later Lewis on this question. The amendment also leaves the Principle fully serviceable: the complete theory is just one more conjunct in the evidence, so an agent who lacks it may substitute her best hypothesis about it and proceed exactly as before.
>
> There is a structural lesson here. The Principal Principle is a deference principle of a familiar kind. Van Fraassen's Reflection Principle requires that my present conditional credence in A, given that I shall at some later time have credence q in A, be q. Lewis's requirement has the same shape, with objective chance standing where my future self would otherwise stand. Both say that a rational agent's present opinion must track a better-positioned opinion; and for Lewis chance is simply what a fully informed agent's credence would be, so the Principal Principle tells me to align myself with the credence I would have if I knew everything about the world. The undermining problem is then one instance of a general puzzle about self-referential deference — what happens when the opinion one is deferring to is itself partly constituted by how one turns out to believe and act.
