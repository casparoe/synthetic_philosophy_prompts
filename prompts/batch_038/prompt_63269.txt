For eleven years I have kept a private notebook of situations that seem to share a shape, and I have stopped trusting my own ability to tell whether the shape is really one thing. I own a share of a regional haulage firm and do much of the dispatching myself, so the first entries are the ones I live inside: shippers at a congested dock began rewarding whoever turned a load around fastest, every competitor tightened the schedule to match, and my drivers mostly asked for the tighter schedule because it paid per load rather than per hour. A federal hours rule eventually put a floor under it, and the race stopped well short of where I had confidently predicted it would go, which unsettled me more than the race itself did. Around that I collected contrasts. A fishing harbor I visited as a child where two generations of families restricted their own catch and the arrangement held for decades without anyone enforcing it. A school district near where my sister lives where grade inflation ran essentially unchecked for twenty years, with everyone complaining and nobody stopping. An app marketplace where every developer bought favorable reviews until the platform made it technically expensive, at which point the practice mostly evaporated. A competition among national corporate tax rates that still appears to be running with no floor in sight. And at least one case where I expected a trap and found none: the haulage firms in my region quietly converged on the same driver-safety standard a decade ago, and to my knowledge not one of them lost a contract for it.

What I want before I read anything further is a specification rather than another candidate theory: the desiderata that an account of these situations would have to meet in order to count as adequate. Call it a theory of multipolar traps, or of races to the bottom, if you must pick a name, though I suspect part of the job is deciding whether those label the same phenomenon. For each requirement, give me the case behind it, drawing on my notebook where possible or supplying another concrete one, so that I can see what the requirement is protecting against. I would like the list to cover at least the following, and I expect you to add what I have missed: how the account handles negative controls, meaning structurally similar situations that never become traps, since my dock case and my safety-standard case look alike from the outside; the floor problem, that is, why races stop where they do and what kind of thing supplies the stopping point; whether the account is expected to work at very different scales, from two rival firms to forty drivers to millions of consumers to a single person racing against their own past choices over years; whether it still works when everyone involved knows perfectly well that they are in a trap, and equally whether it works when no one does; some version of a ledger requirement, meaning the account must be able to say who is gaining, what exactly they gain, and why that gain cannot be bargained away or handed back in exchange for stopping; that it must not smuggle in a benevolent planner, a shared morality, or participants who are simply fools; that it must keep the trap distinct from its neighbors, which as I understand them include common-pool-resource depletion, arms races with a strategic adversary, ordinary coordination failure, status competition, bad luck, and the case of an outcome that is worse than some alternative nobody could actually reach; that it must distinguish a situation where the better state is better only for the group taken together from one where a change exists that each participant would individually prefer even if everyone else refused to join, because those feel very different to me from the inside and I want to be told whether the difference is real or an artifact; that it must handle traps in which the losers are not at the table and cannot be compensated; and, most importantly for my purposes, whether it must come with diagnostic tests an outsider can actually run without access to the participants' preferences, along the lines of the crude ones I have invented, namely whether a single participant could stop on a Tuesday without measurable loss, whether adding one more competitor makes things strictly worse, and whether the arrangement survives a temporary absence of competition. Finally, say plainly whether the demand that the account yield a remedy is a genuine desideratum or a prejudice of people who want to fix things rather than understand them.

Then do the sorting work I cannot do for myself. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of particular camps, and flag any subset you believe cannot be jointly satisfied: my own suspicion is that insisting on an outsider-runnable diagnostic test while also insisting the account never infer what people want is asking for the impossible, since the test seems to require knowing what would count as a loss to the participant, and I would like to be told whether that circularity can be broken or whether the list is itself the result. Tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidates. Only then, and briefly, a sentence or two each on how the accounts I have run into fare against the list: the common-pool-resource story, the commitment-and-arms-race story, the story in which firms are compelled by rivals because they take prices as given, and the currently fashionable story that most of these are really about status and signaling. I am not looking for reassurance or for a generous reading of my notebook. Please say how confident you are in what you assert, requirement by requirement where that is useful, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of and why; and if your considered view is that my cases are not one phenomenon at all, say so directly and show me which of my own requirements breaks first.
