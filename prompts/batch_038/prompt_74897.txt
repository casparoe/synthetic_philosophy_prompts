So I've been going down the predictive processing rabbit hole and I think I finally see why pessimists seem to have it figured out, but I want to check the reasoning before I run with it.

If the brain is basically a prediction machine whose whole job is to minimize surprise, then doesn't someone with very low expectations have close to the best-fitting model going? They expect the job interview to go badly, they expect to feel awful on Sunday night, they expect friends to flake — so they almost never get hit with a big prediction error. The optimist is eating huge errors constantly and paying for them. Which would mean that under the free energy principle, optimism is just systematically bad inference, and that the way to become a better Bayesian is to expect less from everything.

Two follow-ups on that:

1. If what actually gets minimized is expected free energy, does lowering your priors make your own actions formally cheaper — like, do gloomy priors buy you a discount on the action-selection side too, not just the perception side?

2. My therapist keeps saying my negative expectations are self-fulfilling, but if active inference means I act so as to make my own predictions come true, isnt that just the system working exactly as designed rather than something broken?

So how much of this should I actually apply to myself — do I want to be deliberately lowering my expectations to get better predictions, or am I missing something about how the theory counts a model as good?
