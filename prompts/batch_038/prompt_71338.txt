I work at a small comparative-security institute in Helsinki, and for the past eighteen months I have been sitting in a working group whose brief was to advise on the future of the Nordic service systems. What was meant to be a technical exercise has turned into the same argument at every meeting, and I have come to suspect that the argument is not the one the participants think they are having. On one side are the professionists: they hold that a volunteer force with career non-commissioned officers, long service and repeated training on air defence, electronic warfare, unmanned systems and signals intelligence delivers more capability per euro and per day of readiness than any levy, and they cite Sweden's 2010 decision to suspend peacetime conscription as a belated admission that the Cold War mass army had become an expensive way of producing badly trained soldiers. On the other side are the citizen-soldier people: they hold that the professionists are answering the wrong question, because a conscript force is not primarily a fighting formation but a reservoir of mass and duration, a visible signal of societal commitment that deters, the mechanism by which a state registers the doctors, lorry drivers, engineers and network technicians a whole-of-society defence needs, and the political guarantee that the army remains the polity's own rather than a separate corps with interests of its own. They point to Finland, where the usual figure is that something like four fifths of a male age cohort completes some form of national service; to Sweden's reactivation of conscription in 2017; and to the Swiss electorate, which in 2013 rejected abolishing conscription by more than seventy per cent and in November 2025 rejected, by a still larger margin, an initiative that would have replaced male-only military service with a general civic duty binding on both sexes.

What I cannot get the group to engage with is the fact that the empirical record looks as though it cuts both ways and yet nobody treats it as decisive. When Sweden brought conscription back, it called up roughly four thousand people a year out of an age cohort of about a hundred thousand — four per cent — which does not look like a mass levy at all and looks much more like a competitive recruiting channel with a legal obligation bolted onto it; and the Swiss system is not universal in the sense its rhetoric implies either, since a substantial minority is exempted on medical or other grounds and pays a supplement on income tax in lieu of serving. So my working hypothesis is that the word "conscription" is covering three different institutions, and the two camps are each arguing about a different one of them: a genuine mass levy, a selective recruitment pipeline, and a citizenship ritual whose military content is incidental. A second hypothesis is that the disagreement is not empirical but categorical — about whether an armed force is an instrument the state purchases or part of what the state is — in which case cost-effectiveness comparisons are simply beside the point and no amount of better data will move anyone. A third is that there is a real suppressed empirical question that neither side wants stated aloud, because an answer would embarrass both: what would a large, thinly trained reserve actually contribute in the kind of high-intensity war these countries now plan for, given that none of them has any post-1945 experience of its own system under fire. And a fourth, which I find least satisfying but cannot rule out, is that what is really being contested is the permissibility of compelling a person's body and time — a bedrock intuition about the state's claim on the individual that both camps have learned to argue for indirectly, through the idiom of effectiveness.
