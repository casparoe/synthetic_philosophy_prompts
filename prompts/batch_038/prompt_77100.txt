TA'ing a mid-level ethics course and I'm grading midterms tonight, and one answer has me stuck. The question was:

"A promise seems to create an obligation owed to one particular person out of nothing more than the promiser's own act. Explain what is puzzling about that, and contrast two attempts to dissolve the puzzle. For each, say whether the obligation you end up with is still distinctively promissory."

Here's the answer (lightly compressed, nothing changed substantively):

---

The puzzle: if obligations normally come from something outside me — a harm I caused, a relationship I'm in, a rule that applies whether I like it or not — then a promise looks like it makes me the source of my own obligation. But a bare act of will doesn't obviously have that power. I can't will that I owe you ten pounds any more than I can will that ten pounds exists. So either the appearance is an illusion and promissory obligation is something else in disguise, or the will has a normative power nobody has explained yet.

Hume takes the first route. Promising isn't a natural act but an artificial one: it works because a convention attaches consequences to a certain form of words, and the convention is worth having because of the general interest everyone has in being able to rely on each other. Hume's point is that the obligation comes from the expectation the promiser has deliberately raised in the promisee together with the harm of disappointing it, so no mysterious power of the will is needed. Prichard develops this: the real obligation isn't created by the promise at all but is an old one, the obligation not to let down the confidence your own earlier conduct produced. Scanlon generalizes it into a principle about intentionally getting someone to expect you'll do X when you know they want to be assured and they rely on it.

Rawls takes the second route. On the practice conception, promising is a practice with constitutive rules, like a game: saying the words is making a move whose force comes from the practice rather than from my psychology. Searle says something similar and uses it to argue you can get from an is to an ought once the institutional facts are in place. This keeps the idea that I bind myself — the obligation is mine in a way utility isn't — but since the practice itself is justified by its usefulness, the obligation to keep this promise just is the usefulness of the practice, so it lapses whenever breaking it quietly would produce better results.

Scanlon is really giving the same kind of answer as Searle, just with the rules stated as moral principles instead of institutional ones: both treat promising as a normative power, a thing you do when you intend to create an obligation for yourself. That's why on both views the resulting duty is still distinctively promissory. Hume's tradition can't say that. If all that's wrong is disappointing an expectation, then I could discharge the obligation by paying you off instead, or by arranging for you to be deceived into thinking I performed. The expectation theorists dissolve the promise into the wrong of misleading someone, which is a different wrong entirely.
