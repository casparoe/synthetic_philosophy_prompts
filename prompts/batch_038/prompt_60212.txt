So I've been reading about the side-effect effect on and off for like fifteen years — first ran into it on LessWrong, kept bumping into it on every philosophy forum since — and I'm finally in a position to actually run a study. Before I preregister anything I want to pressure-test my prediction against someone who'll commit to a number instead of hiding behind "it depends which account of intentionality you accept."

Assume you're a seasoned experimental philosopher who has personally run a couple hundred vignette studies and has strong opinions about which manipulations actually land and which ones quietly fail. Brainstorm out loud first — list the candidate mechanisms that could be producing the asymmetry, including ones that have nothing to do with morality — and only then start assigning numbers. I'd rather see the scratch work than a polished essay.

The setup: the classic chairman cases. Harm condition, subjects overwhelmingly (~82% in the original) say the chairman harmed the environment intentionally; help condition, only ~23% say he helped intentionally, despite the exact same knowledge-and-indifference structure. My extension keeps the structure and removes the blame. New condition, roughly:

> A founder is deciding whether to launch a new product. His COO tells him it will make the company a lot of money, but as a side effect it will permanently destroy his own sense of taste and smell — he'll never enjoy food or wine or his kids' hair again. The founder says he doesn't care about that at all, he just wants the money. He launches the product, makes a fortune, and loses his sense of taste and smell.

Then the test question: did he lose his sense of taste and smell intentionally?

The point of the manipulation is that the bad side effect lands only on the agent, so moral condemnation has nowhere to go — nobody blames a guy for wrecking his own life, they mostly just think he's an idiot. If blame/moral evaluation is what's driving the effect, intentionality attributions should collapse toward the help-condition baseline. If it's affect, salience, or the weirdness of hearing an agent say he doesn't care about a foreseeable consequence, the effect should survive.

Here's what I want: your credence, as a number, that a majority (>50%) of subjects in this condition say "yes, intentionally." And then the decomposition — the sub-questions that number rests on, each with a rough probability and one or two sentences of justification. At minimum I want you to assign numbers to:

- the probability that the asymmetry is driven primarily by moral evaluation / blame rather than by affect, salience, or norm violation generally
- the probability that "he foresaw it and proceeded anyway" does most of the work in the folk concept of intentional action, independent of valence
- the probability that subjects smuggle blame back in anyway — they read the indifference itself as a vice, or they assume family/employees get hurt too, or they just can't help disapproving
- the probability that demand characteristics carry the result on their own (the vignette bothered to mention the side effect, so it must matter)
- the probability my vignette as written actually achieves what I want it to achieve, which is honestly the one I'm least sure about
- plus any term you think I've left out that carries real weight

Then show me how they combine into your final number, and tell me which single term that number is most sensitive to — i.e. where a 20-point shift in your estimate moves the bottom line the most.

Be specific, don't split the difference politely.

Last thing: describe one concrete pilot result — say, a pattern you'd see in an n=30 run, or an answer to a follow-up question — that would move your number the most, and in which direction.
