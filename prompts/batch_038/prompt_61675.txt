You are a philosopher-economist who works on the socialist calculation debate and on the theory of the firm — equally at home in Mises, Hayek, and Lange and in Coase and Kantorovich. I am drafting a paper for a history-of-economics workshop around an argument I have built, and I now suspect it proves far too much. Please work through the argument itself carefully rather than surveying the debate.

(1) Mises's impossibility claim is that rational economic calculation requires genuine market prices for the means of production, because only actual exchanges between separate owners can supply a common denominator in which heterogeneous capital goods and the production structures they enable can be compared.

(2) Kantorovich's linear programming yields "resolving multipliers" — dual values attached to each scarce input and each binding constraint — that tell a planner which processes to run and what each input is worth at the margin; these are computed with no exchange at all, and the work was later recognized with a Nobel prize.

(3) So (2) is a constructive counterexample to (1): valuations sufficient for choosing among alternative uses of capital goods can be generated internally.

(4) The same machinery is what a vertically integrated firm uses when it sets transfer prices and ranks internal projects. A large firm is a planned economy that calculates rationally, and it does so through administratively constructed valuations rather than internal exchanges.

(5) Nothing in (3) or (4) mentions scale. If a planning department can generate shadow prices for ten thousand machines, a central board can do the same for ten million, because the mathematics is identical and only computing capacity differs.

(6) Therefore Mises's thesis reduces to an engineering claim about the computational resources available in 1920, and Lange's trial-and-error solution was right in principle.

I want to keep (2) — Kantorovich plainly produced valuations that work — and I want to keep the Coasian observation in (4), since firms evidently do plan internally. What I cannot accept is (5) and (6): they appear to show that an entire economy is simply a very large firm, a conclusion I do not believe and one that would also seem to rule out any case for decentralization at all.

Please begin with a short diagnostic pass listing every candidate failure point. Then state which step or steps you reject, and explain why each rejected step looked entirely respectable when I wrote it. Say explicitly whether you take the argument to be valid with one attractive but false premise, or invalid through an equivocation, a scope shift, or a composition fallacy, and name the two senses of any term that is doing double duty. Finally, price the alternatives: what would I have to surrender to keep (5), and what does it cost me to retain (2) and (4) while giving (5) up?
