I've been in the same reading group for about two years and for the last one we've been stuck on higher-order evidence and epistemic akrasia, to the point where people have stopped even trying to convince each other. The case we've been chewing on is one I made up because the stock examples all felt too tidy. Say a translator, Nadia, spends an afternoon working out the rendering of one very hard line in an early modern poem, and she ends up confident she's got it right. She can lay out exactly why. The next day her clinic calls: the migraine medication she'd been on that afternoon is documented, in a minority of patients, to cause quiet distortions in judging word meaning in archaic registers — precisely the skill she was using — and the literature is explicit that people under the effect basically never notice it from the inside. So she now has strong evidence that the process that produced her conclusion was unreliable in exactly this domain. But here's the wrinkle I added: she's been through this before, several times, and in the past when she learned the same thing her original judgment turned out to have been right roughly seven times in ten. And when she goes back over her reasons for the translation, they still look completely compelling to her — she can't find a single flaw in them.

The group has split into two camps that I've watched talk past each other for a year. One camp says she has to give up the belief, or at least drop to something like fifty-fifty, because it's flatly incoherent to believe p while also believing that your own belief in p isn't supported by your evidence; a state like that can't be rational, and the fact that you'd be akratic is itself the reductio. The other camp says that's the wrong way round: the higher-order news is about the provenance of the belief, not about the reasons she actually has for the translation, and those reasons are untouched — so the correct state is to keep believing the rendering is right while also believing that she shouldn't, and if the correct epistemic rules issue conflicting recommendations at the two levels, that's a feature of a two-tiered picture, not a bug. Each side thinks the other's verdict is unthinkable, and I genuinely don't think either camp has produced an argument the other couldn't just absorb and re-run.

So my question is about the shape of the standoff, not about who's right in the case. My working hypothesis is that this isn't really a disagreement about what to do with higher-order evidence at all, but about what rationality is measuring — responding well to the reasons you have versus having a doxastic state that hangs together internally — with both camps using the same word for two different properties and each thinking it's found a contradiction in the other's view. Maybe there's also a scope thing going on, wide versus narrow requirements, and once you pin down the scope most of the heat disappears. Is that right, or is there a substantive disagreement underneath about what belief is for? Or is there a hidden formal question — the seven-in-ten base rate feels like it should be doable math, and one of my friends wants to settle the whole thing by simulating agents with noisy higher-order evidence and comparing the accuracy of the two policies, which I suspect smuggles in the answer as an assumption but I can't say how. Tell me what would actually count as progress here: a distinction that dissolves it, a formal or simulation result of a specific kind, a change of question, or a reason to expect that nobody should ever move and why. And please be careful to separate what people who work on this actually treat as settled from what's genuinely contested — I'd rather you flag which parts of your diagnosis are your own view than let me come away thinking one camp is the default position.
