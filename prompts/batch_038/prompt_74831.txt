I started a job about four months ago at a small nonprofit that does cross-species welfare measurement — basically we try to put a number on how much better or worse off one animal is than another, and a lot of our machinery runs on on a single hedonic scale. We take the intensity of a layer hen's experience in a battery cage and a person's experience of chronic back pain and we put them on one ruler. That's the whole method, and every headline number we publish inherits it.

Nobody on my team thinks this is philosophically innocent, but it came to a head last week when our new research lead, who has a philosophy background, said in a meeting that the ruler presupposes a theory of pleasure that's been under fire for well over a century and that we should be moving to a preference-based framework instead. Someone else said that was just wordplay and we should get on with it. I've been trying to work out who's right and gotten nowhere, partly because the reading is genuinely hard for me and partly because I now suspect the two of them aren't even disagreeing about the same thing.

Here's the shape of it as I understand it. Hedonism says wellbeing is pleasure minus pain, going back to Sidgwick and Bentham. Fine. But then someone asks: pleasures? which things are those exactly? A cold beer on a hot day, finally getting a lemma to work, the relief of scratching a bug bite, sex, hearing that your sister's biopsy was clear, the smell of rain after a drought. These don't feel like variations on one sensation the way red and orange feel like variations on colour. If you go introspecting for the single ingredient they all share you seem to come back empty-handed, and if there's no such ingredient then either hedonism's central term is empty or the common factor isn't a feel at all.

The answers I can find are roughly these. One: there is a common hedonic tone, and the heterogeneity argument illegitimately assumes that if a property is real you should be able to isolate it as a separate introspectable ingredient, when pleasantness might be a way the whole experience is rather than a component of it. Two: pleasure is a determinable with the specific feels as its determinates, so there's a genuinely shared property but not a shared quale. Three: enjoyment is really an attitude, not a sensation — Feldman's whole move to attitudinal hedonism, and Heathwood goes further and tries to reduce sensory pleasure itself to desire, so that enjoying a sensation just is wanting it to be occurring. That relocates the common factor out of phenomenology entirely. Four: pleasure is whatever plays a certain causal role — elicited by such-and-such, disposing you to sustain it, to attend to it, to report it as nice. Five, and this is the one I keep coming back to: the neuroscience has already settled it, because there look to be shared subcortical circuits doing the liking in wildly different experiences, in rats even, so the surface phenomenology being heterogeneous is beside the point. Crisp also seems to defend hedonism without demanding a single shared feel, if I've got him right.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Is it a verbal fight over what 'pleasure' is being made to do in the theory, with no fact of the matter about who wins? Is there a live empirical question buried in it — about introspection, or about those circuits — that could in principle get settled and settle the philosophy along with it? Or is the real split somewhere else entirely, like over how much a moral theory is obliged to say about the metaphysics of its own central term, with one side demanding an analysis the other thinks no theory of anything has ever had to provide? And does any of this touch my report, because if you made me bet I'd guess no, that we could keep using one ruler under all five views above — but I can't tell whether that's a considered judgment or just something I want to be true.
