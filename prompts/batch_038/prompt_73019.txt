I sit on the safety review panel of a mid-sized backcountry guiding company in the interior ranges, and next week I have to file a written recommendation in a case I cannot get a handle on. In March, one of our senior guides took a party of four clients up a line he had skied dozens of times. The morning hazard assessment, the overnight weather, and the results of the snowpack tests were, so far as the panel can reconstruct them, unremarkable and well inside the conditions our guides work in routinely. An avalanche released above the group; two clients were caught and one died. In January of that same season, a different guide in a different valley faced what the two of them agree was a comparable picture — similar forecast, similar test column, similar terrain — and turned his group around at the treeline. His clients were irritated, one demanded a refund, and nothing further came of it. The two guides are friends and have climbed together for years, and both insist, without any evident self-interest in the claim, that the calls were the same call.

Since the death, an outside consultant has drafted a review protocol the company is considering adopting. It has two parts. The first is outcome-blind review: each decision is scored strictly by expected harm given the information actually available to the guide at the time, with the actual outcome struck from the record. The second is what the consultant calls a risk dividend: guides accumulate credit for conservative calls and draw that credit down when they take a more committed one, so a decision is judged partly against the guide's accumulated history rather than standing on its own. The panel is split. One member says that under this protocol the January guide would score at least as badly as the March guide, and that this reveals something our current practice quietly hides; another says it is grotesque to hold a man formally accountable for bringing his clients home. A third observes that the field notes for both trips were written up afterward, so nobody actually knows what either guide knew that morning, and that scoring decisions against a reconstructed information state simply smuggles hindsight back in through a side door. The deceased client's family will receive a copy of my recommendation, and so will the guides' association.

I would like help working out what to recommend and on what basis. Specifically: can the two decisions genuinely be assessed alike, and if so, what would that assessment look like in a document that has to be read by people who have lost someone and by colleagues who work the same terrain? If they cannot be assessed alike, what is doing the work — the outcome itself, the family's standing to complain, the fact that our practices of blame serve purposes beyond scoring accuracy, or something further? I also want to know whether the risk dividend survives scrutiny, because it strikes me that it trades one unfairness for another: whether a decision counts as permissible would then depend on how quiet a guide's previous seasons happened to be, which is not obviously anything he is answerable for either. There is a fourth thing I would rather not raise at the meeting but need thought through. I made the same kind of call myself for eleven years and never had anyone hurt, and I cannot tell whether that entitles me to any confidence about my own judgment or merely to relief. Relatedly, the March guide tells me he does not believe he made a mistake and would make the same call again, but that he does not expect ever to feel differently about how it went, and he has asked whether that combination is something he is entitled to hold onto or whether the panel should read it as evidence that he has not grasped what he did. Please give me a reasoned position on each of these questions rather than a survey of the available positions; I can read a survey off the consultant's slides.
