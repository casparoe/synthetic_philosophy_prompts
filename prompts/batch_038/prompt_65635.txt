I am putting together the closing session of an intermediate game-theory seminar, and I would like to end it by distributing a poem rather than a summary — something the students can keep, which does in verse the work the last two lectures did at the board. The subject is iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, including the refinement that a strategy may be beaten not by any single rival but by a weighted combination of several, and that once the beaten strategies are removed, comparisons that were perfectly safe become unsafe.

Please write a poem of six sestets in unrhymed iambic pentameter, with the exception that the last two lines of each stanza rhyme as a couplet. The scene is yours to choose — a council, a household of rival claimants, a card table, a muster before a campaign — so long as it is an assembly from which members can be formally dismissed. Introduce all six members by name in the first stanza; stanzas two through five should each dismiss one of them, leaving two standing for the sixth. A reader who knows the material should be able to reconstruct the order of dismissals from the poem alone, and should be able to say, stanza by stanza, which of the following ideas the form is enacting:

- That dismissal requires the comparison to hold in every contingency — not on the whole, not in the likely case, but under every deal of the cards, in every weather, whoever sits where. This uniformity should be visible as an image, not asserted as a slogan.
- The turn of the poem: one member who is untouchable against each of the others taken singly, and who is nevertheless dismissed by two of them speaking together in a stated and unequal proportion. This is what the couplet is for. Its two lines must not read as two voices taking turns; they must read as a single voice compounded, with the weights given.
- A member who could not be dismissed in an early round and becomes dismissible in a later one for no reason except that somebody else has already left — the shrinking of the room changing what the word "every" ranges over.
- In the coda, that the names might have been called in any order at all and the same two would still be standing.

Two further requirements. First, the survivors' tenure should be shown to rest on a belief rather than on a fact: one line must concede that every dismissal in the poem is licensed only if all present are rational, and all know it, and know that all know it, and so downward. Second, the technical vocabulary is banned outright — "dominated," "dominant," "rationalizable," "equilibrium," "payoff," and "mixed" may not appear anywhere in the poem. The mechanism has to be carried entirely by the scene and the shape of the stanzas.

If you can find room in the coda for one further thought, I would welcome it: that the fixedness of the surviving pair depends on the standard having been strict, and that had dismissal required only that a member be no worse in every contingency and better in at least one, the order in which the names were called would have settled who stayed. Do not force it if it will not fit.

The poem should be accurate enough that a game theorist hearing it read aloud would not need to append a caveat, and indirect enough that the students do not identify it in the first stanza. I would much rather they recognize it than be told what it is.
