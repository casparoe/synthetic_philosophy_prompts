I run the fortnightly methods seminar in a health economics unit, and in three weeks I've got 75 minutes to teach the levelling-down objection to a room of people who do distributional cost-effectiveness analysis for a living. They're numerate, they've all fitted equity weights, and roughly half of them justify those weights out loud by saying we ought to care about narrowing health inequalities. What I want isn't the argument — I know it — it's a plan for how to teach it to them.

Lead with the bottom line in your very first sentence: the one thing this session either does or doesn't achieve, the hinge the whole thing turns on. Then the plan. No preamble, and don't repeat the question back at me.

What I need in it:

- the order the pieces go in, and why each has to precede the next. I'm fairly convinced you can't run the objection at all until they've got the distinction between weighting a benefit because of the person's absolute level and weighting it because of where they stand relative to others, since thats the distinction the whole thing trades on — tell me if I've got that backwards, and if something else has to come even earlier.
- the one example to build it on, and what it must have and must not have. Constraints from me: non-agential (nobody is choosing to take anything away, or they'll do harm-aversion instead of philosophy), fixed population, numbers small enough to hold in the head, and not income — money drags in desert and property intuitions that swamp the point. Health is fine, but say if there's a better currency.
- the misconceptions they'll bring or form on the spot, and the exact point in the sequence where each one gets headed off. My list so far: that the objection is a recommendation to make the well-off worse off; that if equality isn't intrinsically valuable their equity weights have just lost their justification; that "better in one respect but not better all things considered" is a fudge rather than a distinction; and — the one I care about most — that because their weighting functions take individual baseline severity as input and never the gap, they were prioritarians all along and the objection never touched them. Handle that last one properly: what's right about it, and what it costs them to concede. Keep the comparative claim seperate from the recommendation throughout.
- a check I can run in the final ten minutes that tells me whether it landed rather than whether they were being polite. Something with a fresh pair of distributions in it where the prioritarian and egalitarian verdicts come apart — and say what response would show they've got it and what response would show they haven't.
- what I should openly refuse to cover at this level, and say so out loud. I'd rather tell them I'm skipping things than have them think the map I drew is the territory.

British English throughout, please. And no reading list, no references, no titles — I'm not handing anything out and I don't want you guessing at them.
