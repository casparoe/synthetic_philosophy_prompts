Ok so I'm a fourth year PhD student and in about three weeks I'm giving a 15 minute talk (plus Q&A, thankfully short) at an alignment workshop colocated with one of the big ML conferences. My advisor thinks the framing is too cute and wants me to soften it, but I'd rather defend it properly, so I want to be ready. The thesis is basically: debate and weak-to-strong generalization are not rival engineering solutions to the same problem, and treating them as competing on a benchmark is a category mistake. What the debate framework actually delivers isn't a scalable oversight protocol, it's an account of what an overseer is *entitled* to believe when the thing being overseen is smarter than they are — it changes the meaning of "the judge got it right" rather than making the judge more competent. So the field's default metric, end-task accuracy of the judge after a debate, measures the wrong quantity.

The argument as I'll sketch it on three slides: (1) the PSPACE-vs-NP analogy in the original 2018 paper is doing conceptual work, not empirical work — the point is that adversarial help extends the judge's *authority* past what they could check directly without improving their *competence* at all, and that division of labor is the actual contribution; (2) every debate experiment anyone has run, including the original MNIST sparse-classifier one, has ground truth sitting right there next to the judge, so what's being measured is accuracy on questions whose answers are already known — which is exactly the case debate was invented for, and you can't fix that by making the tasks harder, you'd need tasks where the correct answer is contested or not yet knowable at eval time; (3) therefore the useful output is a taxonomy of the conditions under which the honest policy stops being equilibrium-optimal — cost asymmetries between defending a true claim and defending a false one, classes of questions not closed under the decompositions debaters will actually reach for — and *those* are the quantities worth instrumenting.
