I am assembling a comparative study of how monetary valuation is absorbed, resisted, and re-engineered in markets that people find repugnant. I would like to work past the familiar exchange between the market-design view of repugnance and the corruption view, and instead use a more mechanical idea drawn from economic sociology: that money does not arrive as a single homogeneous substance but in socially specified forms, and that whether a market in a contested good survives depends substantially on the form in which the price is paid.

To fix the apparatus, three elements matter. First, earmarking: a transfer can be made in general-purpose cash or in a restricted vehicle — vouchers, tax credits, non-transferable permits, segregated accounts, in-kind provision — and the vehicle determines what the money can contaminate and who can convert it into something else. Second, the direction and register of the flow: an identical sum carries a different meaning as compensation, reimbursement, honorarium, incentive, penalty, or outright price, and practices often migrate between these registers over time without changing their economics. Third, the persistence of special-purpose monies: the documented historical separation of a household's money into the wage, the wife's allowance and pin money, the child's chore money, charitable money, and dirty money shows that a single currency is routinely fractured into morally distinct sub-currencies. Related evidence includes the monetization of children's death-benefit claims in the American life-insurance industry, the conditional and unconditional cash-transfer programs of recent decades, and the field results in which paying blood donors or paying parents for on-time pickup reduced the very behavior the payment was meant to produce.

With that apparatus, please score each of the following on a scale from 0 to 100 for what I will call monetization durability. A score near 100 means the price form has become unremarkable and self-sustaining: the transaction is conducted at scale, without recurring public controversy, and without institutional backsliding. A score near 0 means the monetary form has been abandoned, prohibited, or persistently displaced by a non-monetary substitute in most places where it was tried, and remains politically radioactive.

1. Cash payment to subjects for participation in a Phase I clinical trial.
2. Compensated living kidney donation, including the one regulated national program that has operated for decades.
3. Commercial surrogacy under written contract.
4. Tradable permits priced per tonne of carbon dioxide.
5. Individually transferable catch quotas in commercial fisheries.
6. Cash transfers to poor households made conditional on school attendance and vaccination.
7. Payments in cash or vouchers to drug-dependent patients for verified abstinence.
8. Lump-sum payments to poor women for accepting long-acting contraception.
9. Paid queue-jumping: commercial line-standing services and premium fast-pass pricing at theme parks.
10. Paid plasma donation in jurisdictions where it is legal, running alongside voluntary unpaid whole-blood donation.
11. Corporate naming rights over public stadiums and, in a handful of cases, over public schools.
12. Performance bonuses for teachers tied to student standardized-test scores.
13. Paying citizens a flat sum for turning out to vote, explicitly not for how they vote.
14. Bounties paid per specimen for invasive species.

The deliverable should contain the following, in order:

(a) Before any scoring, state the three or four criteria you are actually applying, and say which of them you expect to do most of the work.

(b) A score and tier for each item, with two or three sentences locating it. The explanation must name the feature of the monetary form — not of the underlying good — that determines its position, or else say explicitly that in this case no such feature is available and the good is doing all the work.

(c) At least two pairs drawn from the list in which the intuitive ranking by moral repugnance and your ranking by monetization durability come apart. Explain what each divergence shows: whether the apparatus is capturing something the moral intuition misses, or whether the intuition is tracking a harm that the durability measure is simply blind to.

(d) At least one case in which durability is attributable almost entirely to a design choice about the vehicle. Describe the counterfactual concretely: what happens to the practice, and to its politics, if the vehicle is switched from restricted to general-purpose cash or the reverse.

(e) A second ranking of all fourteen items, this time by the degree to which you think a well-designed change in the monetary form could move the item at all, holding the underlying good fixed. Identify the items for which that number is essentially zero, and say what it is about those goods that makes the payment form irrelevant.

(f) Finally, state what evidence would persuade you that your scale is measuring something other than the age of the practice. Age and durability are obviously confounded here: the oldest arrangements on the list are also the ones that have had the most time to become unremarkable, and I want to know how you would break that apart.
