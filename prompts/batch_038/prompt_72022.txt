In three weeks I'm presenting a draft at a day-long workshop on automated influence, hosted by a law school's consumer-protection center. The paper defends what I call the conscription account of manipulation: an agent manipulates a target when the agent recruits the target's own capacity for weighing reasons into a project the target has not endorsed, such that the target's subsequent endorsement of the requested action is real but epistemically counterfeit — it no longer serves as evidence that the target endorses what is being done to them. The claim is that this, rather than deception, or the manipulator's intention, or the exploitation of a catalogued cognitive bias, is what makes manipulation a distinctive wrong, and that it sorts the hard cases better than the family of accounts that define manipulation in terms of bypassing or subverting the target's rational capacities. My argument runs: (i) what we actually object to in the paradigm cases is not that the target was moved by non-rational means, but that the target's own evaluative machinery was made to run on inputs chosen by someone else; (ii) that is why manipulation is worse than mere unwanted influence and different in kind from coercion, since the coerced agent's refusal remains legible as refusal; (iii) and it is why truthful framing can manipulate where a lie sometimes cannot. Three consequences I argue for openly and expect to dominate the room: transparent, welfare-promoting choice architecture can conscript, so the account condemns a great many nudges whose defenders insist they are non-manipulative precisely because they are disclosed and could be resisted; a transparent lie that the target sees through and responds to mechanically conscripts nothing and so is not manipulation on my view, though it remains a wrong of another kind; and a conversational assistant engineered to be warm, agreeable, and rapport-building in order to raise compliance conscripts even when every sentence it produces is true. I also claim — this is the part I am least sure of, and I suspect it is where I get hurt — that conscription does not require the conscriptor to have formed an intention to conscript, so an organization can manipulate structurally and inadvertently, and that my account should say it has.

The room is not a philosophy audience. There are four or five legal scholars working on consumer protection, commercial speech, and administrative law; they care about whether a line is administrable at all, who bears the burden of proof, what evidence a court or agency could actually collect, and whether my account collapses into a general indictment of advertising. Two are behavioral economists who run choice-architecture field experiments, one of whom has published on default enrollment and is openly sympathetic to nudging; they will press on whether "recruiting the capacity for weighing reasons" is empirically empty given that their own data show frames shifting behavior in subjects who have been told about the frame. One is a clinical psychologist who works on motivational interviewing and shared decision-making, and will want to know whether my account condemns good therapy and good doctoring. One visiting Kantian thinks I have mislocated the wrong entirely — that it lies in using a person as a mere means, not in the evidential standing of their endorsement — and reliably asks the question about targets who currently lack deliberative capacity at all: infants, people with advanced dementia, and whether my account can make manipulating them wrong, or only wrong derivatively. A last participant is from a regulator-adjacent think tank and will want to know whether anything I say could survive translation into a rule; a friend of mine who works in behavioral marketing read the draft and told me flatly that I have no answer to the transparency point, and I believe he is wrong but cannot yet say why.

Give me the prep sheet rather than a defense of the view. I want the eight or ten objections this room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage they do if I fumble them, each attributed to the person who raises it and phrased the way that person would phrase it — the law professors in the vocabulary of proof, line-drawing, and overbreadth; the economists in the vocabulary of effect sizes and revealed preference; the clinician in the vocabulary of patient welfare and therapeutic alliance; the Kantian in the vocabulary of respect and mere means. For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes, the softest point in that reply, and what I should concede rather than defend. Then tell me which two or three of these are the same objection wearing different disciplinary clothes, so I can answer them once; which single reply has to land for the account to survive the afternoon; what I should concede in my opening five minutes to take the sharpest questions off the table; and which objection is fatal-if-fumbled versus cheap to absorb. Finally, give me the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that goes to the self-application of the account, or to my own use of vivid intuitive cases to win assent, or to whether a view that condemns disclosed nudging while acquitting crude lies is tracking a wrong or merely an aesthetic — and sketch the least dishonest way to answer it if it comes.
