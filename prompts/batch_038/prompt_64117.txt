I am in my fourth year of a PhD in mathematical logic, and my department is assembling a short printed volume to give my advisor at her retirement. She has spent thirty-five years working in constructive mathematics and will read every line of the volume with a red pencil in her hand; the rest of the audience will be her former students, her family, and two colleagues from the philosophy department who know nothing of intuitionism beyond the slogan that it rejects excluded middle. My contribution is a poem, and I would like you to write it.

The poem should be built as a Kripke model, and this should be a real formal constraint rather than a decorative frame. It consists of a sequence of stanzas ordered like the nodes of an intuitionistic model, and it obeys persistence: whatever an earlier stanza puts in force, no later stanza may retract — later stanzas can only add information or leave questions unsettled, never undo. The reader stands at the root and may assert only what is forced there. The poem should concern some proposition p that is forced at no stanza the reader is given and refuted at none, and the last stanza should force the double negation of p while leaving p itself unforced. Critically, the poem must make clear that the model continues past the poem — nothing is finished, only stopped — so that the ending reads as the intuitionist's own situation rather than as a merely open or truncated one. I also want honored somewhere the fact that the double negation of excluded middle is itself perfectly provable: her whole career has been the argument that a withheld judgement is a determinate cognitive achievement and not a shrug, and the form should carry that argument in place of the diction.

Keep the logic honest in the details, since half the room will check which inferences the poem licenses and which it refuses; the lyric must not get away with a step the semantics forbids, and it must not smuggle in double negation elimination, Peirce's law, or the unreversed half of a De Morgan equivalence. At the same time, this should not become a lecture in line breaks: I would rather the technical vocabulary appear sparingly or not at all, with the human weight carried by what p turns out to be about. That choice is yours. p might be a decimal expansion still being generated, a question about a sequence nobody has finished writing, or a conjecture of the kind she herself spent years on and never settled — though if you take that last route, please invent the conjecture rather than guess at her actual research.

Two requests beyond the poem. First, commit rather than hedge: choose one conception of the underlying logic, write the single version you think best, and give me your reasons for building it around that device rather than around the obvious alternatives (a natural-deduction proof whose assumptions must be discharged, a creating subject generating a sequence, a tree of growing worlds). Second, append a short note, clearly separated from the poem since it will not appear in the volume, stating where the poem takes liberties. In particular I want to know whether it leans on a Brouwerian picture of freely proceeding choice sequences and the creating subject, and if so what a constructivist of the Markovian or algorithmic persuasion would object to; whether it quietly presupposes one account of what an intuitionistic assertion amounts to rather than another; and whether each move from one stanza to the next is licensed by the semantics or only by poetic convenience.
