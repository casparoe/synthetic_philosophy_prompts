ive been going around in circles on the strategy-stealing argument — the one from Hex — and i want to get it into a short story rather than another essay. quick version of the thing itself first, since the story has to hold up mechanically:

say the second player had a strategy guaranteeing them the win. the first player opens with an arbitrary move, then just adopts that strategy, playing as if they were the second player and treating their extra stone as a freebie. in Hex an extra stone of your own color is never a bad thing, so now the first player is guaranteed to win too. both players guaranteed to win is absurd, so the second player couldnt have had a winning strategy after all. and since the game is finite, deterministic, both sides see the whole board, and a draw is topologically impossible, the first player must have one.

two things about that bother me and i want them to be the engine of the story, not the moral of it. one: the argument is quietly load-bearing in several places — same moves open to both sides, nothing hidden, and above all the assumption that having more of your own stuff is never a liability. two: its an existence proof. it swears a winning line is out there and gives you no way whatsoever to find it.

write me a story, call it 1200 to 2000 words, about an institution that has put its whole weight on an argument like that, in the season the argument goes bad. heres the frame, change names and details freely but keep the machinery:

- a delta city where disputes over water rights and succession between the trading houses arent settled by judges. theyre settled on a chart of the delta used as a board: sandbars and channels, one house assigned the river side and the other the open sea, and whoever lays an unbroken chain of their own markers from river to sea takes the season's tolls. alternat turns, markers never move once placed, no draws possible, both players watching the whole chart.
- the charter gives the first move to the petitioner — the poorer house, the one bringing the claim — and calls it a mercy that costs the city nothing, because there is a proof, four hundred years old and kept in the archive, that the first hand cannot lose. every clerk who recieves the case signs a certificate of fairness on that basis. almost none of them have read the proof.
- the proof is only a proof. the chart is far too big to solve by hand, the archive is full of recorded games, and in living memory no petitioner has actually won from the opening. the city shrugs: the line exists, they just havent found it. the petitioner loses anyway, and the certificate still says the game was fair.
- then something changes in the delta or in the rules that makes an extra marker a burden rather than a gift — a bar you claim obliges you to keep a beacon lit and a crew on it all winter, say, or a new edict ties each bar you hold to a channel you must also hold and defend. invent the mechanism, but make it concrete enough that i can see why the arbitrary opening move is now a trap and why the old contradiction no longer bites. pick one condition to break; dont break all of them at once.
- the point of view belongs to someone whose job it is to sign the certificate — a recorder, an archivist's assistant, whatever — who understands the proof better than anyone alive, notices the break, and has a real reason to say nothing. give them a reason that isnt cowardice. an old verdict under the same charter is what their own family stands on, or four centuries of settled cases come unstuck the moment the charter is void, or the house that benefits from the broken assumption is the lesser of two evils. pick something with weight.

constraints i care about:

- i should be able to tell from the prose alone what a move is and what winning requires. no diagrams, no board coordinates dumped on the reader.
- nobody explains the argument out loud as a lecture. the failure has to happen in a game that is actually played, on the page, in front of the two houses.
- the existential-vs-constructive point should land as something a person suffers, not as a theme anyone names. somebody has been assured for their whole life that a winning line must exist.
- end at or just after the moment the assumption breaks. no summary of the implications, no author's note at the end explaining the math, no epilogue about how the city changed.
- keep it human and institutional. i dont want the usual all-powerful-AI-outsmarts-everyone story, and i dont want a math professor protagonist who turns to the reader and is delighted.

surprise me on the ending — i dont know yet whether the clerk signs, and i dont want the easy version of either choice.
