I spent thirty-four years doing stream ecology and a bit of seabird work before I retired, so animals aren't new to me, but the arguing-about-animals part very much is. I've been reading philosophy of biology for pleasure for about a year now and I'm still shaky on where a result stops and a slogan starts. Which is why I want a second pair of eyes on something.

In a collection of magazine essays I picked up at the airport there's a piece by a science writer about the peacock's tail, and it pivots halfway through to human generosity. The core of it goes like this:

'Zahavi's handicap principle says an animal only signals honestly when the signal is costly — costly enough that a weak or sickly individual simply cannot afford it. The peacocks tail is the textbook case: it is a heavy, conspicuous burden that wastes energy and advertises the bird to every predator for miles around, and precisely because it is wasteful it cannot be faked. That is why the female can trust it, and why she has come to prefer it: preferring a costly ornament is preferring good genes. When Zahavi first proposed this, biologists treated it as mystical nonsense, on the perfectly sensible grounds that natural selection cannot favor a trait that reduces survival; it took the mathematical models of the 1990s to prove that costliness by itself is what guarantees honesty at equilibrium. And the logic does not stop at birds. Gala dinners, diamond rings, cathedral-building, conspicuous generosity — these are peacock tails too, which is why no act of showy giving is ever really altruistic. The cost is the message.'

That last bit landed with me, honestly — I have a cynical streak and I half want to sign up for it. But the earlier part doesn't sit right with my field instincts, where I remember plenty of cheap calls and displays that everyone seemed to trust. So please go through it sentence by sentence and tell me: what would a behavioral ecologist nod along to, what is a fair popular simplification, and what is actually wrong? Is "costly therefore honest" a correct statement of the principle, or is it missing a condition that does the real work — and if so, what is that condition, in words I can repeat to my reading group? Are there honest signals that are cheap, and are there different ways a signal can be unfakeable that this piece is lumping together? Is the peacock case as settled as the essay implies? And finally, does the human conclusion survive once you fix the biology, or is it a separate argument that would need separate support — could a costly signal be honest about something like a genuinely prosocial disposition rather than a selfish one? Tell me what I should keep from the essay and what I should throw out.
