Two colleagues of mine spent most of an afternoon arguing after a lab meeting, and I ended up keeping score for no good reason. One of them, N., is a biostatistician on our team; the other, R., is a philosopher of science who consults on our grants and works mainly on causation. The trigger was a result our group had just replicated: children who lived through a two-year regional famine in the early 1960s show elevated rates of a metabolic condition in adulthood, after adjusting for a long list of covariates. N. wanted to write it up as showing that famine exposure caused the later condition. R. objected, and the objection grew legs.

(1) R. said the exposure cannot count as a cause on the interventionist account, because there is no possible intervention on it: no one can assign a cohort of children to famine rather than to non-famine, and no mechanism could vary that variable while leaving the rest of the world intact. On this view, R. said, causal talk presupposes some conceivable assignment mechanism, and here there isn't one — so the honest description is a robust association, not a cause. N. answered that this trades on a confusion between feasibility and possibility: the interventionist framework is explicitly about what would happen under interventions that may be impossible in practice, and that abstraction is exactly what lets the account cover historical, geological, and cosmological cases. If actual manipulability were required, N. said, nearly every causal claim R. personally endorses about climate and about deep time would be disqualified too.

(2) R. conceded the feasibility point but said it costs the account its content: an intervention that no mechanism could produce and no one could perform is a counterfactual in costume, and the framework collapses into Lewis-style counterfactual dependence with a do-operator attached. The supposedly distinctive idea — that causal structure is what underwrites manipulation — is then doing no explanatory work at all. N. replied that the collapse is too quick, because the interventionist has to specify which counterfactuals are relevant: an intervention must be surgical, severing the target variable from its own causes while holding the rest of the system fixed, and ordinary counterfactual dependence supplies no such constraint. R. answered that a surgical-intervention requirement is just a stipulated similarity metric — a rule for picking out the nearest worlds in which the cause is absent — which is the counterfactualist's own apparatus, so the two accounts differ in notation rather than in verdict.

(3) N. then claimed the accounts do diverge in verdict, and located the divergence in the mediation analysis. The paper reports that part of the famine effect runs through an intermediate variable (early-childhood growth faltering). R. said this is precisely where interventionism bites: one cannot intervene on a mediator while holding the exposure fixed, because the mediator is downstream of the exposure; any change in the mediator that leaves the exposure alone is non-surgical by construction, so mediation claims about a fixed historical exposure are not causal claims in the interventionist sense, merely decompositions of an association. N. said that is a reductio of the account rather than a defense of it — mediation analysis is standard practice on exactly these variables, and if a theory declares the whole literature non-causal, the theory is the thing that should be revised. R. said the practice may simply be mislabeled: a useful predictive decomposition need not be a causal one.

(4) R. finished with a complaint aimed at N.'s fallback. If N. retreats to counterfactual dependence, R. said, the account becomes promiscuous: if the famine caused the condition, then so did the failure of the relief shipments to arrive, so did the rainfall two years earlier, so did a foreign government's grain export policy — the counterfactualist's only remedy is to say some of these are salient, and salience is pragmatic. So both accounts, R. argued, make causal claims answer-relative, which dissolves the objectivity advantage philosophers claim for their side, and N. should therefore stop writing that the regression found a cause and write instead that it found a difference that matters for the question we asked. N. said answer-relativity is a virtue, not an embarrassment: the interventionist is explicit that a causal claim is relative to a chosen variable set, whereas the counterfactualist claims to be reading the world's structure off the facts and smuggles context in through the back door of similarity.

Neither of them moved an inch, and I now have to see both of them at the next meeting. Please adjudicate the exchange rather than summarize the two theories. Specifically: (a) rate each of the four numbered rounds from clearly wrong to clearly right for each participant, with a short justification of each rating; (b) say whether the charge in (2) and the charge in (4) are the same objection under two labels or genuinely distinct objections, and whether answering one answers the other; (c) determine whether the mediation dispute in (3) really separates the two accounts, or whether R. and N. are using "causal" in different registers and talking past each other — and if they are, say which register the write-up should adopt; (d) identify the strongest move either of them could have made but did not; and (e) say what both of them left out that would have settled the matter sooner, including anything about the famine case itself that makes it a poor choice of example for this argument.
