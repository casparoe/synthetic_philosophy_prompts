I am in the last stages of a comic novel whose narrator is a recently retired professor of formal epistemology, a woman who has spent four decades calibrating her degrees of belief and who cannot switch the habit off at home. In the scene I am stuck on, she is at a neighbour's dinner party, slightly drunk and determined to be convivial, and she attempts to win the table over by telling a joke about her own specialism. The joke is meant to be only half understood by the other guests and fully understood by any reader who knows the material; it should run to four or five sentences at most, and it should read as the sort of thing an academic tells about herself rather than about some hypothetical stranger in a bar.

Her specialism is van Fraassen's Reflection principle, from his 1984 paper Belief and the Will: roughly, the requirement that if I am confident my future self will hold some particular degree of belief in a proposition, then my present degree of belief in that proposition, conditional on that anticipation, ought to match it. What I want the humour to turn on is not the mere fact that Bayesians revise their beliefs in the light of evidence, nor the tired business of priors, but the specific predicament the principle creates. It obliges me to defer to a future self I may have every reason to distrust, because that self may be asleep, intoxicated, sedated, forgetful, newly converted, or in the grip of a midlife crisis; my character knows perfectly well what she will believe tomorrow, and Reflection apparently requires her to believe it now.

Please write the joke for her. It has to work without notation and without explanation, and the punchline must hinge on the content of the principle rather than on a generic portrait of a pedant. Better still if you can make it turn on the tension between the diachronic Dutch book that is supposed to vindicate the principle and those cases in which the agent knows her future self will have been impaired or will have forgotten what she now knows; but a lay reader should still be able to laugh at the surface of it, at a woman who is obliged to agree in advance with someone she has not yet become.
