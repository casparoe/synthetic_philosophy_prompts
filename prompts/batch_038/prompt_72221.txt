I have read aggregation arguments on the rationalist-adjacent forums for going on fifteen years, and the phrase that reliably ends them is "Condorcet proved it." A proposal has been circulating in a community I follow for replacing a small human review panel — the kind that adjudicates borderline policy-violation cases — with what the author calls an LLM jury: draw twenty-five samples from one model at temperature 0.8 against an identical prompt, take the majority label, and treat that as the verdict. The entire normative and statistical warrant for the design is a passage from a trade-press book on collective intelligence, which I will reproduce as nearly verbatim as I can:

> Condorcet's jury theorem, published in the 1780s, is the most underrated result in political philosophy. It says: if each member of a group is more likely than not to get a yes-or-no question right, and the members vote independently — which in practice means in secret, without talking to one another first — then the group's majority verdict is more likely to be correct than any individual member's judgment, and as the group grows the probability that the majority is right approaches certainty. Two consequences follow. First, enlarging a decision-making body can never make it less reliable, and it matters very little how ignorant the average member is, because individual errors are random and cancel out. Second, this settles the old quarrel between democrats and defenders of expertise: a large assembly of ordinary people beats a small panel of specialists, since the specialists' greater accuracy cannot compensate for the sheer number of independent votes. Condorcet also noticed that majority preferences can cycle — A over B, B over C, C over A — but that is a curiosity about preferences, whereas the jury theorem concerns judgments of fact, where there is a right answer to be tracked.

I suspect this is mostly right in outline and wrong in exactly the places that matter for the LLM-jury proposal, but I cannot say where, and I would rather not argue from a hunch on a forum thread. Please audit it. Go through it claim by claim and sort each into one of four bins: correct as stated; a fair simplification that loses something but misleads nobody; a conflation or an assumption smuggled in as a conclusion; or simply false. I am especially interested in three things I cannot resolve myself. First, the theorem compares the majority to what — the average voter's competence, or the best voter's? The passage slides between them. Second, what does independence actually require, and does casting votes in secret, or drawing them from a pseudo-random number generator, deliver it? Third, "errors cancel" sounds to me like an argument about averaging numerical estimates, not about majority voting on a binary question; is that intuition importable, and if not, why not.

Then tell me what survives for the proposal. Check my arithmetic first, because if I am wrong here the rest collapses: I compute that three jurors at p = 0.9 yield a majority reliability of about 0.97, while a thousand jurors at p = 0.51 yield about 0.74, which would make "adding voters never hurts" false even granting everything else the passage says. Then address the design itself: are twenty-five temperature-0.8 samples of one model twenty-five jurors, or one juror interrogated twenty-five times; what would the correlation structure have to look like for the majority to gain real reliability; does raising the temperature buy independence or merely noise; is a per-sample p even well defined for a question where the "right" label is fixed by a policy document rather than by the world; and is there a point past which more samples make the verdict worse rather than better?

Finally, close with the version of the passage as it ought to have been written — three or four sentences a careful author would stand behind — and flag the omissions, the assumptions the original never states that do load-bearing work.
