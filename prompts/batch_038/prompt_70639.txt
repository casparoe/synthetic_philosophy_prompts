A friend and I have spent most of two days on pragmatic encroachment and have arrived nowhere, so I would like an outside number rather than another opinion.

The proposition I want a credence in is this: it is possible for two subjects to have the same total evidence for a true proposition p, to be equally reliable with respect to p, and to stand in exactly the same safety and sensitivity relations to their belief that p, and yet for the one with comparatively little riding on being wrong to know p while the one with a great deal riding on being wrong does not. Practical stakes, that is, are capable of making an epistemic difference in a case where every non-practical determinant of knowledge has been held fixed by stipulation. I am deliberately framing it as a claim about possibility rather than about any particular intuition pump, so that no bank-queue or airport story has to be adjudicated: a single genuine such pair would make it true, and if no such pair is even coherently describable, it is false.

My friend's move, and the one I could not answer, is this. If stakes can encroach, they are often raised by facts the subject has no way of detecting from the inside. A colleague silently wagers a month's salary on whether the reagent in your hands is what the label says; the diagnosis you give will be acted on by a patient you never meet. On the encroachment view your epistemic standing changes when such facts obtain, yet nothing available to you changes, and you cannot determine even ideally whether you have crossed the threshold. He says this makes knowledge depend on features of the world that are in principle inaccessible to the knower, and that a position advertising itself as taking the agent's practical situation seriously should not end up rendering the agent's own epistemic position opaque to her. I said he had simply restated the skeptical problem from a new angle. We both dug in.

My credence in the proposition as stated is about 0.75; his is about 0.2.

Give me yours, but above all give me the decomposition. Break the question into the sub-propositions the answer turns on — for instance, whether the phenomenon can have any coherent home other than a contextualist semantics of knows, in which case my proposition fails for a merely semantic reason; whether a threshold view on which the evidential support required rises with the ratio of the cost of error to the gain of being right states a fact about knowledge rather than redescribing the practical rationality of belief; whether the intuitive pull of high-stakes cases survives controls that make a particular way of being wrong salient without raising what is at stake; whether encroachment can be combined with the knowledge norms of assertion and practical reasoning without incurring the opacity my friend presses; and whether some deflationary alternative, on which the high-stakes subject merely lacks confidence or the disposition to act and has lost nothing epistemic, absorbs the data. Add or drop terms as you judge right, assign each a rough probability with a sentence or two of justification, say explicitly how you combine them into the final number, and identify which term the total is most sensitive to — the one that, if it moved, would move everything else. Then tell me what argument or evidence would shift your number most, and on which of these sub-questions my friend and I most likely part company, given 0.75 and 0.2.

Finally, a request about your own confidence rather than the subject matter: say how sure you are of the decomposition itself, and single out the point in your answer at which you are least secure, whether or not it is one of the terms above.
