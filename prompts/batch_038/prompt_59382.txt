I sit on the education committee of a foundation that maintains a permanent exhibition on mass atrocity and runs in-service training for secondary-school teachers. Every year our teachers are asked some version of "was X worse than Y?", and our current house policy is to decline the comparison and explain why we decline it. That satisfies almost no one: students hear it as evasion, and several survivor communities have told us, with some justice, that refusing to rank is itself a way of maintaining a ranking by silence. Before we commission anyone to write a comparative framework for the exhibition and the curriculum, I want to know what we should be demanding of one, so that we can tell whether a proposal is any good.

So: write the desiderata — the requirements any adequate account of the comparative gravity of mass atrocities must meet in order to be usable by a body like ours. For each requirement, give the concrete case or consideration behind it, not an abstraction: tell me what specifically goes wrong in a comparison when that requirement is violated. And mark each one as non-negotiable or as a demand that really comes from one camp in these disputes. Please address at least the following, though I expect you will find others: the baseline problem (compared to what — the pre-event status quo, the alternative future the perpetrators claimed to be forestalling, or no counterfactual at all); the unit problem (deaths; deaths relative to the population at risk; years of life lost; the decades lived afterwards by survivors; the destruction of a language, a scholarly tradition, or the records that would let descendants know who they were); the relation between scale and severity, including whether the prolonged torture of one person can be weighed against the deaths of very many, and if so on what terms; the standing problem (whether an account can prohibit comparison by non-victims without prohibiting comparison altogether, given that nearly all of our teachers are non-victims); robustness under contested evidence, since for several cases we teach the scholarly estimates of the death toll differ by a factor of several and for one or two the event itself is publicly denied; and exploitability, meaning whether a ranking can be made action-guiding for prevention and for the allocation of scarce documentation effort without arming the "what about your side" deflection our teachers meet constantly.

Then the part I most want: identify any subset of these requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied. If you think our present refusal to compare is the only consistent option given those conflicts, say so plainly and say what it costs. Also flag requirements you believe are camp prejudices masquerading as desiderata — I have one colleague who insists any comparison must preserve the uniqueness of each case and another who insists it must terminate in a single ordered ranking so we can decide how many teaching hours each case gets, and I suspect both are smuggling something in. Finally, we have been advised to respect what is known about how people actually process large numbers: the flattening of response as figures grow, the pull of a single identifiable victim, the way a proportional framing outruns an absolute one. Tell me whether an account that is normatively correct but cognitively undeliverable by ordinary teachers and students counts as adequate for our purposes, and if not, how that constrains the rest of the list.

Hard limit of 1,100 words. Numbered requirements, each headed by the requirement stated in one sentence, followed by its motivating case and its negotiability tag; put the impossibility claims in a separate short section at the end. No bibliography and no names of scholars — I'll ask for those later if we go ahead with the commission.
