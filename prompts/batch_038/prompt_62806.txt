I am drafting the choice-theory chapter of a dissertation, and before I survey any more accounts of rationality over time, I want the target fixed. Write out the desiderata: what any adequate theory of diachronic rational choice — a theory that tells us what a single agent should do across a sequence of decision points, with new information and possibly shifting preferences along the way — has to deliver in order to count as adequate. The immediate use I have for the list is narrow but demanding: it has to let me adjudicate whether an agent who can be walked through a money pump is thereby irrational, or whether the defect lies in the situation rather than in the agent. So the list must be usable as a neutral specification, not as a disguised argument for one camp; wherever an item is not neutral, I want that said outright.

For each desideratum give me the case or consideration that motivates it, and then two labels: whether it is non-negotiable or a matter of taste, and whether it is genuinely a requirement on any such theory or is in fact a substantive commitment of one school wearing the clothes of a criterion. I expect the second category to be non-empty — my suspicion is that at least one of the things routinely demanded here (something about the separability of decision nodes, or about rationality not depending on an agent's foresight or self-control) belongs to it, and I would rather be told which and why than have the whole traditional list handed back to me.

The cases the list has to be able to handle, at minimum:

- The standard pump: cyclic preferences plus a series of small, cheap pairwise trades that return the agent to her starting point minus a payment. But also the variant where the cycle is not generated by indifference-with-arbitrary-tie-breaking but by options that are on a par, with small sums of money breaking the parity at each step — the agent who is pumped because each trade is a genuine small improvement, not because she is indifferent.
- The agent who, at every node, does what her preferences and her information then support, and who is nevertheless worse off at the end. Is that a failure of her, of the theory, or of nothing?
- The backward-looking agent who anticipates her own future choices and therefore accepts the pump as unavoidable given what she will do; and the agent who commits to a plan and holds to it at nodes where her then-current preferences rank otherwise. Both avoid the loss; must a theory count both as rational, and if it must choose, on what grounds?
- The pumped agent for whom the same situation never recurs, and the one for whom it recurs indefinitely. Does frequency matter to the verdict?
- Preference change that is endogenous: the agent whose ranking shifts because of the trades themselves. The theory must be able to distinguish a rational change of mind from an agent being steered.
- Self-binding in the Ulysses mold, and the case where the only way to avoid the pump is to take an action that is worse by the agent's own lights at the moment she takes it.
- Agents of bounded computational capacity, for whom the cycling pattern only becomes visible over long sequences.

Also tell me which object the norms are supposed to attach to — individual acts, whole plans, standing dispositions, or the preference ordering itself — and treat the choice as something an adequacy condition must justify rather than assume. And say whether the list must include a deliberative-availability requirement, that the agent be able to use the norms in deciding what to do, since a criterion satisfied only by beings who can see the whole tree at once may be no criterion at all for us.

The part I most want is structural. If some subset of these desiderata cannot be jointly satisfied, say so as a conclusion and argue for it; I suspect the combination of a no-sure-loss requirement, a requirement that each choice be assessable on its local merits, and a requirement that rationality not hinge on foresight or willpower is exactly such a subset, but I have not been able to determine whether that is a real result or an artifact of how the requirements are scoped, and I want your judgment on which. Then: which single desideratum does the most work in eliminating candidates, and is a genuinely camp-neutral list achievable at all, or is neutrality here a pretense?

Close with a short assessment of the main contenders — the account that makes dynamic consistency basic, the account that privileges sticking to adopted plans, the account that privileges backward-looking foresight, any account that treats the pump as showing nothing about the agent, and any account that locates the norms in dispositions rather than choices — scored against your list. Keep this part to a paragraph or two; the criteria are the deliverable and the scorecard is a check on whether they discriminate.

Do not hedge. Take a position on every item, give your reasons, and say plainly which of the standard demands you are rejecting and why; if you think the usual framing of this debate is itself confused at some point, flag that rather than working inside it. No bibliography, no citations, and no references to look up: describe views by their content, and name them only where a name is genuinely a label for a defined position.
