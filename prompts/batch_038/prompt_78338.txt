I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training at all, and I got roped into writing the eval section of our model card after two people on my team had a genuinely heated argument at a design review. Neither of them would let it go and now I have to decide what I'm allowed to claim, so I'd like you to referee it move by move.

Background: we built a model that predicts whether a pull request will introduce a bug. We trained on PRs from 2019–2023 and scored it on 2024 PRs, where we knew the real outcomes. It scored way better than the baseline. Then someone noticed that three of the ten eval repos were forks of repos in the training data, and that two of us had been looking at the 2024 outcomes while choosing features. Marcus says the 2024 number is now worthless as evidence. Elena says that's ridiculous. Here's roughly how it went:

1. Marcus: you can't be confirmed by something you already knew. Once you're certain of E, learning that your model implies E tells you nothing new — the probability of the hypothesis doesn't move. So the score is a stat, not evidence.
2. Elena: that proves too much. The classic case everybody agrees about is a theory of gravity that exactly explained a leftover wobble in a planet's orbit that astronomers had already known about for decades, and nobody says that wasn't a huge confirmation. Old evidence can obviously confirm.
3. Marcus: doesn't transfer. Those astronomers didn't have the theory sitting there while they were taking the measurements. We did. What matters isn't how old the data is, it's whether the hypothesis got built with the answers in hand. And there's a fix: keep a "shadow model" — a frozen snapshot of the feature set from before anyone looked at any 2024 outcome — and report the likelihood ratio of the shadow model against the null on the 2024 data. That number would mean something.
4. Elena: you're inventing a prior after the fact. You get to choose which snapshot to freeze, and you'll always find one that flatters you. And the shadow model isn't the model we ship, so even a beautiful ratio says nothing about the thing that goes to production.
5. Marcus: that's exactly what a held-out set is, and you love held-out sets. It's also exactly what pre-registration is in medicine — you freeze the hypothesis before the outcome comes in. Nobody calls pre-registration "picking a fake prior."
6. Elena: pre-registration works because the outcomes are genuinely unknown when you freeze. Your shadow model is being tested on outcomes you already know. It's a rerun of a race whose winner you've already seen. Hand me 2025 data that nobody has opened and I'll agree it's evidence.
7. Marcus: so your rule is "wait a year," which means we can never say anything about the current model at all. And you can't hold that, because in move 2 you cited an old-evidence case and called it confirmation. Elena: that case had a theory built without access to the outcome. Ours didn't.
