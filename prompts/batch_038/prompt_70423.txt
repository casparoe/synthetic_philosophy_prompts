I came at the laws-of-nature debate from the counterfactuals/decision-theory end, years of reading rationalist blogs, and I've accumulated a pile of gut verdicts that refuse to line up. I'd like to know what principle, if any, actually generates them. In rough order of confidence:

1. A single particle drifting forever through an otherwise empty universe: I'd say it obeys an inertia-type law, and that there are facts about what it *would* have done if shoved. Laws: yes.
2. A single particle in an otherwise empty universe tracing an utterly irregular zigzag: no laws, or only trivially disjunctive ones. But notice both worlds consist of exactly one trajectory, and in both worlds infinitely many candidate laws are consistent with the whole of history. I only want to say "law" about the first. I can't explain why.
3. No sphere of gold has ever been a mile across, and no sphere of U-235 ever will be. Both generalizations are true for all anyone has checked, both are universal, both are equally supported by the evidence. I say the uranium one is a law and the gold one is a coincidence about our manufacturing habits.
4. If exactly one event in cosmic history had gone differently — one rock floated upward for a second, once, with no downstream consequences — I still want to call that a violation of a law that otherwise holds. Laws can survive one-off violations.
5. But a candidate law with a clause like "except on the first Tuesday of the month within the city limits of Reykjavik" is not a law, it's a cheat. So exceptions are fine, and exceptions are not fine.
6. The laws would have been the same laws if the initial conditions had been somewhat different. Lawhood feels robust to the particular arrangement of stuff, which is weird if laws are just summaries of that arrangement.
7. I think I can know from my armchair that a perfectly symmetric setup stays perfectly symmetric, without having surveyed every symmetric setup that ever existed. Some access to nomic facts that isn't induction from the data.
8. Causation is real and non-reducible-ish: the window broke *because* the rock hit it. Yet I get squeamish about "the law made it happen." Governance smells like leftover theology to me.
9. A fair coin that has come up heads a thousand times running hasn't thereby changed the chance of the next toss. A coin with a chipped edge has. So there's something *in the coin*, a dispositional fact that isn't just the ledger of its tosses.
10. When I'm deciding whether to hit send on an email, "if I hit send, they reply" is an open counterfactual about *this* world, with these laws, not about some nearby world with different laws. My whole decision-theory hobby horse depends on this and I'm not giving it up.

The pair that genuinely breaks me is 4 versus 5. Second worst is 8 versus 10, since 10 looks like governance sneaking in through the back door while 8 waves it away at the front. And 2 is the one I'd be most embarrassed to defend out loud, but I can't shake it.

So: what's the principle? State it precisely enough that I could check it against a new case myself — not "it's a package of simplicity and strength," which I already know the Lewis-flavored best-systems line says and which feels to me like it just relabels the problem. Then tell me which of my ten verdicts it forces me to abandon, and which it keeps only by making the view baroque. And run it on the case I keep flip-flopping on: is the chance that *this particular* tritium atom decays in the next hour a fact about the world, or a compressed report of my own ignorance? Good days I say single-case propensity, bad days I say I just don't know the microstate. Pick one.

Please actually commit. I don't want a survey of four positions with a balanced pros-and-cons table and a shrug at the end — I want you to say which account you think is right, what it costs, and which of my intuitions you're cheerfully overriding. No bibliography or reading list, I'll match names to views myself. Inventing your own test cases is welcome and probably more useful than recycling the standard ones.
