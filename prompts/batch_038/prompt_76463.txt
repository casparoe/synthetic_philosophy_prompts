**Question 3.** In the article Keynes published in 1937 in reply to critics of *The General Theory*, he maintains that our expectations about the prospective yields of capital assets do not amount to a matter of probability calculation. Explain what he means by this and how it connects to the account of probability defended in *A Treatise on Probability*. Your answer should distinguish clearly among (a) the claim that a probability may not be numerically measurable, (b) the notion of the weight of an argument, and (c) the claim that agents fall back on convention. Finally, assess whether Keynes's position amounts to a repudiation of the probability calculus or to a claim about the limits of its application.

**Rubric.** 80–100: accurate on the interpretation, keeps the three notions cleanly apart, and discharges the final assessment with an argument of the student's own. 70–79: accurate on the main lines with some underdevelopment or one substantive slip. 60–69: competent reconstruction, but the interpretation is loose in places or the assessment is asserted rather than argued. 50–59: recognises the principal ideas while confusing or misattributing one or more of them. **Student's answer.**

(1) In *A Treatise on Probability* Keynes treats probability as a logical relation holding between two propositions — a body of evidence and a hypothesis — rather than as a frequency in a series of events or as a degree of belief located in someone's mind. On that view probability statements are objective: given the evidence, the probability of the hypothesis is whatever it is, and apprehending it is a matter of logical insight rather than of measurement. Two consequences matter here. First, not every probability is numerically measurable; where the principle of indifference cannot be used to divide the evidence into alternatives that are on a par, there is no number to report, and the absence of a number is not a defect in our bookkeeping. Second, some probabilities are not even comparable with one another. Keynes's example of the man deciding whether to carry an umbrella is meant to show that a high barometer together with black clouds can leave it an arbitrary matter whether he takes it, with no rational ordering between the courses of action.

(2) Weight is a second dimension and not a rival scale. The same probability value can rest on a great deal of relevant evidence or on very little, and an accession of evidence raises the weight of the argument whether or not it moves the probability — indeed it may lower the probability while raising the weight, because what grows is the total body of relevant evidence, favourable and unfavourable alike. Weight, like probability, is not in general a number.

(3) The claim of the 1937 article is not that we are ignorant about future yields in the way we are ignorant about the composition of an urn. It is that where the consequences of a decision stretch over decades there is no evidential basis from which a numerical expectation could be constructed in the first place, so we have no knowledge to work with rather than insufficient knowledge. To require the investor to average outcomes weighted by their likelihoods, as one might with a wheel or an annuity table, is to require something that does not exist in this domain.

(4) Since the state of long-term expectation is governed by sentiment, and since a wave of pessimism can destroy the inducement to invest overnight, the probability relations attaching to those investment prospects are themselves altered when confidence collapses: what counted as probable in the boom comes to be improbable in the slump. This is why Keynes says the market operates by convention — the assumption that things will go on much as they have unless there is a specific reason to expect a change — and a convention holds only for as long as it is undisturbed.

(5) This is the distinction Knight drew in the same year between measurable risk and unmeasurable uncertainty, and what Keynes added was the logic Knight lacked. On both accounts uncertainty means that a probability is there to be had but cannot be pinned down by the agent: the distant yield of an enterprise has a determinate probability which our evidence does not license us to estimate. Contemporary decision theory models the situation exactly, by replacing a single prior with a set of them. Keynesian uncertainty is ambiguity, and his weight corresponds to the spread of the set.

(6) The 1937 article also shows Keynes departing from the *Treatise*. Ramsey's criticisms persuaded him that probability talk is best cashed out in terms of partial belief, and the later essay describes long-term expectation as a psychological matter — a state of confidence characteristic of a group of agents at a time. Once expectations are described that way, the objectivity of the probability relation falls away, and what remains is the observation that in economic life our confidence outruns the evidence supporting it. Animal spirits, in the companion discussion of the same material in *The General Theory* itself, name the residue left when calculation gives out and disposition takes over; they mark the point at which the logic of the *Treatise* is abandoned rather than applied.

(7) So Keynes repudiates nothing in the calculus. Its axioms remain sound wherever they apply — games of chance, life tables, well-defined statistical series — but decisions about the distant future do not fall within that range, and treating them as though they did is a mistake of category, not of arithmetic.
