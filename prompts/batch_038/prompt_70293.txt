Okay so I've been going around in circles on something and I want someone to lay out the philosophy underneath it instead of arguing policy with me.

Where I live, if your kid skips school the district sends a letter, then a truancy officer, and eventually you can wind up in court — the state treats one missing school day as a harm serious enough to justify knocking on your door. But if you pull that same kid out and homeschool, in plenty of states you dont have to notify anyone, you need no teaching credential, you don't file a curriculm, and nobody ever administers a test. You have satisfied the compulsory attendance law completely and the truancy machinery just... stops. Meanwhile if the family later wants the kid back in the public system, the district will happily test them and may place them a year or two behind — so there IS a way to check, the state just doesn't bother unless you ask.

What does that combination presupose? I genuinely can't tell whether the state thinks education is something parents own, or whether it thinks attendance and custody are what actually matter and "learning" is just the story we tell about it, or whether it has no view at all and this is an interest-group patchwork that happens to look like a theory. Reconstruct the implicit view as charitably as you can, then separate what the rules strictly commit us to from what merely fits them — I want to know where the practice is underdetermined, where several different theories would license exactly the same setup. And if you swapped in a view where the kid rather than the parent is the one holding the claim, which specific rules would have to change and which could stay as they are?
