Doing a third-year minds-and-machines module this term and we've just had two weeks on whether trained neural nets literally have representations, and honestly the reading left me more confused than when I started. The framing our tutor keeps pushing — and it's in the intro chapter on the list too — is that reverse-engineering a transformer is like disassembling a compiled executable. The original source code is gone, nobody can hand it back to you, but the algorithm is still sitting there in the machine code, so in principle a patient person with the right tools can recover it; on that picture, when interpretability people say they've found a feature — some direction in activation space that tracks whether a position is winning in a game, or whether the text is about a particular country — they're reporting something that was objectively in the weights all along, in the same way a recovered subroutine is objectively in the binary. He uses this to argue for a pretty strong realism: representations aren't in the eye of the beholder, they're discoverable facts, and the whole business is just engineering effort, not philosophy.

Our seminar had a postgrad sitting in who thought this was rubbish, and he offered a rival analogy: it's more like a neuroscientist's brain atlas, or even like a Rorschach blot, because activation space has thousands of dimensions and if you bring a category with you and hunt for a linear separator, you'll find one — so what you've "discovered" is mostly a property of your decoding choices and your vocabulary, not of the model. He also said the compiled-binary story smuggles in an author: real machine code was written by people who shared our concepts and then compiled, whereas the weights came out of a search process that had no reason whatsoever to produce anything modular or human-legible, and in superposition one direction can be doing bits of hundreds of different features at once, which has no analogue in a binary where a byte at an address is just a byte. My tutor's reply, which I found pretty compelling at the time but can't reconstruct now, was that we can intervene: you patch or ablate a direction, or clamp it, and behaviour changes in a specific predictable way, and that's precisely what you can't do with an inkblot — causal purchase is what marks the difference between a real representation and a just-so story.

Can you actually audit both analogies rather than just picking a side? I'd like a proper correspondence table for each — what plays the executable and the machine code and the disassembler in one, what plays the atlas or the blot and the interpreter's categories in the other — and then a separation of the disanalogies that genuinely bite for the realism question from the ones that are just surface complaints. In particular: does the compiled-code framing quietly import an authorial intention that isn't there, and does the Rorschach framing quietly assume that any separable direction is equally arbitrary, when only some of them are load-bearing for what the model actually does? And does the intervention reply rescue the binary picture, or can causal tractability come apart from representational reality — is it possible for a direction to be causally potent and still not be a representation the model has, or the other way round, representation-ish but causally inert? Finally, my own hunch, which I want you to stress-test because I'm probably going to build an essay around it: maybe the better analogy is a map projection. Mercator is wrong about relative sizes but right about angles, there's no fact about whether Greenland "really" is that big on the map, but there absolutely is a fact about which distortions a given projection has and how they trade off — so maybe asking whether a representation is real is really asking which distortions your decoding method has, and whether they're benign for the question you're asking. Does that do better than both of theirs, or does it just relocate the problem? If you think none of the three holds up, tell me what analogy would actually carry the weight instead.
