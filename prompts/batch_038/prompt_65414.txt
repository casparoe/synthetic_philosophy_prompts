For thirty-five years I worked in a national metrology laboratory, mostly on gamma-ray spectrometry and the calibration of detector efficiency, and since retiring I have taken up the philosophy of physics with more seriousness than my former colleagues think advisable. In six weeks I give a forty-minute paper with a long question period at a university philosophy department colloquium. I would like you to prepare me for that question period.

The thesis I am defending concerns the so-called no alternatives argument in the literature on non-empirical theory assessment — the inference, associated especially with Richard Dawid and with the Bayesian reconstruction of it by Dawid, Hartmann and Sprenger, from a community's failure to produce a rival theory to raised credence in the theory it has. My claim is twofold. First, that the premise this inference needs is not a sociological confession about the limits of physicists' imagination, as its critics routinely treat it, but a report about the outcome of a search, and that search outcomes are data. Second, and this is the load-bearing novelty I want you to attack hardest, that such a search can be treated as a counting experiment with a calibratable efficiency. My sketch: fix a search space of candidate rivals delimited by the same constraints that make the incumbent attractive; estimate the community's detection efficiency for a rival in that space not by introspection but by replay, using historical episodes in which a rival was eventually found, to establish how much of the space had been covered at the time of discovery, in the way one injects known sources to calibrate an instrument; then convert the null result into a bound on the number of unconceived rivals, which enters the Bayesian analysis as a constraint on the prior over how many alternatives exist rather than as a substitute for it. If that works, the label non-empirical is a misnomer: the evidence is empirical, only about a community rather than about the world, and the interesting question becomes how tightly the two are coupled.

The audience is a philosophy of science colloquium: a majority of formally trained epistemologists who work comfortably inside Bayesian frameworks and will have no quarrel with updating as such; two historians of nineteenth- and twentieth-century physics who think in episodes and precedents; one sociologist of scientific knowledge who studies consensus formation and will regard my search space as itself a social product; and a visiting string theorist, friendly, who suspects philosophers misdescribe his field. Nobody in the room has ever calibrated an instrument.

Draft me a preparation sheet. Give me the nine or so objections I should actually expect from this particular audience, ordered by how much damage each would do to the talk if I answered it badly. Phrase each in the register and vocabulary the subgroup raising it would use — the formal people will press on priors, likelihood ratios, reference classes and the individuation of theory space; the historians will produce episodes; the sociologist will talk about how a research programme constitutes its own field of possibilities; the theorist will tell me that nobody in his discipline argues this way. For each objection give a reply I can deliver in two minutes aloud, and a candid note on where that reply is soft. Then tell me which of the objections depend on my replay method for estimating efficiency being feasible at all, since I suspect that plank is the weakest and I want to know how much of the talk falls with it. State what I should concede in my opening five minutes to disarm the room cheaply, identify the single reply that must land for the paper to be judged a success, and finally name the question I am dreading — the one I hope nobody asks — together with the most honest answer available to me. I do not want a reading list or citations; I want the argumentative content, and I will chase the sources myself afterwards.
