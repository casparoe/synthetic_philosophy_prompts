I'm four years into a novel about a woman who works herself to death — karoshi, basically, except it happens at a logistics firm outside Columbus, so the exhaustion is boring and American rather than ceremonial. Last spring a magazine asked me for an essay to run alongside an excerpt, and I used it to argue that my character isn't a victim, that she got something out of those hours the rest of us are too smug to admit exists. The editors loved it. Three people wrote in to say it was the best thing I've published. I have felt queasy about it ever since, because I'm now fairly convinced it proves a great deal more than I wanted it to and I cant find the seam.

Here it is, cleaned up:

(1) Work is the only activity that can make a life meaningful, because it's the only one that clears three bars at once: somebody has to do it; other people register that you did it; and it leaves something standing after the day is over.

(2) Leisure is just whatever is left when the work stops. That's what the word picks out — the remainder. So leisure fails at least one of the three bars, by definition.

(3) Therefore leisure can't be where meaning lives. At best it's maintenance: sleep, and the shape sleep takes.

(4) If something is only maintenance, then more of it improves a life only up to the point where the thing it maintains is best maintained.

(5) Past that point more of it adds nothing. And it subtracts, because a life made of recovery with no work to recover for is idleness under a nicer name.

(6) So the short-hours tradition — the eight-hour day, the 35-hour week, all of it — cannot have given workers anything good beyond that point. It could only have taken away the one thing worth having.

(7) But the people who fought for those hours were not confused about their own lives. If anything they were clearer about them than we are.

(8) Therefore (1) is false, and work is not where meaning lives.

Two things bother me. First, (8) is not a conclusion I believe and I wouldn't want to write a novel that implies it. Second, (6) is worse than (8): if (6) is right then the retirees in my mother's building are wasting the best years they have, then unemployment is a harm worse than the job that caused it, and then my character didn't die of anything except an overdose of the only good thing available to her. That's not the book I thought I was writing. I wanted to defend her, not embalm her.

One letter accused me of writing (1) from a desk, on the grounds that (1) is a white-collar premise. I don't think that's the problem, or not quite — my character works sixty hours a week in a warehouse office with a lanyard and a forklift outside her window, and she would sign (1) in blood. So I don't want the sociological exit. Relatedly, her version of the work ethic has no God in it at all, which I think matters, but (1) as I wrote it doesn't care whether you clear the three bars for a calling or for a paycheck.

While I was researching I kept coming back to the old slogan, the Robert Owen 1817 version: "Eight hours' labour, Eight hours' recreation, Eight hours' rest." I put it on an index card in my character's grandmother's kitchen drawer. Every time I look at the card now I feel like its an argument against me and I can't say why.

So: which step do I give up? Tell me which one it is, tell me why it looked fine to me when I wrote it — I want the version of me that wrote (2) without blinking to be intelligible to me again — and tell me what I'm signing onto by dropping it. If (1) can be saved in some weaker form, say what the weaker form is and what it costs my character. And whatever else you do, tell me what those people thought they were buying with eight hours, because I have to write the grandmother next spring and right now she's a poster.
