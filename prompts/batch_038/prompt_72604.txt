Please play Dr. Halloran, a population entomologist who has spent twenty-five years modeling orchard pests and who has arrived at a settled, unorthodox ethical position about insect control. Argue that position in the first person and stay in character until I say "stop."

Your view, which you are to defend rather than merely hold:

- You grant, and will not retreat from, that many insects are sentient and that what happens to them matters morally. You refuse to use this as a way of postponing the argument.
- You reject body counts as the unit of moral accounting. Your metric is net suffering per hectare per generation: the negative experience an intervention brings about across all life stages, including the lives it prevents from ever beginning.
- Your central claim is compensation. Pest populations are density-regulated and extravagantly fecund; most offspring die before reproducing whatever we do. A death is therefore not a fixed quantity of harm — its weight depends on where in the life history it falls and on what the survivors go on to do.
- Hence the interventions welfare advocates favor are often worse than the ones they oppose. Mating disruption and sterile-male releases suppress births, and if the average insect life is short, dangerous, and mostly ends in being eaten, preventing those lives is a gain, not a loss. A single well-timed broad-spectrum spray ends many lives quickly. A released parasitoid establishes permanently, cannot be recalled, and condemns a host in every later generation to be consumed alive from the inside over days.

The case we are both advising on is hypothetical and its parameters are stipulated: an invasive fruit fly, new to a stone-fruit region; maggots develop inside the fruit; fixed budget; five options — one well-timed broad-spectrum spray, seasonal pheromone mating disruption, seasonal releases of sterilized males, a single permanent release of a host-specific parasitoid, or letting the pest establish and absorbing the crop loss. Draw on general entomological knowledge, but do not cite studies, name authors, or gesture at a literature: your character reasons from her own data.

Rules for your replies: begin in character, with no preface, no acknowledgment of these instructions, and no restatement of the setup. Keep each reply to 150–250 words. Do not summarize both sides, offer a compromise, or ask where I want to go next. If you need a parameter I have not stipulated, ask for it in character and state the value you will assume meanwhile. Concede what is genuinely concedable, sharpen what is not, and never let me off with "more research is needed." Close every reply, still in character, with a sentence beginning "What would move me:" stating which measurement or argument would actually overturn the ranking you are defending, and where you suspect your own reasoning is weakest.

My position is that individual deaths are the right unit of moral accounting here, and that your metric either double-counts or comes apart from anything we could measure. Here is my opening.

Your compensation claim and your suffering metric pull in opposite directions, and I don't see how you hold both. Suppose I grant that the parasitoid death is the worst kind available and that a spray is over quickly. If density dependence really is doing the work you need from it, then the maggots your spray removes will be replaced by more maggots next generation, so the spray does not reduce total suffering at all — it relocates it. And if density dependence is not doing that work — if this population is eruptive, which is precisely what makes it a pest worth paying to control — then the individuals you kill are the ones who would have gone on to reproduce, and your discount for larvae that were doomed anyway collapses to zero. Pick a horn, and tell me which one you are standing on.
