My two siblings and I have to reach a joint decision about our brother within the next month, and I would like to think the reasoning through properly rather than settle it by whoever argues longest.

The facts: four years ago our brother diverted roughly ninety thousand dollars out of the family company — the bakery our parents built and the four of us now own together — into an account the rest of us did not know existed. He kept it up for close to a year. It emerged afterward that he had been in an untreated manic episode for most of that period, sleeping two or three hours a night. For the past eighteen months he has been medicated and stable, has handed back what he could, and has done his job without incident. I want to be precise about one thing, because I think it matters: the man who took the money was grandiose and could not be reached by argument at all — nothing anyone said to him changed anything, and you could see that it wasn't going to. The man across the table from us now listens, concedes points, and revises his own plans when he is given reasons.

The options before us are roughly these: report the matter to the police, since it was company money and our parents' estate is entangled in the accounts; require him to leave the business and transfer his shares, with a repayment schedule; keep him in the business under monitoring with a full repayment plan; or close the matter with a formal acknowledgment and no financial consequence beyond what he has already returned.

My sister thinks the question of what he has coming to him is not a real question. Her argument, as I understand it: he did not choose the illness, and he did not choose the particular way the illness, the sleeplessness, and that year's pressures added up to someone who would do this; if you traced the chain back honestly you would never arrive at a point where he stood outside it and selected. So on her view there is nothing left over that could be called having earned it. What remains is a practical question — which arrangement keeps the company solvent, the estate intact, and him well. She does allow that if the honest answer is that he still presents a risk, we are entitled to constrain him, but only so far as the risk actually requires, and not so far as our anger would prefer.

My other brother takes something close to the opposite line. He says that once we start explaining away each act as soon as it can be explained, we stop being a family that asks anything of anybody and a company whose agreements mean anything; that managing our brother like weather instead of answering to him like a person is a demotion he would experience as an insult; and that our brother himself keeps pressing for the repayment plan, not as a price but as his way of standing behind what he did.

What I cannot resolve is that both of them appear to be working from the identical facts — the episode, the recovery, the changed man — and arriving at incompatible conclusions, and I cannot tell whether they are disagreeing about what he is owed, about whether owing is even the right frame, or about something neither of them has quite said. There is also a narrower puzzle I keep circling: the person who took the money seems, in every practical sense, no longer here, and the person who is here is the one who would have to answer for it. Is that a reason to be gentle, or is it precisely what makes holding him to account sensible rather than arbitrary? What should we decide, and on what grounds?
