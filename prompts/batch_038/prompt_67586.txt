My flatmate is halfway through a philosophy masters and for about three weeks now our kitchen has been held hostage by one question: could I have come from a different sperm and a different egg? I keep saying obviously yes, some other pair of gametes would have made a slightly different but still me-ish person. He keeps saying no, that's not a slightly different you, that's somebody else entirely, and if you want to talk about what would have happened to them you need the whole counterpart apparatus. Then he plays identical twins like it's a winning card and I point out that a sperm is mostly water and the molecules get shuffled about anyway, and we go round again. I should of conceded somewhere around day four but I haven't, so instead I've decided to settle it in the dullest way imaginable: by turning his thesis into paperwork.

What I want is a claimant-group notice — the sort of thing that arrives from a firm of solicitors telling you that you appear to be a member of a group and that you should read it carefully, in that dead, courteous, faintly threatening register. Invent the firm, invent the clinic, invent the case name; the story is that a storage mix-up decades ago meant some people were conceived from gametes that weren't quite the ones on the paperwork, and the firm has so thoroughly absorbed the view that where you come from is essential to who you are that it can't stop applying it, with the result that the notice slowly dismantles its own clients' position from the inside. Around 800 words, numbered clauses, plus a short FAQ at the end. None of the parties should be real and nothing should be quoted from an actual document.

The humour has to come from the doctrine being applied correctly rather than from lawyers being sharks, so please build in the specific pressure points: how the group is defined, given that definition by origin is the one thing that makes a group impossible to close or count; the clinic's defence that any alternative procedure would have produced a different child rather than a worse version of you, and what the firm says to clients who want to claim for the life they would otherwise have had; a de minimis clause setting some tolerance for molecular substitution in a gamete before it stops being the same origin, ideally expressed in a unit nobody has any feel for; the twin problem, since one origin apparently produced two people and somebody has to own it; a clause on mitochondrial donation and whether swapping the mitochondria moves the origin or not; and the firm's policy on counterparts, including whether it will act for one. The FAQ should contain the questions a real claimant would actually ring up about — the egg was right but the sperm was wrong, my identical twin didn't get a letter, is the donor a party, what happens if I do nothing — answered with total administrative calm. Don't state any of the philosophical objections outright and don't name the technical terms in the body; the piece should stay deadpan enough that a reader can tell what's being ironised without anyone winking, and funny to someone who's never heard any of this.

Two last things. Write it in British English, and at the end add a short note outside the fiction saying where you've taken liberties — which clauses depend on a contested reading of the necessity-of-origin idea, and which objection a real metaphysician would press first if they read the notice as a serious statement of the view. That's the part I actually want for the argument with my flatmate, so tell me honestly where the piece is cheating.
