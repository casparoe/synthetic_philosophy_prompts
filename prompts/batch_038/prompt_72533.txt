I work on trust & safety for a mid-size platform, no philosophy background, so bear with me. Our published Community Guidelines are supposed to be the standard, and we tell users they'll be applied the same way to everyone. But three things we actually do bug me:

1. Posts that technically comply but are obviously doing what the guidelines were written to stop get removed anyway, under a "spirit of the rules" clause we publish right next to the rules.
2. Every quarter we revise the guidelines, then re-review older content against the new version and take down posts that were perfectly fine when they were written.
3. Appeals only check whether the moderator applied the guideline correctly, never whether the guideline is any good; "your rule is unjust" gets a form reply pointing at the policy page. Meanwhile we have a whole team whose job is drafting this stuff and execs call it "our law," but we also say its not law, we're just a company.

What does this setup assume about what a rule even is? It feels like we're committed to two incompatible things at once — that the rules are just the text we posted, and that there's a right answer past the text that moderators are somehow tracking. Is that an actual contradiction, or can both hold together? Which of the three strains things the most, and what would we have to change in the product (not just the docs) to make it coherent? And is #3 treating "is this a rule" and "is this a good rule" as fully separable questions — is that separable in practice, or is it a dodge?
