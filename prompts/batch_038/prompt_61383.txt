ok so my friend and I spent basically the whole evening yesterday arguing about sex work law and I want a third opinion, because we kept talking past each other and I think the disagreement isn't where either of us thought it was.

Here's the three regimes we were comparing, roughly as I understand them:

Sweden (1999, and copied in Norway, Iceland, France, Ireland, Canada) criminalises the buyer and not the seller. The offence sits in the chapter of the penal code dealing with sexual crimes — the same chapter as rape. Consent is not a defence to the purchase charge; there's no version of "but they were enthusiastically into it" that gets you off. Meanwhile the seller is officially framed as someone to be supported and exited, not punished.

New Zealand (2003) decriminalised it and treats it as an occupation: contracts for commercial sexual services aren't void, so an unpaid fee is a claimable debt, and health and safety law applies to brothels. But the same statute says a worker may refuse to provide, or to continue to provide, services at any time, and that this can't be contracted away. And none of the framework applies to people on temporary visas — for migrant sex workers it's still illegal and still a deportation matter.

Germany went the other way: since 2002 the payment claim is legally enforceable and the old immorality barrier was removed, then in 2017 it added mandatory registration with the authorities, a health-counselling certificate you have to keep current, a legal condom requirement, and licensing for premises.

My friend's take is that all three are just making different empirical bets about how much harm there is and where it sits, and that the philosophy is window dressing. I don't buy that. I think they disagree about something much more basic and the details give it away. So: reconstruct what each regime actually presupposes — about consent, about agency and capacity, about what the body is and whether it can be hired, about harm, and about what the state is for when it legislates (preventing bad outcomes vs. saying something about who we are).

A few things I specifically want you to deal with:

- The Swedish asymmetry. When the law bans a consensual transaction it normally gets both sides — selling a vote, selling a kidney, selling drugs. The main place criminal law punishes one party and exempts the other is where the exempted party has some status incapacity, like a minor in statutory rape. So what does the buyer ban commit Sweden to about the seller's capacity, and how is that supposed to sit with insisting she's a full agent who is being wronged rather than someone under a disability? And what does it mean that consent is the whole dividing line in the rape provisions and legally irrelevant three sections later, in the same chapter?

- New Zealand says it's just work, then removes the one feature that makes an employment contract an employment contract — you can walk away mid-service with no liability. Is that a contradiction, or is it a coherent third view? If it's coherent, what's the theory that licenses the carve-out, and does that theory quietly concede something to the Swedes?

- The migration exclusion. If the argument for decriminalisation is that criminalisation is what produces the violence and the inability to go to the police, then leaving the most precarious workers criminalised looks self-refuting. What premise would make it consistent — something about membership, or about the political community's self-image rather than harm?

- Germany's registration and condom rules. Mandatory state registration exists for no other lawful occupation I can think of. What view of the worker does that require — full contractor, public health vector, both at once?

Then tell me for each one what it strictly presupposes versus what it merely fits comfortably with, since I suspect a lot of this is underdetermined and several rival theories could license the same text. Point out where a regime is a patchwork of theories that don't go together. And say concretely what would have to change in each legal system if you swapped the presupposed view for its main rival — e.g. if Sweden actually embraced the incapacity reading, or if it admitted the ban is purely expressive and should be judged on attitudes rather than on the amount of prostitution.

Finally: who won the argument, me or my friend?
