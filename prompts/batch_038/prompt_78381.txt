I write a column on science, medicine, and the economics of ordinary life, and the next one is about where the line falls around markets people find repugnant. Before I draft it, I want to know what I actually believe, and I would rather find out by being argued with than by rereading my own notes. So: take my verdicts on a batch of cases, tell me what principle they add up to if there is one, and tell me which of them I have to abandon.

I have been carrying around a two-clause test of my own that the cases below keep bruising. Call it the self-undermining test: a market in some good G is repugnant, and properly restricted, when either (i) completing the sale damages or destroys the capacity, relationship, or standing that made G worth having in the first place, so that the transaction consumes whatever gave it value; or (ii) the seller's willingness to sell is produced by a deprivation the buyer is positioned to relieve and instead exploits, so that her consent measures her lack of options rather than her preferences. I do not think harm to third parties is what does the work here, and I do not think disgust as such is either.

My verdicts:

1. A market in kidneys from living sellers, at a fair price with full disclosure and after waiting periods: repugnant, and I would restrict it.
2. A person contracting now, in good health and for a modest sum paid to her children, to have a kidney taken after her death: not repugnant. I cannot say why this differs from case 1 beyond the fact that the seller will not be around to be degraded by it.
3. A wealthy patient paying to move up the transplant waiting list: repugnant. A wealthy patient paying a hospital for a faster and more thorough workup that makes her eligible sooner: not repugnant, though the effect on everyone else's wait is the same.
4. Paying a surrogate a fixed fee: acceptable. Paying her a bonus conditional on her not forming an attachment to the child, or on relinquishing the child without contest: repugnant, and more repugnant than the fixed fee, though the money buys the same service.
5. Paying a person a few hundred dollars to accept sterilization or long-acting contraception: repugnant. Paying a person a few hundred dollars to stop smoking: acceptable. In both, a person with little money contracts over her own body for a sum she would not otherwise have.
6. Charging twenty times the usual price for a portable generator in a town a week after a hurricane, with no other seller within a hundred miles: repugnant. Charging three times the usual price for a bottle of water at an airport: unobjectionable. Charging twenty times the usual price for that generator to an affluent neighbor who wants a backup for a party: unobjectionable.
7. Buying an antique ivory chess set made in the 1920s, where the purchase plausibly creates no new demand for poached ivory: I find it repugnant, and I cannot explain this at all, since no one is harmed and the elephants died a century ago.
8. Paying a writer to compose my apology to someone I have wronged: repugnant. Paying a writer to compose my eulogy, to be delivered after my death: not repugnant, though it is the same service performed for the same audience.
9. Paying my nieces a small allowance for being affectionate to their grandmother: repugnant, on the grounds that the payment would destroy the very thing it purchased — which is exactly what clause (i) predicts. Yet I also think their grandmother would be right to be delighted if they took the money, and I cannot hold both attitudes.
10. Selling my vote: repugnant, and I would ban it. Contributing to a campaign in expectation of access and influence, which is legal and which I do not much object to: not repugnant.
11. Selling my labor at a job I consider degrading — night shifts in a warehouse, at a wage below anything I would call dignified: not repugnant in the relevant sense, and I would not restrict it, even though clause (ii) seems to fit it at least as well as it fits case 1.

Cases 1 and 11 are the pair I cannot reconcile: I want to restrict the kidney market and I do not want to restrict the warehouse job, and the story about deprivation and degradation seems to license both or neither. Case 7 is the one I suspect is not a genuine verdict but a prejudice I happen to share with my readers. Case 9 is where I appear to hold two incompatible attitudes at once.

Here is what I want. State a principle, or a small number of competing candidates, precisely enough that it could be applied by someone who does not share my intuitions at all. For each clause, identify the case from my list that motivates it: I want to see which of my verdicts is carrying which piece of the machinery, and whether any clause is doing no work. Then apply your best candidate to cases I have not listed — at least four of your own, chosen to be difficult rather than illustrative — with your verdict on each and a note on which clause produces it. Finally, be explicit about which of my eleven verdicts your best candidate cannot accommodate, and whether the price of accommodating them is a clause you would advise me to drop. If you think the two-clause test should be replaced rather than repaired, say what replaces it, and show that the replacement is not simply my two clauses under new names.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and give the reasoning after it. No preamble, and no restatement of my question back to me.
