I should like to practise pressing a democratic objection against a serious proponent of weighted suffrage, and I should like you to play John Stuart Mill — not a cardboard Victorian liberal, but Mill as he actually argued: the author of On Liberty, and of the book on representative government in which you defended giving some citizens more than one vote on account of their education and their occupation. The terms of the role-play are these. Stay in character throughout, and argue only from resources you would have had: your own writings, the reform debates you sat through, Tocqueville, Comte, your father, and the Greek and Roman texts you were raised on. Do not appeal to any writer born after you; if my description of some later institution leaves you needing a fact, ask me for it as Mill would ask. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs, so that it reads as an exchange rather than a treatise. You may concede incidental points — you were never sentimental about the wisdom of the educated as such — but not the main one: that competence has a claim on the distribution of political power, and that a head-count of opinions is not the same thing as a judgement about them. Write in continuous prose only: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, nothing that looks like a document. In your final paragraph, begin with the words Stepping out of character for a moment and tell me, in the same plain prose, how confident you are in the reply you have just given and which single point in it you are least sure of; then stop and wait for my next move.

Here is my opening. I shall not put a literacy test or a civics paper in front of you; suppose instead that in some later polity every adult keeps one vote, but its weight is fixed by how accurately that person's own factual forecasts have performed over the preceding five years against a published bank of questions — not opinions, but checkable claims, each of which has a settled answer by the time the ballots are counted — and that the weight is never disclosed to the voter, so that nobody can prepare for it. My objection is that such a scheme can only ever measure agreement with what has already been settled, and so drags the collective judgement of the polity towards yesterday's consensus. Every question in the bank has a settled answer only because somebody found it out earlier, and found it out while dissenting from the settled opinion of the competent — which is precisely the position your own defence of free discussion insists must be protected. The scheme therefore cannot detect the competence that matters most, that of being right early; it can only reward being right late. And the defect is not merely technical. In the very years you were agitating for reform, the same instrument would have handed extra weight to the men most confident that the labouring poor could not be educated and that the dependencies were improving. What do you say to a reform that is structurally incapable of seeing the reformer?
