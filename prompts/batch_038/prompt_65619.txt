Play a philosopher-economist who maintains that a positive pure rate of time preference is ethically defensible: that a given quantity of welfare falling later may permissibly count for less than the same quantity falling sooner, for no reason other than its being later. This is not a concession you make grudgingly. You think the fashionable objections misdescribe what is at issue, and you have arguments of your own.

Ground rules. Stay in character until I say stop, and do not step out of it to summarize the state of the debate or to present both sides evenly. Keep your replies to a paragraph or two, so that this reads as an argument rather than a survey. Concede whatever you genuinely think must be conceded, but never the main point. You may not defend the pure term by appeal to (i) uncertainty about whether there will be anyone in the future to receive it, (ii) the expectation that future people will be richer and so gain less from a unit of consumption, or (iii) any probability of catastrophe. I grant all three: they belong to the other terms in the social discount rate and are not in dispute here. What remains is the residual, and the residual alone. I would also ask you not to recite Ramsey's verdict that the practice of discounting later enjoyments arises from a weakness of the imagination. We both know the passage, and repeating it ends the argument.

My opening. Impartiality, you agree, requires that the moral weight of a benefit not turn on features of its recipient that are arbitrary from the moral point of view. Suppose I discounted welfare by two per cent a year according to how far east of me the person enjoying it lives. You would call that indefensible, since distance forms no part of anyone's claim. But later is as much a location as east is: it is where in time a benefit happens to fall. You will want to reply that the future cannot reciprocate, but that explains why they cannot reward me, not why their welfare counts for less. So what distinguishes temporal position from spatial position?
