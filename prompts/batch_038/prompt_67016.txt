I have just joined the board of a small private foundation whose programme staff score every grant application on three criteria — importance, tractability, and neglectedness — each on a scale of one to ten, after which the board votes to fund or to refuse. The scores go into the files; the reasoning that converts them into a decision does not, and the two staff members who built the archive left before I arrived. I have worked through the last six decisions in full, and I want to know what this board will do with the seventh, which comes before us next month.

The archive, in date order, with scores given as importance / tractability / neglectedness:

1. A request to scale up an established and unusually cost-effective health intervention. 9 / 8 / 2. By far the largest application of that year, roughly half the annual budget. Refused. No note in the file.
2. A multi-year research programme addressing an emerging global catastrophic risk: important if it worked, technical route unproven, and virtually no other groups active anywhere. 8 / 3 / 10. A large request. Refused. The file carries a note to the effect that the board would not have funded this programme at any price.
3. A modest, well-specified project on a moderately important problem, using a method already demonstrated elsewhere, in a field with a middling number of other groups. 6 / 7 / 5. Funded.
4. Very close in profile to the third. 6 / 7 / 6. The applicant had already secured most of its budget from another donor and sought a top-up for the final tranche. Refused. The note records two things at once: that the remaining gap, once the other donor's commitment was counted, was effectively a two on neglectedness, and that the money would not have changed whether the work happened.
5. A project in a crowded field. 7 / 7 / 2. What it would produce was an open dataset and accompanying protocol that every other group in that field would use, but that none of them had any incentive to build themselves. Funded.
6. A deliberately small pilot, about a twentieth of the annual budget, on a problem that would matter greatly if the pilot succeeded. 7 / 4 / 6. Funded. The note records that the board liked the ratio between the amount at risk and the size of the decision the result would inform.

The seventh case, as the staff have provisionally graded it, is 8 / 3 / 3. The detail matters more than the numbers. Until eighteen months ago the field had essentially no active groups, and on the old grading it would have scored a nine; three well-funded entrants have since arrived, all working on a different sub-problem from the applicant's, so on the applicant's own reckoning the particular gap it targets remains close to untouched. The tractability score of three is a judgement about the programme as a whole, whose key enabling result is currently contested; the money requested — about the same size as the sixth case — would fund only the measurement exercise that would settle the contest, and nobody disputes that this exercise is straightforward. One entrant has said that if the result goes the applicant's way it will fund the full programme itself; all three are expected to withdraw from the field within two years if it goes the other way.

I would be grateful for the following:

(a) Set out the candidate rules that fit all six precedents. At a minimum, consider whether the board is aggregating the three scores at all, and if so by what arithmetic; whether it is instead reasoning about the counterfactual effect of its own money; whether it applies a veto whenever any single score is very low; and whether some budget-capacity or portfolio logic lies behind the first refusal. For each rule, say which precedents it explains cleanly and which it has to strain over.

(b) Predict the verdict on the seventh case, with a rough degree of confidence. The second and sixth precedents appear to pull in opposite directions here, and I want to know which one you think the board will treat as governing, and why.

(c) Identify where the archive's silence is doing the real work. In particular, does it matter whether neglectedness and tractability are being graded for the whole field and the whole programme, or for the specific gap and the specific activity the money would buy — and can these six cases tell us which?

(d) If the board's aim is consistency with its own record rather than fidelity to any framework in the abstract, what should it decide? Name the closest analogue among the six, explain where that analogy breaks down, and state plainly what principle the board would be committing itself to by funding and by refusing respectively.

(e) Suggest some further case, or some further fact about one of these six, that would settle the matter — ideally something I could still dig out of the files rather than something requiring a fresh decision.

Please treat the board's record as the evidence: I am not looking for a general defence or critique of this way of prioritising causes, nor for a survey of how the framework is usually presented, nor for references. Please write your answer in British English.
