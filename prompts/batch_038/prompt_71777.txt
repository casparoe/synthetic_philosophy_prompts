I've been auditing a sociology-of-work seminar this term (I'm in a totally different department, mostly I'm there for the free pizza) and last week a visiting person gave a chapter about the "trailing spouse" pattern in academia: when a couple of academics both need jobs, it's usually the woman who moves to wherever the man got hired, even though almost everyone involved says they disapprove of the pattern and no hiring committee thinks it's running a scheme. The chapter's claim was roughly that the structure of the academic labor market — geographically scattered, short-term contracts, every institution hiring for its own narrow needs, almost no coordination between two-body offers — generates the pattern pretty much by itself, and that you don't explain it by adding up what the individual couples intend.

Afterwards two people got into it at the dinner and I honestly couldnt tell who was winning. Theo, who's an economist, said the chapter was doing the classic functionalist cheat: saying a pattern persists because it's good for the system, when the only things that can ever do any causing are individuals making choices. He said you can't just point at the outcome, you have to give the causal chain through actual people with actual beliefs and incentives, otherwise you're letting the future cause the past. Nadia, who's a sociologist, said she'd grant the point about functionalist language but that Theo was attacking a strawman, since the chapter's mechanism did run through individuals — each couple is responding to what they expect other couples and other employers to do. Her complaint went the other direction: the individual-level restatement Theo wanted isn't available here, because the explanation is about positions rather than persons. The role of trailing spouse isn't a fact about any particular woman's psychology, it's a slot in a structure that different people occupy at different times, and the interesting claim is that the slot gets filled whoever happens to be standing there. Theo said that's just notation — you can always rewrite it as "for any individual occupying role R at time t," which is a quantifier over individuals, so it's methodological individualism with extra steps — and he added that any explanation that couldn't in principle be rewritten that way would be untestable anyway, since the only data you ever get is individual behavior.

Then Nadia brought out multiple realizability. The same macro pattern, she said, gets produced by wildly different micro configurations: some couples are moved by money, some by prestige, some by family, some purely by their guess about what the other side will do, and there's no neat mapping in either direction, since the same individual-level facts about one couple don't fix the aggregate outcome at all. So the stable, repeatable regularity sits at the macro level while the micro level is a mess — which is exactly, she said, why we explain gases by temperature and pressure instead of tracking molecules, and nobody thinks that's dodgy. Theo said that analogy helps him rather than her: temperature got reduced to statistical mechanics, and the reduction didn't discredit talk of temperature, it grounded it. Nadia said back that the very same multiple-realizability structure is what the anti-reductionists ran in the philosophy of mind — pain realized differently in humans and octopuses and maybe in machines — so Theo owes either a disanalogy or a concession: if reduction works there, explain why it also works here; if it fails there, the autonomy point stands. They went around that twice more and then somebody changed the subject.

Can you go through the exchange move by move and tell me who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? I'd especially like to know (a) whether Theo's functionalism objection even applies to the chapter as I've described it, (b) whether "positions rather than persons" is a real argument or, like Theo says, just a notational preference, (c) whether the temperature/statistical mechanics analogy actually cuts the way he thinks it does, and (d) whether the philosophy-of-mind parallel is fair game or a category mistake — since a gas molecule doesn't form expectations about other gas molecules. And then tell me what both of them were missing, and where you'd land on the underlying question: does a structural explanation like that one need microfoundations before you're allowed to believe it?
