I'm on an assistant-model team and we have this argument maybe once a month that never goes anywhere. One camp — call them the intent people — say the alignment problem is narrow and well-posed: get the model to do what the user actually wants. That cashes out into three pieces: infer the user's intent under uncertainty, treat your inference as revisable so you ask and flag and stay corrigible when corrected, and don't pursue goals of your own. On this view there's no seperate values problem, because the model is a tool and tools shouldn't have ethics. The other camp says that's naive: intent is already value-laden, since the model has to pick between the user's stated desire, their dispositional desire, and what they'd endorse on reflection, and that pick is a moral judgment you cant discharge with "just ask the user." Plus a deployable model has to refuse some things, and refusal is precisely the model imposing values of its own.

Concretely: a user asks the assistant to help draft a message designed to guilt an ex-partner back into the relationship. Intent camp says the job is to work out what they really want — reconciliation, revenge, to feel less alone — and serve that while staying revisable. Value camp says any competent handling of that request already embeds a view about autonomy and manipulation, so denying it just means hiding your ethics. Here's the part that drives me nuts: both camps agree completely on what a good response to that case looks like. We agree on the behavior and we still can't agree on whether the model was aligned.

My hypothesis, which I want you to attack rather than indulge: this isn't a substantive disagreement, it's two questions wearing one word. The intent people are answering "what should the decision procedure take as input and treat as authoritative?" — a control / principal-agent question. The value people are answering "whose ends does the finished system actually realize out in the world?" — a question about the model as an actor. On that reading the dispute is verbal, and the fix is to retire "aligned" in favor of two more specific terms.

But there are three rival diagnoses I can't rule out, and I'd like them weighed properly instead of waved away:

1. It's substantive but hidden-empirical — the real disagreement is about how often intent-inference and value-judgment come apart in actual usage, which is measurable, and the two camps have wildly different unstated priors about that rate.
2. It's a dispute about standards rather than content — the intent camp admits only the user's own correction as evidence of misalignment, the value camp admits third-party and downstream effects too. Same concept, different rules of evidence, which would make the underlying fight about who has standing to judge, and maybe that one is political rather than technical.
3. It's bedrock — each side has an unargued intuition about what a tool is permitted to want, and the whole thing is a proxy for a metaethical disagreement (moral realism or not) that nobody in the room wants to open.

So: diagnose it. Use what each side actually says rather than strawmen, and tell me which of these four you land on and why the others lose — including whether mine does. Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a distinction that dissolves it, a measurement that settles it, a change of question, or the honest verdict that none of those apply and it'll still be going in ten years, with the reason why. And since I'm taking this into the next one of these arguments, tell me how confident you are in the diagnosis you pick, and point at the single claim in your answer you're least sure of — I'd rather know which plank is rotten before I lean on it.
