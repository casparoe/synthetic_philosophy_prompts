I am a fourth-year philosophy student, and as part of my department's peer-support scheme each of us has to run a thirty-minute "concept clinic" for a small group of first-years who are currently taking the introductory philosophy of mind course. Mine is scheduled for the last Friday of term, twelve students, a seminar room with a whiteboard whose markers are almost all dry and a projector that does not work, so the plan should assume I have my voice, the board, and nothing else. My assigned topic is whether a single self can be distributed across more than one brain — Hofstadter's strange-loop account from *I Am a Strange Loop*, on which a person is a self-reinforcing pattern of symbols that can be realised, partially and to varying degrees, in the brains of everyone who knows them well, and can therefore survive the destruction of the brain that first ran it. The group has just finished personal identity: Locke on memory, Parfit's teleporter and the fission cases, and one week on the extended mind thesis. They have not read any Hofstadter and will not have time to. Two of them are joint honours with psychology and tend to steer everything back to neurons.

What I need from you is the plan for the explanation rather than the explanation: no script, no sample dialogue, and please do not suggest any readings for them or for me. First, the dependency structure — the order in which the components must go in, with a sentence on what each one makes possible. My own guess is that "a self is a pattern rather than a substance" has to be fully secured before "patterns admit of multiple realisations" can do any work, and that "multiple realisations in different media" has to precede "multiple realisations in different brains," but I cannot tell whether the strange-loop machinery — the self-referential symbol system, the level-crossing feedback — needs to be built at all in thirty minutes, or whether the session works without it and I should simply announce that I am leaving it out and why. Second, the carrying example: the one case that holds the whole thing together, and what it must have and must not have. It cannot be the teleporter or fission, since they will pattern-match to Parfit and stop listening; it cannot be a melody or a language, which they will dismiss as merely metaphorical; and it needs to be something I can later lean on to show that distribution is a matter of degree rather than a yes/no matter. Relatedly: I am tempted by Hofstadter's own case, the way he describes carrying on his late wife's pattern in himself and in the friends who knew her, but I worry that it converts the session into a grief seminar and inflames rather than defuses the reaction I expect below. Tell me whether to use it, and if so at what point.

Third, a misconception map. I can already predict four: (a) they will hear the view as the extended mind thesis with a sentimental gloss, since that is what they read last week; (b) they will collapse it into the claim that identity is a matter of degree, which they already accept and will find unimpressive; (c) some will take it as the claim that the dead literally survive, and get either frightened or derisive; and (d) others will hear "a self is only a pattern in a brain" as straight eliminativism, and conclude that nothing about persons is real after all. For each, tell me where in the sequence it should be headed off and with what single move or sentence — ideally before it forms rather than after. Please also name any further misreading you expect that I have not listed, given that they have just come off Parfit and Clark and Chalmers. Finally, give me one question I can put to the room in the last three minutes whose answers would distinguish those who have grasped the actual claim from those who have merely accepted a comfortable paraphrase of it, and a short list of what I should cut, say I am cutting, and be able to defend cutting if my tutor sits in.
