I teach a graduate seminar on political obligation and civil disobedience, and I also sit on the three-person review panel of a nonprofit fund that pays defense costs for people prosecuted after acts of protest. Our bylaws restrict support to acts that qualify as civil disobedience properly so called, as against ordinary lawbreaking, conscientious refusal, and militant action. The founding memo defines the term in broadly Rawlsian terms: a public, nonviolent, conscientious, and political act, addressed to the sense of justice of the community, undertaken when ordinary channels have failed, by people willing to accept the legal consequences. Nine years of decisions have drifted from that definition, I suspect, and I would like the drift characterized before Thursday's meeting, when an unusually hard application comes before us.

Here is our record, in the order decided. For each entry I give the facts as we found them and then the minute we entered, which is the panel's own prose rather than a theory and is rarely more than a sentence or two.

1. Funded. Twelve people chained themselves to the gate of a federal building for four hours to protest a deportation policy. They notified the police two days ahead, read a prepared statement to the press, blocked no route used by emergency traffic, waited to be arrested, and did not contest the charges. Minute: public, nonviolent, and they stood behind it in court.

2. Denied. An engineer copied internal records showing that a state agency had falsified inspection reports, gave them to a reporter, and left the country before charges were filed. Minute: we admire the disclosure, but he did not present himself to be judged.

3. Funded. A congregation openly sheltered a family subject to a removal order, announced what it was doing from the pulpit and in the local paper, and invited prosecution; two members were eventually charged. Minute: they told everyone what they were doing and why, and took the consequences.

4. Denied. Without notice, roughly forty people blocked a public highway at the evening peak to protest a fare increase, stranding commuters for two hours, among them a nurse who missed a shift. No damage was done, a written statement had been distributed in advance, and all remained to be arrested. Minute: the wrong was visited on people who were no part of it.

5. Denied. Two people, masked and at night, poured sand into the fuel tanks of vehicles belonging to a contractor building a detention facility. No one was hurt, nothing was claimed publicly, and when both were later identified through forensic evidence they contested the charges. Minute: covert damage to property, with no attempt to speak to anyone.

6. Funded. A student group occupied an administrative office for three days in violation of an injunction, damaging a door and some records, to protest the institution's investments. They issued written demands, negotiated with the dean, left when the sheriff arrived, and accepted their citations. Minute: the damage was incidental to a public appeal addressed to the people who could act on it.

7. Denied. A physician publicly refused, on religious grounds, to comply with a state reporting requirement she judged unjust, accepted the fines and the lien on her property, and lost. Minute: her objection was to her own share of a burden, not to an injustice done to others.

8. Denied. A group destroyed pumping equipment at an agricultural processing site after a single letter to their legislator and while a lawsuit they supported was actively moving toward judgment. Minute: they had not run out of road.

Now the application before us.

Three people stand charged with burglary and criminal mischief. Eighteen months ago a federal district court held that the automated eligibility system run for our state's department of social services had terminated benefits for many thousands of households without individualized hearings, and ordered the practice stopped; compliance has been slow. The system runs out of a privately owned data-processing center operated under contract with the department. At two in the morning on a scheduled maintenance night, eight masked people entered through a propped service door, disconnected two server racks, and left after forty minutes. They say they had studied the shift schedule and knew the floor was empty; no one was injured and no safety or emergency system was affected. The racks were left undamaged in a rented storage unit, the key to which was mailed to the operator with a note. The operator claims four hundred thousand dollars in damage and lost business. For three days, payments and new applications for roughly twenty thousand households went unprocessed; the applicants say the department fell back on manual review during the outage, which is slower but lawful, and that the households affected are largely the same population the system was injuring.

Two hours after leaving the site, they published a three-thousand-word statement under the real names of four people, setting out the injustice, their demands, and a chronology of the channels they had used: two administrative petitions, a legislative campaign, and material support for the litigation, in which none of them was a plaintiff. It went to two newspapers and to the department's director. None of them remained at the scene or has surrendered. Three were identified from a vehicle registration and a door camera and were charged; all three say they will not contest the facts, will not leave the jurisdiction, and will state their reasons from the witness stand, and the published statement says they regard any sentence as part of the protest. Two of the four signatories remain uncharged. On last resort, the department's counsel points out that the court appointed a compliance monitor six weeks before the action and that the monitor's first report was due within ninety days; the applicants answer that three years of litigation produced one injunction and no restitution.

What I want from you:

(a) Reconstruct the rule the panel has actually been applying. Give at least three candidate rules, each consistent with all eight decisions and minutes, and state each precisely enough that it would decide an application not on our list. Where a rule fits only by reading a minute in a particular way, flag the reading and say what turns on it. Note in particular whether entry 6 is compatible with a definition that requires the act to be political and addressed to the community rather than to a private institution.

(b) Apply each candidate rule to the present application and say what it recommends: funding, denial, or funding on conditions you specify.

(c) Recommend the decision that best coheres with our own record. Identify the closest analog among the eight, and explain why it is closer than the nearest entry pointing the other way. If you judge that the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say so rather than forcing a verdict, and then tell me what principle the panel would be adopting by deciding either way. Where the outcome turns on a contested factual premise, such as whether the applicants had exhausted their channels, isolate that premise rather than resolving it by assumption.

(d) For the two leading candidate rules, describe one further application the panel might plausibly receive that would discriminate between them, and say how each rule would rule on it. Then, for each of the two decisions we could reach on Thursday, name one kind of future application that the decision would commit us to treating the same way, and that I suspect we would not want to.

(e) Throughout, separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature. I am thinking especially of three questions: whether willingness to accept legal penalty is a condition on the concept or only a moral desideratum; whether nonviolence extends to property or only to persons; and whether the act must be addressed to the political community rather than to whoever can remedy the grievance. Do not present a contested position as the consensus, and do not treat the founding memo's paraphrase as authoritative if you think it is not a fair statement of the view it invokes.

I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want the reasoning. Write in American English, and keep the reconstruction in (a) tight so that most of the space goes to (b) through (d).
