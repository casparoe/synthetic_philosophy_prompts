Something happened this spring that's got me trying to work out how my own emotions actually run, and I've ended up with a list of cases and no idea what principle they add up to. My mom died in April. At the funeral I was completely flat — I did the reading, I handled the relatives, I felt basically nothing, and I wasn't suppressing it, I checked. Then about ten days later I watched an animated movie with my niece, one where the little robot loses his friend, and I had to leave the room I was crying so hard. I know it's a cartoon. I'd seen it before.

So I started paying attention, and here's the list. I'd like to know what rule, if any, fits all of it, and which entries I have to give up.

- Crying at the cartoon, knowing full well it's a cartoon: real sadness. Not "as if" sad.
- Nothing at the funeral: also real, in the sense that there was genuinely nothing there.
- The same movie again three weeks later, knowing exactly what's coming shot for shot: still got me, and not any weaker.
- A nightmare where I knew I was dreaming — I've been lucid in dreams since I was a kid — the fear is completely real, I wake up shaking. Whatever people mean when they say you're "not really scared" at a movie, it can't apply here, I'm not fooled for a second and my body is going nuts anyway.
- A horror film: real fear, but it never gets down into my legs. I have never once bolted for the door, and I never cover my eyes at quite the right moment.
- A documentary about a famine, real footage, real people, thousands dead: nothing. I donated some money and felt like a decent person and felt no particular way about any of it.
- A novel about basically the same famine, invented village, invented family: wrecked for two days.
- A memoir I had zero reason to doubt, about a guy whose brother dies — nothing. Maybe the writing was bad, I'll admit I don't know, but I was believing every word and feeling absolutely nothing.
- Anger at a novelist for killing off a character I loved. Aimed at a real living person, about something she made up. That anger feels real, and I also think it's legitimate, not a mistake on my part.
- A video game where I let an NPC die to save my own character. Real guilt, its been months and it still nags. Weird part: I reloaded the save and did it right the second time, and the second playthrough felt like nothing at all.
- No guilt about not visiting my mom in her last six months. She asked twice. I kept saying next weekend. I know this is the thing a normal person would be eaten alive by. Nothing.
- Grief for a character who turns out in the last chapter to have been the narrator's imaginary friend the whole time. I still grieved, and I think I was right to.
- Admiration for a historical figure I only know from a biopic that everyone says is mostly invented. Real feeling, but I suspect it's aimed at somebody who never existed.

The rule I keep reaching for, and I want you to try to break it or patch it: I only feel something when there's nothing I can do about it. The moment an action is available, the feeling either converts into the action or just fails to show up. That seems to handle the funeral (I was busy), the famine documentary (I donated), my mom (I could have just driven over), and the game (I could reload — and when I did, it evaporated). It sort of handles the horror movie too, since there's nothing you can do about a monster, so the fear has nowhere to go except be felt — except it doesn't reach my legs, so I don't know.

But it obviously doesn't handle the memoir, where there was nothing to do and nothing was felt, and it's shaky on the novelist, where I could have thrown the book across the room and half wanted to.

The other thing is I'm running two questions together and can't separate them myself: whether the feeling is genuinely there, and whether it's the feeling a person ought to have. The ad thing shows it. I cried at a car commercial last year where the dog dies — definitely real crying, and I'm still ashamed of it, and I think being ashamed is right. So: real but unfitting. But then is the video game guilt real-and-fitting or real-and-unfitting, and how would I ever tell from the inside?

What I want back:

- the principle — or if there are two or three competing ones that fit, all of them — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I haven't thought of without guessing;
- for each clause, which of my cases is doing the work of motivating it;
- what it says about cases I left out: reading a two-sentence plot summary of the death that got me, on some wiki, no prose and no scene; being completely undone by instrumental music that isn't about anything at all; and a nurse I know who cries at films but says she hasn't felt anything at work in eleven years;
- and a straight answer on whether my "nothing you can do about it" rule is repairable or should be scrapped, plus an honest accounting of which of my verdicts the best principle can't save, so I know which one I have to stop believing.

I haven't read any philosophy on this and I don't want a tour of who said what — no names, no reading list, just the actual rule and the reasoning behind it. Sorry if this is a mess, I've rewritten it four times and it keeps coming out longer.
