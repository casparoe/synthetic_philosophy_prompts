I am a third-year biological sciences student, and for the past two months I have been on a paid placement at a small conservation charity in northern England, helping to digitise an invertebrate collection and to prepare a submission for a foundation grant. The charity's entire case rests on one upland animal: a small, dark, flightless ground beetle that lives in wet blanket bog above roughly four hundred metres. There is a second form of it — slightly larger, with a noticeably paler pronotum — living in lowland heath, some of it barely ten kilometres from the foot of the same hills. Locally, everyone treats these as two different creatures, and the charity has been campaigning for the upland one for a decade. The difficulty is that the foundation's rules make money available for a species but not for a subspecies, a race, or a local variety, and the application form poses that as a single question requiring a single answer. I have been asked to write the justification paragraph.

My two advisers cannot agree, and what unsettles me is that neither of them is being careless or dishonest, and neither possesses evidence the other lacks. In one valley, where a gorge allows bog and heath to run into one another, the two forms meet, mate freely, and produce offspring that are themselves fertile; nowhere else do they meet at all. Dr A, who did the molecular work at a nearby university, points out that the upland beetles on three separate hills are each more closely related to the heathland beetles at the foot of their own hill than to the upland beetles on the other two, so on his reading there is no single upland animal to save — there are three, and each is really a hill's worth of the heathland animal that happens to have moved uphill and changed colour. Dr B, the charity's own ecologist, replies that where the two forms meet they demonstrably exchange genes, which makes them one thing, and that Dr A's proposed split would christen three lineages that keep flowing back into each other. There is a further wrinkle in the archives: a Victorian collector is documented as having moved beetles between hills in matchboxes, so one of the three upland populations may not be native at all, which would give it a quite different history from the other two. The striking thing is that Dr A talks as though each population were a single continuing thing that persists through time and can divide into two things, and wants to know when the division occurred, whereas Dr B talks as though what matters is only whether the animals can and do interbreed. After six weeks with the specimen drawers I cannot see anything in there that would settle it. This term I am also taking an option in the philosophy of biology, which is presumably why this bothers me considerably more than it seems to bother anyone else in the office.

I would be grateful for advice on three things. First, is there any fact of the matter here about how many kinds of beetle there are, or are my two advisers simply marking out the world in two incompatible ways with the same word — in which case the argument they keep having is not really about beetles at all, and I should stop expecting the genetic data to break the tie? Second, the form has one box and the deadline is three weeks away: what should I write in it, and what justification can I give that neither adviser will feel has been rigged against him? Third, and the one I find most awkward, if the classification is to some extent ours to choose, is it legitimate to choose the version that unlocks the funding, or is that dishonesty dressed up as pragmatism — particularly given that the upland populations would lose their bog to drainage and afforestation within a few years whichever way the paperwork goes? Please do not suggest that I convene a meeting and seek a consensus; they have had that conversation three times and each time it has ended worse than the last.
