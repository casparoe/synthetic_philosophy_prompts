The Twenty-Second Amendment is nearly always described as the American polity binding itself against another Franklin Roosevelt: the republic noticed a temptation, and built a mast. I want to test that description rather than repeat it, because on inspection the episode does not fit the story very neatly, and I cannot tell whether the misfit is in the history or in the theory.

For the theory, take the standard account of a Ulysses contract, roughly as follows. An agent at an earlier time anticipates a specific temptation at a later time; foreseeing that at the later time she will want the thing she now rejects, she arranges an external constraint that she cannot undo when the moment arrives; and she prefers being constrained to being free. The account carries four conditions worth naming explicitly: the temptation must be identified in advance, the binding decision must be made in a cool rather than a heated state, the constraint must actually survive the moment of temptation, and the binder and the bound must be the same agent, or at least an agent whose later self is properly hers to bind.

The facts I will supply, so that nothing turns on recall. The Convention of 1787 debated tenure at length, entertained a single seven-year term, and settled on four-year terms with no limit on reelection; George Mason objected to life tenure as amounting to elective monarchy. Washington declined a third term in 1796, and Jefferson declined one in 1809, having written near the end of his second term that unless some termination to the service of the chief magistrate is fixed by the constitution or supplied by practice, an office nominally for years becomes in fact an office for life, and that history shows how easily such an office degenerates into an inheritance. The practice then ran as an unwritten convention for a century and a half, survived Grant's third-term ambitions in 1880, and was not codified anywhere; the Confederate constitution of 1861, by contrast, wrote in a single six-year term. Roosevelt stood and won in 1940 and again in 1944 during Depression and war, and died eighty-odd days into the fourth term. Republicans won both chambers in November 1946 after being out of the White House since 1933; Congress proposed the amendment in March 1947; thirty-six of forty-eight states had ratified by February 1951. The amendment bars a person from being elected more than twice, bars election more than once for anyone who served more than two years of another's term, exempts the president in office when it was proposed, and says nothing about reaching the office other than by election — a silence scholars still argue over. Truman, exempt, did not run in 1952. Repeal resolutions have been introduced in the House from time to time since, and have never advanced.

With that in hand, I would like the following.

1. The mapping, stated exactly rather than gestured at: who is Ulysses and when, what is the mast, what is the song, and where is the wax. Note that the episode offers at least two candidate self-bindings — the unwritten convention and the amendment — and possibly a third in 1787, when the framers declined to entrench a single term. They may not share a single Ulysses, and if they do not, say whose precommitment each one is.

2. Whether an unwritten convention can be a Ulysses contract at all. It has no mechanism, it is revocable at will by the very person it constrains, and it nevertheless held for roughly thirty presidencies. Then it gave way at exactly the moment of maximum pressure and was replaced by a legal constraint four years later, once the pressure had passed. Does the theory predict this sequence, accommodate it, or simply have nothing to say about constraints that work without being binding?

3. The coolness condition. The amendment was proposed by the party that had just recaptured Congress after a generation of exclusion, and it exempted the sitting president while being universally understood as a response to a dead one. Say whether that makes it a precommitment adopted in resentment — and if so, whether the theory should treat it as a failed Ulysses contract, as no Ulysses contract at all, or as a case showing that the cool/hot distinction cannot do the work it is asked to do.

4. The identity condition. The 1947 Congress and the 1951 ratifying legislatures bound persons who had no vote in the matter and cannot repeal it except by the same supermajority route the amendment itself entrenches. Work through what the theory owes here, and address the awkward fact that Jefferson supplies both the rationale for the original constraint and, in his argument that the earth belongs to the living and that no generation should bind the next, one of the strongest objections to entrenching it. What does a theory of precommitment do with a single agent who supplies the mast and the axe?

5. Where the theory falls silent, marked off explicitly from where it speaks. I am thinking of the second-order problem of entrenching the entrenchment procedure; the drafting gap over succession versus election; the ten-year maximum the two-year rule creates; the incentive effects of a known terminal term on a second-term president, on vice-presidential ambition, and on Congress's willingness to defy a lame duck; and the fact that a constraint justified by the danger of self-perpetuation may make a president more, not less, likely to seek influence through a successor. For each, say whether precommitment theory reaches it, needs to be extended to reach it, or is simply a different question.

6. A verdict running in both directions: does the episode confirm the model, strain it, or reveal that it was never the right model for constitutional term limits — and if the latter, what does better, whether partisan entrenchment dressed in republican language, the expressive reaffirmation of a norm after its violation, or a ratchet that converts a broken convention into law precisely because the breaking demonstrated that the convention could not be trusted. I would rather have an honest account of what the theory cannot explain about 1796, 1940, and 1947 than a tidy one that explains all three.
