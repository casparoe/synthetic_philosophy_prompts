For about two years I have been keeping a list of my own judgments about when it is right to keep feeding a losing enterprise, and I have come to suspect that the standard prescription — ignore what is already spent, evaluate only the next increment against its own prospects — does not so much explain my judgments as leave them unsorted. The list began at a game night where we ran the dollar auction: a hundred-dollar prize, five-dollar increments, the high bidder pays and collects, the runner-up pays and gets nothing. I want to know what principle, if any, captures the pattern below, and where the pattern has to be broken rather than preserved.

My verdicts. (i) In the plain dollar auction, sitting at $60 against an opponent at $55, I should bid $65: folding converts a bad position into a certain $60 loss, whereas bidding at least buys the chance that the other player quits. (ii) Before play, the house offers, for one dollar, an exit ticket redeemable once at any later point — hand it over and you recover everything you have bid minus ten dollars. Buying the ticket is plainly correct at that price and almost nobody buys it; and I think that if both players hold tickets, the war never gets off the ground. (iii) In a variant where a third party has agreed to reimburse the runner-up's entire outlay, I fold at $60 without a second thought, which strikes me as strange, since my forward-looking position at $60 is unchanged and only the terms of my exit have improved. (iv) A friend's company has spent eighteen months and two million dollars matching a rival's discounts; the rival has the smaller war chest. He should keep going — and I would add that the two million already sunk is part of *why* keeping going works, because it is what has convinced the rival that he will not stop. (v) My own case against a former partner: eighty thousand dollars already spent, counsel says thirty thousand more gets to trial, expected recovery around fifty. I should take the settlement, even though the arithmetic of the next increment favors continuing. (vi) In a bad year I doubled down on a position that kept falling and eventually got out whole. I thought it was right then and I still think it was right. (vii) In a salary negotiation I refused to move for three weeks although I would have accepted their opening number on day one; that was right, because caving early teaches them that my first figure is decorative, and the lesson compounds across every negotiation we will ever have. (viii) My brother and I have not spoken for four months over something said at a funeral, each waiting for the other to apologize first; that is wrong, and obviously wrong, even though every clause of my reasoning in (vii) seems to apply here unchanged. (ix) A friend six years into a funded, well-regarded doctoral program, who dreads the work and has two chapters she is proud of, should quit.

My own attempt at a principle is this: escalation is permissible exactly when, before entering, you fixed a bound on what you would spend and a condition under which you would walk away, and impermissible when each round looks locally cheap but no such rule exists. I already see trouble — it condemns (i), which I endorsed, and it seems to bless (vi), which I half-suspect is bad reasoning wearing a good outcome. So: give me the best account you can, whether that means repairing mine or replacing it, stated precisely enough that someone who does not share my intuitions could apply it to a new case without asking me anything. For each clause, tell me which of the verdicts above it exists to capture. Then tell me what it says about cases I have not listed — a startup pivoting after burning most of its runway, a government continuing a war whose original justification has quietly changed, someone staying in a marriage chiefly because of the years already invested, a research program that keeps absorbing anomalies rather than conceding them, a company repurchasing its own fallen stock. Say frankly which of my nine verdicts the account cannot accommodate and why I should give them up, and treat (vi) and the (vii)/(viii) pair explicitly: is there anything that separates a bargaining stance toward a negotiating counterpart from the identical stance toward a brother, or is my verdict in (viii) simply inconsistent? Finally, I would like a direct answer on whether the irrelevance of sunk costs survives as a normative principle at all, given that in (iv) the money already spent appears to be doing genuine strategic work — is that a real counterexample, or a misdescription of what is carrying the weight there?
