ok so I keep getting dragged into the same heritability argument and I want to move it up a level, because right now the exchange is always identical: one side waves a twin-study number, the other side recites the caveat list (population-relative, says nothing about individuals, fully compatible with total malleability), and both walk away thinking they won. Before I get back in it I want the spec written down.

Setup: pretend we're designing a statistic from scratch. Call it G. For a given trait in a given population it's supposed to answer "how much do genetic differences matter here, in a form somebody could act on." The somebody is a school district or a clinic, not a philosopher. Write the desiderata for G — and for each one give the case or consideration that motivates it, not just a label.

Things I already think are in play, add whatever I'm missing: G has to behave sensibly when environments get more uniform or more varied (nutrition and height is the stock example, and I want the requirement stated so it doesn't quietly smuggle in a direction of change); it has to have an answer for the much-argued result that heritability of cognitive-test scores differs by family socioeconomic status — note it isn't even settled which way it goes, so G needs to say what that whole genre of finding means for it either way; it has to survive genotype-enviroment correlation, both passive and evocative, plus assortative mating; it has to say what happens to its value when you carry it from the population it was estimated in to a different one; and it has to be honest about the gap between twin-study estimates and what polygenic scores actually predict out of sample.

Then the part I care about most. Mark every requirement as either a genuine desideratum or a camp prejudice wearing a desideratum's clothes. Two I want adjudicated explicitly: is "G must be interpretable for one individual kid" a coherent demand at all, or a category error people keep sneaking onto the list? And can "G must license intervention claims" (change environment E, trait moves by X) be satisfied at the same time as "G must transport across populations"? My hunch is no, because variance components are indexed to the environment distribution of the population they came from — if you agree, the impossibility is the actual result and I want it stated cleanly rather than buried.

Finish with which single requirement does the most work — the one that knocks out the most candidate statistics by itself — and then say what G ends up looking like if you keep that one and give up the rest. No bibliography and no reading list, I don't want citations, I want the requirements.
