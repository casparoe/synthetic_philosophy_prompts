I am a novelist, four books in, and in six weeks I am giving the closing lecture at a two-day workshop on ignorance as a category shared by literary study and decision theory, hosted by a comparative literature department. The lecture is a craft essay rather than a paper, and I would like a preparation sheet for the question period.

The essay's spine is a short 1967 result of I. J. Good's on the principle of total evidence — call it Good's theorem. As I understand it: if you face a fixed set of options and may first learn which cell of some partition obtains, at negligible cost; if your future self is certain to update by conditionalizing and then select the option of highest posterior expected utility; and if learning which cell obtains does not itself alter the utility of any option in any state — then deciding after learning is never worse than deciding now. The proof, as I gloss it, is that deciding now yields the maximum of a weighted average of expected utilities, whereas learning first yields the weighted average of those maxima, and an average of maxima cannot fall below the maximum of the averages.

My thesis is that the theorem is a theorem about a world in which nobody has any reason to shut their eyes, and that its three premises name exactly the three places where a life departs from that world — each departure corresponding to a distinct literary mode of the refusal scene, which is the scene in which a character declines available knowledge. First, the partition is exogenously given to the agent; in a novel the agent chooses the question, and choosing among questions is itself a decision the theorem is silent about. These are the strategic refusals: the client who does not want to know whether the alibi is true, the woman who declines the genetic test she has already paid for. Second, utilities are stable across learning; but learning can change what you want, and you can know this about yourself in advance. These are the protective refusals: the widow who will not open the box of her husband's letters, the reader who stops twenty pages from the end. Third, your future self is assumed to do as rationality requires; self-knowledge instead functions as commitment, since knowing that you would waver, or forgive, or act on what you learn is a way of binding yourself now. These are the identity refusals: the character who will not learn the truth because the truth would make her someone she has decided not to be.

The claim I most want to defend is narrower and, I think, more interesting: that what makes a refusal scene read as reasoned rather than merely weak is that the reader can reconstruct the character's second-order beliefs — not what the character fears, but what the character expects of herself once she knows. Atmosphere, withholding, and delay cannot substitute for this. From it I derive what I call the revelation reversal: because the reader acquires the character's second-order beliefs out of order, supplying the knowledge later can flip the reader's moral verdict on a refusal already made, and prose narrative is unusually good at producing that flip without arguing for it. I close by claiming that the ethics of the withheld letter is less about knowledge than about an author's power to fix the order in which a reader acquires beliefs — that every novelist is, in effect, designing a mechanism of ignorance.

The room will hold perhaps forty people. Most are narratologists and literary critics working in a structuralist idiom, fluent in focalization, restricted narration, and the unreliable narrator, and reflexively hostile to literature being used as illustration for another discipline. Two are analytic philosophers of art: one on narrative and the emotions, one on the ethics of fiction and authorial manipulation. One is a decision theorist who actually works on the value of information, invited as a check on the mathematics, generous in manner but exacting about whether an informal analogy is doing any work. Two run reading experiments — self-report, eye-tracking, comprehension measures — and will want evidence. The rest are novelists and graduate students. Everyone has read the abstract, which concedes that I have no formal training beyond reading around the subject.

Please prepare the following.

1. The nine or ten objections I will actually get, ordered by the damage they would do if I answered them badly — likelihood times severity, not alphabetically. For each: who raises it, phrased in that person's own register as they would actually put it aloud; a reply I can deliver in about two minutes without notes; whether I should concede, hold, or reframe, and if concede, the exact words to concede in; and a single line on where the reply is softest and what follow-up they will press if I fumble it.

2. The objection I am hoping nobody asks — the one that, if it lands cleanly, I cannot answer in the room. Tell me what it is, why it is fatal, and what my least dishonest response would be.

3. Three sentences I should say in the first five minutes that would inoculate me against the two most likely attacks, and a short list of moves I must avoid: the habits of a novelist borrowing authority, such as gesturing at the mathematics of the possible or claiming the theorem shows something about fiction.

4. A candid assessment of whether my statement of the theorem's premises is correct, and of which of my three departures is real and which is decoration. Do not soften this to be polite; if the taxonomy is three labels for one distinction, say so and tell me which label survives.

Finally, two things about your own confidence. Tell me how sure you are of the parts of the sheet that depend on getting the decision theory right, and separately how sure you are that these particular people would raise these particular objections in the forms you have written them. Then single out the one point in your analysis you are least sure of — the objection you suspect is my invention rather than theirs, or the reply you doubt will survive contact with the room — and say why. I would rather have a calibration than a hedge.
