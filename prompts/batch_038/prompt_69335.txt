I spent most of last night arguing with a friend about what a formal account of belief under logical uncertainty actually owes you. His position: adopt coherence in the limit together with a no-exploitability condition relativized to computationally bounded traders, and you have your theory — everything else is decoration. My position: that is a criterion reverse-engineered from one particular construction, and he is mistaking the properties his favorite account happens to have for the properties any adequate account must have. I would like the question settled by specification rather than by appeal to a paper.

Write the desiderata. I want the list of what any adequate theory of an agent's degrees of belief over the sentences of a fixed formal language — arithmetic, say — has to deliver in order to count as a theory of bounded logical reasoning rather than a theory of idealized omniscience: the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it may not collapse, and the results it must be consistent with. For each item, give the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark whether you take it to be non-negotiable or a preference of one camp.

Among the items I want addressed explicitly: what the agent should believe about a sentence it has computed nothing at all about, and why a default of one-half is not an innocent placeholder — if the theory departs from it, where does the extra structure come from, and must the theory declare how much prior it has smuggled in; the tautology too long to verify and the theorem whose proof the agent has failed to find after serious effort, which are not the same case; non-logical testimony, for instance a colleague who reports a proof nobody has yet checked; self-reference, including sentences that talk about the agent's own future conclusions and about its own compute budget being cut off mid-search; and, for every guarantee the theory offers, whether it holds at each finite stage or only asymptotically.

Two things matter most to me. First, if some subset of the list is jointly unsatisfiable, say so and sketch the diagonal argument; I already accept that computability and strict coherence cannot coexist, so what I want is everything else that breaks, and how far the relativization to bounded traders can be pushed before the standard stops discriminating between theories at all. Second, which single desideratum eliminates the most candidate accounts.

Close with a short scorecard — three contenders only: textbook Bayesianism with logical omniscience intact; the bounded-trader no-exploitability approach; and the family of proposals that make a sentence's probability a function of the length of the shortest proof anyone has found for it or for its negation. Keep that section brief, since the thinking I am after is in the list itself. So that you can calibrate what counts as adequate: I want a specification usable for judging proposals that are supposed to eventually run inside an agent with a fixed compute budget, which means any guarantee available only in the limit must be flagged as such rather than counted as a delivery. Please write in American English.
