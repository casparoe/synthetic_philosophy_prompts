I have an argument that began as a limited criticism of the case for acausal cooperation in large worlds and has turned into something that proves far too much. I would like a diagnosis rather than a defense.

The target view, stated as its proponents would state it: when the world contains very many agents whose decision problems and decision procedures resemble mine but who are causally isolated from me, my choice is still evidence of their choices, so evidential decision theory can recommend cooperating in trades I could never cause. My argument against it:

(1) The case for such cooperation rests on the claim that conditionalizing on my act raises the probability that resembling agents perform the corresponding act, and that their resulting payoffs enter the expected utility of my act.

(2) Resemblance of that kind comes in degrees, and there is no principled cutoff: a chain of small differences runs from an agent indistinguishable from me to an agent with whom I share nothing but the bare fact of facing a choice.

(3) Each agent's evidential contribution varies continuously with resemblance, since any discontinuity would be arbitrary.

(4) In a large world, the number of agents rises without bound as resemblance falls: near-copies are few, barely-similar agents are vast.

(5) So the aggregate contribution of the barely-similar many exceeds that of the near-identical few.

(6) But what the barely-similar many do is, by (2) and (3), very nearly independent of what I do.

(7) Hence the aggregate that (5) says governs my calculation is an aggregate of terms that cannot respond to my choice.

(8) So the evidential verdict in a large world is either undefined, because the aggregates do not converge, or indistinguishable from the verdict I would reach by ignoring distant agents entirely.

(9) Either way the case for acausal cooperation fails; and since that case was the only distinctive thing evidential decision theory had to say about large worlds, evidential decision theory has no content there.

(9) is a conclusion I do not want. I am inclined to think that cooperating with close copies is at least permissible and quite possibly required, and I am not willing to buy (2)'s denial — that is, I do not want to be committed to a sharp cutoff on resemblance — so if a premise must go, I would rather give up (5) or (8). But I cannot tell whether the trouble is in the arithmetic or in the picture of resemblance that drives it, and I suspect the two are not separable.

Please tell me which step to reject and why it looked unobjectionable in the first place; what each available repair commits me to, including the repair that concedes a cutoff; and where my own preferred repair is weakest. American English, please.
