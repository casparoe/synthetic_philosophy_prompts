The question was:

"In 'Saints and Heroes,' Urmson argued that the traditional threefold classification of acts as obligatory, forbidden, or permitted cannot capture the whole of morality. Explain the supererogatory and the puzzle it is said to generate for moral theory. Then set out one response available to a consequentialist and assess whether it succeeds." (about 600 words)

The student's answer:

Urmson's complaint is that the threefold scheme leaves out a large part of moral life, because some acts are neither obligatory nor merely permitted. The soldier who throws himself on the grenade does something supremely good, but we don't say he was obliged to do it, and we don't say the soldier who dived for cover instead did anything wrong. So a supererogatory act is one that is (i) permissible to omit, (ii) good to perform, and (iii) praiseworthy in a way that goes beyond what morality asks. Some definitions add (iv) that it costs the agent something substantial, which is why the label fits saints and heroes rather than small unrequired kindnesses.

This generates a puzzle. If an act is the best thing you can do, and morality asks us to do what is best, then it is required, and there is nothing left over to be supererogatory. If instead it is not required, morality seems to license doing worse than you could have done, which is strange for a theory that takes reasons seriously. Singer makes the pressure concrete with the drowning child: if you ought to wade in and ruin your clothes to save a child in a shallow pond, then giving the equivalent to famine relief is obligatory too, not saintly. Act-consequentialism therefore looks committed to there being no supererogation at all — everything short of the optimum is simply wrong.

But the consequentialist has a reply. They can separate an act's being required from its omission's being blameworthy. On a two-level view like Hare's, the intuitive level is where the real moral facts live and the critical level is where we work out which principles to teach people, so the consequentialist can say the saint's act is required at the critical level while the rest of us are quite properly not blamed at the intuitive level for falling short of it. That is all supererogation was ever meant to pick out anyway, since Urmson's worry was really about praise and blame rather than about adding a fourth deontic box. On this reading the puzzle just dissolves.

Whether it does is another matter. Wolf's moral saints paper argues that the saint is not an ideal we should be aiming at, because a life organized entirely around morality crowds out the non-moral goods that make a life worth living; if morality itself tells us not to be saints, then the saint's acts are exactly the ones morality recommends but doesn't require, and the consequentialist can welcome them. Even so, this concedes too much. If blamelessness is all that supererogation amounts to, then any act the consequentialist prefers not to sanction counts as supererogatory, and the category stops doing any work. What the consequentialist has shown is not that there is supererogation but only that we shouldn't blame anyone for there not being any.
