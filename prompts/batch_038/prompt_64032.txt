I am a philosophy graduate student auditing a comparative economic history seminar, and my prospectus is turning into something about the epistemology of contrastive explanation in large-scale history rather than about the history itself. The seminar is built around the old Needham puzzle — why modern science and sustained industrial growth emerged in western Europe when China had been technically ahead for centuries — and around its descendant, the question usually phrased as why Britain and not China. The reading cycles through the coal-and-New-World contingency account Pomeranz built out of the observation that England and the Yangzi delta looked remarkably alike as late as the middle of the eighteenth century, Allen's high-wage-and-cheap-energy incentive story, the institutions tradition descending from North, Mokyr's account of a culture of open scientific inquiry, Elvin's high-level equilibrium trap, and Landes's civilizational version. I have all of it and I am not asking for a literature survey or for references. What I want first is the specification: before I can judge whether any of these answers the question, I need to know what an answer to a contrast of that shape has to deliver in order to count as an answer at all, and I suspect the honest possibility is that the specification comes out unsatisfiable and the question is defective rather than disputed.

So please write the desiderata — the requirements a candidate explanation of why Britain industrialized and China did not must meet — each with the case or consideration that motivates it, and each marked as non-negotiable, as negotiable, or as something that is really a preference of one camp masquerading as a requirement. Among the things I expect the list to reach, and want handled explicitly rather than in passing: fixing the contrast class and the foil (which Britain — England, the North Sea economy, western Europe as a whole — and which China — the empire, Jiangnan, the delta regions that actually resembled England); fixing the explanandum and its date and measure, given that the series being compared are reconstructions with wide error bars and that real wages, output per head, and the appearance of factory production do not diverge at the same time; a difference-maker requirement, that the explanans actually differed between the two units and differed before the outcome rather than merely being the sort of thing that would produce the outcome if present; a ban on redescription, so that an account is disqualified if it amounts to saying that Britain industrialized because it had the economy of an industrializer; the forerunner and false-start problem, including the Song coal-and-iron episode and the Dutch Republic's long eighteenth-century lead, and what those cases demand of an account in the way of not selecting on the dependent variable; the distinction between explaining origination and explaining persistence, since the factors that made growth start somewhere need not be the factors that kept it going, and I suspect accounts trade on that slippage; a unit-independence requirement, which is what the anthropologists have called Galton's problem ever since Francis Galton pressed Edward Tylor in 1889 on whether his cross-cultural cases were independent instances at all — here the worry is that Britain and China were entangled through trade, silver flows, empire, and diffusion in ways that may make a within-case difference-making comparison unlicensed; a modal requirement about whether the explanans had to be contingent, since a factor that could not have been otherwise explains nothing about why things went one way; and a scope requirement about whether the answer must also handle Japan, India, and the Ottoman Empire or is permitted to be a one-off explanation of a singular event. Finally, treat as one of the candidate requirements the demand that the question itself be well posed — that is, that there be some determinate foil for which the explanans is a genuine difference, and that asking about Britain and China rather than about northwest Europe and the lower Yangzi, or about nowhere and everywhere, is not simply a rhetorical choice.

Two things beyond the list, both short. First, tell me which single requirement does the most to exclude the popular answers, and whether any subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable — my own suspicion is that demanding an explanans which is an internal, pre-existing property of one unit cannot be combined with demanding that the units be treated as mutually constituting through trade and empire, and if that is right the list is itself a negative result about the question rather than a filter on answers to it; say whether you agree or whether the conflict can be dissolved. Second, mark which of the demands commonly made in this debate are camp prejudices rather than requirements — the insistence on a single prime mover, the insistence that the explanation be quantified, the insistence that luck and contingency not do real explanatory work, the insistence that credit go to endogenous features of a civilization rather than to its interactions — and then give me a two-line-per-family scorecard of the accounts I named above against the finished list, with the weight kept firmly on the criteria. Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, naming the most exclusionary requirement and stating whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable, and then give the reasoning; no preamble, no restatement of the question. A table is fine for the list.
