Lewis's Principal Principle holds that when the chance of an event A is x, an agent whose evidence comprises that chance hypothesis together with evidence that is admissible ought to have credence x in A. The admissibility clause carries more weight than its parenthetical placement in the formulation suggests: it must screen off not only direct information about whether A occurs, but anything else capable of moving credence in A except by way of moving credence about the chances themselves.

Please construct an argument for the following position. Taken literally, the admissibility condition is satisfied by no actual agent at any actual time, because virtually all ordinary empirical evidence is entangled with outcomes to which the relevant chance hypothesis assigns chances, and is therefore inadmissible. Rather than treating this as a fatal objection, argue that it exposes a miscategorization: the Principal Principle is not a norm governing the credences of real agents at all, but a constitutive principle fixing what chance is. Chance is that quantity which determines the credences of an idealized agent whose total evidence consists of the chance hypothesis and nothing else. Actual deference to chance is then a matter of approximation to that limit, and the familiar pathologies — above all the undermining problem, and the repair that makes the object of deference the chance conditional on the whole chance hypothesis rather than the chance outright — are artifacts of deploying a constitutive principle as though it were a rule that an agent follows.

Your argument should do the following:

(a) Explain precisely why admissibility is so demanding that ordinary evidence almost never qualifies, and why this failure is systematic rather than the product of unlucky case selection.

(b) Draw the constitutive/regulative distinction sharply enough that your reading is not a mere redescription of the standard one, and show how it alters what would count as a counterexample to the Principle.

(c) Confront the strongest rival account, on which a chance function is simply an expert and the Principle is one instance of the general requirement to defer to those you take to be better informed than yourself. Say why expert-deference either fails to capture the Principle or collapses into your constitutive reading.

(d) Address the charge that your view leaves actual agents with no guidance concerning chances, since a principle no one can follow cannot govern anyone. Concede or resist as you judge best, but be explicit about what remains normative on your account, and about whether the notion of chance retains any explanatory role once the Principle is read this way.

Argue from the structure of the concepts rather than from the history of the debate. You need not cite anyone, and you should not spend space surveying existing positions: the value of the answer lies in whether the constitutive reading can actually be made to bear the weight placed on it, and in how honestly it reports what that reading costs.
