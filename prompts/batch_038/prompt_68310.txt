Quick one from my metaphysics seminar. A student was presenting on the identity of indiscernibles — the principle being, roughly, that there's no numerical difference without some difference in how things are — and the whole thing turned into an arguement between two of my PhD students in my office afterwards. I mostly just sat there making coffee, so I never got a clean read on who actually won. I'd like it gone through cold, by someone who wasn't in the room.

Background so the moves make sense: they were arguing about Black's symmetric universe — exactly two iron spheres, same diameter, same colour, same temperature, same everything intrinsic, a fixed distance apart in an otherwise empty space. Ines thinks such a world is possible and so the principle is false. Tobias thinks the case is bogus. Here's the exchange as best I can reconstrue it:

1. Tobias opened with the familiar complaint that the description already gives the game away: to say the spheres are a fixed distance apart is to place them in a space, and once you have a space they differ by where they are, so they're discernible after all.

2. Ines said that just shows Black picked a bad example. Strip the space out: a world with exactly two things and no spatial or indeed any other relation between them, each with exactly the same intrinsic character. Same argument, no coordinates.

3. Tobias: you can't strip it out. A sphere is a shape, and shapes are ways of occupying space, so 'non-spatial sphere' is a contradiction and your cleaned-up case describes nothing at all.

4. Ines: fine, drop the spheres. Use whatever your favourite fundamental stuff is — two exactly similar instances of the same basic property, no relations. If you insist the case be stated in the vocabulary of a particular kind of object, you're quietly changing the subject from whether bare numerical difference is possible to whether spheres are spatial.

5. Tobias then made what I thought was the most intresting move. He said the case can only be *stated* by someone who has already abandoned the principle. To describe a world as containing exactly two such things you have to individuate them, at least implicitly, and there are only two options: a qualitative ground of difference, in which case they're discernible and the principle survives; or a bare thisness, in which case you've assumed the principle is false in order to describe a world that refutes it. Either way the thought experiment begs the question.

6. Ines said that's a use/mention slide. You don't have to individuate anything to say there are two of them: I can truly say that two grains of sand on this beach are exactly alike without being able to tell you which two, and my inability to point isn't doing any work in the truth of the claim. She then offered a test to make 'qualitative' non-circular — a property is genuinely qualitative iff it is had by the same things in any world that duplicates this one exactly, where duplication is spelled out purely in terms of how things are — and said that once you define it that way Tobias's complaint collapses into a verbal dispute: the two of them agree about which worlds there could be and about everything true in them, and disagree only about whether 'there are two' is a further, non-qualitative fact. Tobias said the test is useless in precisely the cases it was built for, since a duplicate of a symmetric world just is that symmetric world, so nothing in it can fail the test.

They went round on 6 for another twenty minutes and I lost the thread.

So: can you adjudicate this move by move rather than just naming a winner? For each of the six, give me a rating from clearly wrong to clearly right plus a sentence or two on why. Then answer specifically — (a) does move 3 land, i.e. is Ines actually obliged to keep the spheres; (b) is move 5 a real question-begging charge, or does the sand-grain analogy in 6 genuinely answer it; (c) does Ines's duplication test succeed in making 'qualitative' non-circular, and is the closing exchange a genuine verbal dispute or is one of them right that there's a substantive fact at stake; (d) who's more right overall; and (e) what should a third student have said that neither of them did. I'm using this for a methods session, so I care more about the shape of the reasoning than about a verdict I'd be willing to defend in print.
