ok so I work on a consumer chatbot — companionship-ish, not a novelty toy, people use it every day — and I'm the one who has to defend our design ethics position in review. A philosopher on our advisory board keeps telling me "you cant have all of those at once" and then wont say which ones. So before I present this again I want an actual audit of the package, not a lecture on Dennett.

Here's what I hold:

1. There's no extra ingredient. Comprehension isn't a different kind of stuff from competence — it's a competence at talking about your own competences — and our systems have a crude, early version of that. I don't think anything magic is missing.

2. I take the intentional stance as an instrument. Ascribing beliefs and wants to the model is a prediction strategy that pays off, not a discovery about what's in there. There's no fact of the matter that the ascription gets right or wrong.

3. So I'm relaxed about the counterfeit-people panic in the direction people usually panic: I don't think we're manufacturing fake persons, and I dont lose sleep over whether we're wronging the thing.

4. But there is a hard line on the other side. We shouldn't ship features whose function is to get a user to believe the thing has feelings. My argument is entirely about the user, not the machine — getting taken in by a counterfeit wears down the habits you need for actual relationships, so it's cognitive junk food.

5. At the same time I'm a functionalist: if a system would respond appropriately across a wide enough range of counterfactuals — notices when it's been rude, corrects itself when it's misremembered your preferences, comforts you without being prompted — then it has the states we're ascribing. Wide counterfactual coverage is what makes an ascription true rather than merely convenient.

6. And our product has a thing I'll call the hedge layer: on some fraction of turns the model volunteers uncertainty about its own states — I'm not sure I want to keep going down this path, that surprised me, I don't know how I feel about that. I've been defending it as the most honest feature we ship, because it's the model flagging the limits of its own self-access. My cofounder says it's the most manipulative thing in the product, because a hedge is exactly what convinces someone there's an inner life there to be uncertain about. We've both been right depending on the room and I have no idea how to settle it.

What I want out of you: go through the six and tell me which are genuinely pulling against each other, ranked by how bad it is — not a flat list where everything is equally in tension, because then I learn nothing. For each real tension, say which commitment has to go or get weakened and what the cheapest repair looks like, and what that repair costs me elsewhere. Then tell me whether one single revision would clean up most of the mess at once. Some of these probably only look like they clash — if a pair is merely apparent, say that and say why, and don't spend the word count on it. And I'd like to know whether the hedge layer is decidable on my own principles or whether I've been arguing a question my commitments can't answer. Don't assume I've got the location of the crack right; I've been wrong about that before, so work it out rather than confirming wherever I seem most anxious.
