Spent 38 years as a marine biologist, mostly staring at pigment patterns on snail shells, and I've only picked up philosophy since retiring — so forgive an old empiricist for wanting the spec before the products.

My catalog was sorted by motif: bands, single lines, rows of dots, zigzags, chevrons, tents, blotches, and those little streaks running across the bands we called flames. Here's the wrinkle that's been eating at me. A shell is a time-series that got written down and frozen — the growing edge lays down pattern as it extends, so the surface is a record of a one-dimensional process running over time. Which means two shells can carry the same bands on the outside and have gotten there by completely different schedules, and the record doesn't tell you which. The pattern and the process that produced it come apart, and my motifs don't obviously belong to one side or the other.

Now the philosophy of analogy I've been reading — the stuff about how a concept slips when you map one thing onto another, like those letter-string puzzles where the alphabet runs out at one end and "rightmost" has to slide over into something else — looks to me like exactly my problem. Is a row of dots a dashed line, or is it dots? Does a chevron slide into a zigzag or into a tent? And when I compare two shells, am I comparing the frozen patterns or the schedules that made them? I don't want answers yet. I want to know what I'm entitled to demand of an answer.

So: write the desiderata. What would a theory of conceptual slippage have to deliver to be adequate to a microdomain like mine? For each requirement give me the case or the consideration behind it, not just the label. Say which ones are non-negotiable and which are really a camp's own commitments wearing a requirement's clothes — I suspect there are more of the second kind than the books admit. And if some subset can't be jointly satisfied, say so straight out: my hunch is that being fluid enough to permit slippage at all and being constrained enough to say which slippages are licensed are pulling in opposite directions, and if that's right then the list is the result, not the warm-up to one.

Adequate for what, since that's the whole question: I want a theory good enough that, handed a new pair of shells and a half-formed analogy, it tells me which slippages a competent observer would license and which are a stretch, and predicts which of my borderline catalog entries people will genuinely disagree about. Anything that can't do that is decoration as far as I'm concerned.

Stuff I'd guess belongs in there, use it or throw it out: fit has to come in degrees rather than yes/no; it has to explain why some cases feel obvious and some feel arbitrary rather than just reporting that they do; repeated runs on the same pair should cluster without the whole thing being deterministic; it has to work on pairs nobody has ever seen, so no lookup tables; it has to be something you could actually build and run, not a story about what an ideal observer would notice; and it mustn't peek at the answer key to decide after the fact which slippage was "right." Add whatever I've missed — especially anything about the vocabulary itself, because I could never find a border between band and stripe, and I want to know whether a theory is allowed to just accept that or has to explain it.

Then tell me which single requirement does the most damage to the candidates on offer. Finish with a couple of lines each on how the Copycat-style architecture, structure-mapping theory, and a plain similarity-metric account (the contrast-model variety) do against your list — brief, no big scorecard. The thinking is in the list; the scoring is a garnish.
