I'm TA-ing a philosophy-of-economics seminar this term — fourteen people, half of them econ majors who've had intermediate micro, half philosophy students who've had no economics at all but have read some Mill and Rawls. Next Thursday I've got the whole 75 minutes to myself and the topic is interpersonal comparisons of utility. I don't want you to explain the issue to me, I've got a decent handle on it — what I need is a plan for how to teach it: what has to come first, what depends on what, and where in the sequence each piece should hit.

The spine I'm toying with is that "interpersonal comparison is impossible" isn't one claim but three seperate ones, and that a lot of the history is people arguing past each other. There's a psychological claim about whether I can detect or know how much pleasure you're having; a measurement claim about whether there's any fact of the matter to a statement like "A's gain is twice B's loss", i.e. whether utility assignments for different people can share a unit and a zero; and a normative claim about how much weight another person's wellbeing gets in my deliberation. My sense is that the Robbins-style methodological hostility is aimed mostly at the first two but gets read as if it settled the third, and that Arrow's impossibility theorem isn't a general refutation of summing wellbeing — it's a result about how little information the social welfare function is allowed to use, so the moment you permit comparisons on a common scale it becomes escapable. Tell me whether that three-way distinction is actually strong enough to carry the session or whether it should come in late as a way of tidying up, and if it is the spine, in what order the three claims should be pulled apart.

I want one carrying example, not five. Maybe a two-person allocation table with just ordinal rankings, where no social ranking is determined, and then the same table with a common cardinal scale filled in, where a ranking pops out — tell me what that example has to have and what it must deliberately not have, because I worry that if the numbers are too neat the students will conclude the whole problem is arithmetic. I also want a map of the misconceptions I should expect and the exact point where each should be headed off. Two are already visible from the pre-session emails: one student thinks cost-benefit analysis and the Kaldor-Hicks criterion smuggle comparisons back in under a different name, and another thinks empathetic imagination just settles the matter, which is basically Harsanyi's impartial-observer move without him knowing it. So tell me whether that's the right moment to bring in Harsanyi on extended preferences and Sen's complaint about people whose preferences have adapted to their own deprivation, or whether both are too much for this crowd and should be flagged as beyond-the-session material. And finally: one question I can ask in the last five minutes that would show me whether the three-way distinction actually landed, plus a short list of what I should openly refuse to cover at this level (proofs, the informational-bases taxonomy, Harsanyi's aggregation theorem) and say so out loud rather than let them flail. No readings — I've got the material, I need the sequencing and the misconception map.
