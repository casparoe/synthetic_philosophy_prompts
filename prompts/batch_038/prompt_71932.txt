ok so I've been going in circles on this for like three days and I want somebody to tell me where the bodies are buried. Context: there's a proposal floating around my local democracy-nerd group where each parent gets an extra half-weight ballot per minor child, split between the parents if both claim it, capped at one extra full ballot per parent. Basically demeny voting with a cap bolted on. A friend of mine thinks arguing about the cap is missing the point because the whole family of proposals is unsalvageable, and he wrote out an argument for that. I don't accept the conclusion but I can't find the bad step, which is annoying because hes using my own premises to do it.

(1) A collective decision is legitimate only if everyone affected by it has an equal say in producing it.
(2) Children are affected by basically everything the state does — more than adults, arguably, since they'll live with the consequences longer and can't exit.
(3) So if children have no say, the state's decisions are illegitimate with respect to them.
(4) A two-year-old can't form or express a political preference, so her say has to be exercised by somebody else, and the only non-arbitrary somebody is a parent.
(5) But a parent casting her own ballot plus her children's now has more voting power than a childless adult. The children's say has been converted into the parent's extra say, which violates (1) just as badly as disenfranchising them did.
(6) The patch is the fractional ballots above. But a half-weight ballot isn't a vote, it's a weight attached to a person's influence. Once you concede that influence can be weighted by something other than one-person-one-ballot, nothing in (1) rules out weighting by stakes, or by competence, or by taxes paid. (1) has been cashed out into its opposite.
(7) So either (1) is unimplementable for the largest class of affected people, or implementing it licenses exactly the inequality it was supposed to forbid.
(8) Therefore (1) is false. Equal say isn't what legitimacy consists in. And note this isn't just about kids — the same move kills the principled case for votes at 16 and for votes for permanent residents, and leaves you with prudence all the way down.

My gut says something goes wrong somewhere around (5)-(6) but I can't name it, and I also don't trust (8) — it feels like he's slid from "this is hard to implement" to "the principle is false," wich seems too fast. But here's my problem: if I try to block (4) by saying fine, no ballots for the under-12s, just real ones at 16, haven't I conceded that what (1) actually tracks is capacity rather than affectedness? That was his whole point.

So: which step is the load-bearing error, why did it look fine the first four times I read it, and what does each repair actually cost — (a) resisting (2) and saying kids aren't affected in the sense (1) needs, (b) denying that a proxy ballot converts a right into power, (c) accepting weighted ballots and trying to hold the line at (6)? I'd rather keep (1) than (8) if I have to pick, but I want the price tag, not the reassurance. Also worth saying whether the cap does any independent damage that the uncapped version doesn't, since that's the thing we were actually arguing about.

And please be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who work on democratic theory and which are live fights — I don't want to walk away thinking there's a consensus diagnosis here when there probably isn't, and I'd much rather you flag the places where the field just disagrees than smooth them over.
