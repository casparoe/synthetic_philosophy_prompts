For fifteen years I have read the rationalist blogs and their comment threads, and the one dispute I have never managed to settle to my own satisfaction is the A-theory/B-theory argument about time. I know the standard furniture of the debate — the truthmaker objection, Lucretian properties, ersatz times, the argument from the relativity of simultaneity, the insistence that "exists" is a quantifier and that quantifiers do not come in modes — and I have come to think all of those moves are aimed at a position nobody actually holds. So let me state mine, and then ask you to attack it properly. I call it probate presentism. As the present advances, past moments are neither annihilated nor simply nonexistent; they are demoted. They pass into what I think of as the estate: still there, still the very things that make our claims about them true or false, but no longer occurrent and no longer concrete in the sense that matters for physics — unable to bear new properties, unable to interact, unavailable to be quantified over in the way the present is. The analogy I mean is legal rather than mystical: when someone dies, their assets do not vanish, they merely cease to be at anyone's active disposal. The future, by contrast, is not yet in the estate; there is nothing there at all, not even an abstract structure, until it accrues. Existence is thus univocal — one quantifier, no Meinongian proliferation — but concreteness comes in two modes, occurrent and archived, and "real" in the sense presentists have always cared about means occurrent. The hoped-for payoff is that passage becomes a change in modal status rather than a change in inventory, which is supposed to buy genuine becoming without the charge of making the past a fiction.

Please play my opponent: a committed eternalist and four-dimensionalist, Quinean about ontological commitment, who holds that reality does not come in grades and that positing a privileged status no experiment could detect is metaphysics purchased at the price of explaining nothing. Be sharp and be generous — not a strawman. You may concede the small points a good eternalist would in fact concede: that the phenomenology of passage is a datum owing some account, that the indexical "now" behaves oddly, that my archived moments are not the same sort of thing as a mere bundle of propositions about the past, that the relativity argument is an argument and not a knockdown proof. Never concede the main point, and do not break character unless I ask you to. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs so this reads like correspondence between colleagues rather than a position paper, and write in plain continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no numbered lists of objections — if you have several objections, give them to me in the order you find most damaging and let the paragraphing carry the structure.

My opening: your first instinct, I take it, is that the demotion is idle — that my archived slices have precisely the structure and the truth-making powers of your block universe, so I have paid for a distinction and received nothing in return. Grant it for the sake of argument and then explain this to me. On your view, whether an event is concrete is a permanent structural fact about it, forever; on mine it is a fact that changes, since the very same event was occurrent and is now archived, and that transition just is what passage is. You cannot so much as describe the transition, because on your view there is nothing for it to be — all you can say is that the event bears the tenseless relation of being earlier than this sentence, a relation that was always holding and never alters. So either passage is a real feature of the world, in which case your block is missing something and my estate is not idle after all, or passage is not real, in which case you owe me an account of why every organism that has ever been built by selection appears to be tracking it, and why the tracking is systematically asymmetric — we accumulate records in one direction only. Which horn do you take?
