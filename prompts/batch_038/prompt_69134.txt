I am preparing a seminar paper on religion in Middlemarch and I want a reading that is not the usual summary of Eliot as a humanist who kept the feelings and discarded the doctrines. Please work inside the following framing, which I want treated as an instrument rather than a theme.

Marian Evans translated Strauss's Life of Jesus, Critically Examined (1846) and Feuerbach's The Essence of Christianity (1854), and Feuerbach's central claim — that God is the human species' own nature projected outward, that theology is therefore disguised anthropology, and that the task is to take the predicates back and hold them as human — gives a sharp test for a novel crowded with pious people. So: for each religious life in the book, ask what exactly is being projected, whether the character ever gets it back, and what the novel does with the gap between recovering the projection and losing what it promised.

Take the following five movements in order.

1. The Prelude on Saint Teresa and the later-born Theresas: the point is that these women had no shared religious framework of the kind that gave Teresa's ardor an epic medium. Is that a Feuerbachian diagnosis of religion's social function, or does the framing quietly preserve something religion supplied that humanity has not replaced?

2. Casaubon's Key to All Mythologies as a parody of the very demythologizing Strauss performed — the search for the origin of all myths undertaken as a pious life's work. Why is his project not liberating, given that it is doing roughly the right kind of scholarship?

3. Farebrother, the latitudinarian vicar who plays whist for money and knows his own worldliness without anguish. Is he the novel's implicit ideal — a religion already cashed out into attention, fellowship, and decency — or its affectionate joke?

4. Bulstrode as the dangerous case. Evangelical providentialism looks like the most corrosive form of projection, because the projector mistakes his own wishes for God's decrees; and Eliot, who had written for the Westminster Review about the moral damage of evangelical preaching, is not neutral here. Attend to the deathbed of Raffles, where Bulstrode permits a death he wants and reads it afterward as a deliverance he did not will. This may be something Feuerbach's account cannot do: not merely the claim that God is us, but a mechanism by which one particular self hears its own voice returned to it as divine command. Decide whether that mechanism is moral psychology, or whether it presupposes something specific about religious language.

5. The Finale's insistence that the world's growing good depends in part on unhistoric acts and on lives faithfully lived in obscurity. Is that a genuinely secular substitute for providence, or providence with the name removed — an assurance that hidden fidelity adds up to something, which is precisely the transcendental backing Eliot had given up?

Then say where the instrument breaks: name a scene, character, or moment that resists being handled by the transposition, and be explicit about where the Feuerbachian reading distorts the novel in order to fit it. Finish by stating what Middlemarch contributes to the debate over a religion of humanity that the philosophy cannot say on its own.

Constraints: 1,000 to 1,400 words of continuous prose, with a heading for each movement if you wish. Do not quote the novel verbatim — I do not have the text to hand and would rather have your paraphrase than a half-remembered line — and no bibliography, no suggested further reading, no footnotes. Open with your bottom line: a single sentence stating whether Middlemarch completes the Feuerbachian transposition of religion into humanism, stops short of it, or does something else entirely. Then develop the reasoning from there, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
