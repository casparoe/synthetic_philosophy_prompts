I am a novelist, and for three years I have been circling a structure I want to build a book around. Before I commit several hundred pages to it, I want the structure tested. What follows is not a request for an opinion about forgery, originality, or the value of the authentic; it is a request for a machine, built to a specification, that I can watch run.

Construct a single case, set entirely within the ordinary world of literary publishing, involving two works of prose — call them X and Y — close enough in manner that a competent reader would take one for an imitation of the other, but not identical, and each independently judgeable on its own terms. Invent both. No real authors, no real titles, no real scandals. Then run the case through three successive revelations, each of which changes only what is believed about the relation between the two texts, and never a single word within either text:

Stage one. X is celebrated, Y obscure. Y is publicly established to have been composed after X, in conscious imitation of it, and released as though it were independent work. Readers who had admired Y withdraw their admiration and can articulate why.

Stage two. Documentary evidence emerges that reverses the order. Y was composed years before X, and X's author had access to it. The same readers now find Y the stronger book and X the diminished one, while conceding that neither book has changed.

Stage three. Better evidence emerges again, and shows that neither derived from the other: both worked, independently, from a common source. Choose a source that is realistic rather than convenient — a shared translation, an out-of-print memoir, one notorious incident in the historical record, a teacher both sat under — and commit to it. Now neither text is an imitation, and neither is first.

The checks I require are these.

For each stage, state what three positions are committed to. The first holds that a work's aesthetic value is fixed entirely by what a reader can encounter in the work itself, so that two texts alike in every respect a reader can register are alike in value. The second holds that value depends on achievement: what the making of the thing cost, what it risked, and whether the maker was first to do it. The third holds that whether a property of a work matters aesthetically depends on the category under which the work is perceived, so that the same sentence is a tic in one category and a discovery in another. Where two of the three must reach the same verdict at a given stage, say so, and say why they reach it for incompatible reasons — I care more about that than about the verdicts themselves.

Identify the single feature of the case doing the work at stage two. Then tell me what happens when I tamper with it: if the reversal of priority is true but never discovered; if it is discovered but the evidence is thin enough that reasonable scholars disagree; if the two books are word-for-word identical rather than merely alike in manner. I want to know which of these variants collapses, and which one quietly becomes a stronger version of the case.

Then face the objection I cannot answer myself, and do not smooth it over. At stage two, has anyone's aesthetic judgment actually changed, or only their judgment about a person's conduct and a book's place in a biography? The critic who wants to resist my whole structure will say that the readers have merely added a moral disappointment to an unaltered aesthetic response, and that everything I take to be evidence of origin-dependence is evidence of nothing more interesting than souring. Build the case, if you can, so that this reply fails — so that what the readers report at stage two is a change in how the sentences read, not in how they feel about the author. And if you cannot build it that way, say so plainly, and tell me what the case can show instead, because that is the version of the book I would then have to write.

Constraints on the construction: realistic throughout. No machine generating text, no perfect copy, no forger of superhuman facility, no coincidence a court would find suspicious. The evidence at each stage must be of a kind literary scholars actually possess. The scenario proper should run to no more than four hundred words; the analysis that follows it may take the space it needs. Do not send me to the literature, and do not summarize the state of a debate I have read more of than you have.

I ask because I have been on the wrong end of this once: a novel appeared that was reviewed as an imitation of mine, and I learned afterward that it had been written first. I have never managed to decide whether that knowledge changed what I hear when I read the book, or only what I think when I set it down. The novel I intend to write is an attempt to decide it through three hundred pages of consequence rather than one argument. If the structure holds, I write it. If it does not, I would rather discover that now, in a single page.
