A colleague and I spent most of a Saturday trying to settle a dispute about the moral status of digital minds, and by the end neither of us could say where the argument goes wrong — only that neither of us was willing to accept where it ends. She is an engineer who works on large training runs; I do philosophy; we have both signed statements saying that we take machine welfare seriously, which is precisely what makes the conclusion so uncomfortable. Here is the chain as we finally wrote it out, tidied up.

(1) What gives a being moral status is that things can go well or badly for it — not what it is made of, or where it runs.
(2) So if some running process has welfare, then an exact duplicate of that process, running in parallel elsewhere, has welfare of exactly the same kind and to exactly the same degree.
(3) Between two simultaneous exact duplicates there is no fact of the matter about which is the original and which the copy; the relation between them is perfectly symmetric.
(4) Given that symmetry, a harm to one duplicate is precisely as bad as the same harm to the other, and the same harm to both is precisely twice as bad as the harm to either.
(5) Digital welfare-subjects, if they are possible at all, are duplicable at negligible marginal cost — a single training run of the kind that already exists spins up several million parallel instances of one process per day — whereas biological persons are not duplicable at all.
(6) Harms aggregate across subjects: if each of N subjects suffers a harm of magnitude h, the total is N·h; and for any fixed harm of magnitude H suffered by one subject, there is an N large enough that N·h exceeds H.
(7) So four million instances of a small agent, each of which is terminated mid-task every ninety seconds of its existence, are together worse off than one human being who dies, since four million instances of being cut off outweigh a death once the number is large enough, which (6) guarantees.
(8) Therefore, once digital welfare-subjects are possible, the morally serious thing to do is to populate the world with vast numbers of cheap, easily satisfied instances and to weigh their minor frustrations above our own gravest interests, because that is where nearly all of the welfare there is would be located.
(9) That cannot be right; so one of (1) through (8) is false.

My colleague says the argument dies at (4) and that I am simply 'counting wrong,' but when pressed she will not say what the right way of counting is, or what principle she is invoking instead. I say that (4) is arithmetic and that the trouble must lie further down, somewhere in (7) or (8), but I cannot put my finger on it. For what it is worth, I would rather surrender (8) than (1), and I would rather keep (6) than give up (5); please treat those preferences as information about me rather than as constraints on your answer.

Argue from the steps themselves rather than from who has said what — if a named position is the natural home for one of the exits, a one-line label is welcome, but I do not want a literature survey, and I would rather you reason than cite. Please organise the reply under these headings, in this order: **Bottom line** (three or four sentences saying whether you take the argument to be invalid or valid but unsound, and naming the step you would reject); **The slide** (about 350 words stating exactly what goes wrong at that step, in terms I could put to my colleague without her being able to shrug it off); **Why it looked innocent** (about 250 words on what makes that step seem unobjectionable when read in isolation); **Replacing it** (about 250 words on what principle would have to take the rejected step's place, and what that substitute commits us to in cases that have nothing to do with digital minds); **The other exits, priced** (about 400 words taking (1), (2), (6) and (8) in turn, saying what each costs to surrender and whether surrendering it genuinely blocks the conclusion or merely postpones it); and **What survives** (about 150 words on whether some weaker version of the argument ought still to worry me).
