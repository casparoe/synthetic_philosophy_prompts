I am a third-year philosophy student in a seminar on the philosophy of perception, and I would appreciate some help disentangling a move that was made in this week's session.

The question on the table was whether perceptual experience is cognitively penetrable — specifically, whether the Müller-Lyer arrows count as evidence against penetration, on the grounds that knowing which line is longer does not make the two lines look equal. My professor waved the question aside with the remark that all observation is theory-laden, that this was settled decades ago, and that the modularist picture of an encapsulated visual system is a relic. I left unconvinced, partly because it seemed to me that the word "theory" is doing very different work in the two claims being compared, and partly because I cannot see why anything established about how scientists describe their evidence should bear on whether experience itself, as opposed to what we say about it, is influenced by belief.

Could you lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses that "all observation is theory-laden" might be standing for? Among the candidates I have in mind: that there is no neutral observation language in which rival theories could be tested; that what one notices and finds salient depends on what one already knows; that perceptual reports and confidence are shifted by expectation and by what an experimental subject thinks is wanted; that the phenomenal character of experience is causally altered by one's beliefs and desires; the stronger claim that scientists wedded to incompatible theories literally see different things, so there is no common evidence to appeal to; and the claim that vision works by combining incoming signal with top-down expectations, where an "expectation" here may be something implemented within the visual hierarchy rather than anything a subject believes.

For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible thesis, false, or merely underdetermined, and what would settle it. I would especially like to know how to tell whether a reported effect is evidence about experience or only about judgment — what the difference between a shift in sensitivity and a shift in the criterion for reporting amounts to in this context.

Then please answer two further questions. First: which of these readings, if true, would actually license my professor's dismissal, and which are perfectly compatible with the Müller-Lyer datum? Second: is the historical claim correct — did the mid-century theory-ladenness arguments really dispose of the question of cognitive penetrability, or were they doing something else altogether? Please be willing to declare some of the readings false rather than hedging across all of them.
