I direct the policy analysis unit of a mid-sized city's human services department. Beginning next fiscal year we will administer a single consolidated household support payment that replaces four categorical benefits. Our enabling ordinance sets the payment level by reference to households' "standard of living in the city" and does not say how that is to be measured, so the choice of measure is ours to make, and it will be contested. The council's budget office has commissioned a wellbeing framework built on life-satisfaction surveys. A coalition of disability organizations argues that the payment must track what households are actually able to do and be, not what they happen to hold. Our legal office wants something administrable and defensible on appeal. We score roughly forty program designs and revisions a year; a single design gets one analyst one afternoon; the analysts are not philosophers; and everything we produce can be challenged before an administrative law judge.

What I need is a decision procedure an analyst can run on any proposed measurement or targeting rule: a worksheet with the questions in order, a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the evidence types that may be admitted at each step and how they rank against one another, the point at which the procedure returns a stop, and the points at which it hands the question up to the council because the issue is one of values rather than fact. It should be grounded in whatever the argument about the right currency of equality establishes that is genuinely usable here — the objection that income and goods are a poor proxy because households differ sharply in what they can convert a given amount into, the objection that reported satisfaction is a poor proxy because expectations adjust downward to circumstances, and the objection that compensating people for preferences they have simply grown into rewards the wrong thing. Tell me which of these distinctions must survive the simplification into a worksheet and which I can drop without distortion, and be explicit about what the procedure cannot settle and never will.

It must also handle failure states: what the analyst does when the relevant evidence cannot be obtained, when the only available proxy is one we are not lawfully permitted to collect, and when two admissible measures point in opposite directions for the same population.

Then run the procedure on three cases. First, a winter energy supplement gated on metered household income, where the coalition has produced data showing that households with mobility-limited or chronically ill members spend considerably more to reach the same indoor temperature, and that single-occupancy older households in our pre-1940 housing stock face costs income alone does not capture. Second, an allocation of discretionary neighborhood funds ranked by average life satisfaction under the council's index, where two of the city's most deprived neighborhoods score in the top third and the index would therefore move money away from them. Third, a proposed supplement for unpaid family carers, paid according to an in-home assessment of the carer's own condition — sleep, health, social contact — rather than by hours of care given or earnings forgone. On the third case I should tell you that I hope the procedure endorses it; if it does not, say plainly why not and what the second-best administrable rule would be.

I want the machinery, not the scholarship: no citations, no reading list, no attributions. Two pages an analyst could actually hold while working.
