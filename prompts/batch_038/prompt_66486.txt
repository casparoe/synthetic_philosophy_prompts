I sit on my campus paper's editorial board and we've been going in circles about whether to print the slur someone spraypainted on a dorm wall, in the story about the incident. The editor who wants it printed keeps reaching for the same picture: quotation marks are like protective gloves. The word is toxic material, but if you insulate it you can handle it safely and nobody gets contaminated, so the gloves settle the question — put it in quotes, print it, move on. I told him that's wrong, quoting it is more like playing a tape recording of the slur over a loudspeaker: what comes out of the speaker is the same noise either way, gloves or no gloves. He said I'm confusing the container with the contents.

Can you audit the gloves analogy properly instead of letting one of us win on vibes? I want the correspondences laid out explicitly — what plays the toxin, what are the gloves, what counts as exposure, who's the handler, who's the bystander downwind — and then where the mapping breaks. In particular: does the picture quietly assume that a slur's offensiveness is a substance that travels on contact, and what happens to it under the three different things people actually mean by that — offensiveness as part of the word's ordinary truth-conditional content, as expressive content that projects straight out of quotes and negation and conditionals and everything else, or as something located in the act of tokening the word at all rather than in the word? Also seperate the disanalogies that actually bear on our decision from the decorative ones, and take into account that the gloves aren't uniform: a headline is not the same embedding as a buried body paragraph, and scare quotes aren't obviously doing the same job as reporting quotes.

Then tell me whether "put it in quotation marks and print it" survives the audit, and whether my loudspeaker version is any better or whether there's a third analogy that carries the point both of us are missing.
