I am a senior at a small public high school, and this year I am doing an independent study in philosophy with the teacher who coaches our ethics bowl team. My assignment is a long paper on what well-being consists in, and I decided early on to argue for some kind of hedonism, mostly because I thought it would be the simplest position to defend. That plan collapsed about two weeks ago, when I tried to write down what pleasures actually have in common. My teacher told me to stop reading about the problem and instead inventory my own verdicts about particular experiences, so that is what I have done. I would rather you work through the analysis with me than direct me to further sources; my school library has essentially nothing on this and I cannot access journals from home.

Here are the verdicts, in the order I wrote them down. (1) Ice water after a four-mile run on a humid day: unquestionably a pleasure, and it seems to have a distinctive feel to it, a cool spreading relief at the back of the throat. (2) The moment a geometry proof I had been stuck on for two days suddenly falls into place: also unquestionably a pleasure, yet I cannot locate any felt quality it shares with (1) beyond a brief warmth in the chest that I suspect I am imagining. (3) A hot bath I am barely attending to because I am reading in it: I want to say the whole bath is pleasant, including the stretches in which I would honestly have to stop and check before I could tell you anything at all about how it felt. (4) My grandmother's morning crossword: she says she does not enjoy it "the way she used to," but she does it every single day without fail and would be genuinely upset if the puzzle book went missing. (5) Laughing at a pun I consider objectively bad: I cannot deny being amused, but I also want the amusement to stop and am irritated with myself for having it. (6) The surge I felt when the student who beat me at regionals flubbed her opening argument: a pleasure, and one I am ashamed of. (7) A friend's description of the euphoria from a stimulant he was prescribed: intensely pleasant, he said, and completely hollow — he would accept it if offered but would never pursue it. (8) The release when a migraine I have had since dawn finally lets go: I count that as a pleasure, though the release when a dull school assembly ends I do not, and both are simply the removal of something unpleasant. (9) A recording I love that reminds me of a friend who moved away and makes me cry: I want to say the listening is pleasant and painful at the very same time, not alternating between the two. (10) The satisfaction of straightening my desk until everything is aligned: small but unmistakable, and I do not think it is a sensation located anywhere in my body. The pair I cannot reconcile is (3) against (5): in (3) I barely notice the experience and still call it pleasant, while in (5) I notice it perfectly well and call it pleasant even though I want it gone — so neither awareness nor wanting seems to be doing the work, and I cannot see what is left.

My own tentative principle is that an experience is a pleasure just to the degree that, while you are having it, you want it to continue for its own sake. It handles (6) and (1) nicely and I think it ruins (5), and I am not sure what it says about (4) or (3), since I doubt my grandmother wants the crossword to continue in any clear sense. Please tell me whether there is a principle — it may need several clauses, and it may need to be about something other than desire — that comes close to capturing all ten. State it precisely, say which case each clause is there to accommodate, and tell me plainly which of my verdicts it forces me to surrender and which of those would be cheapest to give up. Then run it on four cases I have not listed and cannot make up my mind about: an infant; a person with severe depression who reports enjoying nothing yet keeps choosing to play the same records every evening; someone who is delighted that his team won a game that in fact was cancelled; and a monk who has deliberately trained himself not to want any experience to continue. Finally, I want your honest answer to the question underneath all of this: does the principle you end up with imply that pleasures share a single felt quality after all, or does it concede that they are heterogeneous and unified only by their relation to desire, attention, or function — and if it concedes that, has it actually given me a theory of pleasure, or has it quietly changed the subject to something else, so that my hedonism is no longer a view about a kind of experience at all?
