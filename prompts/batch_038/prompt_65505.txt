I'm a senior and I run my school's philosophy club -- usually like 14 people show up, and we get 45 minutes on Thursdays in a room with a whiteboard, a bin of index cards, and one of those rolling chairs that squeeks. I've been reading around this idea that psychiatric labels don't just describe people, they feed back on them: once a category exists and people can recognize themselves in it, they start acting to fit it, and then the people who wrote the category have to adjust it, and it loops around again. I want the club to feel that loop actually happen instead of listening to me explain it for twenty minutes.

Design a game we can run in one session. Big catch: don't use real diagnoses. Two people in my club are actually diagnosed with stuff and I dont want them turned into exhibits, so invent the categories instead. Give me three made-up conditions with names that sound official enough to take seriously but that aren't real, plus what goes on the cards and stickers.

The core thing I'm after: some of us play a diagnostic committee that writes the criteria out loud, then sorts everyone else, and that sorting has to change how the sorted people behave in the next round -- so the committee gets forced into rewriting its own rules by what it created. Build that into the mechanics and the incentives, don't make it depend on me announcing it. Three rounds with a timer, tell me exactly who does what in each one, and give the committee some reason to care about getting it "right" so they don't just goof off.

Then a debrief that maps each moment back onto the loop, tells me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (there are two kids in my club who will absolutely game any scoring system), and is honest about which part of the idea a 45-minute card game just can't reproduce that I'll have to supply myself. Nobody should hear the phrase looping effects until the debrief is nearly over.
