I audit an informal reading group that has spent the better part of a year working through theories of divine providence. I keep the group's shared write-ups, which is why I want to record last week's argument accurately: two of the regulars, Nadia and Ross, went seven rounds on Molinism, and neither could land a blow the other would concede. I share neither of their commitments — I am there for the argument, not the theology — so I would like an outside referee rather than a partisan summary. Their exchange ran roughly as follows.

(1) Nadia opened with the grounding problem, framed as a squeeze on the Molinist's own claim that middle knowledge is prevolitional: God does not decide counterfactuals of creaturely freedom, He finds them. So nothing about the divine will makes them true. Nothing about the creature makes them true either, since the creature does not yet exist and, on libertarian freedom, nothing in its nature or situation settles which way it would go — that absence of settling is precisely what the freedom being posited consists in. And the circumstances cannot make them true, since they are specified as underdetermining. With no candidate settler, Nadia said, such conditionals are neither true nor false, and middle knowledge has no content at all. (2) Ross answered that this assumes every truth requires a truthmaker, which is a substantive thesis rather than a neutral starting point, and that a theory rendering a large class of ordinary claims truth-valueless pays a heavy price: we say without hesitation that if you had offered her the post she would have declined. He added that he was not claiming infallible human access to such conditionals, only that they have truth values, and that God's cognitive situation is a limit case of ours. (3) Nadia pressed an asymmetry. In the actual sequence a brute fact can be an exercise of agency — someone does something — and agency is a tolerable stopping point for explanation; in middle knowledge the brute fact is a proposition's being true before any agent exists, before any choice is made, before any decree is issued. A truth with no subject and no author, she said, is not a stopping point but an orphan. She coupled this to a sovereignty cost: if God selects from a menu of creaturely counterfactuals He neither wrote nor can rewrite, the shape of the created order is partly fixed outside Him, which anyone holding a strong doctrine of divine aseity should find unpalatable. (4) Ross replied that the rivals fare worse, and offered a trilemma: either God determines every choice, in which case He authors sin and the libertarian freedom everyone wanted is gone; or God creates without knowing what free creatures would do, in which case providence is not meticulous, He is exposed to surprise, and any theodicy resting on His foreknowledge of the goods that will come of permitted evils loses its footing; or else middle knowledge. On aseity he said the objection proves too much, since God's will is also constrained by necessary truths He neither made nor can alter, and inability to make cruelty good has never been counted a defect — sovereignty is control over what happens, not authorship of every truth. (5) Nadia then turned to the redeemed. On the traditional fourfold scheme the glorified end up free yet unable to sin, so Molinism needs conditionals of the form: were this person in beatific circumstances, she would freely and forever choose the good. Her dilemma: if those circumstances settle the response, Ross has granted the very thing his opponent says a free agent's circumstances cannot do, and owes an account of why settling obtains in heaven and nowhere else; if they do not settle it, heaven carries a standing risk of defection, and the stability of the blessed is a matter of luck rather than providence. (6) Ross took the first horn but narrowed it: what libertarian freedom excludes is external necessitation, not every form of settling, and a choice flowing from a character the agent has confirmed in herself is settled without being compelled — which, if right, shows that the premise in (1) demands an indeterminism the libertarian has no independent reason to want, and removes the engine of the grounding objection. (7) Nadia's closing jab was that he had changed the subject: if character-in-circumstances settles the choice, the most he is entitled to is a probabilistic middle knowledge — she would very likely choose the good — and a God who manages odds rather than outcomes is exactly the gambler Ross had dismissed in (4). At that point the hour ran out, and they spent another twenty minutes restating the same two positions.

My request is that you adjudicate this move by move rather than as a whole. For each of the seven steps, give a rating — clearly mistaken, weak but pointing at something real, even, strong, or decisive — with two or three sentences of justification. Then, separately: name the unstated premise each move depends on and say whether the other party had a rebuttal available at that moment that they failed to make; identify which single move is the strongest in the exchange and which is the weakest; decide whether (5) is a genuine dilemma with only the two horns Nadia lists or whether a third horn is available, and whether Ross's reply in (6) costs him the libertarian credentials he traded on in (4); and finally say where the dispute actually bottoms out — what would have to be settled first for the rest to fall into place — together with anything of substance that both of them left on the table.
