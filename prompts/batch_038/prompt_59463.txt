Context, because this only makes sense if you know where I'm coming from: I write novels, literary-ish, and I'm four years into one that is basically a thought experiment with a pulse. The setup is close enough to Dennett's "Where Am I?" that my agent made me read it, which I did, and I liked it — the guy volunteers for a job retrieving a radioactive warhead buried under Tulsa, so they take his brain out and float it in a vat in Houston while his body (Hamlet) goes down the hole, everything stitched back together with radio transceivers, and then there's the software copy of the brain, Hubert, and a master switch with a B and an H on it, and the rest of the piece is him wandering around trying to work out where he is and which of these things is him. The story I got fine. What I did not get is the argument running underneath it, because every write-up of the positions I tried to read — the psychological continuity people, the people who say you're the animal and you go wherever the body goes, the functionalists who seem to think Yorick and Hubert are equally him and that this is not a disaster — was in a register that makes my eyes slide off the page. "Numerical identity," "branching," "relation R," "the further fact": these mean nothing to me and I have no intention of acquiring them.

What I do have, in embarrassing quantity, is a vocabulary for how a story gets told. So here's the ask. Take the three or four live positions in that debate and restate each one, claim by claim, in narrative-craft language — who speaks versus who sees, internal and external and zero focalization, free indirect style, the gap between the narrating instance and the character it narrates, unreliable narration, analepsis, diegetic levels, character-as-a-stable-pattern-of-responses-to-situations rather than character-as-a-lump-of-matter, voice, POV, and the fact that a first-person "I" in a book is stipulated rather than discovered or earned. I've taught craft workshops for years and read Genette once and skimmed him twice, so don't explain the terms back to me, just use them the way I use them. One guardrail up front: don't confuse my toolkit with the philosophers' "narrative theory of personal identity," which is a thesis somebody holds, not a set of craft terms — if you think the two overlap anywhere, say exactly where and keep them separate anyway.

Then the part I actually care about: the residue report. For each position, tell me what translated cleanly, what you had to bend out of shape to get it to fit, and what has no expression at all in craft talk. My hunch — tell me if it's dumb — is that the animalists come out worst, because narratology has no way to make a body the subject of a story; bodies only ever get described or seen-through, never narrated from, and the animal never shows up as the thing a chapter is about. I also suspect the fission cases break my vocabulary outright, since "the same character twice" isn't a move you can make on the page — the second you have two POV chapters you have two characters, full stop, and no amount of shared backstory changes that. Please don't cheat to make it prettier: if "focalization" already presupposes a single consciousness behind the pair of eyes, or if "character" already assumes the persistence the philosophers are fighting about, don't quietly cash out their problem in terms with the answer baked into them — call that distortion and show it. And finish with a verdict on the leftover. Is the residue a hole in my vocabulary, meaning there's something real about being one persistant person that craft language structurally can't say (in which case I need to know, because that might be the actual subject of my book and my editor is pushing me toward first person), or is it evidence that the disagreement is thinner than it looks, so that once you translate it the philosophers are mainly haggling over how to allocate the word "I"? No reading list, no bibliography, no page numbers — I won't read it and I don't want you inventing citations.
