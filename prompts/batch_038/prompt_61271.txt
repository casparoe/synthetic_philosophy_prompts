PhD student in animal welfare science here — my thesis is about building indicator-based rubrics for judging when some candidate system plausibly has valenced experience. You know the genre: does the system have states with positive or negative valence that it acts to prolong or terminate, does it show damage-avoidance rather than mere damage-detection, does it trade off between competing motivations at a cost, does past outcome change what it does next, is there something like a stable preference ordering that survives a change of context. We mostly point the thing at invertebrates and at neural organoids, but my supervisor wants a stress-test section with a case where the answer is supposed to be obvious, and I volunteered video game characters, on the grounds that whatever else is true the shopkeeper in an RPG is not sentient.

Two weeks in and I can't get the philosophy into my own vocabulary, whcih is annoying. Can you do the translation for me?

What I want: take the main positions on whether game characters matter morally — the "nobody is harmed so nothing you do in a game is wrong" line, the gamer's dilemma argument that there's no principled difference between virtual murder and virtual child abuse so either both are impermissible or neither is, the people who think some characters might matter in their own right, and the virtue-flavoured stuff about what the playing does to the player — and restate each of them as a claim *inside* my framework. Which indicator does it turn on, or which weighting choice, or which claim about what the rubric is even measuring? Be as faithful as you can, and do not just collapse everything into "the character scores zero on every indicator, question closed," because that's the answer I'm expecting to land on and I'd like to be told if I'm about to make a mistake.

One thing to fold in, because it's the bit I keep tripping over: my indicators are proxies. The target property is valenced experience, and what I can actually score is behaviour and its organization. Games are the case where that gap is widest, since NPC behaviour is authored to *look* like the proxy — a village that has a day/night routine, grudges, and fear responses is a machine for triggering exactly the criteria I use to infer the opposite conclusion in an octopus or a bee. So where a position in the debate turns on behavioural sophistication, tell me whether it's a claim about the proxy or about the target, and flag it if the philosophers are sliding between the two without noticing.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. For each position: does it translate cleanly into an indicator-claim; does it translate only if I bend it, and what got bent; or is it inexpressible in a framework whose entire currency is measurable properties of the system under assessment. And for the inexpressible ones I want your verdict rather than a shrug — is that a hole in my framework, i.e. is moral status simply not a function of the assessed system's properties alone, or is the original position less coherent than it sounds when you strip the vocabulary off it.

One hypothesis of mine I'd like you to test rather than adopt: my suspicion is that most of this debate isn't about the character at all, it's about the player, and that the character-talk is doing something rhetorical. If thats right, say how much of the debate it swallows and say what's left over afterwards — because whatever is left is presumably the part my rubric genuinely can't reach, and I'd rather know that now than in the viva.
