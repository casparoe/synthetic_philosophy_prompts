I am drafting the chapter of my thesis that deals with diachronic rationality and sequential choice, and before I commit to it in print I want the package audited. My reading group has been asked to attack it, and I would rather be told now which of my own claims cannot stand together. Here they are, numbered so you can refer to them:

1. When I argue for the transitivity of preference, the money-pump scenario is not an illustration sitting alongside the argument; it is the argument. The conclusion I take it to establish is a claim about the agent's attitudes — that the cyclic preference set is itself rationally defective — and not merely a claim about what she ends up doing on some occasion.

2. Concretely: an agent prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A, and can switch between them for a fee of one cent each time. She buys the full round and ends the day holding the option she started with, three cents poorer. I am confident that the full round is irrational, and I say so without qualification.

3. I am a sophisticated chooser, not a resolute one. An agent who correctly anticipates her own future choices and plans accordingly can, in principle, steer clear of exploitation without acquiring transitive preferences. I want to keep this possibility open; I am not willing to concede that the only escape from the pump is to abandon the cycle.

4. The only species of rational criticism that ultimately counts is criticism that bottoms out in a cost to the agent herself: she is predictably worse off, in terms she herself cares about, than she could have been. Coherence that pays no dividend buys no complaint, and incoherence that costs nothing is not, as such, a thing to be reproached for.

5. Permissibility is settled at the node, on the basis of the preferences and the information the agent has at that node. Decisions taken earlier in a sequence do not, just by having been taken, alter what is permissible now. In the case under (2), each individual paid switch is permissible when she comes to it.

6. Rational requirements must be feasible for an agent like me. A demand that can be met only by solving a problem she cannot solve, or by foreseeing what she has no way of foreseeing, is not a genuine requirement of rationality, however elegant it looks on paper.

What I want back is an audit rather than a survey of the literature. Identify which of these claims, singly or in combination, are in genuine conflict and which merely look that way. For each conflict you find, state precisely what has to be given up in order to remove it and what giving that up costs me elsewhere — in particular, whether it costs me the conclusion of (1), the verdict of (2), the escape route of (3), or the general principle of (4). Rank the conflicts by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list of worries, and tell me which single revision would restore the most coherence to the set as a whole. Where more than one revision would do, say which one you would make and why, rather than leaving the choice to me; and if you think one of my claims can be retained at a lower price than the obvious one, say what that price is.

Finally, if you think I have stated any of these commitments in a form that is weaker or stronger than I need, and that a differently formulated version of the same claim would survive the audit intact, tell me which one and how you would reformulate it. I will be defending these formulations in person, so I want your best reconstruction of the attack, not a diplomatic one.
