Last spring I sat in on a reading group on consciousness at a nearby university, and afterward two of the members — Mira, a doctoral student working on the metaphysics of mind, and Owen, a former physicist now in industry — argued for the better part of an hour without reaching any resolution. I was not part of the exchange; I was just taking notes, and I found my sympathies alternating from move to move without being able to say who was actually winning. I have reconstructed the argument as faithfully as I can, with the moves numbered.

1. Owen opened with the objection that epiphenomenalism undercuts itself. If phenomenal properties have no causal power, then the causal history of Mira's utterance "I am conscious" is exhausted by physical events, and that same history would have produced that same utterance in a world physically identical to ours but containing no experience at all. So her claim is neither caused nor sustained by the fact it reports, and its truth would be a coincidence.

2. Mira answered that it would not be a coincidence, because her view is not bare epiphenomenalism but a dualism with psychophysical laws. Whenever neural state N obtains, quale Q obtains, as a matter of law. N causes the judgment; Q is nomically tied to N; the judgment therefore tracks Q, and the lawful tie does the work that a causal arrow from Q to the judgment would otherwise have to do.

3. Owen said tracking is a counterfactual notion. In the nearest worlds where Q is absent or different while the physics is held fixed — worlds her own zombie argument says are genuinely possible — the judgment stays exactly as it is. A belief that would be held just the same if it were false is not sensitive to the truth, and insensitivity is unreliability.

4. Mira replied that sensitivity must be assessed over the worlds accessible given the laws, not over worlds where the laws differ. Zombie worlds require different or additional psychophysical laws, which puts them at a distance. Holding the laws fixed, had Q been different, N would have been different, and so would the judgment.

5. Owen said this is special pleading precisely where it costs her most: she invokes the metaphysical possibility of zombie worlds to defeat physicalism, then dismisses those very same worlds as nomically remote to protect her own epistemology. One and the same notion of distance cannot be stretched and shrunk to suit.

6. Mira said she is not using two standards but two accessibility relations, and that sensitivity analyses have always had to choose a relation — physicalists appeal to nomic necessity whenever it suits them. She added a further reply: the connection between an experience and a judgment about that experience need not be causal at all. On an acquaintance account, the phenomenal content of the judgment is not produced by the experience but constituted by it; misfire is not a live possibility, and the demand for a causal link is simply misplaced.

7. Owen said that either collapses into the identity theory she has spent the argument rejecting — if the judgment's content is the experience and the judgment is a neural event, she has just identified them — or it posits a third relation, constitution, that is no more explicable than the causal relation it displaced and is mysterious in exactly the way she accused physicalism of being.

8. Mira offered a comparison. Numbers cause nothing, yet nobody concludes from that alone that mathematical belief is unreliable; so the principle that knowledge requires causal contact with its object is false as a general matter, and once it is false the objection to epiphenomenalism loses its bite.

9. Owen said the comparison fails twice over. Mathematical facts are indispensable to explaining why mathematicians converge and why the proofs behave as they do, whereas phenomenal properties are, on her own stipulation, explanatorily idle. And a mathematical belief can be checked, revised, and recognizably gotten wrong; a phenomenal judgment on her account has nothing it could be checked against, so calling it reliable is empty.

10. Owen closed by saying that even if everything Mira claimed were true, she could offer it only as a third-personal hypothesis about other people's experience, never as knowledge of her own case. Mira said this simply assumes the conclusion.

What I want is an adjudication of the exchange, not a survey of the literature. Please go through moves 1 to 10 and rate each one — clearly mistaken, weak, defensible, strong, clearly right — with a sentence or two of justification per rating, assessing each argument as it was actually made rather than the best version either party could have given. Then tell me where the exchange really turns. My impression is that moves 3 through 5 hinge on which worlds count as nearby, and that moves 6 and 7 hinge on whether acquaintance can be non-causal without being an identity; I would like your view on whether those are the genuine pressure points or whether the dispute is decided somewhere else entirely. Say who has the better of it overall, and identify anything of substance that neither of them raised — I suspect at least one well-known objection to epiphenomenalism never surfaced in that hour, and I want to know which ones they skipped and whether skipping them changed the outcome. Argue from the logic of the exchange rather than from authority: I care about who is right, not about who has said what.

Finally, please say what would change your mind. Identify the consideration that, if it came out the other way, would flip your overall verdict, and point to the place in your own reasoning where you think you are most likely to have gone wrong.
