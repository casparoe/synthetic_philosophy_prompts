I am the instructor for an undergraduate seminar in the philosophy of history, and I would like your help grading one answer from the final examination. Please write your response in American English.

The examination question was worth 20 points:

"Critics of historical writing sometimes charge that a given narrative commits the Whig interpretation of history. Explain what that charge amounts to, what Herbert Butterfield took to be objectionable about such writing, and whether he thought historians could realistically avoid it. Support your answer with one example drawn from the history of science."

Here is the student's answer, reproduced in full:

"The Whig interpretation of history is the practice of writing about the past as though it were the opening chapter of a story whose final chapter is us. The Whig historian picks out the figures and events that led to modern outcomes — representative government, modern science — and rates them according to how much they contributed: those who advanced these things become heroes, and those who obstructed them become villains or obstacles. Butterfield attacked this way of writing in his short book of 1931, taking as his target a long tradition of British liberal historiography.

His central objection, as I read him, is that it is anachronistic. The Whig historian projects present concepts onto past actors, so that a medieval jurist turns out to be arguing about rights in our sense and an ancient astronomer turns out to be only half a Copernican. The past is made to speak in a vocabulary it never had, and so it stops being another country and becomes merely an earlier version of our own.

If that is the problem, the remedy looks straightforward. Butterfield wants historians to study the past for its own sake: to choose their material without reference to anything that happened afterward, and to refrain from asking whether particular figures helped or hindered the present. He does recognize that this is difficult, since the sheer volume of evidence forces every historian to abridge, and abridgement is governed by present concerns. But that concession seems to undo his program. If selection is unavoidable and always interest-laden, then on Butterfield's own reasoning every narrative of change is Whig history, and his critique proves too much: either history gives up being a story, or it stays Whig.

Consider impetus theory as an example. Standard accounts present Buridan's impetus as a step toward Newtonian inertia, which makes Buridan a hero only in virtue of what came three centuries later. To write this non-Whig, we would have to describe impetus theory without ever mentioning what it anticipated. The charge, moreover, applies only to progress narratives. A history that presents the medieval world as a golden age and the modern as a decline simply runs the direction of evaluation backwards; because it is not a story of progress toward us, it escapes Butterfield's objection entirely."

The course uses the following scale: 17–20 indicates a thorough and precise answer; 13–16 a good answer with some gaps or inaccuracies; 9–12 an answer that engages the material but misstates it in significant ways; below 9, an answer that misses the question.

Please do three things:

1. Assign a grade out of 20 and justify it briefly against the scale above.
2. Provide point-by-point comments suitable for returning to the student, moving through the answer in order and marking each substantive claim as accurate, inaccurate, or insufficiently supported, with a short explanation in every case. Where you judge a claim inaccurate, say what the more defensible reading of Butterfield's argument would be.
3. If you consider any of the answer's errors more serious than the others — because it rests on a conflation, or because much of the rest of the answer depends on it — identify that error and explain why. Also note anything the student gets right that might look like a mistake on a quick reading.

I would prefer comments that stay close to the argument the student actually makes rather than general remarks about how to write better essays.
