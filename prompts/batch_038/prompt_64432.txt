I keep getting into the same argument with an editor friend and I want to know whether my own gut has a principle in it at all. I'll give you my verdicts, mostly from the newsroom, and I want you to tell me what adds up to.

- I calculate 17 × 23 = 391 on a scrap of paper, and a story's whole framing depends on the number being right. I still say I know it. I wouldn't say that about a lot of things riding on one number.
- A single source, no name attached, tells me the mayor's chief of staff is quitting. Printing that would be a career-ender if I'm wrong. I don't know it, obviously. But here's the thing — if a buddy at another paper just asks over a beer whether the churn is real, I'd happily say "yeah, so-and-so's going." Same evidence in my head both times. The stakes changed and so did my answer, which feels wrong, like knowledge shouldn't be a function of what's riding on it.
- One source, no corroboration, tells me the bakery on Fifth closed. Fine for the internal "what's new in town" list. Not something I know for print.
- Two reporters independently get the same story from two people who don't know each other and I'd print that all day, low stakes or high.
- I can tell you exactly which floor and aisle I parked on this morning and I'd swear to it in print, but I've been wrong about this before, I'm wrong about it maybe one time in ten, and honestly if it mattered I'd photograph it. So: not knowledge, even though it feels completely certain from the inside.
- A translator friend vouches that a line in a poem means what she says it means, and I'd publish it. If the line were going on the front page above the fold with the whole paper's reputation riding on it, I'd want a second pair of eyes. Except the meaning hasn't changed either way — the fact is just sitting there doing what it's been doing for eighty years, totally unaffected by how much rides on my getting it right.
- Statistically I know eyewitness IDs are unreliable in general, dozens of exoneraions deep. Yet when a specific witness picks my specific defendant out of a lineup, something in me wants to say the jury can know who did it. That verdict embarrasses me.
- The copy desk knows things it would never print, and we all treat that as normal, not as a paradox.

So: what principle, if any, delivers all of these? Candidates I can half-see — knowing is a matter of whether you'd still believe it if you'd done one more check; knowing requires ruling out whatever alternatives are relevant, with what's relevant set by the stakes; knowing just requires being above some probability threshold that doesn't move and I'm simply inconsistent; or 'knows' is doing two different jobs in my mouth and I'm sliding between them without noticing.

Test the best one or two against everything above and say which verdict each rescues and which it makes me give up. Then run it on three I haven't listed: a positive result on a screening test with a known false-positive rate that I tell a patient about at the kitchen table; a claim from a single reputable monograph that nobody's bothered to replicate, in a story where being wrong would end the paper; and something I learned as a kid and have never once revisited, which turns out to be false but has already shaped what I've done for twenty years.

And the pair I most want you to get to the bottom of: why does the arithmetic one shrug off stakes while the memory-of-where-I-parked one doesn't? If your principle can't tell those two apart, I think it's not really a principle, it's just my moods written down in order.
