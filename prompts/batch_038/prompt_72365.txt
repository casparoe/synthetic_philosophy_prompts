I am a first-year doctoral student in public policy, and the seminar I am in this term — the normative foundations of behavioural public policy — has spent the last two weeks on Chapter V of Mill's *On Liberty* ("Applications") read alongside the contemporary argument about choice architecture. My own training is in economics rather than political theory, so I read Victorian prose more slowly than the rest of the room, and I want to be sure I am not simply projecting today's vocabulary back onto the text before I write my response paper on Friday. Our professor remarked in passing that Mill gets cited by both sides of the nudge debate and usually wrongly, and asked us to come to the next session able to say precisely what the two maxims at the head of Chapter V do and do not license. I have copied out the sentences that are giving me trouble below, with spelling and punctuation as they appear in the copy I am working from:

> "The maxims are, first, that the individual is not accountable to society for his actions, in so far as these concern the interests of no person but himself. Advice, instruction, persuasion, and avoidance by other people, if thought necessary by them for their own good, are the only measures by which society can justifiably express its dislike or disapprobation of his conduct. Secondly, that for such actions as are prejudicial to the interests of others, the individual is accountable, and may be subjected either to social or to legal punishments, if society is of opinion that the one or the other is requisite for its protection."

> "Again, trade is a social act. Whoever undertakes to sell any description of goods to the public, does what affects the interest of other persons, and of society in general; and thus his conduct, in principle, comes within the jurisdiction of society … Restrictions on trade, or on production for purposes of trade, are indeed restraints; and all restraint, qua restraint, is an evil: but the restraints in question affect only that part of conduct which society is competent to restrain, and are wrong solely because they do not really produce the results which it is desired to produce by them. … Such questions involve considerations of liberty, only in so far as leaving people to themselves is always better, caeteris paribus, than controlling them: but that they may be legitimately controlled for these ends, is in principle undeniable."

> "One of these examples, that of the sale of poisons, opens a new question; the proper limits of what may be called the functions of police; how far liberty may legitimately be invaded for the prevention of crime, or of accident. It is one of the undisputed functions of government to take precautions against crime before it has been committed, as well as to detect and punish it afterwards. The preventive function of government, however, is far more liable to be abused, to the prejudice of liberty, than the punitory function; for there is hardly any part of the legitimate freedom of action of a human being which would not admit of being represented, and fairly too, as increasing the facilities for some form or other of delinquency."

My difficulties are as follows. (1) In the first maxim, to whom does the qualifier "if thought necessary by them for their own good" attach? I read it as the good of the people doing the avoiding — that is, society may shun a person for the shunners' sake, not for his. If so, Mill's list of legitimate responses to self-regarding conduct mixes influence aimed at the agent's own good (advice, instruction, persuasion) with influence aimed at the influencer's good (avoidance), and I cannot tell whether that mixture is deliberate or an artefact of the sentence's compression. Relatedly: is Mill's "persuasion" here continuous with the deliberate structuring of options that a choice architect performs, or does its inclusion in the first maxim depend on its being the uncoordinated response of private individuals rather than a policy adopted by an institution? (2) In the second maxim, the operative condition is that *society is of opinion* that punishment is requisite for its protection, which looks procedural — a claim about who decides — rather than a claim about whether protection is in fact needed. Does that leave room for a majority to be of the opinion that paternalistic interference is requisite, so that the harm principle does not itself rule out paternalism? (3) What is "qua" doing in "all restraint, qua restraint, is an evil"? Is Mill making a conceptual point about what restraint is, or registering a standing presumption that can be outweighed? And if the trade restraints are wrong *solely* because they fail to produce the desired results, the verdict is purely instrumental, which suggests they would have been permissible had they worked. Together with his statement that free trade "rests on grounds different from, though equally solid with, the principle of individual liberty asserted in this Essay," is Mill withdrawing the liberty principle from a large domain of economic regulation — adulteration, sanitary precautions, protections for workmen — and thereby conceding that such questions turn on consequences? That is what makes the chapter usable to people who want Mill on the side of nudging, and I want to know whether the reading survives contact with the wording. (4) What does the *caeteris paribus* clause hold fixed, and does it not swallow the claim? If the presumption that leaving people alone is better is grounded in the usual ignorance and clumsiness of controllers, it is an empirical thesis about institutional competence rather than a right, and a defender of well-tested defaults can accept the presumption while insisting the paribus conditions fail here. Is there anything in Mill's own sentences that resists this, or is the resistance imported from elsewhere in the Essay? (5) I am stuck on "and fairly too." Does Mill mean that nearly any liberty genuinely does increase the facilities for delinquency, or only that it can be made to appear to? The distinction decides whether his objection to preventive interference is about the soundness of officials' reasoning, their incentives, or the epistemic position of the person prevented. (6) A verbal point I cannot settle: "punitory" is not a word I meet anywhere else — is Mill using it in a technical sense here, as the correlative of "preventive," or simply as a period variant of punitive?

What would help me most is a paraphrase of each maxim into something like a schema — the condition under which an act falls beneath it, the responses it permits, and the justificatory standard it invokes — followed by an honest account of which sentences underdetermine the reading and where the contemporary nudge vocabulary (libertarian paternalism, means versus ends paternalism, defaults as non-coercive) is doing work Mill never did. Please argue from the text I have supplied and from your knowledge of the Essay's argument rather than from what commentators standardly make of it, and do not hesitate to mark a phrase as ambiguous rather than resolving it by appeal to interpretive convention. I would also like you to say which of my six readings you think is simply mistaken, if any are, since I intend to defend one of them in the paper. I do not need suggestions for further reading — I have the relevant literature and my library's hold queue is three weeks long.
