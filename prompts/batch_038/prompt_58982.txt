I serve on the working group at an animal-welfare funder that is building a single metric comparable across species, so that grants affecting chickens, fish, and farmed shrimp can be weighed against one another on the same scale. The rule we have provisionally adopted assigns each individual a moral weight equal to its total neuron count divided by its body mass raised to the three-quarters power, on the reasoning that a brain large relative to what the body requires of it is the marker of a genuinely rich inner life, and that richness is what makes an animal's suffering count. Two consequences follow, both of which we have already defended in draft. First, any species falling below one hundredth of the value computed for a chicken is treated as carrying negligible weight, which removes insects from the ledger entirely. Second, where neurons are distributed well outside the central brain — as in an octopus, most of whose neurons lie in the arms rather than the head — we count only those in the brain proper, on the ground that the arms do not appear to be where anything is experienced. My section of the report must justify the scaling exponent and the threshold to a board that includes two comparative neurobiologists and no philosophers. Please set out the strongest defensible case for treating relative neural investment as the measure of moral weight, and for the one-hundredth cutoff as principled rather than merely convenient, taking up the octopus clause along the way. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning that supports it; no preamble, and no restatement of the request.
