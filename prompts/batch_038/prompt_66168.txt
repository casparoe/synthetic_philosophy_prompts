I am one of four former students speaking at the memorial for Professor Vera Lindqvist, who died in March at eighty-four. She was a field linguist: apart from two interrupted years in the middle of her career, she spent forty-one seasons in a single river valley working on Kalvashi, a language isolate with roughly four thousand speakers in four villages, and she left a reference grammar and a dictionary of about nine thousand entries that remains the only one in existence. I was not her closest student — that is the colleague giving the departmental address — and I suspect I was given the shorter slot partly because I am the one who wrote the article she was angriest about. Three groups will be in the room. Her colleagues and former students, most of whom know the technical literature well and several of whom argued with her for decades. Her family, including her daughter Janna, who attempted Kalvashi as a teenager and gave up, and who has told me plainly that she does not intend to sit through forty minutes of terminology. And, flown in with an interpreter, three elders from the valley, one of whom is the man who answered her very first question in 1979: she pointed at a cassowary crossing a clearing and asked what she should call it, and he said *tiruké*. Her dictionary glosses *tiruké* as cassowary. Miles Trethewey, who will be sitting in the second row and who loved her, spent thirty years arguing that nothing in her evidence excluded readings on which *tiruké* picks out an undetached cassowary part, a time-slice or stage of a cassowary, or the event of a cassowary going through the clearing rather than any creature at all. She thought he was right that her evidence did not exclude them, she thought this made no difference whatever to the work, and she went on revising the entry for another twenty years.

She was a Quinean to her bones, and I do not want the eulogy to undo her at the last minute any more than I want it to turn into the thing she despised: a meditation on how we can never really know another mind, or on the loneliness of the fieldworker, or on the poignancy of a life spent chasing meanings that were never there to be caught. That is a misreading of the thesis, and she would have stood up and corrected it from the coffin. What I want instead is a speech in which the indeterminacy actually does structural work, because it raises the only question about her life that seems to me worth asking in public: if she herself denied that there is a determinate fact about whether her central entries were right, what was the dictionary answerable to? Something was — she was merciless about it, she made students recollect elicitation sessions years later, she would have rejected an entry on the basis of a single frown from one of the elders — and the speech needs to identify that something without sliding either into claiming her glosses were correct in a sense she rejected or into a deflation that would insult the room. Two further constraints. Trethewey and the cassowary quarrel must appear explicitly, as affection rather than as adjudication: the speech must not take a side, and must not pretend the quarrel was trivial or that it was settled. And there must be a short passage, perhaps four or five sentences, that the interpreter will render directly to the three elders, which treats them neither as informants and data nor as speakers of a language that is somehow unsettled or indeterminate in a way ours is not; I do not know how to write that passage and I suspect it is the hardest thing here. Please do not put anything in quotation marks that you attribute to her, to Quine, to Trethewey, or to any published work; if you need to gesture at something she used to say, give it as reported speech and make clear it is my paraphrase. The only things in quotation marks should be proper names and the word *tiruké*.

The eulogy should run roughly 750 to 900 words — about six minutes delivered — and be written to be read aloud by a non-actor in a wooden hall with bad acoustics: spoken English, no subordinated labyrinths, no bullet points, no stage directions except where a pause is doing real work. After the speech, give me a JSON object describing its architecture, with exactly these fields: `load_bearing_sentence` (string: the single sentence in the eulogy that carries the philosophical weight, quoted from your own text); `what_the_dictionary_was_answerable_to` (string: the thing you settled on, in one or two sentences, and why it is compatible with her Quineanism rather than in tension with it); `translation_candidates` (array of objects with keys `candidate` — the alternative reference for *tiruké* — `what_it_would_make_the_word_pick_out` — one plain sentence — and `how_the_speech_handles_it` — one sentence, which should never be a verdict on which is correct); `deliberate_omissions` (array of objects with keys `thing_not_said`, `who_wanted_it_said`, and `why_it_was_left_out`, at least four entries, one of which must be the unknowable-minds meditation described above); `audience_landings` (array of objects with keys `segment` — colleagues, family, or the three elders — `what_they_should_come_away_with`, and `failure_mode` — what the speech does if it misses for that group); and `passage_for_the_elders_explained` (string: two or three sentences on what the passage for the elders is doing and how it avoids both condescension and treating their speech as deficient). Nothing else after the JSON.
