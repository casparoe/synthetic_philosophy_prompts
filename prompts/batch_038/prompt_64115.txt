I've just finished a two-hour argument with a friend about dialetheism, and the whole exchange turned on his repeated appeal to the principle that from a contradiction anything follows. He used it twice. First, against the liar: anyone who grants a single true contradiction has thereby granted everything, so dialetheism collapses into trivialism and cannot be a substantive position at all. Second, against an example I raised — Bohr's early model of the atom, which combines orbits that classical electrodynamics requires to radiate with a postulate that they do not. He said a theory containing that pair entails both the spectral lines and their denial, so it explained nothing and its predictive successes were luck.

Could you lay out, as a menu I can bring back to him, the distinct precise theses that one slogan could be expressing — as a claim about which formulas are theorems of a given logic, about which inferences are valid, about what a contradictory set of premises semantically entails, about what a person who believes a contradiction is rationally committed to, and about whether a body of claims can retain its content while containing a contradiction? For each reading, tell me whether it is true, whether it is a platitude or a substantive and contested claim, and whether it would actually license either of his two moves — or whether it already presupposes the point we were disputing.
