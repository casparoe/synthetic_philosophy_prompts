The episode: the main shrine buildings at Ise in Japan are rebuilt from scratch roughly every twenty years, on one of two adjacent plots that alternate — so at any given moment there's a brand-new building on one plot and a twenty-year-old one on the next to it, and the old one then gets taken apart. All cypress, no nails, thatch roof, everything replaced. Its been done this way for something like 1300 years, with one big exception: a gap of well over a century in the medieval period when warfare made it impossible, after which the cycle started up again. The enshrined kami is transferred to the new building at night, in darkness. The old timbers aren't discarded — the largest become torii gates and the rest get distributed to shrines around the country to repair theirs. And the carpentry, the joinery and the ritual sequence are transmitted through the craftspeople, which is arguably the strand that survives most reliably.

My working claim, which I want you to stress-test rather than flatter: the puzzle only generates its famous embarrassment because it's staged so that exactly one candidate survives the replacement — or exactly two, in the version where somebody hoards the original planks. At Ise there are two standing buildings at once plus a distributed downstream network of reused timber, so "which one is it" never gets a single grip, and the practice carries on regardless. Nobody at Ise has ever supposed the shrine stopped existing.

So run it in both directions.
