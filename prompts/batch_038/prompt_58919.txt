"Indicator semantics holds that a mental state gets its content by covarying reliably with some condition in the world: the frog's optic ganglion cells fire when a small dark object moves across the visual field, and it is in virtue of that lawful correlation that the state means small dark moving thing. The difficulty, as Fodor pressed it, is that correlation is too generous. The same cells fire at BB pellets flicked past the frog, at drifting shadows, and at anything else of the right size and speed. If content is fixed by what actually causes the state, then the content of the frog's state must be a disjunction — fly or pellet or shadow — and on that reading the frog never misrepresents when it snaps at a pellet, since a pellet is among the things its state is about. There is then no room for error at all, and a theory that cannot accommodate error has not captured representation. Dretske tried to limit the damage by confining the content-fixing correlation to a learning period, but this looks like stipulation: it does not explain why correlations formed later should be permitted to misfire.

Teleosemantics resolves the problem by shifting the ground from present causal disposition to history. On this account the proper function of the frog's ganglion cells is what they reliably do under normal conditions, namely fire at small dark moving objects; the content of the state is then read off that disposition, together with the fact that the frog's snapping behaviour was reinforced by the nutrition it obtained from what it caught. A state misrepresents when it is produced outside the conditions in which the mechanism was doing what it was supposed to do. The pellet case is therefore not veridical disjunctive representation but malfunction, and error is restored. Fodor's own asymmetric-dependence proposal is best understood as a version of the same strategy, since it too makes the content of 'horse' rest on a causal dependency that is prior to, and explains, the deviant tokens caused by cows on dark nights.

Two further considerations tell in the account's favour. First, the taxonomy is clean: Dretske's is the paradigm of consumer semantics, because content is settled by what the receiving system does with the signal, whereas Millikan's is producer-oriented, because she looks to the mechanisms that generate the representation. Second, Swampman is handled decisively. Davidson constructed the case in order to refute teleosemantics, and the theory duly predicts that the duplicate's beliefs are false: having no selection history, its states cannot be about anything, so whatever it takes itself to be thinking is mistaken. The consequence is worth stating plainly, because it shows the reach of the view. Content requires a history of natural selection, and it follows that no engineered system — a robot, or a network trained on a corpus — can have representations in the strict sense, however well it behaves. That is a prediction of the theory rather than an embarrassment to it."
