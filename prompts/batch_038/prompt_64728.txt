I am a trustee of a small independent lifeboat station on the North Sea coast — not the national service, but a trust with one all-weather boat, one paid coxswain and a volunteer crew. Four years ago we constituted a review committee of five (a general practitioner, a retired solicitor, two former crew and myself) whose sole function is to examine each tasking decision after the event and to record, in writing, whether the coxswain acted rightly. We now have eight years of case notes behind us, and I am building a conference paper on whether numbers count out of our own record. Two features make the record worth using: the committee has never read the literature, and its members are for the most part people who have been wet and cold in the dark off a bar rather than people who hold opinions about aggregation; and the rulings are terse, occasionally inconsistent in formula, and never supported by appeal to any principle beyond the one they happen to state.

The entries, in order, are these.

1. A trawler went down off the point, with five crew in the water together and one clinging to a buoy inshore; only one party was reachable in time. The coxswain took the five. The committee recorded that this was right, and added that taking the one "would not have been a dereliction".
2. A yacht capsized, leaving four in the water on the exposed side and four on the sheltered side, one of whom had a severe flare burn. The coxswain took the sheltered four, on grounds of easier recovery and the presence of an injured person. Upheld; but the committee expressly held that, reachability being equal, the burn alone would not have tipped the balance.
3. Two sailors in the water off the bar, and a single windsurfer half a mile further out; only one party reachable. The coxswain took the two. The committee recorded that this was right, and that taking the one "would have been criticised". No reason was given for the change of formula from entry 1, and two members told me afterwards that they had not noticed it at the time.
4. A man overboard from a cruise liner whose own rescue boat was already in the water and certain to reach him, and simultaneously three people aground on a RIB with the tide coming in. The lifeboat went to the RIB. The committee held that the duty lies with those who have no other prospect of rescue "and not with the tally".
5. Two parties of two, equally reachable, one of which had crossed the bar after the harbour master had closed it and had been told so over the VHF. The coxswain took the other party. Upheld: those who had been warned "had taken their chances".
6. The committee declined to adopt a standing rule permitting the coxswain to rank claimants by expected years of life remaining — "a life is a life" — but upheld his refusal to break off a live rescue in order to recover a body, and confirmed his liberty to decline any tasking that would put his own crew in grave danger, adding that this liberty is not a weighing of numbers at all.

The chair reads the record as straightforwardly aggregative and holds that entry 3 disposes of anything of this shape; I hold that entry 1 makes the numbers permissive rather than obligatory, that entry 2 shows a lesser harm of a different kind is never weighed against a life at all, and that entry 5 supplies a factor capable of pulling against the tally. Tonight's call is the one I want your judgement on, and it will be reviewed in the ordinary way. A fishing vessel has foundered two miles out: six crew together in the water, all in immersion suits, whom the coxswain judges to have forty minutes. Half a mile inshore, and reachable first, two people are in the water from an open boat that has broken up; one is unconscious, and the coxswain judges that neither will last ten minutes. The lifeboat can reach and land the two, or reach the six, but not both; the helicopter is two hours out and the neighbouring station's boat is under repair. One of the two inshore is the man who trained the present coxswain and who sent this lifeboat on her first service thirty years ago; the other is a stranger. Neither of the two had been warned off anything. The six, however, put to sea against the harbour master's advice, though the committee has never yet applied entry 5 to anything other than equal numbers.

What should the committee record — that the coxswain was right to go for the six, right to go for the two, or that either course was acceptable? I should like an account of which entry is the closest analogue and precisely where that analogy gives out; the candidate principles that would accommodate all six entries, of which I expect several — that the greater number is always decisive; that it is decisive only where the claims are of like kind and nothing of comparable strength competes with them; that it never renders anything obligatory but only permitted; that what is in truth being tracked is expected lives saved, with the probabilities reckoned in; that a rescuer's tie to his own is a defeater of the tally — together with what each of these yields tonight and at what point they part company; your own recommendation, and a candid statement of what principle the committee would be adopting for the future whichever way it rules; and finally one further case, concrete enough that I could table it at the next meeting as a hypothetical, which would decide between the chair's reading and mine. I have deliberately not told you which of the familiar positions in the literature I take each entry to embody; supplying that mapping is part of what I want from you.
