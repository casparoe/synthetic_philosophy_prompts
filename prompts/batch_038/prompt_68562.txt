Sieyès's answer to the boundary problem is radical in a way contemporary democratic theory has mostly declined to follow: the nation exists before any constitution, its will cannot be invalid because no higher procedure exists that could invalidate it, and this is exactly why he could accept a property line between active citizens, who share in making the law, and passive citizens, who merely enjoy its protection — the line is drawn by the nation and need not be justified to those it excludes. Consider now a proposal he could not have contemplated: a referendum, open to every adult resident including non-citizens, on whether long-term resident non-citizens should be admitted to the electorate for all future referendums. Set out how Sieyès would reason about it, treating in particular whether holding such a vote is itself an exercise of constituent power or an abdication of it, and what a negative result would actually settle. Then carry his reasoning to two questions that postdate him entirely: whether a constitutional court's finding that the referendum was procedurally invalid can bind the nation at all, and whether the interests of people not yet born fall inside or outside the constituent power. Where his framework commits him to a conclusion he would have recoiled from, say so plainly rather than reconciling it away.
