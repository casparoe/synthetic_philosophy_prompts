I chair the ethics consultation service at a mid-sized teaching hospital. Each October I run a single orientation session for the incoming consultants: usually twelve to fifteen people, drawn from nursing, chaplaincy, social work, the residency program, administration, and one lay patient representative. Ninety minutes, one windowless conference room, whiteboard and paper only, no screens.

For years I taught it conventionally — the four principles, how to assess capacity, how to run a family meeting. I abandoned that approach after a case in which everyone at the table accepted every fact in the chart and we still could not agree what to do. Afterwards it struck me that the terms we had been repeating at one another — best interests, futility, what she would have wanted — were not shared concepts with contested applications but different concepts altogether, each carrying a whole picture of what a person is and what counts as a reason. Since then I have been reading Rorty, and I now believe the thing my new consultants most need is not a method but an insight: there is no vocabulary standing behind our vocabularies to which we can appeal when they conflict, and when agreement does come, it arrives by redescription rather than by refutation.

I want this to be an activity they run into, not a position I announce. Please design the session under the following constraints.

- Neither Rorty nor antirepresentationalism is named until the debrief. The facilitator's opening is two sentences at most.
- Write a fictional composite case — not a real one — engineered so that the parties agree on every clinical fact and disagree only in vocabulary, so that no amount of additional information can break the impasse. Include the one-page case sheet.
- Provide role cards, each specifying a small closed set of terms in which that person must justify every claim, a ban on borrowing terms from other cards, and one sentence describing how the world looks from inside that card.
- Build into the rules a mechanism by which at least one participant changes their mind without being shown to be wrong, so the group experiences redescription as a real force rather than as a debate that someone lost.
- Include a trap: an official-looking form or checklist handed to them as a neutral instrument, which they eventually discover cannot be filled out without taking a side. Tell me how to construct it so that the discovery is theirs.
- Include one moment where a participant is redescribed in terms they experience as demeaning, since the cruelty of redescription is part of what I want them to feel. Give me the rule that lets us halt and process it, the safeguards beforehand, and what I do if someone is genuinely upset.
- A minute-by-minute timeline, a materials list, what goes on the whiteboard, and adjustments for twelve and for fifteen participants.
- A debrief that maps specific moments of the exercise onto the concepts — final vocabulary, redescription, the unavailability of a neutral tribunal, solidarity as the alternative to objectivity — and only then supplies the name.

Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each instance. I expect the most fluent participant's card to become the de facto common language; I expect some to referee the game instead of playing it; and my real fear is that they leave concluding that it is all merely words and nothing is at stake, which is the opposite of the point.

Be frank about which feature of the view the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply myself. I suspect at least this: my consultants are handed their vocabularies on index cards rather than having inherited them over a lifetime, and I do not know whether ninety minutes can carry that weight.

Finally, these people have to run a consultation the next morning. Explain how the session can produce the insight without producing paralysis or cynicism, and how I can tell, before they leave the room, which of the two I have produced.
