For a chapter on the evidential structure of the Fermi paradox, write a case study of the first pulsar discovery at Cambridge in 1967–68, treated not as a triumph of detection but as an episode in which a search that had been returning nothing for months returned something nobody was looking for, and in which the most exotic hypothesis on the table was entertained and then abandoned.

Take the following as given and do not go hunting for further detail. A graduate student working under Antony Hewish had helped build a radio array whose purpose was the study of quasars, via the scintillation of their signals in the interplanetary medium; the output was chart-recorder paper read by eye, sometimes tens of metres of it a night. In data taken in August 1967 she noticed a bit of "scruff" — a patch of anomalous noise — that tracked across the sky with the stars rather than with the Sun, and she reported it over the initial objection that it must be interference of human origin. Because the paper had to be scanned by hand, the signal did not reappear in her reading until late November. It was then seen to recur at a period of roughly 1.34 seconds with remarkable regularity. Within the group the source was nicknamed after little green men, and the possibility of an artificial beacon was taken seriously enough that the question was raised how such a finding could responsibly be announced and to whom. Before and shortly after the announcement of the discovery in February 1968, further sources appeared at other positions on the sky with different periods, and the artificial hypothesis was dropped; within months the accepted explanation was a rotating neutron star. The detection came from an instrument built for another purpose, reading data no study had been designed to collect.

Part I: reconstruct the group's decision as a comparison between two hypotheses — an engineered beacon and a hitherto unimagined class of natural source — and say what each predicts about the features actually available: periodicity and its stability, sidereal rather than solar motion, sky position, bandwidth, and above all multiplicity. Explain why the arrival of a second and third source did more damage to the artificial hypothesis than any property of the first signal could have, and whether that step was deductively compelled or merely probabilistic. Then take up the priors: how much of the work was done by the low antecedent probability of a nearby beacon as against the equally unquantified probability of a new class of compact radio source, and whether any such reasoning was legitimate in 1967, when neither hypothesis had a developed astrophysics behind it. Fold in one further wrinkle: the anomaly was reported by a doctoral student and first dismissed by her supervisor as artefact, so it is not obvious that "the group" had a single set of credences at any moment — say what your reconstruction should do with that.

Part II: apply a trichotomy I am trying to make precise. A null result can fail in three distinct ways. Coverage failure: the search would have registered the target had the target been where the search looked, but it was elsewhere in the space searched. Recognition failure: the target was in the data, in the region searched, but was passed over as noise, artefact, or interference. Characterization failure: the searchers had no account of what the target's signature would be, so no instrument, protocol, or reduction step was tuned to it and detection could occur only by accident. Classify each of the near-misses in the episode under these headings, and then say whether the trichotomy cuts the episode at the joints or whether recognition failure simply collapses into characterization failure once you press on it. Which of the three plausibly afflicts the null results of radio searches for extraterrestrial intelligence, and does it matter that they differ in kind from one another?

Part III: run the analysis in the other direction. What does this episode do to the inference from our silence to conclusions about a population — the sort of argument that moves from the absence of visitors or artefacts now, plus the age of the galaxy and the modest timescales that colonization would require, to the claim that no such civilizations exist? Press on the disanalogies rather than waving at them: a pulsar population consists of many intrinsically similar members that a sufficiently sensitive survey finds by looking at more of the sky, whereas an engineered signal may be singular, transient, aimed, and unconstrained by any physics we could predict. Which of these differences genuinely weakens an argument from silence and which are rhetorical? Does the serendipity of the pulsar detection suggest that the silence should be discounted, or does it cut the other way, since an anomaly of that conspicuousness sitting in three months of chart paper went unrecognized? And identify at least one place where Fermi-paradox reasoning simply has nothing to say — for instance about how to set priors on hypotheses that are unprecedented rather than merely improbable — instead of forcing the theory to deliver a verdict.

Between 1,200 and 1,600 words. No bibliography, no citations, and no recapitulation of the standard catalogue of proposed resolutions; argue from the structure of the case. Where you are unsure of a factual detail, say so rather than filling the gap.
