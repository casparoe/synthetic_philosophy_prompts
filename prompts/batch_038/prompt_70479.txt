Prepping a talk for a reading group on nationalism and I want to hang it on Braveheart (1995), but I'm stuck on two things and could use a real reading rather than a list of historical errors.

One: Hobsbawm's invented traditions are supposed to be rule-governed, repeated practices that inculcate norms and claim a continuity with the past they don't actually have. A film isn't a practice like that. So when the movie puts 13th-century Scots in kilts and clan tartan — which the chapter in that same volume on Highland dress treats as largely an 18th/19th-century manufacture — is the film inventing a tradition, or backdating one that was already invented five centuries later? Does Hobsbawm's framework have any room for that move, or does it fall outside it? Related wrinkle: folklore studies separates fakelore, judged by origin and by falsely passing something off as genuinely traditional, from Folklorismus, which is just any use of a tradition outside the context that produced it, judged by practice instead. Which one does the film come off worse under, and does it change anything that the films source is Blind Harry's 15th-century poem, already a romance rather than a chronicle?

Two: Anderson's mechanism is print capitalism — newspapers and novels giving readers a new sense of homogenous, simultaneous time, a mass ceremony performed privately. Cinema looks like it should do that job even better, but people watch films at different times, and most of this film's audience was never going to be Scottish. Can a film produce Andersonian simultaneity, or does the medium break the mechanism? Point me at specific scenes — the freedom speech, the Bannockburn coda where Bruce invokes Wallace — and end by telling me honestly whether the film adds anything to the Hobsbawm/Anderson debate that argument alone can't, or whether I'm just using it as a pretty illustration and should say so out loud.
