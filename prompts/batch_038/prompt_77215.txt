Our work book club (12 of us, all engineers, zero historians) just got through Jared Diamond's Collapse and it turned into a genuinely bad-tempered argument — some think it's a masterwork, some think it cherry-picks its cases to fit a moral. I don't want to settle it by shouting, I want to settle it by playing. 75 minutes, one conference room, a whiteboard, a printer, no budget.

Design a game where teams get handed evidence packets on a few societies and have to hand down a verdict: did this one collapse, or did it just change into something else? The hard part needs to live in the packets themselves, not in anything I say out loud. Three things I want baked in:

- Diamond's definition is roughly a drastic drop in population and/or in political complexity, over a wide area, for a long time. Make at least one packet a case where those two come apart — the palaces, the scribal record, the long-distance trade all crater, but settlements scatter and spread and the health markers for ordinary people get *better*.
- Make the evidence they're judging overwhelmingly elite evidence, because elites were the ones writing things down and building in stone. I want the exact moment where someone says "the records just stop" and somebody else says "that only means the people who kept records stopped."
- If you can do it without cheating, make one packet the kind of case where the famous popular version of the story (Easter Island was the one that blew up at our table) has been argued with pretty hard by the people who actually study it — I want them to feel the ground move under a narrative they were confident about.

So: the packets themselves (invent them, keep them plausible and printable — a table per society, a few centuries of rows, mixed metric types), roles and scoring, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps what they actually did onto the real question, which I think is whether collapse is an event in the world or a label we put on a change of state. Also tell me what predictably goes wrong the first time you run it — I've ran a couple of these and someone always games the scoring or flat refuses to commit to a verdict. And be straight with me about which feature of the real thing the game can't reproduce, so I know what I have to supply myself.
