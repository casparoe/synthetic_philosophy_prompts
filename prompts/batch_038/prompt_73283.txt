I write novels, and a character problem turned into a philosophy problem. In the book I'm working on, a woman who's convinced her husband has left starts genuinely seeing him — the tilt of a stranger's head on a station platform, his coat two streets over — and I want to write that as happening in her eyes, not in her interpretation of them. So I went and read up on whether belief and desire can penetrate perception, expecting a settled answer, and instead found seventy-ish years of the same argument: the New Look tradition, and now people like Siegel and Macpherson, saying yes, what you want shapes what you see; Pylyshyn and Fodor saying early vision is encapsulated and can't be touched from above; and the skeptic line that nearly every published effect dissolves into demand characteristics, memory, or subjects shifting their response criterian rather than their experience. Nobody moves. My working guess, offered as a guess: the fight isn't about the experiments at all but about where the border between seeing and taking-to-see is allowed to run — two camps answering different questions under one word, and maybe arguing past each other about the epistemic payoff (can a perception be irrational?) rather than the psychology. Am I right? Diagnose what kind of disagreement this actually is — verbal, substantive, a hidden empirical question, or an ill-posed one — using what each side really says, and tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a better experimental design, a formal result, or nothing, ever. And say where your own diagnosis is most likely to break — what argument or piece of evidence would make you retract it.
