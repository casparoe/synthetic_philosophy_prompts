Four years ago my brother and I each took over one of two small apartment buildings on the same street — his has twelve units, mine has fourteen — and I have become convinced that we have been running nearly identical properties under two different rules for making decisions, and that my rule is the better one even though it sounds lazier. When something needs doing, he gets three quotes and takes the lowest, every single time, without fail. I keep a figure in my head for what a given job ought to cost, worked out from my own books over the years, and I hand the work to the first firm that can come in under that figure and has done decent work for me before. On paper he has saved something like three thousand dollars over four years. In practice he had a boiler out for eleven days in February, he has had three jobs done twice, a plumber told him last spring that he was too busy, and two firms have stopped quoting for him altogether. None of this has happened to me. I do not think I have been lucky and I do not think he is foolish — he is quicker with numbers than I am, and at the moment of each decision he has every fact in front of him that I have. What I think is going on is that the pool of people willing to work for him is something his own rule has been quietly building up or wearing down, job by job, and that the consequences turn up somewhere other than where he is looking.

I have read enough to know that my rule is called satisficing and his is called maximizing, and that the standard line is that satisficing is only a concession to limited minds — that a being with all the time and all the computing power in the world would maximize, and that people like me settle because we cannot do the arithmetic. I want to test that line, and I would like you to build me a clean case to test it with, because my own story is far too tangled to prove anything. Please construct a decision problem in which two agents face the same recurring situation and the one who maximizes correctly at every choice point ends up materially worse off than the one who satisfices — where the difference has nothing to do with the maximizer being rushed, tired, forgetful, or short of information. So that you know what would count as cheating here: the point of the case is to find out whether the advantage of not-maximizing can be structural rather than psychological, meaning that it would survive being handed to a mind with no limitations whatever. A case that produces its verdict by making the maximizer careless, by giving her false beliefs, or by quietly tuning the satisficer's threshold until it lands on a lucky number has not done the job. The verdicts should fall out of the two rules themselves.

The specifications I want met:

1. Ordinary life only. A small business, a building, a stretch of repeat purchasing — no science fiction, no exotic physics, no technology that does not exist, and no auctions, derivatives, or lottery-like payoffs. Keep the scenario to one paragraph of roughly two hundred and fifty words, with the analysis separate and at whatever length it needs.
2. The two agents must be matched on everything: same market, same set of counterparties available to them, same jobs, same budgets, same intelligence, same access to information. Only the decision rule differs.
3. The maximizer must be a genuine maximizer, not a strawman — she takes the option that is truly best given everything correct and obtainable at that moment, and she is neither greedy nor short-sighted by temperament.
4. Whatever information she lacks must be information that nobody in her position could have. Tell me what it is, why it is unavailable, and why an unlimited brain would still not get it. The satisficer gets no free information either.
5. The satisficer's aspiration level must be one she would have set in advance, for reasons of her own that have nothing to do with how the case turns out — and the case must keep working across a range of plausible levels rather than resting on one knife-edge figure. Tell me what that range is.
6. The counterparties may be shrewd and experienced people who have dealt with both agents before, but no mind-readers and no predictors of any kind better than that.

After the scenario, walk me through the reasoning of each agent at two or three decision points in enough detail that I can watch the worse outcome being produced step by step rather than being told about it. Then name the single structural feature of the case that is doing the work, remove it — saying exactly how you removed it — and report what happens; the case should collapse, and if it does not, tell me what was really carrying it. Finally, give me the best reply on the maximizer's behalf. The one I can already think of is that a sophisticated maximizer chooses between whole policies rather than between individual acts, so she would simply adopt the satisficer's rule as her policy, which would make my rule a way of maximizing rather than a rival to it. Say whether that reply succeeds, and if it does, say what is left of the claim that satisficing is a genuinely different kind of practical reasoning instead of maximizing under another name — and whether the idea that satisficing is only for limited minds comes out true, false, or beside the point. I have never taken a philosophy or an economics course, so please write in plain language and define any term you need, in a clause, the first time you use it.
