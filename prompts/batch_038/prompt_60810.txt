I am running a 90-minute session in an upper-level philosophy of mind seminar — 16 students, mostly humanities majors who have had exactly one lecture on Gödel's incompleteness theorems and no mathematical logic beyond it. The session's target is the Lucas–Penrose line of argument: that a mind cannot be a Turing machine, since given any candidate machine we can construct a statement the machine cannot assert but whose correctness we can nonetheless see. I want an in-class game, not a lecture and not a handout to discuss, in which the students carry out that argument themselves and then, from inside the same game, run into the reasons it is supposed to fail. They should not hear the names Lucas, Penrose, or anti-mechanism until the debrief.

Requirements and constraints:

1. No computers, no coding of syntax, no arithmetic beyond counting. The "machine" must be a student or small team working from a sealed paper rulebook, and every move the rulebook licenses must be executable in under 20 seconds with pen, index cards, or a printed grid. The diagonal construction the class performs must likewise be a mechanical paper procedure they actually carry out in the room — not a description of a procedure they merely nod at.

2. Two phases, with tokens standing in for points (no real money). Phase one: the class observes the machine's outputs and attempts to identify its rulebook, earning tokens for correct predictions of its next move. Phase two: having identified it, they must construct a statement that the rulebook demonstrably forbids the machine to make but that the class can vouch for as correct, earning a larger payout if the machine concedes. Specify the token economy, what counts as a concession, and how a stalemate or an ambiguous verdict is scored.

3. Build in at least one hidden defeater — for example a second rulebook the machine may switch to once it appears to have been identified, or a variant rulebook that licenses exactly the assertion the class's construction predicted was impossible. I want their triumph undermined by something that happens in play, not by something I announce from the front of the room. If you think a hidden defeater is the wrong device here, propose a better one and justify the substitution.

4. A minute-by-minute timeline, including when the rulebook is revealed and when the names are introduced.

5. A debrief that maps particular moments of the game onto four pressure points in the real argument: that mere consistency does not supply what the class needs and that an assumption of soundness is silently doing the work; that their diagonal depends on having correctly identified the machine's program, which the actual anti-mechanist is not entitled to assume; that a class member who has beaten the machine is now herself describable as a rulebook open to the same treatment; and that the machine could simply be built to decline the diagonal question altogether. For each, say what the game makes vivid and what it distorts.

6. At least three things that will predictably go wrong the first time this is run — including the near-certainty that whoever plays the machine will improvise beyond the rulebook — each with a concrete in-the-room fix.

7. A frank closing note on the single feature of the Lucas–Penrose argument that the game cannot reproduce at all, and that I will have to supply in talk.

Open with one sentence stating the insight the game exists to deliver, then give the design. Keep the entire response under 1,000 words; a compact table is welcome for the timeline.
