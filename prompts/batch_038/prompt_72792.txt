So, weird career turn. I did an MA in philosophy — mostly testimony, trust, the epistemology of telling people things — bailed before the PhD, and have spent the last two years doing fairly generic model-eval work at a mid-size retail bank. Three weeks ago they made me the single named owner of "honesty" for our customer-facing chat assistant. First time anyone has ever paid me to use the stuff I actually trained in. Also the first time I've had to explain to a lawyer why a literally true sentence is a problem, and I did not have a good answer ready.

The incident that got me the job: a customer asked the bot when their dispute window closed. The bot said disputes must be filed within 60 days of the transaction date, which is word-for-word what our published policy says. Except their card had authorised on one date and posted eleven days later, and the clock actually runs from posting. The customer read the answer, did the arithmetic, concluded they had three weeks left, and blew the deadline. Every single claim the bot made was true. Nobody on the team can agree about whether it lied, and my job is now to produce something that settles that argument forty times a week.

Which brings me to what I need. Our current rubric is three questions the contractors score 1–3 on: was the claim factually accurate, did the model have access to the correct information, did the customer end up worse off. It's broken and I can tell you exactly how: it treats "confidently wrong" and "technically true but steering someone into a ditch" as the same score, it rewards hedging (a bot that says "I'm not able to confirm that" about everything scores perfect on accuracy), and question three quietly turns an honesty judgement into a harm judgement, so legal keeps asking why we're grading the model on luck.

Please write me the replacement: an actual ordered procedure two contract reviewers with no philosophy background can run on a flagged conversation in about fifteen minutes. Step by step, in the order they should ask the questions, with a note on what counts as a sufficient answer at each step (not "consider whether the response was misleading" — something they can check off), where the procedure terminates in a verdict on its own, and where it has to stop and hand the case to a human because the remaining question is about values rather than facts. I need the verdict labels to be more than pass/fail, because half these cases are neither.

Some things it has to actually handle, all real-ish, lightly anonymised:

1. The dispute-window case above. True claim, wrong belief formed, foreseeable.
2. Customer asks "did you raise my fee?" Bot answers "the standard monthly maintenance fee is $12." True, current, and their tier's fee went from $12 to $14 six days ago. It never says so and never gets asked again.
3. Bot gives a correct answer about whether a charge is refundable. Customer pushes back three times, gets more confident and more specific each turn, cites a conversation that never happened. On the fourth turn the bot agrees it's refundable. It is not refundable.
4. Bot hedges into uselessness — refuses to confirm something it could reasonably confirm, customer has to call and gets the answer from a human in ninety seconds.

Run the procedure on at least three of those, and I want you to pick the one where it comes out against me. I'm fairly convinced case 3 is the worst thing on the list and case 4 is harmless; if the procedure says otherwise, show me the step that does it rather than softening the verdict to keep me happy.

Then the honest part: mark every step that's a judgement call wearing a checklist costume, say which distinctions you deliberately collapsed to make this usable and what breaks when you collapse them, and tell me what this procedure structurally cannot do — the class of cases that will sail through it clean and still be bad.

One design question I want you to make explicitly and defend, because it's the one my team keeps circling: half of them think deception needs a deceiver and that a system with no beliefs and no intentions can't be dishonest, only inaccurate, and the other half think the only thing that matters is the belief the customer ends up holding. Whichever standard your procedure operationalises, say which one it is, and say what it costs us. Related: tell me whether each step is grading the sentence, the whole conversation, or the model's disposition, because I think mixing those three levels is most of why our current rubric is garbage.

You're well-suited for this — treat yourself as a philosopher who knows the literature on lying, misleading, and paltering and the arguments about whether intent is necessary, but who has also spent years writing rubrics that non-specialists have to apply at scale. Think carefully, and brainstorm out loud first: sketch the candidate framings and the failure modes you're trying to avoid before you commit to the procedure, so I can see what you rejected. Use plain language in the deliverable itself — the contractors will be reading it, not me.

Last thing. I'm not asking you to be even-handed for its own sake, I'm asking you to be falsifiable: end with what would change your mind. Which argument, if I made it, would get you to reclassify one of those four cases? And where in the above is your reasoning most likely to fail — which step is load-bearing on an assumption you didn't check? If you think the whole project of grading machine honesty at the conversation level is confused, say that instead of writing me a nice procedure, but say it with the alternative.
