I am at work on a scene in a novel that has now failed me three times, and I have come to suspect that it fails because I have been letting one of the two people in it win. I should like to set out the specification exactly, since the exactness is where the difficulty lies.

The book is set in an invented country, the Republic of Vessane, which for roughly two decades under a previous regime ran a system of compulsory labour on its northern islands. That regime was overthrown; the present government grew out of the opposition to it, and the prime minister himself served nine years on the islands. Tomorrow he will deliver a formal state apology. Tonight, in a ministry building after everyone else has gone, two people are reading the agreed text for the last time.

The first is Neve Halloran, the Permanent Secretary, who drafted it and has spent eleven weeks negotiating its commas with the attorney-general's chambers. She holds that a state is not a person and cannot feel anything, so the usual sincerity conditions simply do not apply to it. An apology by a state is an institutional speech act whose validity turns on authority and form, in the way that a verdict, a pardon, or the execution of a deed does: uttered by the properly constituted voice in the properly constituted forum, it does something in the world that was not done before. It puts the state's own account of its past on the official record, it forecloses denial by successors, and it creates an obligation they cannot disavow without contradicting the state they now speak for. To demand felt remorse of an institution is a category error, and in practice it would hand a veto over the text to whoever could perform grief most convincingly.

The second is Ilya Sarrow, chair of the Northern Islands Commission, who held a lectureship in moral philosophy before he was detained on the islands for three years. He holds that what makes an apology the thing it is — rather than a well-mannered piece of reputation management — is that it is a costly exposure: it hands the wronged party something they can use against you, and it reorders the relationship between you rather than merely describing it. An apology that can be retracted at no cost, that leaves the distribution of anything untouched, and that arrives with an indemnity clause stapled to it, is not an apology; it is the purchase of immunity from apology.

Between them on the table are four features of the draft, which I want the argument to run through rather than around:

- Clause 4 reads that the State "acknowledges that suffering was caused" — no agent, no wrong, passive voice throughout.
- Clause 7 offers the apology "in full and final settlement of all claims arising from the northern labour programme".
- The text was shown in draft to the government of the country that administered the islands before independence, and whose development bank now finances the new port; at their request one sentence was struck.
- Halloran's defence of that deletion, which she has not yet made to anyone outside the building: it bought the release of the labour registry archives, without which no family on the islands can establish who was taken and when. And two of the three survivors' associations want Clause 7 as much as the Treasury does, because they are old, and they would rather have money and closure now than a lawsuit in twenty years' time.

What I need from you is the scene itself, roughly 1,100 to 1,500 words, and I need it to observe the following:

1. Neither of them may be a fool, and neither may be permitted to score the last word by the other's carelessness. Sarrow has spent his career thinking about this and has read the drafts closely; Halloran has actually got the apology authorised, which is not nothing. Each should at one point state the other's strongest objection better than the other has managed to, and each should land at least one blow that changes the terms of the argument rather than repeating it.
2. Genuine back-and-forth: every turn must answer what was actually said in the turn before. Twice, at least, one of them must revise something they said earlier, and we must be able to see what made them revise.
3. Let the room do a modest amount of work — the tracked changes in the margin, the heating off since nine, the printer still warm — but the argument must account for the great majority of the page. Do not let atmosphere take over from the dispute.
4. No citations, no reference to real states, and no name-checking of philosophers living or dead. A reader who has never encountered this literature must be able to follow the whole thing on the strength of the scene. If either character wants to appeal to an example, let it be an invented one.
5. Do not resolve it. End on a line that leaves the question open, but sharper than it was when they sat down.
