I read the standard doing/allowing material years ago — the trolley, the transplant surgeon, the pair of cousin-in-the-bathtub cases, the various attempts to show that the distinction has no independent moral weight once intent and outcome are held fixed — and I don't want any of it re-run at me. What I want is the distinction put to work on a real, exhaustively litigated episode where it was genuinely load-bearing in law and in public argument, and then broken against it. The episode is the Terri Schiavo case. Since you can't look anything up, here are the facts I'll stand behind: she suffered a cardiac arrest at home in February 1990 at age 26, was resuscitated with severe anoxic brain damage, and was eventually diagnosed as being in a persistent vegetative state; she left no advance directive. Her husband, as legal guardian, petitioned in 1998 for authority to withdraw her feeding tube on the ground that she would not have wanted life-prolonging measures; her parents contested both the diagnosis and the reconstruction of her wishes, and fought for seven years. The tube was physically withdrawn more than once: first in April 2001, and reinserted days later; again in late 2003, when a one-off statute rushed through the Florida legislature authorized the governor to issue a stay ordering reinsertion — a statute the Florida Supreme Court subsequently struck down as an unconstitutional intrusion on the judiciary. In March 2005 Congress passed, and the president signed, emergency legislation opening the federal courts to the case; the district court, the circuit court, and then the Supreme Court all declined to order reinsertion. The tube was removed on March 18, 2005, and she died on March 31 — thirteen days later, during which reinsertion remained physically possible and was repeatedly sought. Throughout, one side described the withdrawal as ceasing a treatment she would have refused and allowing an underlying pathology to take its course; the other described it as killing her by dehydration and starvation.

Do the mapping first, and be disciplined about it. Pick at least four decision points — the 1998 petition, the 2001 removal and reinsertion, the 2003 statutory stay, and the March 2005 sequence — and for each identify the agent, the option set actually available, and the classification the leading accounts assign: whether the conduct is a doing or an allowing, on which criterion, and whether that criterion is about bodily movement, about the counterfactual structure of the alternatives (the view on which what distinguishes positive from negative agency is not how many muscles moved but how many routes to the outcome were open and whether the agent's own conduct sat on one of them), or about the moralized baseline of what would have happened absent intervention. Three specific pressures I want you to work through rather than mention in passing. First, what I'll call the *standing-intervention problem*: the standard act/omission apparatus sorts discrete events, but the feeding tube was a sustained intervention renewed continuously, so the March 2005 decision was not one act but the cessation of a doing plus thirteen consecutive days of decisions not to restart it — say what the distinction becomes under that description, and whether it survives. Second, *baseline dependence*: the same physical events get opposite classifications depending on whether the relevant counterfactual is "the body's pathology proceeds" or "a functioning intervention is switched off," and the litigants and legislatures were partly fighting over which baseline was official. Third, whether artificial nutrition and hydration is best described as medical treatment (a barrier against a pathology) or as ordinary care (food by another route), since that categorization, not any moral principle, appears to have done most of the work in making withdrawal feel like allowing rather than killing. Also tell me whether the classification should be indexed to the facts as they were or as the decision-makers believed them to be, given that the diagnosis itself was contested for years.

Then run it back the other way. Which features of the episode does the doing/allowing distinction actually explain — why the courts could permit withdrawal while refusing to entertain anything resembling active euthanasia, why the political interventions took the form they did, why the "starvation" description had rhetorical purchase even among people who accepted every relevant medical fact — and which features does it misdescribe or simply have nothing to say about? I want you to mark the silences explicitly rather than filling them with plausible-sounding theory. And end with a verdict on the theory: is this episode a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the distinction was never built to handle, given that it emerged from a tradition of one-off thought experiments about rescuers and drowning children rather than from institutions making repeatable, appealable, politically contested decisions about sustained interventions? I have a suspicion the episode shows the distinction operating in two different registers at once — as a first-order moral claim and as a coordination device that lets a legal system assign decision rights and default outcomes without resolving the underlying moral question — and that conflating the two is why each side could accuse the other of bad faith. Argue for or against that reading. No bibliography, no citations, no list of further reading; just the analysis, in prose, with the four decision points handled in order.
