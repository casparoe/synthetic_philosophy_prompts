My sister's boyfriend Tomas is doing a PhD in virology and my flatmate Ruth did a philosophy degree before switching to data work. They had it out over dinner last week about a documentary line that called viruses the most abundant form of life on the planet, and it went for like an hour. I was just eating and taking notes. Roughly how it went:

1. Tomas: viruses aren't alive, full stop. A virion sitting on a surface is inert — no metabolism, no repair, no energy handling of its own. It only does anything by borrowing a cell's entire apparatus. He said the working definition NASA's exobiology committee landed on in the nineties — roughly, a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution — was written specifically to rule viruses out, and it does.

2. Ruth: then it rules out a lot more than viruses. A mule can't reproduce. A worker ant can't reproduce. A seed or a tardigrade in cryptobiosis has its metabolism effectively stopped. Aphid endosymbiont bacteria have thrown away so much of their metabolism they can't be grown outside their host at all. And mitochondria are literally domesticated bacteria running on the cell's behalf. If how much help you need determines whether you're alive, you've got a scale, not a line, and she asked him where on the scale the line goes.

3. Tomas: those are all capacity cases, not performance cases — the seed restarts, the mule belongs to an evolving lineage, the worker ant is part of a reproducing colony. And mitochondria prove his point rather than Ruth's: a mitochondrion is what happens when a parasite gets domesticated, and a virus is the same relationship that never got domesticated.

4. Ruth: notice what just happened — to save the criterion he moved the subject from the individual particle to the lineage. So "is this thing alive" isn't answerable about a thing at all, only about a population through time. Fine, but that's the known problem with the NASA wording: no single sexually reproducing individual satisfies it. And once the subject is populations with heritable variation, prions get in — prion strains compete with each other and adapt, and in serial transmission experiments they've evolved resistance to drugs, with no nucleic acid and no metabolism anywhere in the story. So his criterion makes a misfolded protein alive.

5. Tomas: no, because the prion isn't replicating anything, it's corrupting a protein the cell already makes, and what evolves is the infected cell line. Same reason a genetic algorithm running in software isn't alive: the definition says chemical system.

6. Ruth: now "chemical" is carrying all the weight, and it's arbitrary — exactly the substrate chauvinism astrobiology is supposed to avoid. If your target concept can't apply to anything that isn't Earth-style chemistry, it can't do the job it was invented for.

7. Tomas: and the boundary he's defending has already moved under pressure, which he thinks shows it's tracking something. He brought up the giant viruses — the ones first mistaken for Gram-positive bacteria, with genomes bigger than some cellular organisms and their own aminoacyl-tRNA synthetase genes plus bits of metabolic machinery — and the virophage discovered in a Paris cooling tower that parasitizes the giant virus's replication factory, i.e. a virus infecting a virus. So "borrows all its machinery" isn't uniformly true.

8. Ruth: that's her conclusion, not his — it shows the transition is continuous, so "alive" isn't a binary predicate and the argument was never going to be settled by a criterion. She then said something stronger: the concept does no work in biology at all. Nothing is true of all and only living things; explanations run on membranes, metabolism, replication, selection. So the question has the status of whether a hotdog is a sandwich — a dispute about a word, and we should retire it.

9. Tomas: you can't retire the word while you're still looking for the thing. Life isn't a checklist property, it's a historical kind — everything alive here shares one origin, so it's a single lineage, the way water turned out to be H2O rather than whatever list of wet, clear, drinkable properties people started with. Plus the term has traction outside the lab: biosafety, what counts as synthesizing an organism, patent questions.

They never converged and my sister made them stop. Can you go through it round by round and say who's right at each step — including where a move just relabels the problem rather than answering it? Then two things I couldn't work out: does Ruth's eliminativism in 8 actually follow from the criteria failing, or is it a separate claim that could be false even if everything before it went her way? And is there any fact about viruses — giant ones, virophages, prions — that could settle this, or is it a dispute no fact touches? I dont have a side here, I genuinely couldn't tell who was winning.
