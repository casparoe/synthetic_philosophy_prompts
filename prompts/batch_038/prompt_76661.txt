I'm a staffer for a city councilmember and we're about to put out a memo defending a proposal with two parts: a four-day-week pilot for city employees, and a no-strings monthly stipend for a few hundred residents who aren't in the labor force at all (carers, people on disability). At the public hearing a guy from a business association stood up and said, twice, that idle hands are the devil's workshop. Two councilmembers have since repeated it back to us, apparently sincerely, and now I have to write something better than "that's just a proverb."

So: give me a menu of the precise claims that sentence could be making, from the ones that are basically platitudes to the ones that are substantive to the ones that are flat false. For each reading, tell me whether it's an empirical prediction, a moral claim, or just a work ethic wearing a descriptive costume; whether it's the kind of thing that could be checked and roughly how; and — the part I actually need — which of our two components it would be an objection to if it were true. My suspicion is that most readings only touch the stipend and not the four-day week, and that any reading that reaches the four-day week has to be using "idle" to mean something other than "not at your desk," but id like that done properly rather than waved at.

Two things I keep coming back to. First, there's an equivocation sliding through the whole debate on "idle": unstructured free time, unemployment, rest, and simply not being available for pay get swapped in and out mid-sentence. Sort out which of those swaps are licensed and which are doing the real work. Second, I've started calling the policy intuition behind the proverb precautionary busyness — keep people occupied as a prophylactic against trouble. Does any reading of the proverb actually support that as a principle of policy, or does it only predict mischief without ever recommending occupancy? Those feel very different to me and the hearing blurred them.

One more, maybe too cute: the version I heard gives the workshop to the devil, but the other form I've run into has the devil finding work for idle hands instead. Those disagree about who the agent is — a person generating their own trouble versus an outside recruiter exploiting availability. Does that difference carry any argumentative weight, or is it just phrasing?

Bottom line first: your opening sentence should be your actual verdict on whether the proverb, on its best available reading, is an objection to either component of our proposal. Then the menu and the reasoning. No preamble and don't restate my question back at me.
