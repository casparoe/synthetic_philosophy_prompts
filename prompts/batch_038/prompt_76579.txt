So here's my problem and it's a slightly embarrassing one. I'm three years into a novel whose middle section turns on a letter written in 1907 by a country doctor who has given a dying man morphia, and I've drafted the letter myself out of notes on period medical ethics — but somewhere around the fourth rewrite it got away from me and now I genuinely can't tell you what my own character is arguing. My editor wants the scene where it gets read aloud to work, and I keep fudging my way through it in conversation. So I need someone to explain my own prose back to me.

The letter as it currently stands (it's my composition, not a quotation from anything real, so please dont go hunting for a source or telling me what More or whoever said about the same thing — I want reasoning about this text, in plain English, no reading lists, no history-of-ideas survey):

"You ask me whether I would repeat what I did on the Tuesday, and I answer that I would, though not for the reason you suppose. There are two things a physician may do to a dying man which the coroner's vocabulary will not distinguish: he may procure a death, or he may decline to stand in the way of one already under way. The first is murder and the second is only the acknowledgment of a fact. But it will be said, and you say it, that a dose given to ease the breathing does not merely acknowledge a fact; it hastens. To which I reply that the hastening is an accident of the remedy, and accidents are not what the law of intention looks to. Here is my test, and I ask you to apply it strictly: consider what I should have done had the man been one whom I knew to be recovering. If I should have given the same dose at the same hour for the same distress, then the dose was given for the distress, and the death that followed belongs to the disease and not to me. If I should not have given it — if the certainty of his recovery would have made me stint the syringe — then I gave it in order that he might die, and all the talk of mercy is a curtain. On the Tuesday I applied the test to myself before I drew the morphia, and I found that I should have given it in either case. So I gave it. And yet I am not easy, for I perceive that the test does not reach the case that troubles me most, which is the case where the honest answer is that I should have given it, and given it sooner, and more of it, had I known he was dying — not in order that he should die, but in order that he should not have to be watched dying for so long. In that case the test acquits the smaller dose and condemns the larger, and I cannot see that the larger is the worse of the two."
