DRAFT: "What Camus is actually arguing against the guillotine"

The usual three-line summary of Camus's 1957 essay on the guillotine goes like this: the death penalty doesn't deter, innocent people get executed, and the whole business is barbaric. Anthology introductions tend to give those three roughly equal billing, and the essay gets taught as a humanitarian protest piece with some statistics stapled on.

I think that summary is misleading, and misleading in a way that hides the most interesting and the most vulnerable part of the case. The deterrence point and the judicial-error point are scaffolding. Camus uses them, but he doesn't lean on them, and he's oddly relaxed about whether they survive scrutiny — he's happy to grant the retentionist his statistics if the retentionist will grant him the scene. What does the work is a structural claim about time and premeditation, and it goes roughly like this.

The retentionist says the death penalty is a repayment: the murderer took a life, so the state takes his, and the books balance. Camus's objection is that the books can't balance, because the two killings differ in a respect that has nothing to do with quantity of suffering. The murderer struck without warning; his victim did not know. The state kills on a schedule. Between sentence and execution there is a period in which the condemned man knows, precisely and institutionally, that he will be killed, and that knowledge is manufactured for him by the very apparatus that claims to be settling a debt. So the punishment contains an element the crime did not contain. It isn't a repayment with interest; it's a different act wearing the grammar of repayment.

That's why the famous opening matters more than it looks. Camus tells the story of his father going to watch a public execution in Algiers shortly before the first world war — a farm labourer who had murdered a whole family — expecting to see justice done and possibly thinking the sentence too light, and coming home unable to speak and then vomiting. The anecdote is normally read as pathos, a way of getting the reader's stomach on side. I want to read it as an argument. The father is the honest citizen the punishment exists to protect and to vindicate; the punishment's effect on him is not vindication but nausea. If the supreme justice produces revulsion in the man it was supposed to reassure, then it has failed at its own stated function and added a second harm to the first crime. The vomit is evidence, not decoration.

Here is where I get into trouble, and this is the part I most want attacked. If premeditated foreknowledge is the load-bearing wrong, then the wrong looks removable. Imagine a jurisdiction that executes people without notice — no date, no ritual, no chaplain, no last meal, the condemned man killed in his sleep on some night he cannot predict. Combine that with a hypothetical where the risk of error is zero and deterrence is actually demonstrated. My reading seems to say Camus should now be out of arguments. He obviously wouldn't be. So either I've mis-located the weight, or the essay contains a deeper claim I'm not stating.

My patch is this: the premeditation that matters isn't the condemned man's dread, it's the state's. A society that kills must first become a society that plans killings — that budgets for them, assigns shifts, maintains equipment, files the paperwork. The wrong is located in what the state has to turn itself into, not in what the victim feels. On this version the sleep-execution hypothetical doesn't help the retentionist at all; it makes things worse, because now the state is doing by stealth and surprise what it used to do in public.

Two further things I want to fold in. First, the biographical tension: in Combat in 1944–45 Camus was arguing for a purge that was short, limited, and severe enough to end in executions for the worst collaborators, against Mauriac, who wanted clemency — and Camus came over to Mauriac's side within a year and said so afterwards. I want to use this not as a gotcha but as a test: if Camus's own position moved, then whatever he thought was doing the work in 1945 wasn't doing the work in 1957, and I'd like to know which of my candidate readings is compatible with both. Second, a closing section on modern analogues — decades-long death row as the institutionalization of the very foreknowledge Camus describes, and the medicalization of execution as a new set of euphemisms doing what the hooded apparatus did. My worry is that this is me smuggling in an American debate and calling it Camus.
