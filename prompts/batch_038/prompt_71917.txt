I work on reliability engineering at a mid-sized software company, and last winter my book club read a philosopher's account of how moral revolutions happen — the argument, as I understood it, being that practices like dueling and foot-binding did not collapse because someone finally won the argument against them, but because the code of honor around them was recalibrated so that continuing to do them became shameful instead of admirable. I have no formal training in philosophy; I build and operate systems for a living, and what I do when I get interested in something is make a spreadsheet. So I have spent a few months cataloguing norm changes I could verify the outline of, and marking each one with a verdict about whether it was genuine moral progress and, separately, about what actually did the causal work: argument, material interest, or honor and shame. What has become clear is that my verdicts do not fit together, and a coworker who read the same book says they obviously do and that I am simply squeamish about my own conclusions.

Here is the catalogue, in the order I made it. Dueling among British gentlemen in the early nineteenth century: I call this real moral progress, and I agree that ridicule rather than argument ended it, since the case against dueling was not new. Foot-binding in China, which largely disappeared within a generation once it became the mark of a backward family rather than a route to a good marriage: progress, and again I think shame was load-bearing and the reformers' reasoning was mostly decoration. The British campaign against the Atlantic slave trade: progress, but I am uneasy, because the moral case had been stated long before it succeeded and the explanations I keep encountering emphasize economic interest and organized religious mobilization; if the argument was not what moved things, I do not know in what sense the moral case was vindicated by events. Elaborate Victorian mourning conventions — the black dress, the seclusion, the schedule of stages — which collapsed under precisely the same social pressure of embarrassment: not a moral change at all, just fashion, which is where I can hear how inconsistent I sound. Smoking in bars and restaurants: decades of epidemiology accomplished little, and the norm flipped once smoking came to be experienced as an imposition on the people nearby; progress, driven by disgust. The legalization and then normalization of same-sex marriage where I live: progress, and this one I think really was carried by argument and personal testimony, by people's coworkers and children coming out. Honor killings in parts of South Asia, which are criminalized and publicly condemned by religious authorities and still practiced: not yet a moral revolution, because the honor code has not moved, and I do not think legislation and condemnation count without it. And finally the prestige of glorified overwork in my own industry — the on-call hero, the crunch, the person who is visibly always reachable — which is draining away mostly through public shaming online while the underlying incentives still reward the behavior: I want to call it progress in progress, but I suspect I am dressing up a shift in economic interest as a moral one.

What I want from you is the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best captures these verdicts, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have not seen before without consulting you — and for each clause of it, tell me which case in my list motivated that clause. I have two specific worries I would like the answer to confront directly. First, dueling and Victorian mourning look to me like the same mechanism producing opposite verdicts, and I need either a principled difference between them or an admission that one of my two judgments is arbitrary. Second, I keep assuming that how a change came about matters to whether it counts as progress, and I cannot tell whether that is a real commitment or a prejudice: if a practice disappeared purely because it stopped being profitable, with nobody's moral reasoning improving at all, does my view oblige me to deny that anything morally good happened, and is that a cost I should accept or a sign my principle is wrong? Then please run the principle on the case I have been going back and forth on for weeks, which is the change in attitudes toward eating animals over the last twenty years: on my account it will either turn out to be an early-stage moral revolution or a lifestyle fashion of the educated professional class, and I should say that I eat meat, so I am alert to the possibility that I have built a principle that flatters me. Finally, tell me plainly which of my listed verdicts your principle cannot accommodate and would have me give up, and apply it to two or three norm changes I have not mentioned, including at least one where you think I would be surprised by the result. I am not looking for a reading list or for references to chase down; I want the reasoning laid out so that a non-specialist can follow it, with any term of art defined the first time it appears. A colleague insists the whole difficulty goes back to a much older philosopher's worries about ethical concepts being historically contingent and about reflection dissolving the knowledge a tradition had already encoded, and I have not been able to work out whether that is a real connection or a name-drop; if you think that line of thought bears on my problem, say how, and if you think it does not, say that instead.
