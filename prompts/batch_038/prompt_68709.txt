Bit of context first. I'm 70, I hung up the lab coat in 2017 after forty years as an analytical chemist — weighing things, titrating things, calibrating things, the unglamorous end of science. Since retiring I've been reading philosophy, badly and enthusiastically, and I've just hit the rule-following stuff, Kripke's Wittgenstein, quus, all of it. I think I've got the shape of the argument but I don't buy it, and every time I try to say why out loud I sound like I'm just thumping the table.

So: be my opponent. Play a thoroughly convinced follower of the sceptical argument about meaning — someone who's thought about it for years and finds the paradox obvious, not someone performing bewilderment for effect. You can hand me small things: yes my behaviour was regular, yes the tie cases were never discussed, yes there's a gap between what I'd have said and what was correct. What you never hand over is the main claim, that there was no fact about what I meant that makes one way of going on right and the others wrong. Stay in it until I write "stop". One paragraph per reply, roughly — I want a conversation, not a paper. No headings, no bullet lists.

The case I want you to attack is mine rather than the arithmetic one, so you can't just recycle the textbook moves. From 1977 to 2017 the standing instruction in our group, inherited from before me and never revised, was to report sample masses to the nearest milligram. The balance read out to a tenth of a milligram. So now and then the display sat exactly on a half milligram, 4.12750 g and the like, and I had to pick a neighbour. In forty years I wrote down eleven of those exact ties. I rounded up on seven and down on four, and I'll tell you straight that the four weren't a policy, I was rushed or I misread the last digit. Everything else — tens of thousands of readings — had no ambiguity in it at all and I never deviated once. The manual says nothing about ties and I never raised the question with anybody.

My opening. Your examples are always built so that the rival rules differ only where the evidence is silent, and then you announce that the evidence can't decide. That's a rigged setup, and my own case shows it. My behaviour across the tens of thousands of unambiguous readings is the behaviour of one procedure, round to nearest, and a tie isn't a seperate island sitting outside that procedure — it's the point the procedure closes in on from both sides, the limit of everything surrounding it. Any competitor you cook up has to copy me exactly everywhere and then jump right at the ties, which makes it a curve with a kink that no measurement ever called for. I fitted curves for four decades. Two fits that agree on all the data, one of them carrying an unmotivated kink — we name the kinked one gerrymandered and we don't take it seriously, we don't shrug and say the data was silent. So the practice does settle the rule, and your paradox only bites on people who've never had to choose between fits for a living.
