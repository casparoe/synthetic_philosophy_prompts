I have sat for six years on the Historical Redress Committee of a private foundation — seven voting members, a staff lawyer, and a modest endowment whose own origins in nineteenth-century land dealings are the subject of a standing joke we never quite resolve. We evaluate claims brought by descendant communities, and our charter gives us only this much guidance: the fund exists to address wrongs whose consequences continue to structure the lives of the communities that suffered them, and the committee has discretion over the form of any remedy. We have never written down a decision rule. One member, a former appellate judge, keeps a private ledger of our votes and insists we are running a single test — that there must be somebody still answerable. A second, an economist, thinks we are really running a different test — whether the wrong still shows up in the claimants' lives today. A third, a historian, thinks both are rationalizations of something else, namely that we approve when the respondent institution has never admitted anything and reject when it has. I suspect at least one of those three glosses fails to fit at least one of our six decided claims, and I would like to know which. Our record is as follows:

1. **Approved.** Descendants of a farming community in Calder County whose land was stripped in the 1920s through collusive tax foreclosures run out of the county treasurer's office. The county still exists, still holds and leases several of the parcels, and the claimant families remain markedly poorer and far less likely to own land than their neighbours. Remedy: a community land trust plus a formal acknowledgment by the county; no payments to individuals.
2. **Rejected.** Descendants of workers in a company-run contract labour scheme of the 1870s and 1880s. This group's current income and health outcomes are the worst in any file we have seen. But the operating company was dissolved in 1893, its records were destroyed in a fire, and no part of its assets can be traced to any existing firm, endowment, or public body. Our written rationale was that we could find no one who owed the debt.
3. **Approved.** A state boarding school that between 1900 and 1950 punished children for speaking their own language. Both the successor state agency and the sponsoring church body still exist. There is no property loss to point to, and the community's material position is close to the regional average, but there are eleven fluent speakers left, all past seventy. Remedy: an immersion school, teacher training, curriculum revision, and a memorial; again, no payments to individuals.
4. **Approved at roughly a third of what was sought, with reasons.** A township defrauded of its common land in the 1930s — whose own leaders, in the 1960s, used the compensation they received to buy out and clear a smaller hamlet of tenants downstream. We said the later wrong did not erase the earlier one, and reduced rather than denied.
5. **Rejected.** A diaspora association whose members were expelled from a port city in 1905. The confiscated properties passed, through a chain we could document without gaps, into the endowment of a university that has since issued a formal apology and established its own programme. The association's members are now better educated and wealthier than the national median, and no institution disadvantages them. Our rationale: the wrong was real, but nothing in their lives today is shaped by it, and the holder of the benefit had already repudiated the transfer.
6. **Approved with conditions.** A public hospital system's non-consensual study, closed in 1963. The hospital argued that every individual responsible was dead. We held that the institution's continuity was enough, and awarded lifetime health monitoring for survivors plus a trust for their children, on documented intergenerational health effects. No cash to individuals here either.

The claim now before us concerns the Ash Fork valley, flooded in the 1940s to build a hydroelectric reservoir. The utility that built the dam operates it still, is solvent, and has never disputed that the compensation paid at the time was below what the land would have fetched on an open market — but it maintains the taking was lawful, that the prices reflected genuine agricultural value, and that it owes nothing further. Compensation was paid to registered household heads only; the valley's landholding was in practice communal under informal arrangements the assessing office declined to recognise, and in several families the money went to one relative who kept it. Today about two-fifths of descendant households trace themselves to a compensated head, three-fifths do not. The descendants' material position is now slightly above the state median: the dam brought rural electrification, roads, and eventually a tourism economy in which some of their own families built businesses. What is gone is the cemetery, under forty feet of water; the church; the annual reunion; and the valley dialect, spoken fluently by fewer than forty people, none under sixty. The descendants' association is split, and an organised minority drawn mainly from the compensated two-fifths opposes the claim publicly, saying it will brand them as victims and reopen arguments about money their grandfathers received. Finally, the utility has announced it will decommission the reservoir in roughly twelve years, whereupon the lakebed will be exposed and, under current law, will revert to the utility unless some public body asserts a claim to it. The petition asks for four things: a cash payment to the uncompensated three-fifths; title to the lakebed, held in trust, when it emerges; exhumation and reinterment of the cemetery, or failing that a surveyed memorial; and a formal statement from the utility that the compensation was inadequate.

What I want from you is a prediction and a reconstruction, not a general essay on redress. Predict our verdict, and because we have always separated remedies, give a separate verdict on each of the four requests — approve, reject, or approve with conditions, and in the last case what the condition would be. Then lay out the two or three decision rules that are each genuinely consistent with all six of our past votes, being explicit about which of my three colleagues' glosses, if any, actually survives the record, and showing how each surviving rule disposes of the new case; where they part company, say so plainly. Tell me which single precedent is the closest analog — I keep going back and forth between the Calder County case, because the respondent still holds and profits from the very land it took, and the boarding-school case, because what remains is a language and a cemetery rather than a measurable disadvantage, and I know the diaspora case is being quoted at me in dissent — and justify the choice rather than assuming it. Address two specific difficulties. First, we have never once awarded money to individuals in six votes: is that a principle about communal wrongs requiring communal remedies, or an artifact of the cases that happened to reach us, and does the compensated/uncompensated split inside this petitioner group give us a reason to break the pattern now? Second, our reduction in the fourth case: did it rest on the claimants' own misconduct, in which case it has no application here, or on not paying twice for one loss, in which case the electrification and tourism benefits these descendants received are exactly the kind of thing it was meant to net off? One of our members has also said that approving anything here commits us to a view he considers absurd, since had the dam not been built none of the petitioners would exist; tell me whether that objection has any force against a fund like ours, and whether any of the six votes has already committed us for or against it. Where the precedents genuinely run out — and I expect they do on the lakebed — say what principle we would be adopting by deciding either way, given that in the diaspora case we let an institution keep a benefit it had apologised for. Finally, propose one hypothetical claim, not on our docket, that would discriminate among the candidate rules better than anything we have already decided, and say what each rule would predict about it.
