I'm writing the final for a grad philosophy-of-physics seminar and I want one essay question on probability in the Everett interpretation that can't be answered just by naming the standard approaches. Here's the thing I want to build it around — a little derivation I cooked up for the exam:

Suppose an electron is in sqrt(1/3)|up> + sqrt(2/3)|down> and gets measured along z. A student argues: (i) after the measurement both outcomes happen, so the only thing left to be uncertain about is which branch I'm on; (ii) I have no information distinguishing the branches, so indifference gives equal credence to each; (iii) the amplitudes are unequal, but I can always refine the apparatus so the down-branch splits into two branches of amplitude sqrt(1/3) each — now there are three equal-amplitude branches, two of them down; (iv) so credence 1/3 in up, 2/3 in down. Born rule, derived from counting plus indifference.

Write the question itself (25 marks, 90 minutes, closed book), a model answer of about 700 words, and a marking scheme. The question has to make students say whether step (iii) actually derives the weights or just repackages them, and it should make them deal with what happens when the squared amplitudes arent in a rational ratio, so the equalizing move can't be run exactly. For the marking scheme, spell out the moves an A answer must contain that a B answer typically leaves out — I want it explicit that saying "branch counting is basis-dependent" on its own doesn't earn much — plus the specific errors that should cost marks. No bibliography or suggested readings, please; students aren't allowed to cite anything anyway.
