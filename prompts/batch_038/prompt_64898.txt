I serve on the acquisitions committee of a mid-sized university art museum, and on Friday we vote on a purchase I expect will split us. I would like you to draft the written opinion I will circulate to the other six members in advance of the meeting.

The case. A living artist is offering us a durational installation: a single sealed room running continuously for thirty days, in which a thin film of pigment-laden water is released onto a plaster floor and slowly evaporates, leaving a sediment record that no one, including the artist, can predict in advance. The work exists only while it runs; the sediment is the residue. What is being sold is the residue plus the right to re-perform the piece under her supervision at some future date. She will not permit viewers during a cycle — the room is kept empty except for one attendant — on her stated view that the piece behaves differently when it is watched. The current cycle ends next week, and the next one begins in four months, in another country. No member of this committee can see the work before the money is committed.

What we have instead. Two people attended the previous cycle in full. One is a curator who is also the artist's closest friend and who wrote the essay for her monograph; she holds that the piece is the most important thing the artist has made. The other is an independent critic with no relationship to the artist, who published a review saying that she found the first four days nearly unbearable and was converted around the fifth. She now ranks the work highly, but she told me in an email — which I intend to read into the minutes — that she does not think she can argue anyone into her change of mind, only report it. We also have thirty-one dated photographs taken by the attendant, a written description of the mechanics from the artist, and a condition report on the residue. A donor will cover half the price, but only if the vote happens this month.

Two live disagreements inside the committee. One of our trustees, a collector, keeps saying that you cannot buy a thing nobody has seen, and he has invoked the acquaintance principle by name in writing. A junior curator answered him with a list of Hume's qualifications for the ideal critic and argued that both witnesses satisfy them; I think she has not noticed that the two witnesses attended the same cycle, in the same room, and appear to have formed their views largely in conversation with each other. Separately, nobody has yet said whether the artist's no-viewers rule should be read as a substantive claim about the work or as a device for protecting its price, and the two readings seem to imply different things about how much we can learn from anyone who did get in.

What I need from you. Draft the opinion in American English, in a formal register suitable for circulation to trustees, and keep it to roughly 1,300 words. Commit to a recommendation rather than weighing options indefinitely. Do not invent additional facts about the artist, the witnesses, or the work beyond what I have given, and do not supply a bibliography, footnotes, or a reading list — the members want reasons, not references. Use exactly the following sections, in this order:

**Bottom line** — three or four sentences stating the recommendation in a form that could be lifted directly into the minutes, including whether we buy, decline, or buy subject to conditions.

**What the witnesses are witnesses to** — about 250 words separating what the curator and the critic can reliably hand over to us (facts about how the work behaves, what attending it is like at various stages, what the residue will turn out to be) from what they cannot hand over (the judgment that it is good), and saying which side of that line our decision ought to rest on.

**The trustee's objection at its strongest** — about 250 words making his case better than he has made it himself, including a version of it that does not depend on any general thesis about aesthetic testimony at all.

**Why that objection does not settle the vote** — about 300 words. This must confront rather than sidestep the fact that both witnesses saw the same cycle and talked to one another, and must say what weight their agreement actually carries once that is granted.

**Conditions** — about 200 words proposing concrete contractual and institutional terms that would let us commit this month while preserving a route to first-hand acquaintance before the full sum is spent. Be specific enough that our registrar could cost them.

**The dissent we should expect** — about 150 words anticipating the strongest thing that will be said in the room against your recommendation, and giving me one or two sentences with which to answer it.
