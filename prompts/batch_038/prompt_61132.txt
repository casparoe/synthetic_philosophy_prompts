so I'm putting together a position paper on replacing our lower house with a sortition chamber, and I'd like a reality check on whether my views actually cohere before I present this to people who will tear it apart. here's the list:

(1) elections are aristocratic in character — "pick the best" is a filter for the prominent and the ambitious, so any elected body ends up run by people who are good at getting elected. this is my headline reason for liking the lot.

(2) but the deeper justification I lean on is descriptive: the assembly should be a statistical mirror of the country (class, education, region, gender), because that's the only way the perspectives of people who'd never survive a campaign actually make it into the room.

(3) I'd add a competence gate — you have to pass a basic civics and numeracy test to be in the draw pool, since a pure draw off the electoral register will hand seats to people who can't follow a budget.

(4) I don't think losing the vote is a real cost, because a chance at holding office is a more equal share in ruling than one ballot among forty million.

(5) empirically I know the invitation problem bites: when you actually mail people, the ones who say yes skew hard toward the educated and already-engaged, so real mini-publics aren't mirrors unless you fix up the sample after the fact.

(6) I'm against sortition for courts though. judges should be appointed on expertise, even though (4) looks like it commits me to the same equality argument for judging.

(7) design detail: three-year non-renewable terms, plus a second chamber drawn from the people who *declined* the first invitation, which can veto bills but not propose them.

something in here is costing me more than I'm admitting and I can't tell what. audit it: which pairs genuinely conflict, which only look like they conflict, rank the real ones by severity, and for each say the cheapest fix and what that fix costs me. please don't just flag everything as a problem — I want to know which of these are fine as they stand.
