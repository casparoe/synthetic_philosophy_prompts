I'm putting together handouts for a seminar on herding and I want a case designed to order, not the usual textbook one. Here's the spec.

A street where five households decide one after another, in full view of each other, whether to evacuate before a flood. Purely informational — nobody's payoff depends on what the others *do*, only on whether the levee holds. I want (1) each later person to hold a strictly more accurate private signal than the one before, (2) an information cascade to form anyway and land on the wrong action, and (3) the first person's signal to be barely better than a coin flip, such that had it come out the other way the whole street would of ended up right. The wrong consensus should be pinned to the least informed person in it.

Constraints: full Bayesian rationality, common knowledge of the signal structure and of each other's rationality, no payoff externalities, no ambiguity aversion, no bounded-rationality gimmicks smuggled in to make it work. Set the costs so the decision rule flips at exactly even odds and say that you did. Realistic, a paragraph or two of setup, and give me the actual numbers so I can check the arithmetic myself.

Then show the work: each household's posterior and why the action follows, so the bad cascade falls out of the calculation rather than being asserted — a scenario that just declares the verdicts is useless to me. After that, tell me which feature is doing the work. My guess is common knowledge of rationality: redo it with each household believing there's a 20% chance any given predecessor ignored their own signal and picked at random, and say whether the cascade survives and why or why not. Finish with how a defender of the standard model would attack my case — for instance, whether requirement (1) is even coherent once precisions climb fast enough that accumulated weak evidence can't be overturned by one strong signal.
