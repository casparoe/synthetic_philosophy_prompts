I spent thirty-four years as an analytical chemist in a government metrology laboratory, most of that time concerned with measurement uncertainty and with the unglamorous question of when an instrument's output is good enough to act upon. Since retiring I have taken up epistemology with more seriousness than my former colleagues would have predicted, and I have become preoccupied by an institutional practice I brushed against professionally for decades without ever examining it philosophically: the way the American regulator of drugs and devices grades the evidence it demands according to what is at stake. For a patient with a rapidly fatal illness and no alternative treatment, a new compound may be approved on the strength of a surrogate endpoint — tumour shrinkage, say, or a laboratory marker — that is only thought to predict clinical benefit, subject to confirmatory trials being conducted afterwards and to withdrawal of the approval if they fail. The same compound, offered for a mild and non-life-threatening complaint, would be refused outright on that showing; the agency would require direct demonstration of benefit and a far larger safety database. Something structurally parallel obtains for devices, which are sorted into three classes according to the harm their failure could cause, so that a moderate-risk device can often be cleared by demonstrating that it is substantially equivalent to an already-marketed predicate, with no new clinical data whatever, while a high-risk device serving the same anatomical purpose must carry clinical investigations of its own. So far as I can determine, the agency does not describe any of this as a relaxation of its epistemic standard; its official account is that the standard of evidence is fixed and that what varies is the balance of benefit against risk which it is acceptable to strike. A former colleague of mine now sits on a hospital pharmacy and therapeutics committee and assures me this is simply how the business is done, but I do not find that an answer.

I should like to know whether the official account can be made coherent. Consider two dossiers that are token-identical: the same compound, the same trials, the same effect sizes and confidence intervals, submitted once for a lethal orphan disease and once for a nuisance condition. The reviewers reach opposite conclusions. Since the data do not differ, the difference must reside somewhere in the framework, and the risk-benefit story does not seem to me to locate it: if the question put to the reviewers is whether the data show that the drug works, then either they do or they do not, and the gravity of the illness is beside the point; if the question is instead whether marketing should be permitted, then the verb show has quietly changed its sense between the two submissions. My own half-formed hunch is that everything turns on whether an approval is an assertion or a permission. If it is an assertion — a public statement by the agency that this drug works — and if assertions are governed by some knowledge-like norm, then a stakes-graded approval policy appears to commit the agency either to the contextualist line illustrated by DeRose's bank examples or to the related and more radical claim, pressed by others, that whether one knows depends on what stands to be lost by being wrong; and it would then owe us a reply to the invariantists, who insist that what varies with the stakes is not knowledge but only the practical rationality of acting on it. If approval is nothing but a permission, the agency may remain a strict invariantist and let the stakes enter solely through decision theory, in which case the practice presupposes nothing epistemologically interesting at all. The provisional character of accelerated approval confuses me further and seems to cut both ways: the agency appears to grant and later revoke a warrant, which suggests it never treated its own conclusion as knowledge in the ordinary sense, yet a withdrawal is announced in precisely the language of having been mistaken, and the confirmatory obligation is imposed as though the original finding were already, in some attenuated way, established. A third datum troubles me still more. Within the same institution the conventional threshold of statistical significance does not move with the stakes: a p-value below 0.05 counts as the same achievement whether the indication is a fatal malignancy or a cosmetic complaint. And in the criminal law, where the standard of proof plainly differs between civil and criminal proceedings, the reasonable-doubt standard does not, so far as I can tell, tighten as the potential sentence lengthens; a defendant facing five years and one facing life are convicted on the same epistemic showing. Whatever theory licenses the graded approval scheme must therefore also explain why it does not license these neighbouring variations.

What I want is a reconstruction of the epistemology this practice actually presupposes, conducted with care about the difference between presupposition and mere compatibility. Specifically: which accounts of evidence, and of knowledge, render the graded-evidence scheme intelligible, and which does the scheme positively exclude; whether the assertion/permission distinction I have proposed is a genuine hinge or a distinction without a difference, and if it is genuine, whether either horn remains available to an agency that describes its own decisions in the language it actually uses; whether the several parts of the practice — surrogate endpoints, post-marketing confirmatory obligations, risk-based device classes, an invariant significance threshold — presuppose mutually consistent things, or whether the scheme is a patchwork that no single theory underwrites; and what would have to change in the institution, both procedurally and in the wording of its decisions, were it rebuilt on a strict invariantist epistemology in which the sufficiency of evidence never depends on what turns on being right. Please be explicit about where the practice is philosophically underdetermined. I expect a good deal of it is licensed equally well by rival theories, and I would rather see that underdetermination mapped than quietly resolved by stipulation.
