Your task is to write an internal revenue playbook — the kind of onboarding document circulated to new hires at a subscription company — for a fictional firm whose entire business model is a money pump. Target length 700–900 words, formatted as a corporate training document: a title block, numbered sections, and at least two boxed callouts (a compliance FAQ, a field tip, a risk-register entry). The prose should read as deadpan, competent, commercially shrewd corporate instruction.

Set the case up as follows, and keep the arithmetic exact wherever it reappears:

- The firm sells three subscription tiers. A given customer strictly prefers Tier 1 to Tier 2, Tier 2 to Tier 3, and Tier 3 to Tier 1 — a genuine cycle, no ties, no indifference anywhere.
- Every change of tier carries a small switching fee, calibrated below each gap in the customer's ranking, so that each switch is strictly preferred to staying put even after the fee is deducted.
- Consequently a customer holding Tier 3 will pay to move to Tier 2; holding Tier 2, will pay to move to Tier 1; holding Tier 1, will pay to move back to Tier 3. One full circuit returns them to their original tier minus three fees, and the circuit can be repeated without limit.
- Not every customer is exploitable. The playbook must segment them: myopic customers, who evaluate only the switch currently in front of them and are therefore fully pumpable; sophisticated customers, who reason backward through the whole circuit and consequently refuse the very first switch even though that switch looks attractive considered on its own; and resolute customers, who also see the circuit but adopt a plan at the outset and decline later temptations to deviate from it, and who are safe only for as long as they can actually hold to the plan.

The satire lives in what the firm therefore sells. Its real product is not the switching fee but a portfolio of countermeasures against foresight and against commitment: presenting switches one at a time so no circuit is ever visible in a single view; splitting the circuit into steps that never appear on the same screen; marketing the absence of any binding arrangement as a customer benefit; injecting urgency so that no plan has time to form; a program for re-opening commitments the customer has already made, on the grounds that flexibility is a feature; and a candid note that a resolute customer stays resolute only while nothing makes deviation locally appealing.

The narrator must never argue for the firm's position, only presuppose it, and must maintain it without a single crack in the voice: nothing objectionable occurs, because at every moment at which the firm asked, the customer preferred what they chose to what they already had. Establishing harm would require comparing the customer with themselves across time, and the firm declines to make comparisons of that kind. The narrator is not stupid and never calls the customer stupid.

Land the objections by implication only. Required elements:

- A compliance FAQ answering "is the customer harmed?" and, separately, "is the customer irrational?" — where the second answer visibly costs the firm more effort than the first, and the extra effort shows only in what the document declines to say.
- A risk-register entry recording that the firm's own pricing committee, voting pairwise over three candidate bundles, produced a cyclic majority ranking, and that an outside consultancy has since offered to charge the committee a small fee per switch. Log this as a legal exposure. Let the entry miss entirely what it implies about the firm's own account of rational choice.
- One short invented transcript of a customer-success call and one invented line from an internal memo.
- A closing boilerplate sign-off that inadvertently restates the firm's exposure.

Constraints: no citations, footnotes, bibliography, or reading list; do not name, cite, or allude to any real philosopher, economist, paper, or company; do not reproduce any real text in quotation marks — every quoted voice is invented. Present the technical apparatus in the firm's own commercial idiom (rankings, circuits, switch fees, foresight, commitment) rather than as lecture notes, but let it be precise enough that a decision theorist recognizes that each feature doing work in the playbook is a feature doing real work in the argument the playbook parasitizes — including the fact that the whole scheme depends on the customer's inability to see the sequence and to bind themselves. No section may state the moral, and the document may never wink: the reader should be able to tell what is being ironized from what the firm says about itself.
