I am six years into a novel whose central figure is a woman born without sight who undergoes surgery for congenital cataracts in her late twenties. To render her credibly I have spent two years reading two bodies of work that, so far as I can tell, have never once addressed each other, and what I want from you is a diagnosis of that silence rather than a précis of either side.

The philosophers treat the case as settled in principle by an account of what concepts and perceptual content are: if the shape concept acquired through touch either is or is not the very concept a visual experience can present, then no operation performed on any patient can change the answer. They further hold that the experiments have not tested the case as specified — the objects were novel block constructions rather than a cube and a sphere, the subjects' acuity was degraded, the displays were static and viewed from a single position, and the restoration of sight was gradual rather than instantaneous.

The vision scientists treat the case as an underdetermined stipulation waiting to be operationalized. In one study now routinely appealed to, five children treated for congenital blindness matched objects within touch and within vision at close to ceiling, but across the two modalities performed barely better than guessing, and improved markedly within five days without explicit training; the authors read this as a qualified negative answer.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: these are not two answers to one question but three questions wearing a single coat — a question about the format of shape representation, whether amodal or modality-specific; a question about a behavioural capacity; and a question about the character of the experience itself — and the impasse arises because each side takes the thought experiment to be authoritative for a different one of them. A rival hypothesis I cannot rule out is that the empirical camp has smuggled in a normative premise, namely that a capacity is exhausted by measured performance under testing conditions, in which case the dispute is about standards of evidence rather than about facts.

Which of these is it — substantive, verbal, a clash over evidential standards, or two questions under one name? Support the diagnosis with what each side is actually committed to, not with a survey, and then say what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a better experiment (moving stimuli, canonical solids, testing within hours), a distinction, a reformulation of the question, or nothing at all.

One last thing, and I ask it because the book depends on it. Three months after her operation my character separates cubes from spheres reliably at a glance, yet maintains that she does not so much see the difference as recall how her hand would have to travel. Would that report discriminate among your candidate diagnoses, or is it precisely the datum both camps would claim?
