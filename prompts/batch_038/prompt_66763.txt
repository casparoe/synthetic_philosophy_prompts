I am a data engineer by trade, with no philosophy training behind me, and I arrived at Nāgārjuna sideways: a meditation group I attend handed around a paperback of the root verses with a commentary, and I have been working through it slowly for about a year. What I cannot get past is the four-cornered argument — the pattern in which a matter is put as: that P, that not P, that both P and not P, that neither P nor not P — and then all four corners are refused. Most people in the group treat the refusals as a kind of koan meant to break your concepts, and one member, who reads a good deal of contemporary logic, insists that the third corner commits Nāgārjuna to contradictions being true and that you therefore need a non-classical logic to model what he is doing. I want to argue against both of those readings, and I would like you to make that argument for me in the strongest form you can manage — as an advocate, not as a referee surveying the options.

Here is the position I want defended, in my own words and probably badly put. The four corners are not four candidate answers to a well-posed question; they are four answers to a question that fails a well-formedness check, because each inherits the same unexamined presupposition, namely that the term in the subject slot picks out something with a nature of its own, independent of the relations and designations that make it the thing it is. If that presupposition is false, then refusing all four corners is neither the assertion of a contradiction, nor the reverent withholding of an ineffable truth, nor a revision of the underlying logic: it is what a type checker does when it rejects an expression outright rather than returning false. On this reading the target is never the law of non-contradiction; it is the practice of asking questions whose grammar promises more than the world can deliver. In my day job, when a query returns nothing at all, the usual cause is not that the data is contradictory but that the query was malformed — and I suspect that is the right model here.

Please press that case against the two rival readings I mentioned: the one on which the third corner is a true contradiction, and the one on which nothing is asserted at all and the negations merely erase themselves along with the views they cancel. Three difficulties have to be handled head-on, because I do not think my analogy survives them untouched. First, if the real point is that the question is malformed, the fourth corner — that neither P nor not P — looks like precisely the corner that should be accepted, and yet it is refused along with the rest; the position owes an account of why. Second, the thesis must be turned on itself: if every view is empty of own-nature, what is the status of this well-formedness claim, and does dissolving questions dissolve it too? Third, the reading threatens to turn Madhyamaka into a merely linguistic doctrine, which sits badly with the fact that the whole enterprise is supposed to do something to the person doing it — to loosen a grip, not to correct a usage — so the argument should show how the transformative aim survives a view on which nothing is asserted and no logic is amended. It would also help to note where the early discourses already do something structurally similar, for instance when the Buddha declines to say whether a fully liberated person exists after death and compares the question to asking which direction an extinguished fire has gone; but treat that as an analogy whose limits you should mark, not as an authority you can lean on.

I am not asking for a reading list, references, verse numbers, or a survey of which scholars hold which line — I cannot check any of it and I do not want to be sent off to look things up. I want the reasoning, and I want you to be explicit at each step about whether it rests on the text, on a general logical point, on an interpretive choice a competent reader could reject, or on your own reconstruction. Where the position is genuinely vulnerable, say so plainly rather than papering over it, and close by telling me what would most convincingly defeat it.
