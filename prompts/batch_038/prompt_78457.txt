I'm a second-year graduate student putting together a problem set for our epistemology section on tracking theories, and last night I got into a two-hour argument with a friend of mine — a former classmate who now works outside philosophy but still reads everything — about whether a tracking account can generate a genuine failure of closure under known entailment without simply smuggling skepticism back in through the side door. His position was that once you strip away the exotic machinery, every such case collapses into one of two failures: either the conditional linking the two propositions is an ordinary empirical reliability claim that the subject has no business claiming to know, in which case the antecedent of the closure principle is never satisfied and nothing interesting has been shown; or the nearby world in which the subject's belief in the entailed proposition goes astray is a world containing a deceiver with the resources to fool her, which is just the skeptical hypothesis wearing ordinary clothes. He offered, as his Exhibit A for the first horn, a case in which a technician knows that an assay plate came back clear and infers that the sample is free of the pathogen; I think that one is broken exactly the way he says, since a clear plate doesn't entail an uncontaminated sample, so the conditional is empirical, is false in worlds quite close to actuality, and the subject never knew it. I want to hand him a counterexample he can't dispose of that way, and I'd like you to build it.

Please construct a single case in which a subject knows an ordinary empirical proposition p, knows that p entails q where the entailment is necessary or knowable a priori rather than a contingent reliability generalization, competently deduces q from p and thereby comes to believe q, and yet fails to know q specifically because the variation (sensitivity) condition fails for q — not because she fails to believe q, not because the adherence condition misfires, and not because any of the other tracking conditions fail. The constraints are as follows. The scenario proper must be one paragraph of no more than 150 words, set in a plausible workplace or field setting with no science fiction. No skeptical hypotheses may appear anywhere in the case or in the analysis: no demons, vats, simulations, dream scenarios, or omniscient deceivers; and equally no stock examples already doing the rounds in the literature — no painted mules, no barn facades, no lottery tickets, no Gettier-style job candidates or stopped clocks. The proposition q must be an ordinary empirical claim with practical stakes that a layperson hearing the story would unhesitatingly say the subject knows. In the nearest worlds where q is false, the subject's total evidence must be numerically identical to what it actually is — same readings, same experiences, same testimony — so that the case cannot be solved by making her evidence differ. And the modal-distance asymmetry has to be earned rather than stipulated: the nearest not-p worlds must be single-change departures from actuality that her method catches, while the not-q worlds must require a conjunction of at least two independently unlikely conditions, and you must state those conditions explicitly and say why their conjunction is genuinely farther off than the single change. If the case turns on a technical fact about an instrument, assay, material, protocol, or species, the fact must be correct, and you must state it in one sentence so I can check it.

Then show your work, in this order. First, an itemized specification check demonstrating that each constraint above is met, since a case that asserts the verdicts rather than generating them from the tracking conditions doesn't count. Second, a full tracking analysis for each of the three beliefs — p, q, and the conditional — naming the method M by which each is held, listing the nearest relevant worlds, and reporting whether truth, belief, variation, and adherence each succeed or fail, with the failure diagnosed precisely. Third, a treatment of the method-relativization reply: run the sensitivity assessment for q twice, once with M individuated narrowly as the deduction itself and once with it individuated broadly, and say which individuation is better motivated and whether either one restores knowledge of q. Fourth, identify the single structural feature of your case that does the work, and run at least two removal tests — for instance, what happens if exact duplication is ruled out, or if the subject has a second independent method available, or if she has some positive reason to suspect interference — saying in each case whether the closure failure survives, weakens, or collapses. Fifth, deliver your own verdict on whether the conjunction "she knows p, and she knows p entails q, but she doesn't know q" sounds tolerable or outrageous in your case, and say how it compares with the familiar hands-and-vat version: is the tolerability you've achieved a real advantage for the tracking theorist, or evidence that you've merely hidden the deceiver? And sixth, write the strongest single attack a defender of closure could make on your case — specify which premise they target and whether, in your judgment, the attack lands. I'd rather have a case that is modest and clearly correct than an ambitious one that fails on inspection, so if you conclude at any point that one of my constraints cannot be jointly satisfied with the others, say so explicitly and tell me which one to drop rather than quietly weakening it.
