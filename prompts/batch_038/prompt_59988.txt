Take Ruth Benedict's wartime work on Japan — the stuff she did for the Office of War Information, which came out afterwards as The Chrysanthemum and the Sword — and run it through the question of whether national character is even a coherent notion. I know the basics: she never went to Japan, she worked from films, novels, newspaper clippings, propaganda and interviews with Japanese-Americans, the whole culture-at-a-distance setup; and the brief was predictive — will Japan surrender, what has to be in the terms, what happens under occupation. The motivating puzzle was that Japanese POWs behaved in a way Americans couldn't parse: they talked freely and didn't try to get word to their families, which is backwards from what an American soldier was expected to do. The shame-culture / guilt-culture split came out of this.

Here's what I want, and I want it disciplined rather than a vibe.

First, the mapping. What is the actual claim-form? Is the Japanese-are-a-shame-culture thesis a claim about a mean, about a distribution, about a set of conditional dispositions (when face is at stake, they'll do X), or about the culture rather than the people at all? Those have wildly different evidential requirements and I doubt the OWI brief cared which one it was buying. Say what would have to be true for the POW puzzle to be evidence for a character claim rather than for something else — incentives, the sheer weirdness of being captured alive, selection effects on who survived to be interviewed.

Second, score it. What did the approach get right, what did it get wrong, and — harder — what couldn't have counted either way because it was unfalsifable? Be careful here: how much her material actually shaped the decision to leave the emperor institution standing is disputed, so don't rest anything on that. Use what isn't disputed instead — the book was translated in Japan within a couple of years, sold millions there, spawned an entire genre of writings on Japaneseness, and turned shame vs. guilt into ordinary Japanese vocabulary. Which gets at the twist I care about most: the description helped make itself true, or at least made itself untestable. What does a national characterology do epistemically once the target population has read it? Is there any principled difference between a character claim that's confirmed and one that's self-confirming, and do the Japanese readers' embrace of the book count for it or against it?

Third, the normative half. It's one thing to ask whether generalizing about peoples is coherent, another to ask whether this one was permissible: made by an outsider, about an enemy, under wartime conditions, for the purpose of predicting and managing them, with surrender terms and occupation policy downstream. Does the use change the epistemic standing or only the moral one? And there's an asymmetry that bugs me — the same claims in a Japanese author's mouth about Japan read as self-understanding, in Benedict's mouth they read as stereotyping. Is that a real distinction or motivated reasoning in a standpoints costume?

Then tell me where the national-character vocabulary goes silent: on disagreement inside the population, on change over time, on who counts as a qualified informant (her sources were heavily one class and one dislocated moment, and Japanese critics said roughly that almost immediately). Say whether some other apparatus — norms as institutions, situational pressure, distributions instead of essences — does the same predictive work without the bagage. Finish with an honest verdict: does this episode rescue the notion, kill it, or show that it was never the sort of thing an episode could confirm?
