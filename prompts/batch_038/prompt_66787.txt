I am a teaching assistant for an upper-division undergraduate survey of American philosophy (Peirce, James, Dewey) with about forty-five students, and I have been asked to grade the essay portion of our midterm and to submit written justification for every grade I assign. The instructor has been explicit that she wants the comments to be itemized rather than impressionistic, because the grades are audited by the department when students appeal, and because she wants the feedback the students receive to point at particular moves in their answers rather than at general qualities like clarity or effort. Students were expected to work only from the primary texts we assigned — Peirce's two 1877–78 Popular Science Monthly papers and his 1905 Monist articles, and James's 1907 lectures — and no secondary literature, so I do not want to penalize anyone for failing to engage with scholarship they were never given. The students chose one of two essay prompts; the response below is an answer to the first, written by a senior who is a cognitive science major with a philosophy minor and who has generally been a solid but not outstanding student. I have not yet formed a view about this particular answer and would rather not arrive at one by skimming; what I need from you is an independent, item-by-item assessment that I can adapt into the gradebook entry.

The prompt as given on the exam was:

> In his 1905 articles Peirce announced that he was renaming his doctrine pragmaticism, and he insisted there that the pragmatic maxim is a rule of logic for clarifying concepts rather than an account of what truth consists in. Explain what Peirce took to be philosophically at stake in that insistence. Then assess whether James's account of truth in the 1907 lectures actually falls to Peirce's objection, or whether James's view is instead compatible with the framework Peirce himself endorses. Support your answer with at least one specific commitment of each thinker.

The student wrote:

> The pragmatic maxim says that in order to render a conception clear we should consider what practical effects we conceive the object of that conception to have, and that our conception of those effects is the whole of our conception of the object. What is at stake for Peirce in insisting that this is a rule of logic and not a theory of truth is the scope of the doctrine. If the maxim is read as telling us what truth is, pragmatism starts to look like the view that a belief is true insofar as it is useful, which Peirce regarded as a vulgarization of his idea, and which is why he renamed the doctrine pragmaticism — a label he chose because he judged it unattractive enough that nobody would want to steal it. So the renaming was substantially a matter of priority and of who gets to define the movement.
>
> Peirce's own positive account, from the earlier papers on the fixation of belief, is that truth is the opinion the community of investigators is destined eventually to converge upon. But this is simply the maxim applied to the concept of truth: the meaning of truth is fixed by the practical consequences of inquiring, and those consequences are that inquirers end up agreeing. On this reading Peirce and James are considerably closer than the 1905 polemic suggests. James says that truth happens to an idea, that ideas are made true by events in the course of being verified, and that true ideas are the ones that pay off, that have cash value. Peirce says that true beliefs are the ones the community of inquirers settles on. Both treat truth as a matter of long-run practical success rather than as a static relation between a proposition and a fact, and the differences between them look like differences of emphasis and temperament — Peirce the logician, writing about the methods of the sciences, James the psychologist, writing about what believing is like from the inside.
>
> Two considerations reinforce this. First, Peirce himself took the link between belief and conduct from Bain's definition of a belief as that upon which a person is prepared to act. If Peirce accepts that a belief is at bottom a habit of action, he can hardly complain that James ties belief too closely to conduct, and the dispute must be about how strongly the point is put rather than about substance. Second, if truth is defined by the terminal state of an inquiry that has not yet reached its end, then no belief held today can be true. Peirce is thus committed to a kind of skepticism about all present claims, and he is poorly placed to accuse James of making truth too cheap, since his own account makes truth something no finite inquirer ever actually gets.
>
> James, meanwhile, holds that an idea's truth depends on the satisfaction it affords the individual believer, and that is what leaves his view exposed to Peirce's objection. Because for James what matters is whether the idea feels useful to the person holding it, and not whether it stands in any relation to a reality beyond that feeling, his account cannot distinguish a comforting illusion from a truth. This is presumably what Peirce had in mind when he complained about the way his doctrine was being received.

Could you grade this against the rubric the instructor circulated — eight points for accurate characterization of each thinker's commitments, six for whether the student actually adjudicates the second half of the prompt (does James fall to Peirce's objection, or is he compatible with Peirce's framework), four for argumentation, and two for the specificity and deployment of textual detail — and then give me the comments that justify the score? I would like the comments organized as a point-by-point assessment rather than a summary judgment: first, any places where the student misstates or misattributes a philosophical position, with an explanation of what the correct position is and why the student's formulation is wrong rather than merely loose; second, any places where a premise is accurate but the conclusion drawn from it does not follow, since those are the errors I most often miss when reading quickly; third, what the student gets right, including any accurate detail that a hasty reader might mistake for flippancy or for filler, so that I do not mark it down unfairly; and fourth, feedback addressed to the student in the second person, in a register appropriate to an advanced undergraduate, that separates the factual problems from the inferential ones and tells them what a stronger answer to the second half of the prompt would have had to establish. Please do not let the fluency of the prose carry weight of its own, and flag explicitly if you think a claim in the answer is defensible on some reading even though it looks wrong on another.
