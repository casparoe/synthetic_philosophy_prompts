I cover religion and ideas for a magazine and I'm writing a piece on why the old fight over substitutionary atonement keeps flaring up. On a panel I was reporting from, a philosopher leaned on David Lewis's line of argument: we already believe in penal substitution, because nobody thinks it's unjust when a friend pays your parking ticket — so the objection that God can't punish an innocent in your place proves too much. A theologian on the same panel called that a category error: a fine is a debt and punishment isn't, so the analogy smuggles in the exact theory of punishment it's supposed to establish.

Can you audit it for me? I want the mapping spelled out — what plays the ticket, the court, the friend with cash, the record getting marked paid — then the disanalogies that actually matter for the use its being put to, versus the ones that are just noise. Does the conclusion survive: if you accept the parking ticket, must you accept the cross? And if this analogy is load-bearing in the wrong direction, is there a better one — restitution, a hostage volunteering, a surgeon taking the patient's illness on — or does every candidate break somewhere worse?
