I've spent more years than I care to admit in rationalist-adjacent comment threads, where the accepted way to settle a dispute about historical causation is to reach for counterfactuals and ask which factor was the difference-maker. Lately I've been trying to formalize that instinct, because I suspect that arguments of the form *was the July Crisis overdetermined?* are mostly people using one word for two incompatible structures, and I want an instrument that tells them apart.

Here is the instrument. Call the **causal slack** of a historical explanation the number of factors you could strike from the explanans before, on your best counterfactual judgment, the explanandum would no longer have occurred. An explanation with slack zero is a fragile conjunction: every listed factor is doing indispensable work, so the list is not a menu but a machine. An explanation with high slack is redundant: the factors are substitutes for one another, and striking any one of them leaves the outcome standing on its feet. Slack, on this account, is a measure of how much the world had up its sleeve.

I already know the obvious objection — that slack is granularity-relative, since you can manufacture or destroy it by carving your factors more finely or more coarsely, and a historian who lists "the alliance system" and another who lists "German war planning, Franco-Russian coordination, and the Dual Monarchy's Balkan commitments" may be describing the same world. I regard that as a real but second-order problem, solvable by fixing a level of description before comparing explanations. What I want to know is whether the instrument gives the right verdict where it matters.

Consider 1914. The historians I read emphasize a dense thicket: the alliance system, the mobilization timetables, the naval and land arms races, the domestic politics of each great power, the doctrine of the offensive, and the particular decisions of July. They are also quite willing to call the war overdetermined, in the sense that any one of these would have brought a general European war in short order. That reads to me as maximal slack, and three consequences appear to follow. First, the counterfactual about the Archduke's driver is not merely hard to evaluate but idle, since removing that factor removes nothing from the world's supply of sufficient causes. Second, any attempt to rank the factors is false precision, because ranking presupposes differential contribution where by hypothesis there is none. Third, the only honest form of historical explanation is a flat list with no internal structure, and the interpretive work lies entirely in getting the items on it right.

Do you accept those three consequences? If not, I would like the diagnosis rather than a defense of my instrument: which premise is doing the damage — the identification of slack with redundancy, the reading of the historians' "overdetermined" as a claim about the individual sufficiency of each listed factor, or the inference from high slack to the idleness of counterfactuals? I have a strong suspicion that I am skating over a technical distinction in the theory of causation, but I have been unable to locate it, and I would rather be told where the seam is than be reassured.
