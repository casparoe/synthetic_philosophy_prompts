so i TA a philosophy-of-medicine seminar and for three years running the session on biological function has landed in exactly the same place: nobody can say whether the selected-effect line (wright, millikan) or the causal-role line (cummins) is right, and nobody budges. the arguments are the familiar ones — selected effects only explain why ancestors had the trait, not why this individual has it or how it works now, and they fall silent on novel, engineered, or recently acquired traits; whereas causal-role functions look like they can't ground malfunction, since a heart that fails has whatever causal role it actually has, and 'dysfunction' comes out incoherent.

my working theory, which i want you to attack: this isn't a substantive disagreement at all. it's two groups answering two different questions under one word — one asking what a part currently does that helps explain the containing system, the other asking why the trait is there at all — and the only reason it feels like a real fight is that both sides are chasing the same prize downstream, a notion of malfunction strong enough to define disease and to police the therapy/enhancement line. if that's right the stalemate is a symtom of a badly posed question rather than a failure of argument.

tell me whether that holds up, or whether something genuinely substantive is left once you subtract the question-switching:

- match the arguments up one by one. where do the two sides actually engage each other and where do they just talk past? i don't want a survey of the positions, i want to know which objection lands and which only looks like it does.
- is the malfunction point decisive? can a causal-role account really not have malfunction, or does that just show the etiological people are loading 'malfunction' with history before thier own argument gets going? and do the middle-way accounts — the dispositional/organizational ones, the ones that talk about persistence instead of reproduction — escape the dilemma or paper over it?
- the case that keeps coming up in our group is the brain circuitry for reading: a robust, hugely important causal role, and a history far too short for selection to have done anything. some people now say the currency should be retention rather than reproduction, and that cultural or developmental retention could cover it. does a case like that decide anything between the two accounts, or does it just relocate the dispute to whether retention counts?
- there's also a third crowd who say function ascriptions are interest-relative, so you just pick whichever account serves the explanation you're giving. that sounds like giving up rather than holding a position — is it, or is it actually the resolution?

and then the honest part: what would count as progress here — a new distinction, a new kind of counterexample, some formal machinery, a change of question — or should we expect another fifty years of the same two sides making the same moves? if it's the latter, say why we should keep teaching it as a live debate at all.

no bibliography please, and don't send me hunting for papers, i know the literature. i just want the diagnosis and the reasons for it.
