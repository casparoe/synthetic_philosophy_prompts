A friend of mine who runs a systematic book at a proprietary trading firm and I argued until two in the morning about whether I should keep trading futures at all, and I have not managed to settle the matter with myself since. I would rather have the argument reconstructed carefully than be told which of us won.

My position, which I took to be the orthodox one, ran as follows. The no-trade theorem of Milgrom and Stokey shows that if traders are rational, share a common prior, begin from an allocation that is Pareto efficient relative to that prior, and it is common knowledge among them that they are rational, then they will never agree on a speculative trade — the reasoning being that the fact of both sides accepting is itself common knowledge, so each party must treat the other's willingness to accept as evidence. Volume, on that account, is not neutral. On any liquid contract, daily turnover is in effect a running tally of the market's departures from those hypotheses, and the only candidates for a departure capable of generating that much activity are counterparties who know something I do not, or who are fooling themselves. I therefore concluded that the honest thing for me is to stop trading on my own views entirely and to touch the market only to rebalance back to my target weights.

She said I had it backwards, and offered two objections I could not answer. The first: the hypotheses are simply false in a central limit order book, where I do not know who is on the other side, let alone that they are rational, and where nobody supposes the initial allocation of a modern economy to be Pareto efficient — and a theorem whose hypotheses fail cannot license an inference about volume in either direction. The second: I had invoked the Monderer and Samet results weakening common knowledge to common p-belief, on the grounds that approximate agreement implies real disagreement must be small, so the volume remains damning; she said those results do not say that, and that if anything they make my position worse rather than better. She left it there.

Her conclusion appears to be that the theorem can be deployed neither for me nor against me, which would leave the question where it started; mine, stubbornly, is that it is being misused by everyone who treats a price as a summary of information. So: does my inference from volume to the irrationality or superior information of my counterparties follow from the theorem, or does it rest on a misunderstanding of what its hypotheses are for — are they idealizations to be checked off against reality, or something more like a partition of the possible motives for trade, whose failure is informative in a way I have not grasped? What exactly do the approximation results bound, and why does their content not support the small-disagreement premise I leaned on? If she is right that the hypotheses fail, why should that make prices more credible to me rather than less — my instinct being that a model which cannot describe the actual market tells me nothing about whether that market is informative? And is rebalancing to target weights genuinely the non-speculative refuge I assumed it to be, or is it a bet wearing a smaller name, particularly given that a policy of never trading on one's own views, adopted generally, would leave very little to inform the prices I propose to defer to?
