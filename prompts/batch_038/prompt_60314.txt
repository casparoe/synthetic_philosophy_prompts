Here's the passage: "David Lewis's Principal Principle is the bridge between objective chance and rational belief. It says that chance is whatever plays a certain role in our degrees of belief: if you know that the objective chance of some event is x, and you have no evidence bearing on whether that event happens, then your degree of belief in it should be exactly x. Rationality, in other words, requires you to defer to the chances. Chance on this picture is a fixed property of a physical set-up, like a mass or a charge, and it stays fixed however much of the future unfolds. Strictly speaking the principle isn't an optional extra bolted onto probability theory, since a credence function that violates it is incoherent in the technical sense and can be Dutch-booked. That is also why Lewis thought chance earns its name: chance is defined as whatever quantity obeys the constraint, so the Principle is a piece of conceptual analysis rather than a discovery about the world, and which chances the world actually has is settled by the same stroke. Philosophers of statistics treat this as a deep result, but it is really just the requirement of calibration written in a different notation — a forecaster whose degrees of belief match the true chances is calibrated, and one whose degrees of belief violate the Principal Principle is not. Lewis did note one wrinkle. Some evidence is admissible and some is not, and inadmissible information can license you to depart from the chances, but he showed that information about the past is admissible, so in practice you may conditionalize on anything you've actually learned. The upshot is uncomfortable for the Humean, and Lewis admitted as much. If chances are just a summary of what happens, then knowing what is going to happen would settle what the chances are, which is to say his own principle makes chance unknowable in exactly the cases that matter. He never found a way out."
