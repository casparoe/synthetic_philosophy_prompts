I am a final-year secondary-school student preparing an extended essay of roughly four thousand words on the ethics of social sanction — deplatforming, public shaming, and the withdrawal of employment or association — and I have been working closely with the opening chapter of John Stuart Mill's *On Liberty* (1859). My supervisor warned me that the passage I have chosen is usually skimmed because it looks like mere history, and that most writers jump straight to Mill's better-known remarks about the tyranny of the majority later in the chapter. I would rather understand this earlier section properly before building anything on it. Here it is:

"A time, however, came in the progress of human affairs, when men ceased to think it a necessity of nature that their governors should be an independent power, opposed in interest to themselves. It appeared to them much better that the various magistrates of the State should be their tenants or delegates, revocable at their pleasure. In that way alone, it seemed, could they have complete security that the powers of government would never be abused to their disadvantage. By degrees, this new demand for elective and temporary rulers became the prominent object of the exertions of the popular party, wherever any such party existed; and superseded, to a considerable extent, the previous efforts to limit the power of rulers. As the struggle proceeded for making the ruling power emanate from the periodical choice of the ruled, some persons began to think that too much importance had been attached to the limitation of the power itself. That (it might seem) was a resource against rulers whose interests were habitually opposed to those of the people. What was now wanted was, that the rulers should be identified with the people; that their interest and will should be the interest and will of the nation. The nation did not need to be protected against its own will. There was no fear of its tyrannizing over itself. Let the rulers be effectually responsible to it, promptly removable by it, and it could afford to trust them with power of which it could itself dictate the use to be made."

There are five things I would like help with.

1. Voice. The passage reports a position Mill goes on to reject, but it never says so outright. Which words and phrases signal that he is reporting rather than asserting, and how reliable are those signals for a reader who has not yet reached the rest of the chapter? In particular, what work is the parenthetical "(it might seem)" doing, and what does "habitually opposed" add — is the inference from identification to security supposed to turn on interests rather than on consent?

2. Exaggeration. Mill's reporters want "complete security" from making rulers "tenants or delegates, revocable at their pleasure." Is that phrasing a fair statement of a view people actually held, or a caricature being set up for the next move? How much of the passage's force depends on the answer?

3. The two devices. Just before this, Mill distinguishes two ways of limiting a ruler's power: recognised immunities or rights, whose infringement counts as a breach of duty, and constitutional checks, which make the community's consent a necessary condition for the more important acts of government; the second he calls "generally a later expedient." Why would it come later? And this is the part I most need help with: does either device have any purchase on power that is not exercised by an office at all? If a sanction arrives as the accumulated separate acts of many private people, there is no one whose duty can be breached and no procedure to which consent can be attached. Is Mill leaving that gap open here, or are the materials for closing it already present in the passage?

4. A hypothesis I would like tested rather than flattered. I have been reading the reported error as a principal–agent error: the earlier liberals assumed that if the agent is identical with the principal, agency problems dissolve, whereas a collective principal is not a single will and can harm its own members, so identification removes the need for monitoring without removing the possibility of abuse. Is that a faithful reconstruction, or does the borrowed economics vocabulary distort what Mill is doing — for example by turning his point into one about monitoring costs when his real concern may be about how a collective will gets formed in the first place?

5. Bearing on my topic. Writers on deplatforming cite Mill in both directions: some to argue that private platforms exercising quasi-public power need constitutional-style checks, others to argue that collective expressions of disapproval are precisely what Mill thought incapable of tyrannising, being the people's own will. Taking only what this passage says, which use is better supported, and what would have to be added to or set aside from the passage to make the other one work?

Finally, please indicate where the passage is genuinely ambiguous and where I risk over-reading it, and flag any of my questions that assumes a context the quoted text does not supply.
