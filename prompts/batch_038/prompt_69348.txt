I'm maybe eighty thousand words into a novel and I've hit a wall on the chapter I want to use as an epigraph page, so I'm outsourcing the poem. Quick setup, since the thing has to stand alone for a reader who never opens the book. The novel is set in a small northern town across two timeframes. In the present one, a schoolmaster in his eighties — call him Bell — is visited by a young biographer. Bell taught a boy for two years when the boy was nine and ten; the boy grew up to be the doctor who organised the response to an epidemic everyone now dates from, had three buildings named after him, and died last spring. The biographer has come for anecdotes. The problem is that the only thing Bell actually remembers about the boy is that he couldn't whistle — a whole summer spent on it, one cracked note achieved, and a fury about it that had nothing whatsoever to do with anything that came after. I know the biographer in my book is going to put that in, and I know exactly what she'll turn it into. I want the poem to be the moment that conversion happens, on the page, in the reader's ear.

What I've been reading while stuck, because it's the whole trouble: Butterfield's little 1931 book, where he says it's part and parcel of the whig interpretation of history that it studies the past with reference to the present, and where his complaint isn't about actual Whigs but about a shape of narration — the abridgement that keeps only what looks important from here, the sequence that gets mistaken for a line of causation so nobody bothers looking for real causes, the habit of turning the past into a morality play because you can't stand loose threads until you've said which side was right. (Skinner has a name for the same move in the history of ideas, something about anticipation, I think — describing a man in terms of a concept he couldn't have had.) And Hartley's foreign-country line, which every historical novelist quotes and which was actually written by an old man opening his own childhood diary, i.e. conscripted into a methodological quarrel it was never part of, which is precisely the crime. So: the poem should carry the selection problem — an afternoon has indefinitely many consequences and only the ones that reach us get called causes — and it should carry the fact that Bell's life is not a prelude, that most of what he did went nowhere and that going nowhere is not the same as not happening. But and here's the part I care most about, it must not become a scold or a piece of antiquarian nostalgia. The honest position is that nobody can narrate except from where they're standing, Bell included, me included; he's already editing the whistling by telling it to a stranger with a notebook. Keep the tension open. Don't let anyone win, and don't use the words progress, legacy, destiny, footnote, or the march of.

Form: a pantoum, five stanzas, second and fourth lines of each quatrain recycled as first and third of the next, with the last stanza folding the opening's first and third lines back in as its second and fourth, so the poem ends by re-reading its own beginning — hindsight arriving at the start. Here's the real constraint, the one I don't want you to dodge: a repeated line may change nothing except its punctuation. Same words, same order, maybe a comma or a full stop or a dash where there wasn't one, and that has to be enough to move the line from Bell's memory into the biographer's evidence — from a fact about a hot July into a sign of early character. No labelling which register you're in, no "she writes," no quotation marks doing the work. Keep the lines short enough to be held in the mouth, twelve syllables tops, no rhyme beyond what the repetition gives you, no abstract nouns anywhere. Afterwards, in two or three sentences and no more, tell me which recycled line you think does the heaviest lifting and why, so I can check you actually built the machine instead of decorating it.
