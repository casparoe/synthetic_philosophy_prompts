I am a high school senior, and for an independent essay I have been trying to work out whether misleading someone without ever saying anything false is on a par with outright lying. What has most struck me is that American law seems to give this question different answers in different places, and I would like help seeing what account of lying and misleading each of those practices assumes. I will describe three as concretely as I can, since the differences between them are what puzzle me.

First, perjury. In *Bronston v. United States* (1973), a witness under oath was asked whether he had any bank accounts in Swiss banks. He said no. Asked whether he ever had, he answered, "The company had an account there for about six months, in Zurich." In fact he had held a personal account in Geneva for nearly five years, and the government's theory was that he had answered literally truthfully about the company precisely in order to imply that he had none of his own. The Supreme Court reversed the conviction: the federal perjury statute does not reach an answer that is literally true but unresponsive and misleading by implication, even where the witness intends to mislead, because "it is the questioner's burden to frame his interrogation acutely to elicit the precise information he seeks."

Second, securities fraud. Under the Securities and Exchange Commission's Rule 10b-5, it is unlawful "to make any untrue statement of a material fact or to omit to state a material fact necessary in order to make the statements made, in the light of the circumstances under which they were made, not misleading." So here a literally true statement followed by a studied silence is itself the violation.

Third, advertising. Where an ad is literally false, it can be condemned with no proof at all that any consumer was fooled. Where it is literally true but leaves a false impression, the plaintiff must produce extrinsic evidence — usually consumer surveys — showing that people actually took away the misleading message.

With that in front of us, here is what I want:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory in each case. Does the first assume that lying is a property of the sentence rather than of the speaker's intention? Does the second assume that the wrong consists in the false belief produced in the hearer? Does the third assume that deception is a fact about audiences, to be measured rather than judged from the words? Please explain any technical vocabulary you need rather than assuming I have it.

2. Are these three presuppositions compatible? Can the same legal system say that literal truth completely shields a witness yet gives an issuer of stock no shield at all, without holding contradictory views about what deception is? If there is a contradiction, locate it precisely; if there is not, say what the difference turns on.

3. How much of the pattern is really a view about lying, and how much is about proof and institutional design — that juries cannot reliably reconstruct what an evasive witness meant, that a cross-examiner is a trained professional who can ask again, that an investor is entitled to rely while a shopper is not? Tell me where the practices are under moral pressure and where they are merely pragmatic.

4. Distinguish what each practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably. For instance, does the perjury rule commit anyone to thinking that misleading is less wrongful than lying, or is it equally consistent with thinking misleading is just as wrongful but unprovable?

5. Say where the practices are philosophically underdetermined: which rival accounts — one making the assertion of what you believe false the fundamental wrong, one making the production of false belief fundamental, one making a relationship-specific duty of candor fundamental — would license these very rules unchanged?

6. Finally, suppose we replaced the mixture with one consistent principle, such as that inducing a false belief is the wrong regardless of the means. What would actually have to change? Would evasive testimony become perjury, would the survey requirement disappear, would silence in ordinary bargaining become actionable? I would rather have an answer that admits where the practices prove nothing than one that finds a tidy theory everywhere.
