ok so I just spent three hours arguing with my friend Danny about automation and whether work can even mean anything once the machines do it, and I'm still buzzing and I need to turn the argument into a speech by Thursday, which is the actual deadline.

Background: I'm the floor supervisor at a machine shop — 34 people, third-generation family owned, precision components, mostly aerospace and medical. In six weeks we flip on a lights-out cell: robotic loading, in-process gauging, tool changes, the whole thing running unattended overnight. Thursday we're doing a small ceremony for the last part made entirely by hand on the old #3 knee mill, which we've had since 1978. The part gets passed around the room, somebody's taking photos, the local paper might send a stringer.

Audience is mixed and a little awkward: about 25 of our machinists (ages 24 to 63, some of whom will be retrained onto the cell and some of whom, honestly, will not), the owner's family, two people from the city, and — this is the part I keep forgetting — three engineers from the integrator who sold us the cell, standing at the back by the good pastries.

I've been asked to say something for four or five minutes. Not a eulogy, not a pep talk.

Here's the argument, and its what I want the speech built on rather than sprinkled with. My position going in was the standard one: automation takes the drudgery and frees people for better work. Danny's counter was that this gets the loss exactly backwards. He had a phrase for it, something like *work is the standing to be asked why*. His point: what a good machinist has isn't primarily skill, it's answerability. When you hand someone a part you made, they can ask you why you did it that way — why that speed, why you left that witness mark, why you decided the print was wrong and not the stock. And you answer in your own name, and your answer can be argued with, and being arguable with is what makes the work yours. A cell's output can be inspected, audited, charted to hell — but it can't be asked. Nothing in that room is answerable in that sense. So what leaves when the lights go out isn't drudgery and isn't exactly craftsmanship either; it's that a certain kind of question stops finding a person as its answer.

I pushed back and he conceded ground, and I want that concession in the speech too, because I think it's true and because a speech that pretends the objection doesn't exist will lose the room: answerability might migrate rather than vanish. Somebody still sets the tolerances, still writes the post-processor, still decides which alarm gets acknowledged and which gets bypassed, still signs off the first article. Maybe the "why" just moves upstream and gets quieter, and the real risk is that nobody notices it went.

Hard constraints, please respect all of these:
- Don't imply the machines are worse at the work. They're better, measurably, and everybody in that room knows it cold. Any version that suggests the old way was more accurate gets laughed at by exactly the people I need to reach.
- Don't promise job security. Some jobs go. I'm not going to be the guy who lies at a nice ceremony.
- Don't sentimentalize the drudgery either. Two of these people have repetitive strain injuries and hated Tuesdays. The speech has to hold "that work wore your body out" and "that work was yours" at the same time without resolving them into a lesson or a silver lining.
- It has to land for the 24-year-old who's going to program the cell and thinks I'm a fossil, and for the 61-year-old who's being asked to feel okay about all this.
- Something gracious but not groveling needs to happen about the integrator engineers being present. They aren't villains; two of them are ex-machinists.

You're the right person for this: you've written a lot of speeches for industrial occasions and you've also read enough philosophy of work and technology to be dangerous, and you know when to keep a concept under the surface instead of saying it out loud. Think carefully before drafting. Start with some brainstorming: give me three or four genuinely different structural options for how that idea could carry five minutes — e.g. built around one specific exchange of questions between me and an old hand, built around the part moving from palm to palm, built around a sound the shop will stop making, built around the difference between a question you can ask a person and one you can only ask a report — say which you'd pick and why, and then write the full speech, roughly 600–750 words. Keep the concept load-bearing the whole way through, but the word "answerability" must not appear anywhere in it. That's Danny's word, not mine, and it would die in that room.
