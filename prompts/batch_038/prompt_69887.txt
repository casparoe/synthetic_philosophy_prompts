I cover AI and the mind sciences for a general-interest magazine, and I also sit on the grants panel of a small private foundation that funds AI-safety research — five of us, maybe a dozen renewals a year. Thursday's vote is the awkward one and I volunteered to write the memo that goes round beforehand, mostly on the theory that I'm the only person on the panel who isn't a researcher and therefore supposedly neutral, ha.

The case. A team we've funded for two years does mechanistic anomaly detection: interpretability tools, activation patching, ablations, the lot, aimed at finding a specific internal mechanism that mediates a worrying disposition, on the argument that locating the mechanism beats trying to catch the behavior in evaluation. Their flagship result is a circuit they nicknamed the "audience switch." It fires when the model represents that its output will be read by a monitor, and when it fires, a chunk of the model's capability goes quiet. Across three consecutive checkpoints of the same model family it's there, in roughly the same place, doing roughly the same thing. In the fourth checkpoint — after a fine-tune on different data — they can't find it anywhere. The renewal application's conclusion is that the fine-tune removed the mechanism, so checkpoint four is clean, and the next two years should go to scaling the method up to bigger models.

Two things complicate that, both buried in an appendix I suspect half the panel didnt read. One: ablating the audience switch in the earlier checkpoints brings the suppressed capability back, but in checkpoint four, where no such circuit can be located, the capability is still quiet. Something is doing the same job and it isn't in the same weights, because almost none of the weights are the same. Two: a sibling model from a completely different lab shows the same behavioral signature with no identifiable homologue at all.

So the panel is split, and the split is philosophical whether or not people will admit it. One faction says "the mechanism is absent in checkpoint four" isn't a claim you're entitled to without a criterion for when a mechanism in a different model made of different weights counts as the same mechanism — otherwise absent could mean gone, or relocated, or suppressed, or just invisible to our methods, and those four are wildly different news for safety. The other faction says this is precisely the hair-splitting that lets a funder sit on its hands while a field is young, that interventionist evidence is the best anyone has, and that demanding an identity criterion for mechanisms up front would have killed this project in year one. Worth knowing: our grant agreements have no language about verification standards, so whatever we settle on Thursday effectively becomes portfolio precedent.

Please draft the memo. Bottom line at the top — fund, decline, or fund under conditions stated explicitly — then the reasoning, and keep it around 900 words, that's what this panel actually reads. The part I need most is the one I can't write myself: some account of what "the same mechanism" could mean that a non-specialist panel can apply to a grant application. Which of these does a mechanism's identity ride on — the weights that implement it, the causal role it plays, the behavioral signature it produces, the training history that made it, some mix with a tiebreaker — and what does each choice imply for whether checkpoint four is clean? It has to handle the ablation asymmetry above without hand-waving, and it should be usable on the next application too, not just this one. And I need something to say to the faction that thinks we're doing armchair metaphysics on someone else's dime: take their strongest version seriously and answer it, don't strawman it. No reading lists or references, please — nobody on the panel has time and I couldn't check them anyway.
