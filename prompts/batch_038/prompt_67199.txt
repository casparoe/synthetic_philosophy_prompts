I have been asked to deliver a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on the politics of infrastructure — the room will be a mix of political philosophers and people from science and technology studies, several of whom work in municipal planning — and I have a complete but unstable draft that I would like a careful read of before I commit to it. My friend, who designs bus networks for a living, told me the central move is either obvious or wrong, and I have not been able to work out which. The draft is below; please treat it as a real piece of argument rather than a summary of a position I already hold, because I am genuinely undecided about whether to defend it or to retreat to something weaker.

**Draft: "Standing Vetoes: A Costly-Reversal Account of What It Means for an Artifact to Have Politics"**

(1) Winner's essay poses a deceptively plain question and then answers it in two distinct registers. In the first register, the design or arrangement of a technical system becomes a way of settling an issue in the affairs of a community: the Long Island parkway overpasses built to a clearance that kept twelve-foot buses off the route to Jones Beach; Haussmann's boulevards, laid out so that the street fighting of 1848 could not recur; McCormick's introduction of pneumatic molding machines into his Chicago works in the 1880s, which served among other things to dislodge organized skilled labour; the mechanical tomato harvester, whose arrival pushed breeders toward tougher, more uniform fruit and thereby toward larger operations. In the second register, certain technologies are inherently political, in that their operation appears to require, or to be strongly compatible with, particular forms of political relationship — the atom bomb being Winner's chosen illustration, since its lethal properties demand a centralized, rigidly hierarchical chain of command closed to unpredictable influence. He is careful throughout that recognizing politics in the shapes of technology does not require positing conspiracies or malicious intent, and much of his most interesting material concerns neglect rather than design.

(2) My dissatisfaction is that both registers answer a binary question with binary material. An artifact either settles a dispute or it does not; a technology either demands a hierarchy or it does not. There is no account of degree, and consequently no way to discipline the attribution. Almost anything constrains somebody's options, and almost anything was the upshot of somebody's choice, so a critic armed with Winner can find politics in a kerb radius. What I want is a criterion that lets some artifacts come out as robustly political and others as merely consequential.

(3) The proposal is to separate the decision from the deposit. An artifact has politics in the robust sense when a distribution of costs and benefits that was once openly contestable has been deposited in material form, such that reopening the question now costs far more than settling it did originally. Define the lock-in multiple L as the cost of reversing the sedimented arrangement, summed over all parties it affects, divided by the cost of making the arrangement in the first instance, summed the same way. Politics is then a matter of degree in L. Note what this does to intent: I deliberately stake nothing on the archival question of whether the parkway clearances were chosen in order to exclude bus-borne riders. On my account the purpose is beside the point, which is either the account's great virtue or a sign that I have defined the phenomenon until it stopped biting.

(4) The account makes predictions I find awkward, which is why I want them checked. The celebrated overpasses score modestly: the constraint attaches to identifiable routes, and the affected population could be served by transfers, by shuttle operation, or by rebuilding the spans at municipal expense — a real political fact, but reversible on the timescale of a capital programme. Meanwhile the dullest artifacts score extremely well. The standardization of intermodal container dimensions and corner fittings redistributed the world's waterfronts, ended longshoring as a mass occupation, and made the prospects of a port city turn on whether it happened to have flat acreage and gantry cranes; undoing that would require un-standardizing a global fleet. So the paradigm cases in the literature are not the paradigm cases of the phenomenon, and a philosophy of technological politics that trains its attention on dramatic bridges is looking in the wrong place.

(5) Winner's second register does not fit, and I fold it in by a manoeuvre I am not sure I believe. The bomb's demand for hierarchical control is not itself a sedimented decision; it is a functional requirement of the artifact's operation. But the organizational form that answers the requirement was chosen, and it is the decentralization of that form that would now be costly. L therefore attaches to the organizational arrangement rather than to the artifact, and the second register becomes a special case of the first. This is the least comfortable paragraph in the draft.

(6) The obvious objection is that I have renamed path dependence. I claim three differences. First, L is distributional rather than efficiency-oriented: the numerator counts costs falling on parties who were never at the table when the decision was taken, which is exactly what a welfare measure of switching costs ignores. Second, L is indexed to a moment of decision, so it measures foreclosed agency rather than suboptimal equilibrium. Third, L can rise over time with no further decision at all, as the affected population disperses, the expertise atrophies, and the complementary investments accumulate; the phenomenon I want to name is a question quietly leaving the agenda without anyone voting it off. I call this agenda foreclosure, and I take it to be the political core of the whole account: what makes an artifact political is that it operates as a standing veto over something that used to be asked out loud.

Please give me an audit rather than encouragement. Specifically: (a) order the failures of the argument by severity and, for each, state what I would have to surrender — the definition of L, the container example, paragraph (5), or the whole degree-theoretic ambition — rather than telling me that everything needs a bit of work; (b) tell me whether my rendering of Winner in paragraph (1) is faithful or a strawman, since the two-register framing is doing a great deal of load-bearing and I may have imposed it; (c) test claim (6) hard, because if the three differences do not hold then the account is path dependence in a distributional costume and the paper has no thesis; (d) tell me whether the numerator of L is even well defined, given that the costs fall on agents with very different capacities to absorb them, and whether any non-arbitrary aggregation is available — and if the only fix is to index L to a particular constituency, say plainly whether that destroys the objectivity I claim for the measure in paragraph (2); (e) assess whether the manoeuvre in paragraph (5) preserves the point of Winner's second register or dissolves it, since I suspect I have converted a claim about what technologies require into a claim about what organizations chose. One constraint on your reply: I am not able to chase references, so please do not send me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to consult. If you believe a factual or interpretive claim of mine is wrong, say that it is wrong and explain why on the merits. Close by telling me which of the six paragraphs I should simply cut.
