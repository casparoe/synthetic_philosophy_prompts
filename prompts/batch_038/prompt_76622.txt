I'm the faculty representative on the Acquisitions and Collections Committee of a mid-sized university art museum — three trustees, two curators, the registrar, and me, an appointment that has mostly involved voting on framing budgets. A case comes to us in January and it has thoroughly divided the staff, so the interim director has asked me to circulate a written opinion in advance, and I want the memo to be one the committee can actually act on rather than a survey of everyone's feelings.

Here are the facts as the registrar and the conservation lab have given them to me. The piece is a small bronze, roughly forty centimetres tall, called *Meridian*, by a sculptor who died in 1987 and who had a modest reputation in his lifetime and a smaller one now; the entry for it in the catalogue raisonné compiled after his death lists the edition as two casts, unnumbered and unsigned. Two such bronzes have now come to us as a promised gift from a donor in Zurich. Each arrived in its own crate, and each crate was consigned by one of the artist's two adult children, who are the co-executors of the estate. Each child maintains that the cast they shipped is the one their father pulled from the mould in 1968, and that the other is a posthumous cast the same foundry made in 1994 for a dealer, using materials and moulds retained in the studio. The paperwork crossed somewhere between the freight forwarder and our dock: the two consignments were consolidated at a single port and the manifests were re-issued, so we have no reliable record of which crate came from which sibling. The foundry's own archive confirms two casts were made in 1968 from a single pour and that a further pair was cast in 1994 for that dealer, which is to say that the alternative story about each object is not wildly implausible on its face. Our conservation lab did everything available to it: alloy composition and trace elements, lead isotope ratios, core and chapline material, patina chemistry, surface tooling and finishing marks under magnification, and micro-CT of the internal structure. Their conclusion, in the conservator's words, is that the two objects are indistinguishable by every method at their disposal, that the only difference the lab could document between them is which crate each was in and which route it took to get here, and that they would stake their professional reputations on that report in a dispute. Each sibling, for their part, offers family recollection — one says their father kept the first cast in the studio and gave the second to their mother, the other says the reverse — and neither recollection is supported by a document, a photograph, or a letter. Both children were eleven and eight in 1968. Our Collections Standards, adopted in 2009 and never amended, require that before the museum attributes a work to an artist it must be able to point to features intrinsic to the object that a competent later copy could not also bear. Separately, the donor has made clear she will give both casts or neither, wants them accessioned as a pair and displayed together; the insurer wants one valuation for the pair and a single credit line; and one of the two children has said that if the museum designates one cast as the 1968 original and the other as posthumous, the estate will treat that designation as defamatory and will contest it publicly.

Three options are live and I'd like the memo to recommend one of them, with reasons the other members could engage with rather than merely be informed of. The first is to accept the pair and to refuse to rank it — accession both as lifetime casts of 1968, or at most as casts of undetermined priority, and label them that way on the wall and in the catalogue. The second is to accept the pair but designate one as the 1968 original, on whatever basis we can defend, including an admittedly random one such as accession order or a coin toss, on the theory that the collection record cannot honestly carry an unresolved disjunction. The third is to decline the gift, on the ground that we cannot satisfy our own Collections Standards and that a public designation we cannot support is worse than no acquisition at all. I'd also like the memo to address the following, and please don't simply assume the answers are obvious:

- Say plainly which reading of the Collections Standards your recommendation depends on — in particular, whether a property like having arrived in the crate from Antwerp, or having passed through one sibling's hands rather than the other's, counts as intrinsic to the object or is excluded by the word — and whether the committee should read the standard as written or formally amend it, and in what direction.
- Treat seriously the objection I expect from the registrar and from one of the curators, who think all of this is a waste of an afternoon: the two bronzes are perfectly discernible, they say, because one was in one crate and one was in the other, they sat on different shelves, and they reached us by different routes, so the Standards are satisfied and we should simply record what we know. Explain why that objection is either right or a confusion, and if you think it is a confusion, say what the museum loses by conceding it.
- Address the symmetry that I cannot get past and that I suspect the committee will not want to look at: if the two objects were swapped overnight, nothing we could measure would change, and every fact we would then assert about them would be exactly as well supported as it is now. What, if anything, entitles us to suppose that our record describes two objects with settled individual histories rather than one history told twice, and how should the memo handle the possibility that a designation we make is true but unknowable to us?
- Say what the memo owes the losing sibling if we designate, and what it owes both of them if we do not; and whether any procedure for choosing between two people whose testimony is equally unsupported and equally self-interested can be non-arbitrary rather than merely random. If you propose a tiebreaker, tell me what we would say to the child it goes against.
- Flag the single point at which your own reasoning is most exposed to a trustee who wants to attack it, instead of leaving that to be discovered in the meeting.
- State the precedent for the registry. Our accessioning practice is followed by two peer institutions and this case has already been mentioned in a trade newsletter, so whatever we decide will be cited back at us; say what rule you want the committee to adopt for future cases in which objects are indistinguishable by all available tests but differ in documented chain of custody.
- Close with a concrete action calendar between now and the January vote, including what we tell the donor, the insurer, and the estate, and in what order, and who signs it.

Aim for something a busy committee member will read all the way through: a clear recommendation on the first page, then the reasoning, then the strongest case against your recommendation stated at full strength and answered rather than dismissed. My colleague in the philosophy department keeps telling me at lunch that this is a famous old problem wearing a museum badge, and that a well-known mid-century thought experiment involves nothing but two spheres sitting in an otherwise empty universe; I have not found this helpful and I would rather the memo not gesture at it either. What I need is the reasoning itself, in our vocabulary, about our two bronzes.
