I'm one of three moderators for a volunteer-run wiki documenting our county's history, and we're rewriting our sourcing policy after a six-month fight over a single claim about a 1920s mill fire — a claim that now sits at the center of a landmark-designation petition. Our existing rule is that every claim needs a citation, any citation can be challenged, and a challenger is entitled to demand support for the reliability of the source cited. That is how we got a four-hundred-comment thread with nobody closer to an answer.

The reform proposals on the table are these three. First: a standing list of anchor sources — the county recorder, two local papers, one academic press — whose reliability is assumed and not open to challenge; note, though, that one of those papers was owned by the mill company, which is a large part of why people challenge sources at all. Second: a claim counts as settled once it is corroborated by enough other settled claims, even where that corroboration eventually loops back through claims that were themselves settled by it. Third: nothing is ever finally settled — chains stay open indefinitely, and a claim's standing simply grows the longer its chain survives challenge without breaking.

Each bothers me in a way I can't articulate. The first seems to rest the entire archive on a decree; the second seems to license believing anything, provided you believe enough of it; the third seems to promise a justification that no one ever actually receives. Which should we adopt, if any — or is there a fourth option we've missed? I'd rather not be told to weigh things case by case: that is the current policy, and it is what produces the four-hundred-comment threads. Please answer in terms of something we could write down and enforce.
