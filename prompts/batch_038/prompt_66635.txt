So, fourth novel, and I've been living inside the head of a coffee competition judge for about nine months now, which has turned out to be a weirdly good way to accidentally read a lot of philosophy. The book covers a single harvest season at a regional cupping competition — three-person panels, blind tasting, scores that get turned into auction lots. My judge, Callum, has a scoring rule he swears by and that everyone else thinks is insane. He rates every lot 0–10 on three things only: aroma, acidity, body. Then instead of doing a weighted average like a normal person, he says lot X is better than lot Y just in case X wins on at least two of the three, and a win only counts if the margin is at least a half point — anything smaller he calls "noise" and scores as a tie, because that's roughly the limit of what his palate resolves on a good day. The three lots that structure the middle of the book are A (aroma 8, acidity 6, body 9), B (aroma 7, acidity 9, body 7), and C (aroma 9, acidity 8, body 6), and under his rule he ranks A over B, B over C, and C over A, and he's completely unbothered by it. Worth noting: in this particular triad every margin clears the half point, so the cycle isn't coming from the noise rule at all.

The antagonist of the middle section is an agricultural economist named Vero, hired by the auction house, and she wants to prove Callum is a broken measuring instrument. Her plan is basically a money pump: get him to trade up from B to A, then B to C — sorry, C for B — then A for C, charging a handling fee each time, so he ends up exactly where he started minus three fees. She also has a sharper version, which I think is the one with real teeth: even if you cant pump Callum individually, because he refuses to taste the same lot twice in one day on account of palate fatigue and the auction rules bar a judge from reselling a lot he's scored, you can pump the panel as a whole by running the three trades through three different judges who each only ever see their own step. My editor keeps asking whether the book is "about" anything, which is the question every novelist dreads, and I've decided the honest answer is that its about whether a person can be a reliable instrument without being consistent, so I need this argument to actually work on the page.

Here's what I want: argue for Callum. Argue that Vero's pump does not show his comparative judgements are irrational, and that his rule isn't defective as a rule. Real argument, not a hedge, 900 words-ish, and please take her strongest version seriously rather than the strawman one — engage the group pump directly. Be explicit about what the pump is quietly assuming: does a set of pairwise judgements have to be backed by a single all-things-considered ranking of bundles? Do the three trades have to involve the very same objects when in the book the cups are literally consumed and the palate has moved on between them? Is she smuggling in a claim about choice that's stronger than the claim about betterness? Then tell me where the case for Callum is weakest and what Vero's single best comeback line would be, because I'm not trying to write a novel where the smart character turns out to be dumb — I want that scene to end kind of undecided.

One more thing I keep tripping over, feel free to fold it into the above. I have a growing suspicion that Callum's "half point is noise" stipulation is where the real trouble lives, independently of the cycle: if 8.0 and 8.4 are indistinguishable to him but 8.0 and 9.0 plainly aren't, doesn't a long enough chain of half-point steps wreck the ordering on a single axis, no matter how many axes you stack up? Tell me whether the vagueness-shaped problem and the intransitivity problem are two separable problems or one problem wearing two coats. If they're one problem I have to restructure about eighty pages, and I'd rather find out now.
