The question we set was:

The White Horse dialogue attributed to Gongsun Longzi argues that a white horse is not a horse. Set out the two main arguements the text gives for that thesis. Then say what, if anything, the thesis commits him to about the standing of horseness and whiteness — are they items in the world, or features of how we use names? Finally: do the later Mohists refute him?

The student's answer, typos and all:

Gongsun Long's thesis looks absurd at first but the dialogue gives two arguements for it and both deserve to be taken seriously.

The first is about names. Horse names a shape, white names a colour, and what names a colour is not what names a shape. So the compound white horse is not the same thing as horse, and since they aren't the same, a white horse is not a horse. This argument only works if naming is a one-to-one business, where each name picks out one item and two names picking out different items means two different items. That commits Gongsun Long to horseness and whiteness each being a thing in its own right, existing separately, with the white horse as a combination of the two. This is basically Platonism: horseness and whiteness are like Forms, and particular horses and particular white things participate in them. Once you see that the thesis stops looking crazy, because what he's saying is that a combination isn't identical to either of its ingredients.

The second argument is about asking. If you ask for a horse, a bay one or a black one will answer the request; if you ask for a white horse, neither will. So horse and white horse don't have the same satisfaction conditions and can't be the same term. This one is the stronger of the two, because it doesn't depend on any weird metaphysics — it just observes that the two phrases aren't interchangeable in every context.

Though actually I think the whole thing dissolves once you notice the is. In the sentence a white horse is a horse, the is can't be the is of identity, since white horse is more specific than horse. So Gongsun Long must be denying an identity, and that identity is false anyway, so he is right but he is saying something trivial. There's no real paradox here.

On the Mohists: they reply that a white horse is a horse and that riding a white horse is riding a horse, and they're obviously right about the second half — anyone who speaks any language says it. So yes, they refute him, in the sense that they show he's contradicting ordinary usage, and there's nothing to be gained by contradicting ordinary usage. The School of Names was interested in word games rather then in real questions, and the Mohists putting them down is part of why they disappeared.
