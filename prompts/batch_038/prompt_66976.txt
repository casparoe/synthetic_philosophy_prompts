I am working through the foundations of probability — the choice between frequentist, propensity, and subjectivist interpretations — and I have twice encountered the same argumentative structure. I want to know whether it has an established name, and, more importantly, exactly how it should be carved up.

First instance. A colleague holds that to say a physical coin has probability 1/2 of landing heads is to say that the relative frequency of heads converges to 1/2 in the hypothetical infinite sequence of tosses of that coin. I object that nobody could ever observe the limit of an infinite sequence, so it is unclear what the claim has to do with actual evidence: any finite run of tosses is compatible with any limiting value, and the limit is not a thing one could encounter. The reply is that this misses the point, because a classical convergence theorem guarantees that the relative frequency approaches the limit with probability one; the definition therefore does have observable consequences. But the guarantee thus offered is itself a probability claim, now about sequences of tosses, and so on my colleague's own account a further claim about a limiting relative frequency in an ensemble of sequences. The theorem was invoked to secure the empirical content of a definition of probability, and it does so only by borrowing the notion that definition was supposed to supply. When I say this, the answer is that the theorem is pure mathematics, proved from the axioms, and so no circularity arises. Yet on that reading the theorem concerns an uninterpreted formalism: it says what follows from the axioms, not that anything in the world behaves accordingly. The defence succeeds only if the theorem is read empirically and is innocent only if it is read formally, and my colleague passes between the two readings within a single paragraph without appearing to notice.

Second instance, structurally parallel but in the opposite camp. A subjectivist defines probability entirely in terms of an agent's coherent betting commitments. Asked why such commitments ought to be responsive to observed frequencies at all — why a purely internal notion should have anything to do with data — they cite the representation theorem for order-insensitive, infinitely extendable sets of commitments, which shows that any such set can be written as a weighted mixture of fixed biases, so that the agent's own commitments already entail, with subjective probability one, that observed frequencies settle down. And when it is noted that this theorem is stated and proved in the vocabulary of the very thing being defined, the reply is once again that it is a coherence result about attitudes carrying no empirical import — which is true, but leaves the original question standing.

Stated abstractly, the pattern is this: an account defines a theoretical notion by reducing it to something more respectable; pressed on how the reduction connects with anything observable, it produces a theorem that restores the connection; the theorem's guarantee is expressed in the reduced notion itself; and once this is noticed, the account retreats to the theorem's purely formal status, thereby losing the connection the theorem was produced to restore. What strikes me as distinctive is not the circular element alone but the unremarked alternation between the reading on which the theorem is informative and the reading on which it is harmless.

This does not look like a plain petitio principii, since no premise is being used to establish itself. Nor is it the general complaint that induction presupposes induction, since in the second instance no inductive rule is in question and the circular element is a definition rather than a method. Neither is it the familiar objection that limiting frequencies are unverifiable in principle, which both of my colleagues would happily grant and which my objection is meant to go beyond.

Three questions. First: is there an established term, or a small family of terms, for this configuration, either in the philosophy of probability or in the broader literature on the interpretation of uninterpreted formal theories? Please give the nearest candidates and state for each how far it covers the cases above and precisely where it falls short. Second: if nothing existing fits, propose a term, define it, and specify its boundary conditions — what would have to differ for a case to fall outside it. I would want the definition to exclude the trivial situation in which a mathematical theorem merely clarifies a definition and nobody claims it has done empirical work. Third, independently of the naming: is there a principled criterion separating the vicious instances of this configuration from the benign ones, such as a definition legitimately illuminated by a theorem that happens to be phrased in the defined vocabulary? If such a criterion is known, apply it to both instances; if it is not, say so plainly.

A bare label would not answer any of this. What I am after is the shape of the phenomenon: what it is, what it is not, where its edges lie, and whether the two instances are genuinely one phenomenon or merely look alike from where I stand.
