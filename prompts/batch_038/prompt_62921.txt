I am in the sixth form and writing an extended essay on what the living owe to people who do not yet exist, and I have come across Parfit's nonidentity problem in a form that seems to me to settle the question — although I am aware that very few philosophers think it does.

Here is my reasoning. Suppose a government chose, decades ago, to build coal-fired stations rather than nuclear ones. Because that decision altered the timing of countless lives, nobody alive today would have existed had the other choice been made, and everyone alive has a life well worth living. It is surely a definitional truth that you harm someone only if you make them worse off than they would otherwise have been, and these people would otherwise not have been at all; so although the pollution has made their lives less pleasant than they might have been, it has harmed nobody. If nobody is harmed, nothing wrong has been done — and the same must hold for any policy whose effects reach far enough into the future, since any such policy changes who is born. It would follow that we can owe nothing to future people, that Parfit's argument amounts to a vindication of leaving the future to look after itself, and that the steady outpouring of work on duties to the unborn is a refusal to accept an unwelcome conclusion rather than a piece of philosophy.

Am I right that this is where the argument leads? Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five points, each of one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion; each point should take up one step of the reasoning above and say what you make of it.
