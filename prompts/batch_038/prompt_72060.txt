A reading-group argument about psychiatric kinds that I helped draft now strikes me as proving far too much, and I cannot locate the leak.

(1) Some classifications are objects of the knowledge they produce: the people classified can learn that they are classified, and change in response. This is Hacking's looping effect.
(2) When the classified change, the classification no longer picks them out as it did; its target moves.
(3) A classification whose target moves under it cannot be assessed for accuracy against any reality independent of the classification itself.
(4) A classification that cannot be so assessed does not report a real kind; at best it stipulates, and thereby partly constitutes, its own subject matter.
(5) Therefore diagnostic categories in psychiatry are not real kinds.
(6) Nothing in (1)–(4) mentions psychiatry. The argument turns solely on a kind's being available to be known and acted on by its members.
(7) So by the identical route, race, gender, unemployment, adolescence, refugee status, and "the middle class" are not real kinds either — no classification of people survives.
(8) But if (7) holds, then the class invoked in (1) — kinds whose members can recognize themselves as classified — is itself not a real kind, and (1) cannot pick out the phenomenon the argument needs to get going.
(9) Something between (7) and (8) has to give, and I am not willing to give up (1), which seems to me the least questionable claim in the whole chain.

Which step do you reject? I want the step named, an account of why it looked harmless on the way in, and a price tag — in particular, what is left of the comparative claim that some psychiatric categories are more objective or more real than others once the leak is plugged.
