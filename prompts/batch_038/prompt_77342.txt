I am in the fourth year of a philosophy-of-science doctorate, and my dissertation is supposed to be about the interpretation of probability, but I have discovered that I cannot state my own position except case by case. Rather than keep arguing from the standard taxonomy, I sat down with a list of situations, wrote my honest verdict on each, and now want to know what those verdicts add up to. Please do not give me a survey of the available interpretations, and please do not attach a bibliography or a list of who holds what; I know the landscape and I will handle attribution myself. What I want is a principle — or, if my verdicts cannot be unified, the smallest set of notions that can carry all of them — stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have never seen without coming back to you, with each clause tied to the situation that motivates it.

Here are the cases and what I say about them. (1) A coin I know to be fair, next toss: one-half, and I take that to be a fact about the coin and the tossing arrangement rather than about anybody's state of mind. (2) A particular carbon-14 atom in this sample: there is a definite probability that it decays within the next hour, fixed by the half-life; my evidence has no bearing on it; and it is not shorthand for a long-run frequency among atoms like it, since there need never be any such atoms. (3) "Members of the dark morph have a 0.8 probability of surviving to reproduce in this environment": real and mind-independent, but a property of a type in a setting, not of an individual organism — and not a summary of the frequencies this population will actually display, since it could be wiped out before the tally is complete. (4) The lottery machine and ticket 4827: one in a million, objective, and it stays objective on my view even though I am confident the drawing is deterministic down to the last molecule. (5) A hurricane, judged before the models were run and now long settled: the 0.4 we assigned was a genuine feature of the atmosphere at the time, not a report of the forecaster's uncertainty. (6) Whether the asteroid impact at the end of the Cretaceous caused the mass extinction: here I think the request is confused. There is a confidence we ought to have, but I resist saying the event had a probability of having had that cause. (7) The Riemann hypothesis, probability 0.99 given the heuristic arguments and the verified range: I say this cheerfully, and I think anyone who had examined the same material would be rationally required to land near it — even though what is at issue is a necessary truth about the numbers, not an event with a chance of occurring. (8) Where my keys are: I would take three-to-one odds that they are in my office, and the three-quarters is wholly about my information; it would change the instant I looked, and there is no sense in which the keys were objectively three-quarters likely to be there. (9) This 45-year-old smoker, dead of lung cancer before seventy: there is a real number here, but it moves with the description — smoker, male, urban resident, that postal code — and I do not think any description is privileged; still, I do not think the number is mine to choose. It is real relative to a way of describing him. (10) A DNA cold hit: I will say the probability that a person chosen at random would match this profile is one in a billion, and that given the match the probability the sample came from someone else is very small; but I will not say there is a probability that the defendant is guilty, and I think a jury instructed to reason in those terms is being asked to do something incoherent. (11) A duplication case: I refuse to say the probability is one-half that I will be the left-hand successor. Either the question has no answer or the answer is one — even though the physical arrangement is exactly as symmetric as the machine in case (4), and symmetry is precisely what makes me call the lottery objective.

The pairs I keep circling are (7) against (6) and (10): all three concern matters that are settled rather than open, yet in one I happily assign a number and insist that any rational agent must agree with it, while in the others I say the request is confused. Similarly (4) and (5) against (6), and (11) against (4). So, concretely: state the principle, clause by clause, saying which case forces each clause; then tell me what it implies about three situations I have not listed — an engineer's failure probability for a unique bridge under a storm that occurs once a century, where she insists the number is not about her; the probability that an unthrown coin would have landed heads, where the coin was never tossed and never will be; and my own claim that the probability I go to the gym tomorrow is 0.6, which I want to hold onto while also maintaining that I could freely decide otherwise. Then say which of my eleven verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and if a simpler view requires overturning one, name the verdict and tell me what the loss costs me — I would rather have a clean principle that breaks one of my intuitions than an elaborate one that saves all of them by brute force. Finally, if your honest answer is pluralism, tell me whether it is a principled pluralism, by giving the sorting criterion that decides for a new case which notion is in play, or whether it amounts to a shrug; and if you think one of my verdicts is not merely unrevisable but outright incoherent, say so and say why. A page or two is plenty. What I care about is precision, not coverage.
