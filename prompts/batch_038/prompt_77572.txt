Novelist here, six months into a book about a paramilitary unit in occupied Eastern Europe. For research I've been reading the perpetrator history, including the two books from that famous nineties fight — the one about the reserve police battalion of ordinary middle-aged men, and the one that answered it argueing the killers were volunteers who'd been raised to want the killing. Not my question, though. My question is about a move I keep seeing in the writing AROUND those books — reviews, afterwords, festival panels, my own book club twice — and I can't find a name for it.

The move. Someone says: the terrifying thing is how normal they were beforehand, bakers and drivers and clerks, which proves it was the situation doing the work and not anything inside them, because if it had been inside them you'd have seen it in the bakers. Then, sometimes in the same breath, the same person says: and look how keen they were once it started, volunteering, joking about it, staying late, which shows it had been inside them all along. Compressed version, heard at a dinner party: "the fact that they were ordinary is exactly what makes them monstrous." Nobody blinked. The ordinariness is load-bearing on both sides — as evidence that character didn't matter, and as evidence of how far character had rotted. Same with the keenness: one side calls it conformity, of course they were keen when everyone else was; the other side calls it character revealed. The facts never change, neither reading ever looses, and nothing anybody could go and look at would settle it.

So: does this have a name, and if it doesn't, can you give it one? I want the carving more than the label.

I know the near neighbours and I don't think any of them is it. The bias where people over-credit personality and under-credit circumstances isn't it, because neither of my two speakers is misjudging one particular person — they're each claiming the same datum as their own confirming evidence. Ad hoc rescue isn't it either, because nothing is being rescued; both accounts get asserted at once, cheerfully, and neither one is in trouble. Theory-underdetermined-by-evidence is closer but still off, I think, because that's about two accounts both fitting the data, whereas here it's one specific fact being advertised as decisive by both sides — and if you deleted the fact, both accounts would be standing exactly where they were.

Which brings me to the term hunt, and please be concrete rather than gesturing: what are the closest established concepts in social psychology, in the perpetrator historiography itself, and in philosophy of science, and how does the thing I've described differ from each of them? I'd also like to know whether the actual historians in that nineties fight were doing this, or whether it's something piled on top of them afterwards — my impression from reading them is that they disagreed about which facts mattered, not about what one shared fact meant, which would make the move a distortion of the debate rather than the debate.

Two edge questions, and these are the ones I actually care about. First: when is it legitimate? Sometimes a fact really is compatible with two readings and the honest position is that you don't know yet — keenness might be conformity or it might be appetite. What exactly turns the honest version into the rigged version, and can you hand me a test I could run on a single paragraph to tell which one I'm reading? Second: does the move need an attribution dispute to exist, or is it the same animal when one critic says a sentence is so flat it must be deliberate and another says it's so flat it's just bad?

Reason I'm asking: I'm about 90% sure my own manuscript is doing it. My antagonist is an amiable man and I keep wanting his amiability to be both the proof that he's harmless and the proof that he isn't. Tell me what to look for when I go back through the draft.
