I'm setting the end-of-term paper for an MPhil module on formal epistemology this year — twelve students, all of whom have done the Dutch book arguments, Joyce's accuracy-dominance argument for Probabilism, and enough about proper scoring rules to know what the Brier score is. Two hours, closed book, and I want one compulsory essay question worth 25 marks. My problem with last year's paper is that it was recitable: a student who had memorised the shape of the argument could get a decent mark without ever having thought about why the assumptions are the ones they are. So this year I want a question that hands the students a scoring rule they've never seen, defined completely inside the question, and makes them work out what the accuracy-first framework actually says about it.

Here's the rule I've built, which came out of a reading group argument with a colleague who thinks accuracy has a holistic component we always leave out — roughly, that a credence function can be inaccurate not just by being far from the truth but by leaving the truth out of the set of possibilities it treats as live at all. Concretely, for the students: three mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive hypotheses H1, H2, H3; a credence function b = (b1, b2, b3) with each bi ≥ 0 but no requirement that they sum to 1; and two components. The first is the usual Brier-style closeness, β(b, w) = −Σj (bj − vj)², where vj is 1 if Hj is true at w and 0 otherwise, so bigger is better. The second is a safety term, σ(b, w) = 1 if the true hypothesis at w is one of those to which b assigns at least 0.4, and 0 otherwise. Total accuracy is A(b, w) = 0.1·β(b, w) + 0.9·σ(b, w). I chose 0.4 deliberately: it's above 1/3, so even the maximally spread-out coherent credence function falls outside the safety net, but 0.4 + 0.4 ≤ 1, so a coherent agent can still keep two hypotheses live.

What I want the question to turn on is this. I'm pretty confident that b* = (0.4, 0.4, 0.4), which is flatly incoherent, accuracy-dominates the uniform coherent function (1/3, 1/3, 1/3) under A — b* scores 0.832 in every world while the uniform function scores about −0.067 in every world — and that b* is not dominated by any coherent credence function at all, since any coherent c must have some cell below 0.4, and at the world where that cell is true c gets σ = 0 and hence A ≤ 0. So on this measure Probabilism comes out not merely unvindicated but inverted: incoherence is accuracy-mandated. I'd like the question to have the students establish that, then diagnose which of the conditions on an acceptable measure of epistemic disutility A violates (I have propriety, additivity, dominance and non-trivialty in my notes from Joyce — correct me if I've mangled the list), and then confront the obvious reply, which is that A simply isn't a genuine measure of accuracy so nothing has been shown. The interesting bit, and where I want the marks to be concentrated, is whether that reply can be made without begging the question — the Leitgeb and Pettigrew style worry that the conditions are doing all the work and the conclusion is smuggled into the choice of scoring rule. I'd also like a part that contrasts A with the spherical score, which as I recall is proper but scale-invariant and so can't tell b apart from 2b: a case where propriety survives and the Probabilism argument loses its grip anyway. And a final stretch part asking whether *any* positive weight on the safety term can preserve propriety — I think the answer is no for any threshold above zero, because you can always find a credence function with a cell sitting just under the threshold where buying your way into the safety net pays, but I haven't proved it and I'd want the model answer to either prove it or tell me I'm wrong.

So: please write the question exactly as it would appear on the paper (fully self-contained, no outside reading assumed and no references to give, since they can't look anything up in the exam), a model answer of about 900 to 1000 words, and a marking scheme totalling 25 that names the specific moves a first-class answer has to make and says what each is worth, plus a short note on what separates a first from a 2:1 and on the mistakes that should cost marks — I expect at least one of those to be students who announce that the rule is "not proper" and think the question is over, and one to be students who do the arithmetic but never say what it shows about the epistemic status of the framework's assumptions. Write the whole thing in British English, and don't attach a bibliography or a reading list: anything a student needs has to be inside the question itself.

One more thing, and please take it seriously rather than being polite about it. Check my arithmetic and my claims before you build a marking scheme on top of them. If the example doesn't work the way I think it does — if b* is dominated after all, or if the safety term turns out to be proper at some threshold, or if the spherical-score contrast is a red herring — say so plainly at the top and tell me how you'd repair the question instead. Same if you think the crux as I've framed it is the wrong one to hang 25 marks on, or if a two-hour answer can't realistically do all five parts justice and the question needs cutting down. And where you're going on a recollection of the literature rather than working something out from the definitions I've given you, flag it as such; I'd much rather have an honest "I think this is right but I'd check it" in the marking scheme than a confident point that penalises a student for not reproducing something that isn't true.
