I am a postdoctoral researcher in comparative cognition, and in five weeks I am giving a twenty-five-minute talk followed by a fifteen-minute discussion at an interdisciplinary workshop on animal minds, hosted jointly by a psychology department and a philosophy department. My thesis is deliberately unfashionable: Dennett's four-tier ladder of Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian creatures does not carve nature at its joints, and the tier that most people assume does the interesting work — the Popperian one — is not a discrete level at all. The argument runs in three steps. First, the diagnostic property the ladder assigns to Popperian creatures, namely the capacity to have one's candidate actions tried out and killed off inside the head so that the conjectures perish instead of the animal, is not a special addition to associative learning but simply what any organism acquires once it possesses a learned forward model it can query off-line. Second, model-based reinforcement learning of precisely that kind has been reported in rats around blocked paths, in pigeons, and on contested readings in honeybees, which means the property the ladder reserves for a distinct tier is distributed continuously and widely among taxa the ladder would classify as merely Skinnerian. Third, the ladder should therefore be replaced by a two-dimensional space: one axis I call model depth, running from a one-step valuation of the present situation to counterfactual rehearsal of outcomes the animal has never encountered; the other I call model provenance, tracking whether the model was installed by genes, by the individual's own trial and error, by observation of conspecifics, or by uptake of external artifacts, tools, and — in our species — language. On my account only the upper corner of the provenance axis answers to anything like a Gregorian creature, and the entire Skinnerian-to-Popperian dispute is an artifact of having projected two independent dimensions onto a single scale.

The composition of the room matters to me more than the argument does. Roughly half the thirty attendees will be comparative psychologists and field ethologists working on corvids, parrots, and primates; they are reflexively suspicious of any framework that ranks species, they will press me for what the two-axis scheme predicts that the ladder does not, and several have data of their own that they believe already settles the matter. Two are computational reinforcement-learning modelers, for whom model-free versus model-based is a formal distinction with a precise technical content, and they will find my appropriation of their vocabulary loose or, worse, decorative. One is a philosopher of biology who takes the taxonomy seriously not as an empirical hypothesis but as a piece of conceptual engineering, and who will insist that I have failed to engage it on its own terms. There will also be a senior ethologist, well known in the field, who distrusts mentalistic description of any kind and will suspect that my model-depth axis is merely a respectable-sounding route for smuggling consciousness back in.

Build me the question-period prep sheet I will actually carry to the podium. Give me the eight or nine objections this particular audience will raise, ordered by how much damage each would do if I handled it badly, and phrase each in the idiom of the person most likely to raise it — the corvid researcher will not put it the way the reinforcement-learning modeler will, and neither will put it the way the conceptual engineer will. For each objection, supply a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, a candid note on where that reply is soft, and a verdict on whether I should concede part of it up front rather than attempt a defence. Identify the single exchange that has to go well for the talk to be judged a success, and the one objection I should simply expect to lose. Finally, include the question I am dreading: I suspect it concerns whether the two axes are independently measurable at all, or whether provenance collapses back into depth on the grounds that a model acquired from a conspecific is just a shallower copy of what the animal could have learned alone. Write that question out in full and tell me honestly whether a survivable answer exists. I do not want a defence of my position and I do not want encouragement; I want the questions and what to say back.
