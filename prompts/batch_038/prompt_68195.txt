I chair the Community Standards Board of a residential university in South Africa — five members, two of them students — and on Tuesday we have to settle a case that has split us down the middle. The facts are not in dispute. A third-year resident who chairs the residence committee was found responsible, after a full hearing, for a sustained campaign of exclusion and humiliation aimed at a first-year from a rural area: she mocked his accent in front of the whole residence, had him struck from the meal rota, assigned him cleaning duties given to no one else, and told other residents that he had no business being there. The complainant has almost no ties in the residence — no club, no team, no religious group, family eight hundred kilometres away — and in his statement he said that the residence was the first community he had ever belonged to, which is why the exclusion undid him. He declines to take part in any mediated meeting, has asked not to be housed in the same building, and wants the standard sanction, which is removal from residence for a year. There is a rumour he will simply transfer, and the residence has an open day next month, which several members seem to think is relevant.

Against this, the respondent and forty-seven other residents have proposed a restorative alternative: a circle with the residence, a written apology, and the respondent designing and running an inclusion programme for next year's orientation, in exchange for keeping her place. The Dean of Students backs the proposal and points to a conduct policy that commits the university to restorative practice and invokes ubuntu by name. One board member — a philosophy lecturer — has now twice said that ubuntu is being used as a label for something quite different from what its defenders actually argue, and that there is a live dispute in the literature about whether the community is prior to the individual or whether individuals retain claims that do not derive from what the community says about them. I find his objection powerful, but I cannot tell the board what to do with it, and I do not want a memo that simply recites that restorative justice is transformative. What I need is a decision memo of roughly a thousand words that I can circulate before the meeting, recommending one concrete outcome — the standard sanction, the restorative track, or a specific conditional hybrid — with a timeline the administration can actually work to, and reasons the losing half of the board could respond to rather than dismiss.

Four things I would ask you to handle head-on. First, a restorative process ordinarily presupposes a relationship to repair; here the complainant denies that any such relationship exists and refuses to attend, so the memo should say what a circle would be restoring, for whose benefit, and whether proceeding without him converts the process into a ceremony that readmits the respondent while leaving his exclusion intact. Second, on a relational account the respondent's dense web of ties and the community's testimony about her look like evidence in her favour, while the complainant's isolation looks like a deficit against him; the memo should say whether our implicit weighting scheme penalises precisely the person who was harmed, and whether that is an unfortunate feature of the view or a distortion of it by people invoking the view. Third, consider whether expulsion from a community is the harshest sanction we can impose or, on a relational picture, the one that most directly aims at what makes someone a full member at all — that is, whether leniency and severity invert here, and what follows for the Dean's proposal. Fourth, give the board criteria it can apply next term about when the restorative track is available at all, including whether the complainant's consent is a necessary condition or only a weighty consideration. Please argue the strongest version of the losing side rather than a caricature of it, keep it free of citations and reading lists (name a thinker only where the name does work the reasoning cannot do on its own), and be explicit about which parts of the analysis are settled ground among people who work on African moral philosophy and which are genuinely contested — I do not want one interpretation of ubuntu, or one resolution of the priority dispute between community and individual, handed to the board as though it were the consensus view. If you think we are framing the case wrongly, say so and tell us what the better framing is, but still end with a recommendation we can vote on.
