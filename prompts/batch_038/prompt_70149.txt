For a cross-disciplinary workshop on the demarcation problem — half philosophy of science graduate students, half researchers from public health, engineering, and data science — I need a sorting instrument rather than a lecture: a branching questionnaire of seven to nine items that places each participant at a named position according to what they say about cases, not according to which label they claim. No item may name a criterion, a philosopher, or an ism; each must be a self-contained scenario with the relevant facts supplied, so that a participant with no background in the debate can still answer it.

Draw the items from the borderline cases this audience actually meets: a machine-learning model that predicts reliably but whose mechanism no one can state; an epidemiological model fitted to the same outbreak data it is then credited with predicting; a fundamental physics programme that has produced no novel successful prediction in decades but keeps yielding new mathematics; a nutritional correlation with no proposed mechanism; a historical science such as the impact account of the end-Cretaceous extinction, which makes few risky predictions but coheres with a great deal of independent evidence; and a commercially sold personality assessment. At least two branches should turn on a distinction the audience habitually collapses: whether an inquiry is scientific, and whether that entitles anyone to denounce it as pseudoscientific.

Then supply the scoring key, mapping answer patterns onto strict falsificationism, falsificationism softened by Duhem–Quine holism, Lakatosian research programmes, Kuhnian puzzle-solving, Feyerabendian anarchism, Laudan-style deflationism about the whole problem, and a Bayesian confirmation-theoretic position. For each, identify the single item that does the separating work; flag every pair of positions the questionnaire cannot in fact distinguish; and list the answer combinations that no position occupies, each with a one-line diagnosis of whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored.
