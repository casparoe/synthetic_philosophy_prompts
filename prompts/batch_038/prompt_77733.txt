I keep having intuitions about when a prediction market should actually get to make a decision, and they don't seem to line up with each other. Here they are.

- A state deciding whether to build a light rail line, betting on ridership and commute times ten years out: yes, let the market decide.
- The same state deciding where to site a hazardous waste facility, betting on the same kind of welfare measure: no. The market would pick the poorest town and I don't think that's a reason to.
- A central bank setting rates off markets for inflation and unemployment: yes.
- A parole board using a market on reoffending: no, even though it would probably predict better than the board does.
- A school board choosing curriculum off a market on test scores: no — feels like the metric eating the thing it's supposed to measure.
- War, a market on whether a strike sets a nuclear program back five years: no, because the people who know most are on the other side and can trade on it.
- Approving a new drug: yes. Allocating one available organ between two patients by market: no. Both are life-and-death predictions, so I cant seperate these.
- A market on whether a regional bank fails: no, since the price moving can help make it fail.

Is there a principle, or a short list of them, that gets most of these and explains why the two pairs I can't separate (waste site vs. rail line, drug vs. organ) come out different? And what does it say about a case I haven't thought of, like a city betting on whether removing a dam raises property values and fish counts? Tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. Assume the standard futarchy setup where you run a market for each option and take whichever predicts better welfare, so the market for the option you rejected never resolves — I suspect that matters to some of my verdicts but I couldn't say how.
