I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and for the past year I've been stuck in the gap between two tribes that barely acknowledge each other exists: conservation people, and the animal-welfare crowd who think most wild animals' lives are net miserable. I'm trying to write a piece about a pattern in their arguments and I don't have a word for it, which is a problem, because the piece only works if I can describe the pattern without making either side look like they're cheating.

Here's the shape. Two people are looking at the same numbers — say a study of how many larvae a pair of songbirds has to fledge to replace itself, or the counts from a wetland after it's drained for housing. They agree on the numbers. They even agree that suffering counts. But one concludes the habitat loss is a catastrophe and the other concludes it's a relief, because it means less lives of the starvation-and-being-eaten variety. Now push each of them on what would change their mind and you get different answers — not different conclusions, different *standards*. The one who's worried about suffering wants a very high bar cleared before anyone gets to say a given wild life was worth living; the other wants a very high bar cleared before anyone gets to say clearing a woodland is a harm to anybody. And each will tell you, sincerely, that they're just following the evidence and holding to the normal scientific standard.

Same thing with predators. Reintroduce them and one camp says you'd better be very sure the ecosystem benefits before you accept all the extra killing, while the other says you'd better be very sure it produces net suffering before you're allowed to block it. The burden has flipped, and neither side can see that it flipped, because to each one the burden obviously sits exactly where they put it.

So — is there an established name for this? A couple of candidates I've already talked myself out of. It isn't motivated reasoning or confirmation bias, or it doesn't feel like it: I don't see wishful thinking in either camp, nobody's comforting themselves with a nicer picture of the world. They just weigh being wrong in one direction vastly more than being wrong in the other. And it isn't a plain double standard or special pleading, because those words imply someone's being slippery, and I've met these people and they aren't. Nor is it simply that the data is uncertain — though it is — the thing I keep noticing is that the uncertainty gets resolved by something that isn't in the data and isn't acknowledged as having been supplied by the person doing the resolving.

What I actually want is the carving, not a label I can drop in a headline. Where does this thing start and where does it stop? At what point is it just ordinary disagreement about evidence, or ordinary disagreement about values, rather than this? Is it a reasoning failure, or is it the correct response when two people genuinely have different things at stake — because if it's correct, my piece has a completely different ending. If there's no name, coin me one, with a defintion tight enough that a general reader would know what I meant and neither camp would feel ambushed. And tell me which of the nearby ideas is the closest fit, and precisely what about my description it fails to capture.
