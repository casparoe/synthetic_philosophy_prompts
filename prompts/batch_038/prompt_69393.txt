You are a historian and philosopher of physics with expertise in Poincaré's philosophy of science and in the prehistory of what we now call chaos theory. I am working through the chapter on chance in Book I of *Science and Method* (1908; English translation by Francis Maitland, 1914) and need help getting the argument straight. What follows is a close running paraphrase rather than a quotation, reconstructed from my own reading, so please flag any place where I appear to have distorted the original.

The passage: Poincaré begins by supposing that we knew the laws of nature exactly, and also the state of the universe at the initial moment; then, he says, we could predict exactly its state at any later moment. But even if the laws held no further secrets for us, we could still know the initial state only approximately. If that approximate knowledge sufficed to foresee the later state with the same approximation, we should say the phenomenon had been predicted and is governed by law. It is not always so: sometimes small differences in the initial conditions produce very great differences in the final phenomena, so that a small error in the former yields an enormous error in the latter. Prediction then becomes impossible, and we say the phenomenon is fortuitous. He illustrates this with meteorology — a slight atmospheric disturbance, had it been situated a little differently, would decide whether a cyclone passes here or there, and so whether ships are lost; we see the great effect, we do not see the small cause, and we call it chance. He sets alongside this a different kind of case, of the sort where a tile loosened by the wind strikes a passer-by: there two causal series that are independent of one another — the meteorological history of the wind, and the personal affairs that brought the man to that spot at that hour — happen to intersect, and it is the intersection we call fortuitous. Elsewhere in the same chapter he insists that the statistical laws of the kinetic theory of gases, where we cannot follow the individual paths of molecules, are nonetheless among the most precisely verified laws in physics.
