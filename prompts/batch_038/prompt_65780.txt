In three weeks I give a departmental colloquium in a history of science department, and I would like to be properly prepared for the question period. Please write your answer in American English.

My talk defends a view about simultaneous invention that runs against the standard use of the evidence. The thesis, in brief:

1. The usual move is to point at the multiples — the pairs and triplets of independent arrivals catalogued ever since Merton's 1961 paper on singletons and multiples in scientific discovery — and conclude that such results were in the air, that they were inevitable, and that it does not much matter who made them. I think that inference is backwards.
2. I define the latency of a result relative to a field: a result is latent when every component it needs is already in that field's public stock — instruments, notations, corpora, techniques — but the expected cost of assembling those components exceeds the reward on offer, which for most of the period I study means priority credit plus whatever patronage or position follows from it. A discovery arrives when its latency crosses zero.
3. Multiples, on this account, are simply what near-zero latency looks like. When a cost falls for everyone at once — a new notation, a cheaper instrument, a translation, an archive becoming accessible — several people cross the threshold within the same decade, and each crossing is individually contingent even though the pattern is regular. Multiplicity is evidence that a result had become cheap, not that it was bound to happen.
4. My most provocative claim: priority disputes are the observable proxy for near-zero latency, because a dispute only breaks out when several people are close enough to the threshold that each can plausibly assert arrival. It follows that singletons — the quiet, unchallenged, one-person discoveries — are the evidentially interesting cases, since they suggest high latency paid down by one person's unusual resources, training, or tastes. Singletons are therefore the real evidence against inevitability, and they are systematically underrepresented in the catalogue precisely because they generate no disputes, no letters of complaint, and no gossip to preserve them.
5. I close with an institutional consequence: because the priority system pays the whole reward to the first arrival regardless of how little latency was left to consume, first-past-the-post credit is a windfall in exactly the cases where several people were about to arrive anyway, and it is properly earned only in the high-latency singleton cases. The credit norm is thus miscalibrated in precisely the cases the multiples give us.

The audience is roughly twenty-five. Four or five senior social historians of early modern Europe who explain everything locally and who are hostile in principle to any account that treats a discovery as a natural kind spanning two different intellectual worlds — one of them has written that Newton's fluxions and Leibniz's differential calculus are not the same object under two names, and they will not be delighted that I use exactly that pair as my illustration. One historian of astronomy in the Islamic world, who will press the point that every multiple in my examples comes from a community that had a priority norm, a correspondence network, and a printing trade, so latency is being measured only where the credit machinery makes it visible, and fields without that machinery will look artificially singleton-heavy. Two sociologists of scientific knowledge, who will say the singleton/multiple ratio is an artifact of a post hoc classification negotiated by the participants themselves. One scholar who works with large-scale publication and citation data and will ask what in my account is operationalizable and what is merely an elegant redescription of the historian's oldest phrase about conditions being ripe. One philosopher of science who works on discovery and serendipity and will argue that latency is computed after the fact and can be tuned to fit any pattern, so the view is unfalsifiable. Several graduate students, at least one of whom will go after my point 5 as naive about how scientific careers actually get made.

Give me a prep sheet rather than a defense of the view. Specifically: the nine or ten objections this audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the talk rather than by the order in which they will arrive; each objection phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, in their own vocabulary and with enough of their characteristic tone that I would recognize it in the room; a reply I could deliver in about two minutes for each; and an honest note on where each reply is soft — which objection I can absorb, which I should concede early and openly, and which has no good answer and where I should aim to survive rather than win. Tell me which two or three I should pre-empt inside the talk itself, and what I should say in the opening ten minutes so that the concessions I will have to make are made on my own terms instead of under pressure. Identify the single reply that has to land for the talk to be judged a success. Then tell me the question I am hoping nobody asks: the one I suspect is in the room, probably from the philosopher or from the person working with citation data, that would expose the gap between my account of latency and anything a historian could actually measure. If you think that question is fatal, say so plainly, and tell me what I should have in my pocket for it.
