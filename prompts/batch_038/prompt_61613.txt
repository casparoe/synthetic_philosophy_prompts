Something about how history journals handle crank submissions bugs me and I can't work out whether it's principled. As I understand it, a paper arguing that some minor treaty got dated a decade wrong goes out to referees, but a paper arguing the entire chronology of the ancient Mediterranean is inflated by a thousand years — phantom centuries, duplicate emperors, the usual — gets bounced by the editor without anybody reading the arguments. And the guidelines tell authors they have to work through the existing literature before proposing to overturn it, plus have the relevant languages.

What theory of knowledge does that two-tier system presuppose? Is an editor entitled to know the big claim is false without looking at the reasoning, or is this just triage dressed up as epistemology? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, the rival views it quietly rules out, and the places where the practice is underdetermined so that several different theories would license it.

Then the part I cant resolve: working historians accept almost all of their own chronolgy on testimony they've never personally checked, which is precisely what the crank says makes the field unscientific rather than merely wrong. What would the discipline have to change if it actually took that complaint seriously?
