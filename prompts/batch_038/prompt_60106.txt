I am a novelist, not a philosopher, and this autumn I am delivering a six-thousand-word essay to a collection on resentment. The volume's editor has asked each contributor to take one canonical text seriously rather than to write about resentment in general, and I have taken Nietzsche's *On the Genealogy of Morality*. My argument is finished in my head but not on the page, and I would like a reader who knows the book better than I do to find the cracks before I do.

The thesis, stated plainly: the distinction Nietzsche draws between noble and priestly valuation is usually glossed psychologically — the noble affirms spontaneously, the man of ressentiment reacts out of impotence — but it is more exactly a distinction of aspect. The noble type is constituted by the capacity to end a scene; the man of ressentiment is constituted by the inability to. Moral vocabularies, on this reading, are the sediment of two different relations to one's own unfinished business: "good and bad" belongs to a past that is over, "good and evil" to a past that is still running. What makes the essay mine rather than anyone's is that I argue this from craft — from what it is like to fix a chapter that will not close — and that I claim Nietzsche's own aphoristic form is not incidental to the argument but is the argument's proposed remedy.

Below is the draft of the section that carries the weight, followed by an outline of the rest and a paragraph I have not yet resolved. I have deliberately left in the places where I think I am weak, because I would rather have them named than smoothed over.

*Draft, Section II — "What the Lambs Cannot End"*

Nietzsche's First Essay is usually summarized as a story about who names values first. The noble type, he says, does not begin with the world: it begins with itself, and its "bad" is only a late and pale after-image of its own "good." The priestly type inverts the order, since its creative act is a refusal, a No aimed outward, and so it requires an outside to act upon at all. The standard gloss is psychological — the slave feels powerless and converts powerlessness into virtue. I want to suggest that the more exact description is temporal.

Consider the lambs. Nietzsche permits them to hold a meeting and to decide that the birds of prey are evil, and that it is consequently praiseworthy to be a lamb. The point is normally read as a point about agency: the lambs invent a doer behind the doing, a subject that could have refrained, because only such a subject can be held answerable. But notice what the lambs' sentence cannot do. It cannot stop. "The bird of prey is evil" is not a report on a completed transaction; it is the opening line of a case that has no natural terminus, because the condition it describes — being edible — is unchanged by the utterance. A grievance whose cause is a standing feature of the world is a grievance that must be recited forever, and recited identically, since there is no new information to add.

The noble type is defined less by what it affirms than by what it can drop. In the Second Essay Nietzsche names the relevant faculty and insists on its positive character: forgetting is not mere inertia or the absence of a record, but an active suppression, a doorkeeper who maintains order in consciousness and keeps room for what is new. Without it, he says, there is no happiness and no present. Set beside the First Essay, this suggests that the two modes of valuation differ not in content but in the aspect under which the past is held. The noble past is composed of closed units — a deed performed, an insult answered on the spot and finished. The past of ressentiment is composed of open ones: scenes re-entered without change, each re-entry confirming that nothing has been settled.

Here the grammatical claim does its work. Nietzsche has already told us that the doer is a fiction the deed attracts; I want to extend the observation from agency to tense. If the subject is added by language, then the tenses available to a moral vocabulary are also added by language, and a vocabulary built on the accusation has been built on a form that cannot be completed. That is why the priestly revolt looks to me less like a new set of values than the installation of a new form — the interminable case — and why Nietzsche's remedy is stylistic rather than doctrinal: the aphorism as a machine for producing endings.

*The paragraph that follows* moves from all of this to my own work. It describes a chapter I rewrote eleven times over two years, in which a character returns to a single conversation with his dead father; every draft added detail and none added change, and the chapter became possible only when I stopped trying to resolve the conversation and let it break off unresolved, with the character leaving the room. My claim in that paragraph is that the fix was not a matter of taste but the discovery of the thing Nietzsche describes — that a past becomes available for valuation only once it has been given an ending, and that the failure to supply one is a failure of form rather than of feeling.

*Outline of the remaining sections.* (I) The etymology of guilt and debt in the Second Essay, used to introduce the idea that debt is the one moral sentiment with a built-in terminal condition: it can be discharged. (III) Bad conscience as the internalization of instincts that have lost their outlet, re-described as a grievance that has lost its addressee and therefore its possible conclusion. (IV) The ascetic priest, whose genius on this account is that he supplies ressentiment with a temporary ending by reassigning the cause — you yourself are the cause of your suffering — which closes the scene without altering anything, and so guarantees its reopening; priestly consolation is addictive rather than curative because it is a form of relief that manufactures further work. (V) Objections and coda.

What I want from you:

1. Accuracy. Flag every place where I have misstated or over-compressed Nietzsche. For each, say whether the error is substantive or a tolerable compression, since the essay is addressed to general readers. I do not need citations or a bibliography; I will locate and check the passages myself.

2. Novelty. Does the aspect reading add anything to the familiar claim that the noble is spontaneous and the priestly reactive? Tell me what a philosophically trained reader would say is genuinely new here, and if the answer is that nothing is, say so directly and tell me what would have to change for something to be.

3. The inference I most distrust. I move from Nietzsche's remark about the doer being a fiction added to the deed to a claim about tense and aspect. That remark concerns agency; my conclusion concerns time. Is the move licensed, or am I trading on the fact that both are features of grammar? If it is a bad move, suggest the least costly repair.

4. A counterargument I plan to concede. On Nietzsche's own account the sense of guilt reaches its extreme precisely where the debt becomes infinite and unpayable, which strikes me as a direct counterexample to the closure thesis: the moral sentiment peaks when the transaction can never be terminated. Tell me whether conceding this is survivable, whether the thesis can be reformulated so that the unpayable debt becomes its paradigm case rather than its refutation, or whether it is fatal to the whole piece.

5. Falsifiability. Section II ends by treating Nietzsche's aphoristic form as evidence for the reading. Is that a real test, or a test that any sympathetic reader will pass regardless? If you think it is empty, tell me what would count as evidence against the reading — something I could look for in the text and fail to find.

6. Terminology. I currently describe the failure mode as a scene that will not close and the capacity as the ability to end. Are these serviceable, or do they read as precious? Propose alternatives if you think the concept needs a name at all.

7. Objections I have not thought of. Give me two or three that a hostile but fair reader would press, ordered by how much damage each would do, and say for each whether the essay should answer it or concede it in a footnote.

8. Structure and that last paragraph. I have roughly two thousand words left. Which of the outlined sections is load-bearing and which could be cut? And is the turn to my own fiction a legitimate use of the essay's authority — the reason I, rather than a scholar, am writing this — or is it self-indulgent? Please be blunt about any passage that seems written to sound right rather than to be right; I would rather have six hundred words of hard comment than two thousand of encouragement.
