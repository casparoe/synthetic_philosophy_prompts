I am a junior at a public high school of roughly 1,900 students in a suburban district in the Midwest, and on the nineteenth of next month I will have twelve minutes to present to our school board, followed by about twenty minutes of questions from the board and from the public. This will be the first time I have addressed a public body. What I need is a preparation sheet for the question period, not a defense of my position.

The building has carried the name of Ezra Holloway since 1927. He donated the land and his estate paid for the original structure. He also ran a mill that employed children under twelve into the 1910s, he campaigned against this state's compulsory-schooling law on the grounds that it would take wages out of working families' hands, and he barred Black workers from skilled positions at the mill and from the company-built housing.

The view I intend to state plainly and defend is what I have been calling the standing-endorsement view. An honorific name is not a sentence uttered once in 1927 and then finished; it is a sentence the district re-utters every day, on the sign out front, the letterhead, the diplomas, the jerseys. The question before the board is therefore not what people decided a century ago but what the district is saying now. From this I draw two conclusions the board will not enjoy. First, the familiar compromise — keep the name, add a plaque explaining the mill — is not a middle position but the most committed position available, since after the plaque the district can no longer claim it never considered the objection; it will have chosen the name with the objection in front of it. Second, the coherent response is not to replace Holloway with a worthier person but to stop attaching people's names to buildings at all and to name them functionally, by neighborhood or cardinal direction, because the same liability attaches to any honoree and there is no reason to trust a future board to discharge it.

The argument I will sketch for them to attack runs: names are re-utterances rather than completed dedications; so inheritance is no defense, because continuing is choosing; a reaffirmation made after hearing an objection carries more weight than an unexamined inheritance, so a plaque raises the district's commitment rather than lowering it; substituting a new honoree preserves the practice and hence the problem; and functional naming is not erasure, because the records remain in the district archive and the plaque can be relocated into a hallway exhibit on the school's own history.

The audience is specific, and the same objection will sound different coming from each of them. The board president is a retired insurance claims adjuster who runs meetings on procedure and precedent and wants to know what rule we would be applying the next time this arises. The facilities chair owns a landscaping company and has already priced new signage, uniforms, and stationery at about $46,000. One member teaches eighth-grade social studies in this district, believes nearly any conflict can be converted into a lesson, and is the likeliest to propose the plaque. One pastors a congregation of about four hundred and talks about repentance and forgiveness rather than harm and consent. One is an attorney who does estate work and is worried about the conditions attached to Holloway's bequest and about donor confidence generally. One is a parent who ran the booster club for six years and whose father and grandfather graduated here. The seventh is a senior holding the student seat who agrees the name should go but wants it replaced with that of the district's first Black teacher, and who will treat my refusal to substitute anyone as protecting the status quo by making commemoration impossible. In public comment there will be the chair of the county historical society, a retired archivist who volunteers four days a week and who will bring photocopies, plus the alumni association's treasurer and a reporter from the local weekly.

Please give me: the objections in roughly the order they will actually arrive, each marked for how much damage it would do; each phrased the way that person would phrase it, in their vocabulary rather than mine, so that I can hear the adjuster's version and the pastor's version of what may turn out to be the same objection; a reply I can deliver standing at a podium in about ninety seconds; and a one-line note on where each reply is softest. Tell me what to concede in my opening two minutes so that I am not conceding it later under pressure, and identify the single reply that has to land for the position to survive the evening at all. Then give me the questions I am hoping nobody asks, especially any that turn my own principle against me. I already expect the $46,000 question and some version of "where does this stop — the town, the street, the football field"; rank those as well, but what I mainly want are the ones I cannot see from here. Finally, since I am seventeen and will graduate before any of this takes effect, tell me which objections will be aimed at my standing rather than at my argument, and how to answer those without dismissing them or sounding wounded.
