I'm putting together week six of a grad proseminar on metaethics — the week we do the evolutionary debunking arguments — and I'm thoroughly sick of the examples everyone reaches for: disgust, the incest vignettes, trolleys, food aversions. I want to hand my students a case that's engineered to isolate one specific variable, and built carefully enough that they can't wriggle out of it by pointing at something I've stipulated away. So: build me the case to spec, then show me it meets the spec.

Here's what I want. One scenario, a single paragraph, entirely realistic — two ordinary people in the contemporary world. No isolation chambers, no anthropologists with clipboards, nobody being shown an fMRI of their own amygdala, no possible-worlds machinery. Both of them hold the same substantive moral belief: that they are under a serious, genuinely demanding obligation to give up most of their disposable income to aid anonymous strangers on the other side of the planet who will never learn their names. Both are well-informed laypeople who fully accept the standard evolutionary geneology of the moral faculty — kin selection, reciprocal altruism, coalitional psychology, the parochialism of the evolved moral sense. Nobody is surprised by anything, and neither of them needs to be told about their own psychology. The only difference between them is *how* the belief is held. Agent A has it as an immediate, articulate conviction she cannot give reasons for. Agent B arrived at it by a long, careful argument from premises that are entirely non-moral and that she'd hold regardless — roughly, that how bad a child's dying of malaria is doesn't depend on where the child happens to be standing, and that she can prevent it at a cost to herself that is trivial by comparison. And here's the twist I want you to build in honestly rather than just assert: both beliefs run *against* the grain of what a fitness-shaped moral sense would be expected to produce. Parochialism is the evolved default; costly impartiality toward anonymous distant strangers is not. Don't tell me that in the scenario — arrange the scenario so it's true of it.

Then do the work. For each of three positions — (i) the hardline geneological debunker, (ii) the third-factor realist who says survival or flourishing is itself good, so selection pushed us toward at least some moral truths, and (iii) the tracking realist who thinks selection handed us a normative reasoning capacity which, once it exists, can latch onto moral facts it wasn't selected to detect — give me the verdict on A and on B separately, with the reasoning. One constraint on the debunker that I want *enforced*, not merely mentioned: she may not retreat to a global argument from reason. No Plantinga-style or Lewis-style move where the reliability of every faculty gets called into question at once. She has to say what is specifically wrong with *this* moral belief that isn't equally wrong with, say, the belief that the sun will rise tomorrow, or with her own belief about what selection favored. If the setup makes that impossible for her, say so plainly — that's a result I'd happily have, but only if you've shown it rather than rigged it. Stipulating verdicts instead of generating them out of the positions is cheating, and I'll catch it.

Finish with an audit. Name the single feature of B's case that is doing the work. Tell me what happens to the three verdicts if you strip that feature out and give B an argument a fitness-shaped mind would have been perfectly happy to produce — does B collapse back into A, or does the gap between them survive the change? Then give me the best attack each of the three positions has on the case as specified: which constraint would each of them say is doing illegitimate work, and why. And since I'll be standing in front of fifteen grad students defending this thing, be straight with me about the weak points — tell me which of the three positions you actually think handles B best, what argument or consideration would move you to a different one, and whether there's a hole in my specification that I haven't noticed and that a sharp student will walk straight into.
