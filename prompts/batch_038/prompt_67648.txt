I am preparing a short position statement for a workshop on the legacy of the analytic–synthetic distinction, and I would like a rigorous consistency audit of the package I have arrived at before I circulate it. I am not asking for a summary of Quine's paper or for background on the debate; assume I know it. The deliverable is an audit of my own commitments: which of them pull against one another, which pairs only appear to, and what the least expensive way of restoring coherence would be and what it would cost me.

Here is the package. The numbers are mine, and I would like you to refer to commitments by these numbers.

1. Quine's central charge succeeds as applied to natural language. No non-circular explication of analyticity is available for ordinary English, and the notion therefore has no role to play in an account of meaning or of a priori knowledge.
2. Carnap's meaning-postulate programme is untouched by that charge. Within a stipulatively fixed formal system, a sentence's following from the stipulated rules is a matter of how the system was constructed, not a discovery about it; analyticity relativised to such systems is well defined and philosophically unobjectionable.
3. What makes 2 work where natural language fails is that, in the setting I actually work in — specification languages, dependent type theories, machine-checked proofs — the semantic rules are not merely notational but executable. When a lemma is discharged by a checker against declared signatures, there is a fact of the matter that no further discovery about the world could overturn.
4. On the strength of 3, I want to say that such checking yields genuine a priori knowledge about the artefact specified: that a well-typed module, for instance, cannot exhibit a certain class of failure. The knowledge is about the system being modelled, not merely about the calculus in which the model is written.
5. I remain a thoroughgoing Quinean holist. Confirmation attaches to whole theories; every sentence is revisable in principle, including the laws of logic; and, following the indeterminacy considerations in *Word and Object*, there is no determinate fact of the matter about what a theory's expressions mean.
6. I reject the gloss of analyticity as maximal entrenchment. Entrenchment comes in degrees, and a graded notion cannot recover the sharp boundary that 1 says cannot be drawn at all.
7. Nevertheless I keep a graded, pragmatic use of the term: some sentences — arithmetic, the conservation principles of a mature theory, the axioms of a specification — are so central that giving them up would amount to changing the subject, and that is all the work "analytic" needs to do.
8. I hold that universal revisability is an epistemic thesis only. A sentence can be metaphysically necessary in some suitable sense and still rationally revisable, so 5 does not commit me to anti-realism about necessity.

Please work through the package and tell me where it fails to hang together. Some of what looks like a contradiction here I suspect is not one, and some of what looks unobjectionable I suspect is not; I want the discrimination, not a list of everything that can be made to look uncomfortable. Report where your audit is most fragile: which consideration, if I pressed it, would move you to a different verdict on the worst tension, and what argument or piece of reasoning would most plausibly show that your central diagnosis is wrong.
