ok I'm trying to write something about Goethe's politics and I keep getting tangled up in what feels like a contradiction, and I'd rather not just wave it away.

The story everyone tells is that he was appalled by the French Revolution and thought you cant impose form on a living thing from the outside — institutions, peoples, whatever, they have to develop, and the revolutionaries broke that by trying to build a constitution from scratch in a season. Fine. But the same guy then spends roughly a decade as a minister at a small court, running mines and road works and pushing changes through at Jena, all of which is... reform from above, by decree, on a population that didn't ask for it. So does his Weimar career refute his anti-revolutionary line? If his objection to the Jacobins is really that you can't shape organic wholes by fiat, doesn't that commit him to opposing state-led reform generally, in which case the administrative years are a straight-up betrayal of his own principles?

Related thing I cant make sense of: he apparently adored Napoleon. If Napoleon is the Revolution's product and executor, what is Goethe doing admiring him — is it just that Napoleon won and restored order, i.e. he liked the coup but not the revolution, which seems like a pretty thin distinction to hang everything on?

My own guess, which might be me projecting completely: maybe he thinks of institutions the way he thinks of plants, where you can prune, graft, redirect growth, even force it a bit, but you cant start a new organism from nothing and expect it to survive — which would make the Weimar work not just consistent with the anti-revolutionary stance but an expression of it, and would put Napoleon in the category of the grafting hand rather than the storm. But I have no idea whether that's a position Goethe actually held or whether I'm dressing up a plain old conservative preference for stability in botanical language. Also I suspect I'm wrong to assume his political views are downstream of his nature philosophy at all — maybe he never had a political theory and people keep manufacturing one for him.

So I want an actual verdict, not both-sides mush: does the reformer-Goethe contradict the organicist-Goethe, is there a single view underneath both, or is the question itself malformed because it assumes a systematic Goethe that never existed? And then be straight with me about the soft spots — which part of your answer are you least confident in, and what would flip you? Like, what kind of thing showing up (a remark from his late years, the way he described his own job in Weimar, his reaction to some later upheaval) would make you drop your reading for the opposite one.
