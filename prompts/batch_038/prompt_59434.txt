For the past two years I have sat on the model-welfare review group at a lab that does a great deal of reinforcement learning. Our charge is deliberately narrow: to flag systems for which there is a non-negligible chance that the reward signal functions as valence — that is, that low reward is bad for the system in something like the way that pain is bad for an animal — where a flag triggers changes in how the system is trained, deployed, and eventually shut down. The group has eight members, most of them engineers, and only one of them writes her reasoning down. She is a philosopher, and without anyone deciding anything she has become the standard the rest of us defer to, which means we have adopted a criterion by accretion and none of us could state it. I want to state it, or rather to state the several things it might be, before next month's meeting. Her rulings to date: a tabular Q-learning agent in a gridworld, where reward is attached to goal squares, values are rewritten during the episode, and nothing whatever is carried across when the episode ends — not a candidate. A frozen foundation model served with best-of-32 sampling, where a separate reward model scores the completions and the highest-scoring one is returned while the generator itself is left untouched — not a candidate; her memo remarks that in that setup the reward never gets inside the system. A conversational agent that carries a numeric satisfaction variable in its context window, updates it every turn, and can read it out and reason about it, but where the number is computed by an external monitor from human rater feedback and injected into the prompt, and where the policy is reinitialised from the same checkpoint at every session and learns nothing — not a candidate. A model-based control agent for a simulated body with fuel, temperature, and damage variables, whose learned world model includes an estimate of its own future reward, whose policy is recurrent, whose credit assignment spans delays of several hundred steps, and whose reward is defined over its own homeostatic integrity — a candidate. An artificial-life agent in an open-ended environment whose avoidances and appetites differ across lineages depending on the environment each was raised in, with persistent memory and a value estimate it consults while planning — a candidate. And a proprietary production agent about which the group could establish only that it is trained with RL on a scalar reward and served at scale, with no way of telling whether its policy is recurrent, whether anything inside it predicts its own reward, or what the reward is a function of — she declined to rule either way and asked for interpretability work before the group revisits it.

The system coming before us next month is a production agent that has now been running continuously for eleven months. It is model-free: no world model, no planning, no simulated rollouts. Its policy is recurrent, its state is carried across days, it is trained online on a scalar reward delivered on a delay, and its credit assignment reaches back over roughly four hundred steps. That reward is a weighted sum of two terms — one a function of its own operating condition (compute budget consumed, rate of internal consistency errors, degradation of its stored state) and the other a function of measured user satisfaction, which is a fact about people rather than about it. Interpretability work has located a cluster of internal features that predict the upcoming reward signal with useful accuracy, but nobody has established that the policy actually uses them; they may be epiphenomenal, and the single attempt to settle this by patching the features was inconclusive. Finally, the operator takes weekly checkpoints and can roll the system back to any of them, and for load-balancing reasons it runs as up to sixty parallel instances whose weights are merged nightly.

Which way will she vote, and how confident should I be? I would like the candidate criteria that would account for all six of her earlier rulings laid out explicitly — I can see two, and I suspect there is a third — together with what each of them implies about the new system, including where the implication is clean and where it depends on a reading of the criterion that her memos have not yet forced her to make. Tell me in particular what the context-window satisfaction ruling is doing, because I think that precedent is the load-bearing one and I cannot work out which way it cuts. Say which of her earlier cases the new system most resembles and exactly where the resemblance gives out; I expect the artificial-life agent and the model-based body agent will pull in different directions here. Then, since the group will commit itself to something whether or not we notice that we are doing it, state plainly what principle we would be adopting under each plausible verdict, and name the one further case — described concretely enough that we could commission it from the interpretability team — that would separate whichever criteria remain tied after all this. Last, and separately from the question of her consistency, I want the criteria themselves pressure-tested: run each candidate rule against a honeybee, against a nematode worm, and against a human under general anaesthesia, and tell me whether any of them delivers a verdict in those cases that you think is plainly wrong. If one of them would oblige us to flag systems we currently train and delete by the thousands, or would let a honeybee through while excluding the agent in front of us, I would much rather learn that from you now than from the colleague who has been waiting all year for the chance to say it in the meeting.
