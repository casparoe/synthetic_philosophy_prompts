I teach a first-year section of a compulsory methods course shared between an economics and a history department: forty minutes, one whiteboard, twenty-two students who have just spent a fortnight on trend extrapolation and who now hold that macroeconomic forecasting is a law-governed science in the same sense as physics. I want to teach them Popper's case against laws of history without naming him for the first half-hour — no Marx, no Comte, no appeal to a book none of them will have read — so that they reach the distinction between a trend and a law under their own steam and the label arrives last. Plan the session for me; do not explain the argument to me.

What I need from the plan:

- What must already be in place before the argument gets any traction. I assume some notion of a law as what supports counterfactuals and survives intervention; tell me whether that can be built informally at this level or whether it has to be assumed, and if assumed, how to prevent the students from noticing the assumption only at the point where the whole thing turns on it.
- The single case that carries the session, and the properties it must have and must lack. My own candidate is a national fertility rate falling by roughly a tenth of a birth per woman per year across two decades, from which a student can produce both a bare extrapolation and, using the same figures, a policy-conditional forecast. Say whether that case can bear the whole weight, and what it loses if it can.
- Where the knowledge-growth argument goes. In my draft it comes first, because it is the most arresting, and I now suspect it cannot survive that position; tell me why, and what it depends on.
- Whether the self-fulfilment and self-defeat material belongs in at all, or whether introducing it hands the historicist an escape he was not otherwise entitled to.
- The misconceptions they will bring or form mid-session, each with the precise point in the sequence at which to head it off. I expect 'a trend is just a law nobody has tested yet', 'Popper thinks social science is worthless', and 'a prophecy that comes true because people believed it has been confirmed'; tell me which of these is the most damaging and which I am underestimating.
- How to handle two interventions I anticipate: one student who has read enough to object that the knowledge argument is itself a prediction about the future and so undermines itself, and another who will dismiss the exercise as Cold War polemic. Say whether to engage either, and when.

No formal notation on the board — that is settled and not up for discussion. Close with one question I can put to the room that separates a student who has actually grasped the distinction from one who has merely learned to say the word 'trend' disapprovingly. Finally, state what I should leave out at this level and what I should tell them I am leaving out, so the omission is visible rather than silent. Please write in British English.
