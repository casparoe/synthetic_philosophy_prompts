I use Hofstadter's term sphexishness in design reviews: the Fabre story of the digger wasp that hauls its paralyzed cricket to the burrow, goes inside to inspect the nest, and, finding the cricket dragged a few inches away while it was in there, hauls it back to the threshold and begins the inspection again, indefinitely. It is my shorthand for code that re-runs a fixed ritual instead of noticing that the situation has changed.

The philosopher Fred Keijzer has argued the example is bad on two counts: wasps in the lab do adapt after enough iterations, and re-inspecting a nest that has genuinely been disturbed is plausibly what a wasp ought to do. That would make sphexishness not a property of the routine but a property of the match between the routine and the disturbances it faces — the wasp only looks mindless because an experimenter supplies a perturbation that paralyzed prey never would.

Give me a worked example of a software system where the same reversal holds: behavior I would have flagged as sphexish in review turns out to be the correct design, and the person calling it rigid is the one who is wrong. Make it concrete enough that I could sketch it — the inputs, the check-and-redo loop, and what must be true of the environment for that loop to be right — and identify which property of the environment does the work, so that I know what to verify before accusing a system of being a wasp.
