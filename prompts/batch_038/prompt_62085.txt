I'm 69, retired three years ago from a state ag experiment station, where I spent my career on beetle larvae and soil, so philosophy is a wierd second act for me, but I'm enjoying it. Once a month I drive over to a discussion group that meets in the back room of the public library. One of the regulars, Marta, spent thirty years writing software for a freight company and has very firm opinions about what counts as a mind.

I've been keeping track of her verdicts. The question is always the same — does this thing literally believe something, or is that just loose talk — and here's what she's said over the past year:

- House thermostat: no. It doesn't believe the room is cold, it's a strip of metal bending.
- The spreadsheet cell that totals a column: no, obviously.
- Honeybee doing the waggle dance to point the hive at a clover patch: yes, she really does have a belief about where the food is and how far off.
- The dog that parks itself by the door at 5:30 on weekdays: yes, it believes somebody's coming home.
- A Go program that learned by playing millions of games against copies of itself, handed the legal moves but no strategy: yes, it has beliefs about the board.
- One of those 1980s programs that diagnosed crop diseases out of a few hundred IF-THEN rules a plant pathologist typed in one at a time: no. She said whatever that thing believes is the pathologist's belief, on loan.

In February somebody is going to ask her about a large language model. The one they'll have in mind reads and writes, was trained on enormous piles of text other people wrote, and hasn't taken in anything new since — its weights have been frozen for a couple of years. But I've used it myself, and if you push back on something in its answer it will often locate the mistake and repair it, without being told how.

My hunch is that more then one rule fits all six of those verdicts, and that the language model is sitting right where the rules split apart. I can't tell which one Marta is actually running, and I'd like to show up at that meeting with something better than a guess.

Give me exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, with no introduction and no wrapping up at the end: (1) the rule that best accounts for all six verdicts; (2) the strongest rival rule that fits them just as well; (3) what each of those two says about the language model; (4) your bet on what Marta will answer, and why; (5) one further case I could bring up in February that would force her to show her hand.
