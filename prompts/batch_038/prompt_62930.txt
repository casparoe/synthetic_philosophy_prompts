Everyone in my research pile says the incompleteness theorems show minds can't be machines, everyone else says that's a total howler, and I can't tell which requirement each side is actually failing. Before I write the scene I want the spec, not another opinion.

Give me the desiderata: what any argument that runs from the incompleteness theorems to a conclusion about minds and machines has to deliver in order to be worth a reader's time. Each item with the case or consideration behind it, so I can tell a genuine requirement from a hobby of one camp.

The ones I especially need pinned down:

- what exactly is being denied. "Not a Turing machine," "not algorithmic at all," "not deterministic," "not physically explicable" — my characters slide between these constantly and I need to know which slides are fatal and which are harmless.
- what counts as the human side of the comparison. One person with a pencil? The species? An idealized mathematician with unlimited time and no memory limits? Whatever the argument picks, it has to say, and it has to say why the idealization isn't smuggling in the conclusion.
- the stuff it can't contradict. Gödel's second theorem applies to us as well as to the machine, so nobody gets to establish their own consistency for free. Rosser's version means you can't lean on soundness without paying for it somewhere. And a machine can hedge — it can be built to emit conditionals of the form *if I'm consistent then G* — so the argument can't turn on the machine being too dumb to think of the Gödelian move. Also the each-versus-all problem: showing that for any given machine we can cook up something it can't prove looks like it proves nothing at all, because the same holds between two different machines, and my AI character says this, smugly, in act two. Tell me whether that objection is as deadly as I think it is or whether there's a version that survives it.

Then sort the list. Which of these are real desiderata and which are just prejudices — of the physicalist camp, or of the logicians who want everything finitary and surveyable, or of people who've decided in advance that consciousness must be non-computable. I want to catch myself writing a character who "wins" only because he's been granted a premise the other side never agreed to.

And flag any pair of requirements you think can't both be satisfied. My hunch is that *no double standards, the human is a system too* and *the human can see the consistency the machine can't see about itself* is that pair, and if it is, that's basically the whole book and I should just lean into it.

Finish with two things: which single requirement knocks out the most versions of the argument in circulation, and whether a successful argument would have any consequences — like, if it's right, what follows that anyone could check or that would change anything?
