I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently reporting a long feature on the "accuracy-first" program in formal epistemology — the view that the fundamental epistemic good is accuracy, that degrees of belief are assessed by strictly proper scoring rules, and that the familiar norms of probabilistic coherence and conditionalization are not primitive rules but consequences of a ban on being accuracy-dominated. For the past month I have been talking to people on both sides of a dispute that I want to build a substantial section around: whether the dominance argument for probabilism survives contact with the conditions under which actual agents form beliefs. One of my sources holds that it is untouched, since a dominated agent can always switch to a coherent alternative. The other holds that the argument quietly assumes coherent alternatives are always on the menu, and that this assumption fails in ordinary life far more often than the literature admits. I cannot settle that by quotation, and I have no intention of turning the section into a seminar on scoring rules. What I need is a worked example that a numerate reader can follow with a pencil and check for herself.

So I would like you to construct a thought experiment to the following specification. The design requirements are these: (1) the setting must be realistic and mundane — a clinic, a newsroom, a trading desk, a research group, a public agency — with no demons, no neurosurgery, no science fiction, and no stipulation about what the agent is somehow incapable of conceiving; (2) the protagonist is a committed accuracy-firster who accepts both that epistemic rationality consists in maximizing expected accuracy under a strictly proper measure and that being accuracy-dominated is what makes a set of credences irrational — she is neither confused nor weak-willed, and she is doing precisely what her own framework tells her; (3) her evidence supports a perfectly coherent set of credences over a small partition of three or four cells, and you must describe that evidence in enough detail that a reader will grant the coherence of the resulting credences rather than take it on trust; (4) something about her situation restricts the credences she can actually adopt to a proper subset of the available space, and you must justify that restriction in a sentence or two as a feature of the world — institutional, technological, or psychological — rather than a philosopher's convenience; (5) the restricted set must nevertheless contain some coherent options, so that the case does not reduce to the trivial observation that coherence is simply out of reach; (6) under her own criterion, the best option available to her violates the probability axioms; and (7) no coherent option available to her accuracy-dominates that incoherent one. Give me the scenario itself in one or two tight paragraphs. A word on what the case is for, since that fixes what would count as cheating here: the incoherence has to be generated by the framework's own criterion operating on a restricted menu, not asserted. If it works only because the numbers have been chosen by hand to make it work, or because the conclusion has been smuggled into the description of the constraint, it is no use to me at all — I would be printing something a reader could break in five minutes.

After the scenario, walk me through the demonstration: state the partition, the evidence-supported credences, the available set, and the measure (the Brier score is fine, or another strictly proper measure if you prefer, but name it and stick to it); show the expected-accuracy arithmetic for the top two or three candidates; show the dominance check world by world; and state plainly whether any available option is both coherent and undominated. Then give me the analysis the piece actually turns on: which single feature of the case is doing the work; what happens to the verdicts if the menu is refined or the restriction removed altogether — does the case collapse, or does it survive in weakened form; whether this is a one-off curiosity or something that generalizes, in the sense that any fine-but-finite grid would produce it; and the two strongest objections a defender of the dominance argument would press, with an honest verdict on how far each succeeds. One of those objections must be the charge that the restriction bites on what the agent says or reports rather than on what she believes, so that the case tells us something about reporting rules and not about doxastic norms at all — if you think that objection is fatal, say so. Please do not cite papers, give publication dates, or attach names to results; I will handle all attribution from my own interviews, and I cannot print references I have not verified. Keep the set-up on scoring rules to two or three sentences at most, and write for a reader who has never met the phrase "strictly proper." Aim for 1,000 to 1,400 words. The feature will run in our British edition, so please write the entire answer in British English, spelling and idiom alike; the Americanisms in this message are habit rather than a request.
