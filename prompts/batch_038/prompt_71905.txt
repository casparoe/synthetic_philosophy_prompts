I have been developing what I thought was a modest defense of ordinary consumption, and it now delivers considerably more than I want it to. The argument is this.

(1) An act is wrong only if it leaves some identifiable individual worse off than that individual would have been had the act not been performed.
(2) My purchase of one carton of eggs leaves no such individual worse off: orders are aggregated before they reach producers, shipments move in batch sizes that dwarf a single carton, and restocking follows forecasts built from historical demand rather than from any particular sale.
(3) Therefore my purchase of one carton is not wrong.
(4) Everything said in (2) fits any single purchase equally well, since nothing in it referred to me, to this carton, or to this year; so no single purchase of factory-farmed eggs is wrong.
(5) Wrongness attaches to acts rather than to descriptions of them: if no act in a set is wrong, then the outcome those acts jointly produce cannot be wrong either, because there is no further act for it to be wrong of.
(6) Yet a million such purchases leave a million hens worse off than they would have been had those purchases not occurred.
(7) So one of (1), (5), and (6) is false, and (1) is the one I am least willing to surrender, since it is the only thing keeping my account of wrongness anchored to the difference anyone's conduct actually makes.

Steps (4) and (5) are where my confidence runs out, though I cannot say what is wrong with either. Tell me which step to reject, and explain why that step looked sound on the way in. Then price the alternatives: if I abandon (1) instead, do I incur duties whose violation harms nobody in particular, and what work is left for the notion of wrongness to do; if I abandon (5), am I committed to holding collectives answerable for outcomes that no member brought about, and does that collapse into blaming each member for the whole? Please write your response in American English.
