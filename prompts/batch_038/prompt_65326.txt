At a decision-theory seminar, someone defending a Pascal's-mugging-style argument waved away my objection — that no sane person would pay much for a St. Petersburg ticket — with a single line: "The expected payoff is infinite, so any finite price is a bargain." That sentence did all the work of dismissing me, and I could not say at the time what it was committing him to.

Give me a numbered menu of the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be making, keeping apart, for instance, the arithmetic claim about the game's expected monetary value, the normative claim about what an agent ought to pay, and the background claim about how expected value is supposed to relate to rational choice. For each, say whether it is trivially true, contentious, or false — and where it holds only given further assumptions (bounded versus unbounded utility, the payer's solvency, whether any real agent could offer the game at all, whether the relevant series even converges). Then tell me which readings would genuinely refute my objection, which I should reject and on what grounds, and at which point the argument slides between them.

Finally: does any reading survive when the payoffs are adjusted so that the expectation is finite but astronomically large? That is the version I actually care about, since real mugging cases tend to work out that way.
