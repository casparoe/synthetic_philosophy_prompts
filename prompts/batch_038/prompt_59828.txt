I produce a fortnightly history podcast with an unusually well-read listenership, and next month's guest is a historian of the July Crisis whose recent book defends what has become, over the last decade, the most popular account of 1914: that responsibility for the outbreak cannot be pinned on any single government, because agency was dispersed across alliance commitments, mobilization timetables, and military cultures in which each decision-maker was reacting to a rival's moves that he had already misread. He is at pains to say that this is not a rehabilitation of Wilhelmine Germany, and he leans heavily on the claim that the opening article of the reparations section of the Treaty of Versailles was a financial formula that later generations moralized — a clause in which the Allied and Associated Governments affirmed, and Germany accepted, "the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage to which the Allied and Associated Governments and their nationals have been subjected as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies." He is, however, extremely good at two deflections, and I need questions that make them progressively harder to deploy. The first is methodological: whenever the conversation reaches the Fischer controversy, he separates the origins of the war in the summer of 1914 from the war aims formulated after it, and treats the September Programme of that autumn as evidence about the latter and not the former. The second is evidential: when confronted with a document that looks decisive — for instance the record of the December 1912 meeting at the Berlin palace, where the army's chief of staff pressed the view that war was unavoidable and the sooner the better, while the naval secretary asked for a postponement of roughly a year and a half pending the completion of the Kiel Canal widening and the Heligoland U-boat base — he observes that the chancellor was absent, that no decision was recorded, and that our account rests in large part on a diary note by the chief of the naval cabinet to the effect that the meeting's result amounted to almost nothing.

Please draft nine to eleven questions, ordered so that these deflections become harder to use as the interview proceeds, and supply for each a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give. I want at least one question on each of the following: whether a dispersed-responsibility account can still mark the difference between a government that pressed for war and one that merely failed to prevent it, and what is lost if it cannot; the December 1912 meeting, and specifically whether the chancellor's absence and the lack of a recorded decision are evidence of the meeting's insignificance or evidence of something rather different; the origins-versus-aims distinction, pressed to the point where he has to say whether it is a genuine methodological principle or a way of neutralizing an entire class of documents in advance; Article 231 itself, and what it would even mean to hold a nation rather than particular ministers and generals responsible for a war — whether that clause states a moral verdict, a liability rule for compensation, or neither, and whether his reading of it is doing philosophical work he has not argued for; the counterfactual, given that the archives of every great power have been open for a century — what asymmetry in the surviving record would actually discriminate between his account and a one-sided one, and whether his position makes any claim that a partisan account does not also make; and the politics of reception, since a distributed-responsibility reading has tended to travel better in some capitals than others, handled in a way that does not let him dismiss the point as ad hominem. The final question must be about falsification: what archival finding, document, or argument, surfacing tomorrow, would count against his thesis, and what he would be willing to concede as having already counted against it.

Do not include softballs and nothing along the lines of what drew him to the period. Where you judge that he has a genuinely strong answer available, note that in a line after the question, and give me the harder follow-up regardless — I would rather the audience hear the best version of his position survive pressure than watch it dodge.
