I write a long-form column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have spent the better part of a year reporting a feature on a question that sounds simple and evidently is not: can culture be a cause? My anchor case is the argument over Weber's thesis about ascetic Protestantism and the spirit of capitalism, which has been running since the essays appeared in 1904–5 and which, to my reading, has never once produced a change of mind on either side.

From my reporting I can distinguish three camps, and they do not appear to be having the same argument.

The first, largely economic historians and quantitative social scientists, treats the thesis as a causal claim of a familiar shape: affiliation with a particular confession raised the likelihood and intensity of a particular economic orientation. They code confession as a variable, control for the obvious confounders, and report either a null, an effect better accounted for by a mediating channel such as literacy, or reverse causation, with commercially dynamic towns selecting their religion rather than the other way around. For them, the claim that culture caused capitalism has to mean something like: variation in the cultural factor produced variation in the outcome, at an estimable magnitude.

The second, largely historians and interpretive sociologists, replies that this is not the claim. On their account the thesis concerns intelligibility: a religious vocabulary made a certain posture toward work, profit, and the postponement of gratification thinkable and honorable to the people who adopted it, where it had previously been morally suspect. They note that Weber himself cast ideas as switchmen setting the tracks along which material interest pushes action, not as the engine; that the survey and archival measures of a Protestant ethic are constructed out of the very behaviors they are supposed to explain, which renders the test circular; and that a null result in a variance decomposition cannot refute a claim that was never about variance.

The third camp thinks the first two are talking past each other because they have never agreed on what the explanans is: whether a value is a property of individual psyches, a feature of institutionalized scripts, or a publicly available repertoire that actors draw on strategically in particular situations. On this view, the methodological quarrel is downstream of an unargued ontology of culture.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis rather than a conclusion, is that there is no single debate here. The phrase "the Weber thesis" bundles at least three distinct disagreements: one verbal, over what "cause" and "culture" pick out; one over standards of evidence; and one substantive but largely hidden, over whether the explanandum is a rate of occurrence or the meaning of an action to the people performing it. A related asymmetry puzzles me and I cannot account for it: empirical null results have accumulated for decades, yet the thesis's standing has not declined, and its defenders dismiss those results as category errors rather than as counter-evidence.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey and not a verdict on whether the thesis is true. Which of these disagreements, if any, is doing the real work? Are the quantitative null results genuinely evidence against anything Weber claimed, or does the interpretive reading render the thesis untouchable by any evidence — and if the latter, is that a legitimate clarification of a historical claim or an immunizing maneuver? Is the impasse a sign that the question is ill-posed, or only that it is hard? And given your diagnosis, what would count as progress: a distinction, a reformulation of the explanandum, a new class of evidence, a formal result — or should we expect none, and why? Please argue from what each side actually says rather than from the course of the literature; I have the names and the bibliography already and need neither from you.
