I am a doctoral student working on a late-sixth-millennium settlement in the northern Mesopotamian plain, and next week I am presenting a chapter to our department's reading group in which I argue that the community had no centralized grain administration. My evidence is entirely negative, and I have tried to make it as robust as I can: over six seasons we opened roughly 1.4 hectares of an estimated 13-hectare site in contiguous blocks rather than sondages; all deposits from the phase in question were wet-sieved at 2 mm and fully flotated with a 300-micron light fraction; and the phase yielded not one clay sealing, no impressed bulla, no token concentration, and no archive, although sealings do occur at three comparable sites in the same region and period — always, as far as anyone can tell, in storerooms destroyed by fire.

At the rehearsal my supervisor's entire reply was a single sentence: absence of evidence is not evidence of absence. He then added that unfired clay survives only where a building burned, that our phase shows no destruction horizon anywhere in the exposed area, and that our recovery process was therefore insensitive to precisely what I claim to be inferring from. Two things trouble me. First, I cannot tell whether that sentence is a truism I should simply respect or a substantive claim about my inference that happens to be false. Second, the same supervisor argues in a published paper that the absence of fortification walls and of unhealed perimortem trauma in the cemetery shows the community was not regularly at war — an inference from absence he treats as decisive. Nobody in the room could say where the sentence comes from; it appears to belong to everyone.

Could you set out, as a numbered menu I can bring to the reading group, the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making, ordered from the near-trivial to the radical? For each reading, state what it commits one to — in particular, what it requires about the likelihood of finding nothing given that an administration did exist — and say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false, and whether on that reading it does the work my supervisor needs, that is, whether it actually blocks my inference. Then commit to a view: which reading he is most plausibly invoking given that he also pressed the fire-preservation point; whether his own warfare argument is compatible with that reading or whether he is entitled to the asymmetry; and whether my chapter's inference survives at all, and what I would have to establish about the recovery process for it to. Give me your reasons and take a definite position rather than leaving the options with me, and flag explicitly where you think I have overstated or understated my own case. You need not cite anything — I want the argument, not the references.
