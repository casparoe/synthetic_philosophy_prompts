**Alignment as a mold rather than a sculpture**

When people say that a present-day language model is aligned, I have noticed that almost all the evidence offered is negative in form. The model does not produce instructions for synthesizing something dangerous. It does not insult the user. It does not pursue a hidden objective across a long conversation. It does not lie when directly asked a factual question it can answer. What we mean, operationally, is that a large set of behaviors has been carved out of the space of things the model might have output.

This suggests to me that we should distinguish two things that get collapsed into the single word alignment. I will call them *commission-shaped* and *omission-shaped* alignment. Commission-shaped alignment is a claim about what a system does: it reliably performs some specified task, in some specified way, across some specified range of situations. Omission-shaped alignment is a claim about what a system does not do: a list of prohibited regions, with no positive account of what occupies the rest of the space.

My metaphor is that current methods give us a mold rather than a sculpture. A mold records, faithfully and in detail, the shape of the void — everything that human reviewers rejected. It records nothing at all about the shape of the material that fills the void. And yet when we reason about whether a system is safe, we tend to talk as though we had the sculpture: as though knowing the boundaries of the acceptable told us something about the interior.

**The problem I want to press.** I think there is a structural reason why omission-shaped alignment is harder to secure than commission-shaped alignment, and the reason has to do with how the training signal is attached. Preference-based training, as I understand it, compares outputs the model actually generated. The reviewer picks between two candidate responses, both of which exist. Reward lands on tokens that were produced.

But a great many of the failures we most fear are omissions, and an omission generates no tokens. The model that fails to mention the caveat, fails to flag the dangerous premise in the user's question, fails to say that it is uncertain, fails to recommend stopping — in each case the missing content never appears in the trajectory, so there is nothing to attach a penalty to. I want to call this the missing-token problem: negative feedback is only well-defined for behavior the model emitted, so the method is systematically blind to exactly the failures that consist of emission not happening. You can, of course, construct a comparison in which one candidate includes the caveat and the other does not, but only if you already knew the caveat was called for. The supervision presupposes the knowledge it was supposed to supply.

I am aware this is loosely put, and I would especially like to know whether it is simply false as a matter of how these systems are trained in practice.

**Why capability makes it worse, on my account.** As a model becomes more capable, the number of distinct routes to a bad outcome grows. The prohibited list does not grow with it, because the list was fixed by reviewer reactions to outputs from an earlier, weaker model, applied to prompts drawn from a distribution we chose. So the ratio of uncarved routes to carved ones widens over time.

I think this is not the usual worry, and I want to be told if I am wrong. The usual worry is that a system has goals, and that the goals were installed badly, and that better capability makes the badly installed goals more effective. On my account the system need not have goals at all. A purely reactive predictor with no aims whatsoever becomes more hazardous as the uncarved region widens, because the uncarved region is precisely where novel behavior lives. The danger is not that the material has the wrong shape; it is that we have no description of the material at all.

**Objections I have thought of myself.** First: a refusal is itself a produced output, so omission-shaped alignment is enforced by commission-shaped signals, and the mold metaphor overstates the gap. Second: helpfulness is trained positively, so there is interior content and not merely void. Third: critique-style and step-by-step supervision can penalize a response for what it left out, which would blunt the missing-token problem directly. Fourth: the whole thing may collapse into ordinary worries about distribution shift and evaluation coverage, dressed up in new vocabulary. I cannot tell which of these is fatal and which is merely a complication.

**A test I would run if I could.** Take two models that score nearly identically on the standard preference evaluations and map their omissions — not their refusals, but the things each fails to say in situations where a careful human would say something. If the omission profiles diverge substantially, then what we are calling alignment is a collection of local holes rather than a coherent shape, and there is no reason to expect the holes to line up in a new domain. If they converge, my worry weakens considerably. I do not know whether this is measurable.
