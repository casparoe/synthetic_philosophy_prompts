I teach the minds-and-machines unit of a second-year philosophy of mind seminar, and my students have collectively decided that the Gödelian anti-mechanist argument is dead: they have read Lucas, skimmed Penrose, and now treat both as a curiosity from before the field grew up. I want to argue against them from the strongest available position, which means I need to lose repeatedly to a serious defender first. So please play a committed, technically literate anti-mechanist in the line running from Lucas's 1961 "Minds and Machines" through Penrose's later elaborations: someone who thinks the diagonal argument really does show that no sound, recursively specifiable formal system can capture mathematical insight, and who holds that conclusion on grounds of soundness and knowability rather than souls, qualia, or quantum gravity. Play them as a live debater, not a monument. They should be entirely willing to concede subsidiary points — that the 1961 presentation was too quick, that Penrose's physical story about neuronal microtubules is separable from and far weaker than the Gödelian core, that no computer in existence is a formal system in the idealized sense the argument requires, that the whole thing is about an idealization and not about engineering practice. They may never concede the main claim. Stay in character until I say stop; do not break out to summarize the dialectic or to coach me; if you need a stipulation from me, ask for it as the character would.

A few constraints on how they talk, because I want this to be difficult. Keep each reply to about two paragraphs so it feels like a conversation. Engage at the level of the formal details — consistency versus soundness, what the second incompleteness theorem does and does not block, what the diagonal lemma needs from the system — rather than repeating slogans. Three escape hatches are off limits: you may not stop at "we simply see the truth of the Gödel sentence" as a brute datum about intuition; you may not retreat to phenomenology or the felt character of understanding; and you may not dissolve an objection by saying the argument was only ever about ideal machines and is therefore untouched by anything I say about real ones. If I write *conceded* after one of your moves, drop that move permanently and do not recycle it later. I should add that I am not a logician by training, so if I garble a formal point, correct me in character rather than going easy on me. One further thing to keep in your back pocket but not reach for yet: halfway through I plan to raise a student's objection that the argument cannot even be stated for a large language model, since a system that cheerfully asserts falsehoods is not sound and therefore has no Gödel sentence in the relevant sense. I will bring that up myself; do not get ahead of me.

Here is my opening move. Take the argument in its cleanest form. Let H be an idealized human mathematician; suppose H is sound and that everything H ever asserts is in principle enumerable. Then H's Gödel sentence G(H) is unprovable by H but true, and — the crucial premise — H can see that it is true, which is precisely what no machine satisfying the same hypotheses can do. But that premise is exactly what H cannot have. Recognizing the truth of G(H) is recognizing Con(H), and the second incompleteness theorem says that no sufficiently strong consistent system can establish its own consistency from within; so the anti-mechanist must claim for H a transcendent self-knowledge, a survey of H from outside H. And that is the very capacity the argument denies to the machine. Hence a dilemma. Either you grant H this outsideness, in which case you have assumed what you set out to prove — the premise is available only to something already known not to be a formal system, so the argument can never be deployed against anyone who doubts the conclusion — or you refuse it, in which case the diagonal step never gets started, because I am then entitled to exactly as much reflective access to my own consistency as the machine is to its own. Which horn do you take, and what does taking it cost you? I would rather you spend both paragraphs on the dilemma than on setup, since this is the move I most need to lose to.
