I'm a second-year history grad student, and my program runs this awkward interdisciplinary seminar where half the room are analytic philosophy types and half are working social historians, and last week it blew up over counterfactuals. The historians did the Carr routine: speculating about what might have happened isnt history, it's a parlour game, you explain an event by what actually happened and by the forces that were actually operative. The philosophers said that's incoherent, because every causal claim a historian makes — the rail network mattered, resuming submarine warfare brought America in, the harvest failure did it — is a counterfactual claim wearing historians' clothes, so you can't have the causal analysis without the counterfactual. I mostly side with the philosophers but I think both sides are arguing about the wrong thing, and I want you to argue for a thesis I've been kicking around, ideally better than I can argue it myself.

The thesis: when historians say an outcome was inevitable they are almost never making a modal claim about the world. They aren't saying the outcome came about in every nearby possible world that shares our laws and our broad starting conditions. They're making a claim about the option set open to the agents inside the situation — that no choice actually available to anyone who mattered, given what they knew or could reasonably have come to know at the time, would have produced a significantly different outcome. Call it closure of the option set rather than nomological necessity. Argue that this reading (a) makes inevitability claims defensible instead of a metaphysical extravagance that historians have no business asserting, (b) explains why inevitability sits so comfortably with a narrative full of contingency — things genuinely feel like they hung by a thread even when the outcome was closed, because what hung by the thread was which route inside the closure got taken and who took it, and (c), the part I actually care about, turns the anti-counterfactual position against itself: you cannot assert that an option set was closed without reasoning counterfactually about each of the options in it, since closure is a claim licensed only by that reasoning. So the historian who insists on inevitability while banning what-ifs is using a tool they've declared illegitimate.

Stuff I'd want you to handle, because my seminar partner will raise it within thirty seconds: whether closure of the option set is really non-modal or just modal talk in disguise, since it asserts what would happen were various things chosen, which sounds exactly like a counterfactual conditional — if you think there's a real difference, say what does the work. Then how the account deals with the standard complaint that counterfactuals smuggle in single-cause explanations when actual outcomes have thick webs of causes, and where structural forces show up in your picture — as constraints that shrink the set, or as causes operating within the branches? Run the account on one concrete case; I'd suggest either the Allied victory in Europe in the Second World War or the collapse of the Soviet Union, and tell me what your view licenses a historian to call inevitable there and what it pointedly doesn't. Finish by telling me where the thesis is weakest and what kind of counterexample would kill it — I'd rather you be straight about the soft spots than sell it to me, since I have to defend it in class. No need for citations or a reading list, I just want the arguement itself, laid out tightly.
