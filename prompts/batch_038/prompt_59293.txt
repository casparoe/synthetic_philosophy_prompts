I am in the third year of a history degree and currently taking a seminar on the philosophy of historical explanation, where the set piece this term is the quarrel between great-man and structural accounts. The seminar frames it, as these things always get framed, by putting Carlyle against the philosophical chapters in Tolstoy's novel about 1812, and then asking us to take a side. My difficulty is that my verdicts on particular cases do not line up with either camp, and I cannot tell whether that is because I have no principle or because I have two incompatible ones. Below are the verdicts, roughly in the order we covered them.

1. Germany without Hitler. I say there would still have been a revanchist, authoritarian, virulently antisemitic German state, and probably a European war within two decades. I do not say there would have been an industrialised extermination programme, and I do not say that war would have begun when and where it did. Verdict: the regime is structural; the catastrophe is not.

2. Lenin's return to Russia in the spring of 1917, which the German government permitted and facilitated. Before he arrived, the party's Petrograd editors were urging conditional support for the Provisional Government; after he arrived the party was oriented toward insurrection, and no one else in it was positioned to insist on that. Verdict: individual.

3. The calculus. Two men assembled essentially the same body of techniques within about a decade of one another, and both were working out of a common inheritance — the method of indivisibles, the tangent problems of the French geometers, the infinite series of the English school. Verdict: structural, emphatically; this is the case about which I feel most confident.

4. Nuclear fission and the bomb. Within months of fission being demonstrated, physicists in several countries independently grasped that a chain reaction was in principle available. Verdict: structural as to the discovery, and I cannot decide whether it is structural as to the decisions to build the weapon at industrial scale and to drop it. My suspicion is that my moral feelings about those decisions are reaching backward into my explanatory judgements, and I would like that suspicion tested.

5. Gavrilo Princip. A bomb thrown at the motorcade earlier that morning had already failed; the killing succeeded only because of a wrong turn. Several co-conspirators had been posted along the route, the archduke's visit was in the newspapers, and the alliance system, the mobilisation timetables and the Balkan question were all sitting there waiting. Verdict: structural — and yet the assassination is the hinge on which every narrative of the war hangs.

6. Alexander. I do not think anyone else alive could have done what he did; he is the narrowest bottleneck on my list. Verdict: structural anyway. The army was designed by his father, the Persian administration was in fiscal and military difficulty, and Greek mercenaries were a functioning market. The counterfactual runs through him, but I cannot see the causal work as his.

7. Rosa Parks. There had been other refusals by Black passengers in Montgomery in the preceding months and years, including one by a fifteen-year-old, and none produced a boycott; a local women's organisation had been discussing a boycott for years and printed the leaflets overnight once the arrest happened. Verdict: individual.

The principle I have been working with is that an individual-level explanation is correct to the extent that the individual was a counterfactual bottleneck: to the extent that, in most of the nearby worlds in which they are absent, the outcome is absent too. Three things already go wrong with it. Alexander is a bottleneck and gets no explanatory credit. I also treat timing inconsistently — for Lenin, a revolution arriving two years later under different auspices is enough of a difference to make him indispensable, whereas a two-year delay in the physics of case 4 seems to me to make nobody indispensable. And the binary itself looks wrong: the Meiji Restoration was made by a committee of mid-ranking samurai, and I want to say neither great man nor structure. I cannot tell whether that intermediate category of small groups, parties and ministries is doing real explanatory work or is merely the great-man category with n greater than one.

What I would like is a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I had never seen. For each clause, tell me which of the verdicts above motivates it. Then give me its verdicts on two cases I have not listed: the collapse of the Soviet bloc in 1989, and scientific discovery in fields whose detection papers carry more than a thousand authors. Finally, say honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate and which of them it implies I should give up. If you judge two of my verdicts to be simply inconsistent, please identify them and show the inconsistency rather than smoothing it over.
