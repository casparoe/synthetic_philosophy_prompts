I have been reading Aldous Huxley's Brave New World again for a small group I meet with once a month, and for reasons I want you to help me sharpen, it has become inseparable in my mind from a debate I stumbled into from the other direction: the argument over coherent extrapolated volition and, more generally, over theories that say what is good for a person or for a society is not what people actually want but what they would want under idealized conditions — knowing more, thinking faster and more clearly, being more the people they would rather be. I have no academic training in any of this, but I have spent the last few months working through what I could find on CEV, on Railton's full-information account of a person's good, on Brandt's idea that a desire is rational if it survives exposure to all relevant information vividly imagined, and on Elster's account of adaptive preferences, which I believe he distinguishes from what he calls character planning — the case where you deliberately reshape your own desires rather than having them quietly reshaped by your circumstances. My working hypothesis, and the thing I want tested rather than flattered, is that the World State is not a crude machine for producing pleasure but a *finished extrapolation*: the Controllers ran the process once, decided what humanity's coherent volition amounted to, and then froze the result and defend it against drift with conditioning, caste assignment, and soma. Mustapha Mond's long dialogue with John reads to me less like a defense of hedonism than like an argument that idealized agents — better informed, quicker, more numerous — would converge on exactly the arrangement he governs.

If that is right, I want to know what the novel does to the idealization project that the philosophical papers do not. Specifically: (1) Reconstruct Mond's case as an argument about convergence rather than about pleasure, and say whether Huxley has given the Controllers a genuinely strong version of it or a version that only looks strong because nobody in the room can answer it. (2) Bernard, Helmholtz, and John seem to me to be three different kinds of counterexample to the claim that idealized desires converge — Bernard's discontent looks like wounded status, Helmholtz's like the complaint of someone with too much talent and nothing worth saying it about, John's like a refusal of the whole frame — and the ending, where one of them is desperate to stay and another chooses exile on the Island, seems to sort them out. Tell me whether that sorting actually distinguishes failures of convergence from failures of something else, and whether the novel supports or undermines the view that an extrapolation run to completion would produce agreement. (3) Which of the World State's techniques count as producing adaptive preferences in the sense that matters, and which count as character planning that the citizens themselves are party to — I suspect hypnopaedia, the feelies, and the Solidarity Service do not all fall on the same side of that line, and that soma may fall on neither. (4) There is a worry in the CEV literature, as I understand it, that if the starting values are already corrupted, extrapolating from them amplifies the corruption instead of correcting it. Does Brave New World dramatize exactly that failure — that the Controllers' error was not idealizing too much but idealizing from a starting point that conditioning had already ruined — or is that a distinction the novel has no room for? I also have a memory, possibly garbled, of Mond mentioning a trial in which an island was settled entirely with high-caste Alphas and promptly destroyed itself in civil war, after which the survivors asked to be governed again; if you recall that episode, it looks to me like Huxley putting an empirical test of the convergence assumption in the mouth of the regime's own defender, which would be a remarkable thing to do. If I have confused it with another scene or another book, say so plainly.

Please treat this as a piece of literary criticism written by someone who also works in value theory and AI alignment, and think carefully before you answer: sketch two or three competing readings of the novel first, including at least one on which my hypothesis is simply wrong — on which Huxley's target is not idealization at all but something about art, religion, or the necessity of suffering, with the preference-theory apparatus being my own projection — and only then commit to the reading you can defend. Be faithful in both directions: do not turn the novel into an illustration of CEV, and do not soften CEV into a strawman that a 1932 satirist could easily beat. I want you to be explicit wherever the analogy strains, and I want an honest verdict on whether John's demand for the right to be unhappy is an objection to idealized-preference theories or an objection to something else entirely that the idealization debate has no vocabulary for. Do not quote the novel — neither of us has the text open, and I care about the argument, not the wording — and do not give me a bibliography; if you need to name a technical term or an objection, name it and explain it in a sentence. Close by telling me what the novel contributes to this debate that argument alone could not have produced, and what I should strike from my own thinking going into the meeting.
