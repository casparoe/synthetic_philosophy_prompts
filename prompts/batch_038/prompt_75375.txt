I'm a staff engineer on a warehouse-robotics team, and I'd like you to write a piece of satire for our internal engineering blog. I have no philosophy training; I picked up the idea of "sphexishness" from a colleague who reads that sort of thing, and I've been turning it over ever since. I want the underlying argument to be correct, not merely the jokes plausible, so please treat the conceptual content as the deliverable and the humor as a byproduct.

The premise, which I'd like you to take as given rather than as a claim about real entomology: years ago a contractor wrote the control loop for one of our provisioning robots by copying a famous account of a digger wasp. The robot collects a payload, carries it to the mouth of its charging bay, sets it down, enters the bay to confirm the bay is clear, exits, and then carries the payload in. If anyone moves the payload while the robot is inside, it comes out, locates the payload, carries it back to the mouth, sets it down, re-enters the bay to confirm the bay is still clear, exits, and carries the payload in. That cycle repeats without bound. The robot never learns to simply pick the payload up where it left off.

Please write it as a public issue-tracker thread, roughly 900 words: ticket ID, title, priority and labels, an initial report with numbered reproduction steps (the steps should require a second engineer willing to move a crate forty-one times) plus expected and actual behavior, then ten to twelve comments from invented engineers with invented handles, spread over several weeks, with the ticket status changing at least twice.

The thread should be arguing about the following, without anyone ever using the word philosophy:

1. Whether the behavior is a defect at all. Someone must defend it as the only reason the robot reliably finishes the task, and that defense must be strong enough that the reader half-believes it.
2. The fix attempt. An engineer ships an interrupt handler and a resume-from-last-checkpoint state; the robot then stalls indefinitely at the bay mouth, or resumes carrying a payload into an occupied bay. The point that should surface from the wreckage, without being announced: removing the rigid loop just installs a different rigid loop, and the monitor that is supposed to notice the robot is stuck is itself a routine that can be interrupted. Someone should ask how many layers you need before the thing counts as flexible, and whether the outermost layer has to be rigid.
3. The diagnosis problem. One commenter should insist that no amount of external observation can distinguish a genuinely flexible system from one with a sufficiently large repertoire of canned routines, and should challenge the thread to state a test for escaping sphexishness that is not simply "behavior I approve of." Nobody should pass that challenge.
4. Two incompatible readings of the eventual closure. The ticket ends as Won't Fix / Works as Designed. One engineer reads the closure as vindication, because the robot is doing exactly what it was built to do and nothing more is needed for it to be called competent; another reads the same closure as the whole problem, because the robot could not have done otherwise, and a system that could not have done otherwise is not the kind of thing you thank, blame, or promote. Both should sound reasonable; neither should win.
5. A running gag in which one commenter keeps finding sphexishness in the team's own rituals — the stand-up, the on-call rotation, a habit-tracking dashboard somebody installed specifically to eliminate their own repetitive behavior — and the dashboard itself acquires a ticket.

The target of the satire is not the wasp and not determinism. It is the way "you're just being sphexish" functions as an unanswerable insult, deployed exactly like "you're just being biased," with no criterion ever offered for what would count as escaping it.

Constraints on execution: stay deadpan throughout, with no winking and no asides to the reader; no character may resolve the problem or deliver a moral; no engineer may be a strawman, so each should be right about something the others miss; use no real people, no real books or papers, no citations, and no allusions to actual philosophers by name; and do not explain any joke. The result should read first as an ordinary, slightly tedious bug thread that an engineer would recognize, with the argument emerging from the procedural details rather than sitting on top of them.
