I teach an upper-level seminar, split roughly evenly between philosophy and religious studies students, on religion treated as a natural phenomenon: the cognitive and evolutionary accounts of it and the objections to those accounts. Week three is reliably a mess, because the students cannot distinguish positions they believe they hold. They will say in the same breath that religion is a spandrel of an agency-detection mechanism and that it evolved by group selection to promote cooperation, without noticing that these are rival answers to the same question rather than complementary answers to different ones. I intend to fix this with a diagnostic instrument they complete anonymously before class, so that I can sort them by what they actually believe rather than by which camp they announce.

Please construct for me a decision tree of the explanatory positions on religion as a natural phenomenon. I want nine or ten branch nodes, at most three options at each, and on the order of fourteen to sixteen leaves. Deliver it in two stages: first the branch structure itself, laid out as the logical space of permitted answer-combinations; second, the named positions located within that space.

The nodes must be answerable by a student who has no idea which view a given answer selects into. Build them out of cases such as the following, and supply the remainder yourself:

- a foraging society with no moralizing high god, but with ancestor veneration, food taboos, and painful initiation rites: which accounts predict this configuration, and which are embarrassed by it;
- children raised with no religious input who nevertheless attribute purpose and design to natural objects: whether this shows that belief in gods is natural, or only that something narrower is;
- adults who abandon religious belief entirely with no attendant cognitive deficit, in contrast to what happens when a face-recognition or language capacity is lost;
- a believer whose unreflective, on-the-fly reasoning about gods contradicts the theology she officially endorses;
- the historical fact that the peoples being classified often had no term corresponding to religion, and what that shows about the category being imposed as against about a kind existing before it was named;
- a natural explanation that is complete, such that every religious belief is accounted for by mechanism plus transmission: whether anything remains to be explained, and whether completeness bears on the warrant for those beliefs.

I want the tree to run along at least the following axes: whether religion picks out a single kind with a shared causal history or a family-resemblance cluster; whether the explanandum is belief, practice, or institutional form; whether the responsible mechanism is a domain-specific module or general-purpose inference; who, if anyone, benefits — the individual's genes, the group, or the cultural items themselves; whether the account is selectionist at all, or functional and historical without selection; and whether the genealogy, if complete, is epistemically neutral, undermining, or partly vindicating.

The leaves must include: genetic adaptationism at the level of the individual; multilevel or group-selection adaptationism; the costly-signaling account; dual-inheritance and cultural group selection accounts of moralizing high gods; the byproduct account in both its agency-detection and its minimally-counterintuitive-concept versions; the memetic-parasite account; Durkheimian functionalism without selection; the view that religion is a byproduct and nonetheless true; the naturalist denial that religion has any single source; the family-resemblance retention of the category alongside its eliminativist retirement; the non-naturalist position that no special explanation is owed because believers are responding to testimony and experience; and the debunking position that a complete natural genealogy defeats warrant. Where two of these turn out to be one position wearing two hats, say so rather than listing it twice.

Then give me at least three combinations the space permits that nobody occupies, each with a one-line diagnosis: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. And flag explicitly which pairs of leaves the tree cannot in fact separate — I expect costly signaling and cultural group selection to be one such pair, and I would like to know whether it genuinely is.

Each leaf should carry a name (or the label "unoccupied"), a one-sentence statement of the view, and its single most awkward commitment. No references, no bibliography, no reading list; where a name genuinely identifies a position, one name will do.

Finally, tag each leaf and each load-bearing node as settled, as genuinely contested among specialists, or as not an empirical question at all. I am conscious that some of the empirical claims these theories rest on — the generality of the agency-detection findings beyond Western, educated samples, the cross-cultural uniformity of the phenomena being gathered under one heading — are themselves disputed, and I do not want an instrument that quietly presents the byproduct camp, or any other camp, as the professional consensus when it is not.
