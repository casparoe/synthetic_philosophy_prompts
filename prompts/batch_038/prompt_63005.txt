I've been in a small fortnightly reading group for about three years now — most of us came up through LessWrong and the old Slate Star Codex comment threads, which means we have the somewhat annoying habit of turning literary texts into argument diagrams and then arguing about which boxes are load-bearing. We're working through *Thus Spoke Zarathustra* slowly, in a public-domain early-twentieth-century English translation by Thomas Common, and last week we hit Part III, the chapter Common titles "The Vision and the Enigma." I've deliberately truncated the excerpt at the point where the argument for recurrence is complete and before the chapter swerves off into the shepherd-and-snake imagery, because I want the gateway reasoning isolated. Here it is (§1, in part):

Upwards:—in spite of the spirit that drew it downwards, towards the abyss, the spirit of gravity, my devil and arch-enemy.

Upwards:—although it sat upon me, half-dwarf, half-mole; paralysed, paralysing; dripping lead in mine ear, and thoughts like drops of lead into my brain.

And §2 in full, up to my cutoff:

"Halt, dwarf!" said I. "Either I—or thou! I, however, am the stronger of the two:—thou knowest not mine abysmal thought! IT—couldst thou not endure!"

Then happened that which made me lighter: for the dwarf sprang from my shoulder, the prying sprite! And it squatted on a stone in front of me. There was however a gateway just where we halted.

"Look at this gateway! Dwarf!" I continued, "it hath two faces. Two roads come together here: these hath no one yet gone to the end of.

This long lane backwards: it continueth for an eternity. And that long lane forward—that is another eternity.

They are antithetical to one another, these roads; they directly abut on one another:—and it is here, at this gateway, that they come together. The name of the gateway is inscribed above: 'This Moment.'

But should one follow them further—and ever further and further on, thinkest thou, dwarf, that these roads would be eternally antithetical?"—

"Everything straight lieth," murmured the dwarf, contemptuously. "All truth is crooked; time itself is a circle."

"Thou spirit of gravity!" said I wrathfully, "do not take it too lightly! Or I shall let thee squat where thou squattest, Haltfoot,—and I carried thee HIGH!"

"Observe," continued I, "This Moment! From the gateway, This Moment, there runneth a long eternal lane BACKWARDS: behind us lieth an eternity.

Must not whatever CAN run its course of all things, have already run along that lane? Must not whatever CAN happen of all things have already happened, resulted, and gone by?

And if everything have already existed, what thinkest thou, dwarf, of This Moment? Must not this gateway also—have already existed?

And are not all things closely bound together in such wise that This Moment draweth all coming things after it? CONSEQUENTLY—itself also?

For whatever CAN run its course of all things, also in this long lane OUTWARD—MUST it once more run!—

And this slow spider which creepeth in the moonlight, and this moonlight itself, and thou and I in this gateway whispering together, whispering of eternal things—must we not all have already existed?

—And must we not return and run in that other lane out before us, that long weird lane—must we not eternally return?"—

Our group has ended up split between two reconstructions, and I'd like your help deciding whether either of them actually fits the text. The first we call the pigeonhole reading: the two capitalized "CAN" premises do counting work, so that with infinitely many past opportunities and only finitely many possible configurations of things, every possible configuration — including this gateway, this spider, this conversation — has already been realized, and infinitely many times. The objection one of us raised is that the passage nowhere asserts finitude of states, of forces, or of anything else, nor supplies any notion of an equiprobable space of possibilities, so on this reading the argument is an enthymeme whose suppressed premise is doing essentially all the work. The second we call the draw-along reading: the real engine is the sentence beginning "And are not all things closely bound together in such wise that This Moment draweth all coming things after it? CONSEQUENTLY—itself also?" — a premise about the mutual entailment of moments rather than about how many of them there are, so that fixing what follows from this moment also fixes this moment as a link in a chain that can only be re-run. The objection there is that on this reading the backward-lane steps become decorative, and it's unclear whether the premise invoked is a causal-determinist one (this moment plus the laws suffice for what comes next) or something stranger and more like an identity claim (a moment fully exhausted by what it entails is not a genuinely particular moment at all, so there is nothing left over to distinguish this occurrence of it from another).

So: can you go through the excerpt sentence by sentence and give a premise inventory — what must be assumed at each step for that step to go through, and which assumptions are stated versus imported by the reader? Then tell me whether the pigeonhole reading or the draw-along reading better accounts for the passage as written, or whether there is a third reconstruction that dissolves the dilemma between them, in which case please defend it rather than merely gesturing at it. Two smaller things. First, the dwarf says precisely what the doctrine says — "time itself is a circle" — and Zarathustra's reply is not that this is false but that the dwarf is taking it "too lightly," having just told it "thou knowest not mine abysmal thought! IT—couldst thou not endure!"; what is the content of that objection, and does the gateway's being inscribed "This Moment" rather than with some general name for time have anything to do with it? Second, if any part of your answer depends on this archaic translation being faithful — on the capitalization, say, or on "antithetical" or "weird" — please flag that explicitly as a place where my excerpt might be misleading me, rather than quietly assuming the English carries the weight. I'm not looking for a survey of the secondary literature or for references of any kind; I want the reasoning worked out from the text in front of us.
