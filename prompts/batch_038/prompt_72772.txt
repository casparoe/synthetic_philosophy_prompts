so I've been chewing on the Chinese Room for ages and I cooked up a variant that I don't think any of Searle's own replies cover, and I want an essay about it that actually picks a side.

The setup: instead of one guy in a room with a rulebook, there's a coordinator in an office somewhere in Reykjavik. He receives questions written in English. An ordinary piece of software chops each one into roughly a billion tiny symbol jobs, each of which is a single lookup of the form "given this squiggle, return that squiggle." Every job gets farmed out over the internet to a different human being, and every one of those people is a fluent speaker who does their lookup with complete understanding of what they're up to — they know it's a fragment of a translation, they know what their little piece means, some find it tedious, one guy in Osaka is doing it to pay for his kid's tuition. Nobody anywhere is shuffling symbols blindly. The outputs get recombined by the same dumb software and handed back as an answer.

The stipulations that matter: (i) no leaf worker ever sees more than their own fragment, so nobody in the whole arrangement has the conversation; (ii) there's no feedback at all, nobody ever learns whether their bit was right or wrong, so no learning happens anywhere in the system; (iii) over a year of correspondance the thing is indistinguishable from a witty, self-aware English speaker, including when asked about its own inner life.

What I want answered: does this system understand English, and is the answer the same as for the original room or has the case actually changed? I keep getting pulled in two directions. On one hand every cognitive act in the entire thing is performed by somebody who genuinely understands what they're doing — there's no ignorant symbol-manipulation left to point at, and that ignorance seemed to be the engine of the original argument. On the other hand it's the same arrangement of ink as before, just spread out and staffed by people who each understand something other than the conversation.

I know it's adjacent to that old thought experiment where a whole country's population simulates the firings of a single brain, and I know the stock "the system understands, not the man" move. Don't just recycle either of them at me — the second one in particular doesn't seem to engage the fact that here the leaves aren't clueless about meaning in the first place.

Write it as an essay with a real argument and land on a verdict. "It's complicated" and "both sides have a point" aren't acceptable landing spots, though "understanding comes in degrees and here's roughly where this sits, and here's why the scale behaves that way" is, if you can defend it. And whichever way you go, pay your debts: if you say it does understand, tell me what the man-in-the-room case was getting wrong that made it look decisive; if you say it doesn't, tell me what work the fact that every worker understands their own part is doing in your account, because it can't be allowed to just be decoration.
