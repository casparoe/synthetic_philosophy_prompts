I am in the final year of a philosophy of history course, and for the last six weeks our seminar has been circling the same standoff: Ranke's founding formula for the historian's task, Carr's attack on it, Elton's counter-attack on Carr, and Hayden White's more radical claim underneath both. Each week the discussion reproduces the same pattern — someone invokes the historian's ineliminable selectivity, someone else replies that selectivity is not the same as invention, and nobody gains ground. What puzzles me is that the two supposed sceptics are not obviously on the same side, and I suspect the impasse is not one disagreement but several wearing one label.

To be concrete about the positions as we have read them. Ranke's slogan, from the preface to his 1824 history of the Latin and Teutonic peoples, is usually rendered as showing the past as it actually was, though the key adverb is itself contested between "actually" and "essentially." Carr distinguishes facts of the past from facts of history, holding that the latter are constituted by the historian's selection and by the concerns of the historian's own age, while insisting he is no relativist and that history is a continuous dialogue with the facts. Elton answers that this dissolves the authority of the document and reduces historical knowledge to the historian's temperament. White, working at a different level, arranges the historical work into a chronicle, then a story, then a mode of emplotment, argument, and ideological implication, and denies that real events present themselves with beginnings, middles, and ends; on his account narrative form is not found in the past but conferred on it, and the choice among modes of emplotment is ultimately aesthetic and moral rather than cognitive.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the standoff is at least three disagreements tangled together. First, a quasi-verbal one over "objectivity" — whether it means freedom from perspective or answerability to evidence and corrigibility by shared standards, on which reading Carr and Elton largely agree. Second, a dispute about the unit of assessment, since Carr's claims are mostly about particular causal and explanatory judgments while White's are about the narrative whole. Third, a reference-class problem: White's test cases are nineteenth-century grand narratives and philosophies of history, whereas Carr's and Elton's are archival political and administrative history, so each camp's paradigm cases may predetermine its verdict. And a further wrinkle — Carr and White may disagree more sharply with each other than either does with Ranke.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict and not a survey. Which of these strands, or what else entirely, is doing the work in keeping the debate stuck? Are the Elton–Carr exchange and the Carr–White difference the same kind of disagreement? Is there any consideration that could bear decisively on White's claim that narrative form is absent from events themselves, or is that claim not the sort of thing that could be settled by evidence? Given your diagnosis, say what would count as progress — a distinction, a change of question, a test case, a way of splitting "objectivity" — or argue that none should be expected and why. Please reconstruct each side's argument in your own words rather than quoting at length, and please do not append a reading list.
