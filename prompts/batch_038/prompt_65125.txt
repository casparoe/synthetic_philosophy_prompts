In three weeks I give a twenty-five-minute paper at a one-day workshop jointly run by a centre for the history of early modern philosophy and a value-theory reading group. I want a question-period prep sheet, not a defence of the paper.

The thesis. I argue that in her private treatise on happiness — the text never intended for publication, printed only after her death, now usually called the Discours sur le bonheur — Émilie du Châtelet does not recommend illusion as a means to pleasure. Illusion is constitutive. On my reading, the varnish she wants us to thicken is what makes the objects of the passions appear as the goods they must appear as for a passion to grip at full strength; remove it and you do not get the same pleasure more reliably, you get no great pleasure at all. I take her to say so when she concedes that even if the passions make more people wretched than happy they are still to be desired, "parce que c'est la condition sans laquelle on ne peut avoir de grands plaisirs" — because it is the condition without which one cannot have great pleasures. My second pillar is that this does not contradict her demand that we shed our prejudices: the two kinds of false belief differ in role. Prejudice is inherited, general, and hostile both to virtue and to the agent's own passions; illusion is cultivated, local, and passion-supporting. Her doctrine is therefore a selective dispensation from truth — undeceived about the frame of one's life, deliberately over-endowed with appearance inside it.

The slide sentence, in the original and in my own translation:

"Il faut pour être heureux, s'être défait des préjugés ; … être susceptible d'illusion ; car nous devons la plupart de nos plaisirs à l'illusion, et malheureux est celui qui la perd. Loin donc de chercher à la faire disparoître par le flambeau de la raison, tâchez d'épaissir le vernis qu'elle met sur la plupart des objets." (To be happy one must have shed one's prejudices; … be susceptible of illusion, for we owe most of our pleasures to illusion, and wretched is the one who loses it. Far from seeking to make it disappear by the torch of reason, try to thicken the varnish it lays over most objects.)

The audience, as precisely as I can describe it. Two senior historians of early modern philosophy, both contextualists: one works on Du Châtelet's natural philosophy and will want to know why the moral text can be read apart from her physics, the other thinks that importing vocabulary like "constitutive" and "epistemic dispensation" into an unpublished eighteenth-century document is a category mistake. One scholar of Enlightenment French literature who treats the text as a private, unfinished thing written only for what she calls "gens du monde," and who will resist treating it as argument at all. The respondent is a Kantian moral philosopher who will ask whether a view that makes error a condition of the good life is an ethics or a therapy for the comfortable. One philosopher of mind who works on self-deception and will press the paradox that one cannot intend to acquire a belief one takes to be false. Two analytic graduate students who will want the claim disambiguated — descriptive or normative, constitutive of the pleasure or of the appearance of the object — and will ask whether hedonism plus illusion collapses into the familiar objection that a deceived life feels no worse from the inside. One feminist scholar of the period who will say my reading launders a document written by a woman with few options into a general epistemology, and that the counsel to be content with one's station is what her circumstances forced on her rather than a discovery about the mind.

What I need back:

- The eight or so objections this room will actually raise, ordered by the damage they would do to the thesis rather than by the order they will arrive in, each attributed to the kind of person who raises it and phrased in their register — the contextualist will not put a worry the way a value theorist does.
- For each: a reply deliverable in ninety seconds; the one concession I should make in my first sentence; and an honest note on where the reply is soft, that is, the point at which a determined questioner pushes a second time and I have nothing left.
- Separate the objections that target the thesis from those that target the status of the text (private, unfinished, posthumous, addressed only to the well-off). For the second group, draft the single sentence I should say in my first three minutes to head them off, and tell me what to do if they return anyway.
- Name the question I should be hoping nobody asks. My candidate is the self-deception paradox; my fear is that the worse one is biographical — that the text is an apology for her own situation and my constitutive-illusion thesis a rationalization of it. Say whether you agree, and give me the least bad answer to either.
- If I must abandon one pillar on the day, say which, and what the thesis becomes without it. I have not properly worked out where her line between prejudice and the social proprieties falls, and I need to know whether the thesis survives if that distinction is not in the text as I have assumed.

No bibliography, reading list, or citations: I cannot check them offline and I am not asking for them. Work from the passage above and from what my thesis commits me to. For each objection, state how confident you are that this is what that questioner will actually press, and whether that confidence comes from knowing this kind of room or from a guess about how the rest of her text reads beyond the passage I have quoted. Close by singling out the point in your own analysis you are least sure of, including anywhere you suspect I have mis-stated what she says.
