You are a moral philosopher with serious expertise in both African relational ethics and the day-to-day practice of clinical ethics consultation. Please think the problem through carefully before writing: begin with a short brainstorming section, no more than 350 words, in which you identify the three or four points at which a relational account of personhood and a standard autonomy-plus-best-interests consultation protocol genuinely come apart, and decide for each of those points whether the relational content must survive the translation into procedure or can be dropped as unusable. Then write the deliverable.

The setting. I chair the clinical ethics consultation service at a large provincial public hospital. We receive roughly fifteen requests a month, almost all concerning patients who cannot decide for themselves: who speaks for them, what counts as their interest, and when treatment may be limited or withdrawn. A consultation is carried out by a rotating pair — a physician and a senior nurse — neither of whom has any philosophy training. The pair has about forty-five minutes between the bedside, the corridor, and a telephone call to a relative who is in another province, after which it must write a note in the chart. That note goes into the permanent record, families sometimes act on it for years, and two of our cases have already been threatened with litigation. The pair signs; I countersign any note recommending limitation or withdrawal of treatment; final clinical authority rests with the treating team. Assume the law in our jurisdiction permits the treating team to rely on family members as substitute decision-makers but does not require it, and does not recognise advance directives as binding.

The problem with our policy. The policy these consultations are supposed to follow opens with a line I drafted, and it is the line causing the trouble: the team must act so as to sustain the patient's standing in the web of relations for as long as that standing can be sustained. Everyone who reads it agrees it sounds right; nobody can tell me what to do with it on a Tuesday night. I want a decision procedure that gives it operational content — or, if you conclude that it cannot be given content without quietly substituting a capacity threshold for a relational one, I want you to say so plainly and draft a replacement line, explaining what the replacement loses.

The material you have to work with. Two strands of relational personhood are live in our policy discussions, and the procedure must say which one it is operationalising at each step, or must keep them explicitly separate: first, a graded or achieved view, on which personhood is something one grows into through participation in communal life and can hold more or less fully, and can on the harsh reading lapse; second, a constitutive view, on which what makes someone a person is that they stand in relations of care, recognition and shared identity with others, so that personhood is a property of the relational network rather than of the individual's internal capacities. The standing objection to the first strand is that it licenses excluding people who cannot reciprocate — the very old, people with advanced dementia, people with severe intellectual disabilities, the socially isolated — and your procedure has to declare whether it inherits that objection and, if so, at exactly which step. There is also an administrative fact you should treat as a hazard rather than a resource: our chart already contains a field, used until now purely for logistics, recording who visits and how often. Decide whether that field may enter the procedure as evidence, and if it may, build in the guard against the obvious abuse, which is visit frequency becoming a proxy for how much of a person someone is.

What I need, in this order:

1. The procedure itself: a sequence of numbered questions the pair works through in order, each with the evidence that answers it, the threshold for a sufficient answer, what the procedure does when the question cannot be answered, and the specific failure mode of that question. Mark every irreducible judgment call with a dagger and every point at which the question leaves the pair and goes to me or to the treating team with an (H). Include a stop rule for when the procedure terminates in a recommendation, and a two-question fast path for the emergency case where there is no time to run it.

2. Explicit handling of the situations that break our current practice: relatives who disagree with one another; relatives who have been absent for years and now arrive; the patient with no relatives and no visitors at all; a previously expressed wish, written or reported second-hand, and how it fares against a view that treats a person's own choices as themselves products of relationships.

3. A one-page bedside form: the questions in order with a field beside each, in plain language a tired nurse can use without reading anything else.

4. Three worked applications, 300 to 400 words each, ending with the recommendation the pair would write and the single sentence they would have to say to the family:
   - A 74-year-old woman with advanced dementia and no written directive. Four adult children; three want continued antibiotics and assisted feeding, and the fourth — the daughter who has been the daily caregiver for six years — reports that her mother said repeatedly she did not want to be kept alive in that state. The family insists she is fully present because they still speak to her, bring her to gatherings, and name her in their prayers.
   - A 41-year-old man in a minimally conscious state after a road-traffic collision. He was estranged from his siblings for twenty years and they will not engage; two people visit weekly, a former neighbour and a member of his church. The treating team wants to withdraw dialysis; the two visitors object.
   - A 58-year-old man with a catastrophic brain injury, ventilated, where the family accepts that he will not recover but asks for three more days because an uncle must travel from another province and certain rites must be performed before he is allowed to die. Their claim is that his standing in the web of relations is not finished at the point of biological death.

Do not rig the procedure to produce answers I will like. I am a committed defender of patient self-determination and I expect a faithful operationalisation of relational personhood to overrule me somewhere; show me the step at which that happens and do not soften it. At least one of the three applications should end with a recommendation I will oppose.

Close with a section titled what this procedure cannot do, naming at least three specific ways it will misfire in our hands, and one thing it systematically gets wrong that no procedure of this kind could get right.

Constraints: no bibliography, no reading list, no citations, no statute numbers, and no invented references of any kind — refer to positions and arguments, never to works. Do not spend words summarising the theory beyond what the procedure needs, and do not deliver the familiar four-principle consultation protocol with relational vocabulary sprayed over it; if you find that is all the material supports, say so instead. Write in clear markdown, formal but unadorned, and keep the whole response to what a working clinician would actually read: roughly 1,800 to 2,500 words.
