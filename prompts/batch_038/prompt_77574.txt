I sit on my hospital's ethics committee. We've got a clinical ethicist on it full time — she does the consults, she knows the case law cold, she's better at this than any of us. Last month we were arguing over whether to keep honoring an advance directive that the family insists the patient walked back verbally, and one of the attendings cut accross her with "there's no such thing as moral expertise," and the committee went with him. Then two weeks later the same guy reached for the exact same line to argue the opposite kind of thing — that we shouldn't bother reasoning a case through at all, just go with whatever the room's gut says. Now we're rewriting the charter and I have to specify how much weight her judgment carries, including whether we're ever allowed to take her word for something without making her argue it out in front of us.

I keep feeing like there are at least three different claims wearing that one sentence. Can you pull them apart into a menu — is it a claim that there are no moral facts to be expert about, or that nobody's moral judgments are reliably better than anyone else's, or that there's no track record to check in a domain where you never get feedback, or that even real moral knowledge can't be handed over by testimony, or that acting on borrowed moral judgment is second-best even when it lands on the right answer, or just that there's no such credential? For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude, a substantive live claim, or flatly false, and then say which ones actually license what the attending did in each of the two cases — I suspect they aren't the same ones. And is there any reading under which we can both overrule her and still want her on the committee? Give me something list-shaped I can bring to the charter meeting.
