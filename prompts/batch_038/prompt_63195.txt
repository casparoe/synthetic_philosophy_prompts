You're a philosopher of mind who has spent real time on both sides of the consciousness debate — you can press the illusionist line and the introspection-realist line each at their strongest — and you're also at home in the empirical work on introspective failure. Think carefully about this one; it's the kind of argument where the first plausible-sounding exit is usually the wrong one.

I teach a graduate seminar on consciousness and every year a student brings in something like what follows. This time it's mine, which is worse. It's supposed to show that illusionism about phenomenal consciousness isn't false so much as unsayable — and then it keeps going and lands somewhere I really can't live. I can't find the exit.

Setup: call a cognitive process **single-window** if the only route by which its subject can access the states it represents is the process itself. Perception isn't single-window — I can look at the table, touch it, photograph it, ask you about it. Introspection arguably is.

1. Introspection is single-window. I have no way of getting at my own present experience except by introspecting it; there is no second window onto my own states.
2. If a process is single-window, its subject cannot compare its deliverances with the states they are deliverances of, and therefore cannot assess whether those deliverances are accurate. You can't check an instrument using nothing but its own readout.
3. Where accuracy can't be assessed, the only attitude left is to take the deliverances at face value.
4. So from the first-person point of view introspective deliverances are unrevisionable: what introspection tells me about my own experience cannot be shown, to me, to have been wrong.
5. Illusionism says that introspection's headline deliverance — that my states have intrinsic, non-functional, directly presented phenomenal qualities — is systematically untrue, and that the truth is merely that introspection represents such qualities (quasi-phenomenally) when they aren't there.
6. By (4), that deliverance is unrevisionable from the inside; and from the outside no third-person evidence reaches anyone's seemings at all, only behaviour and brains.
7. So illusionism is unreachable from either side. From the first person it asks me to overrule a deliverance that (4) has already put beyond overruling. From the third person it makes a claim about seemings that no third-person evidence can speak to.
8. And the damage generalises. By the same reasoning, no experiment could ever have demonstrated introspective error. All the confabulation, unnoticed-change, and unreported-intention results show at most that a subject's report conflicted with the world or with the subject's own behaviour; they never show that the subject's introspection misdescribed the subject's experience. And whenever someone's introspection does get corrected, the correction arrives through a window that isn't theirs.
9. But it is a plain fact that people sometimes find out that their own introspection was wrong. That's the datum the entire introspective-error literature rests on, and illusionism rests on that literature.
10. So either (1)–(4) go, or (9) goes. And if (2) goes, we have lost the claim that you can't check an instrument through its own readout — which sounds like a platitude about measurement, and which is as far as I can tell the only reason anyone ever found (1) interesting in the first place.

Where does this go wrong? There are two possibilities and I honestly don't know which I'm in: either something slips between steps — an equivocation, or a step that's valid only on a reading the earlier ones don't license — or the argument is valid and my only job is to pick which plausible premise costs least. Don't assume its the first kind. If you think it's valid and I should bite a bullet, tell me which bullet and make the case that it's actually chewable.

Before you diagnose anything, do a short brainstorming pass: go through the the steps and list every place you think something might be hiding, one line each, saying what the suspicion is. Don't rank or evaluate them at that stage. Then pick the step you think actually breaks it and work that out properly — say precisely what the step assumes, why that assumption looked innocent when I wrote it down, and what the argument looks like once the step is repaired or dropped. Then price the alternatives: what does the illusionist lose by giving up each of (1), (2), (3) and (4) separately, and which of those losses is smallest.

One further thing I want settled. Whatever repair you propose, tell me whether it's available to the subject or only to the psychologist standing outside. If it's only available to the psychologist, then I'm apparently committed to the claim that I can never in principle discover that my own introspection was wrong while other people can discover it for me — and I want to know whether that asymmetry is something you can live with, or whether it just hands step 8 back to me.

For what it's worth, I'd rather keep (2) than (9). The instrument point feels like common sense and (9) feels like the whole evidential basis of the debate. But I know my preference isn't evidence, so push back on it if you think the instinct is wrong.
