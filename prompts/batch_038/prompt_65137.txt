I'm helping run an internal reading group at my lab and we've spent the last few weeks on the debate-based approaches to alignment (the Irving/Christiano/Amodei line, plus the stuff that grew out of it), and there's this one move everybody in the group keeps making that I've started to find suspicious. Whenever the obfuscated-arguments problem comes up — the worry that a superhuman model can win a debate by producing something the judge can't actually check — somebody always says the fix is a better judge: amplify the judge, decompose the question, let the two debaters cross-examine each other so the flaws get surfaced for free. Basically "improve per-step judging reliability and the problem goes away." I want the strongest possible case that this is wrong, and I want it argued as a positive thesis, not as a shrug.

Here's the framing I want you to work inside, because it's my own and I don't think I've seen it stated this way anywhere. Say a step of an argument is *locally checkable* if the judge can assess it using only that step's stated premises plus the judge's own background, without re-deriving anything from earlier in the chain. Say the *serial depth* of an argument is the length of the longest chain of steps where checking step n requires the judge to have independently re-derived the conclusion of step n-1. My claim is that obfuscation is fundamentally a serial-depth phenomenon, not a per-step-reliability phenomenon: in a serially deep argument the model's cost of *generating* the chain grows roughly linearly in the number of steps, but the judge's cost of *verifying* it grows superlinearly — up to quadratic, because at each step the judge has to reconstruct upstream results they couldn't retain — so the asymmetry that debate is supposed to exploit (checking is cheaper than searching) inverts. If that's right, then anything that improves the judge's reliability *at a step* only changes the constant in front of the exponent, while the adversary can always buy more depth with the same budget. The real fixes would have to be structural constraints on argument shape: depth caps, forced parallelization, requiring every lemma to carry a warrant that's checkable without the surrounding chain, and so on.

So: argue for that thesis. I want the mechanism spelled out carefully — why the verification cost actually compounds rather than just adds, and why a debater who is *trying* to obfuscate can always find the depth that beats a given judge budget — and then I want you to face at least three objections head on rather than gesturing at them: (a) the opposing debater does the checking for free, since cross-examination converts verification back into search; (b) real-world obfuscation is mostly rhetorical, exploiting fluency, length, confidence and the judge's biases, so the structural story is aimed at the wrong target; (c) human judges never verify every step of anything anyway, they use heuristics and deference to sources, and that works fine in science and law, so the "quadratic cost" complaint proves too much. End by telling me what observation or result would make me give the thesis up — a genuine falsifier, not "more research is needed." Don't hedge into a both-sides conclusion, and don't bother with references or citations, I'll fill those in myself.
