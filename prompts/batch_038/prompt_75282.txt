I attended a panel on the end of the eastern Mediterranean palatial systems around 1200 BCE, and I am now trying to write up the part of the exchange I found least persuasive, which was the analogies. Two of them did real argumentative work, and they were deployed in support of nearly opposite conclusions.

The first speaker characterized the Late Bronze Age palace economies as a highly leveraged investment bank, and offered the mapping himself: the palace is the firm; its storerooms, workshops, and rations obligations are its positions; interregional exchange in copper, tin, textiles, and prestige goods is interbank exposure; the scribal bureaucracy is the back office keeping the ledger; a run of bad harvests, seismic events, and raiding is the shock that a thinly capitalized institution cannot absorb. On his account the systems were optimized for throughput rather than redundancy — the condition sometimes called hypercoherence — and so a disturbance anywhere propagated everywhere. He used this to defend the word *collapse* in something like Tainter's sense, a rapid and substantial loss of an established level of sociopolitical complexity, and then drew an explicit moral for the present: our own tightly coupled logistics networks are the same instrument at the same leverage, and the lesson is to buy redundancy we will probably never use.

The second speaker rejected the whole framing. The palace, she said, is not a firm but a landlord: an extractive layer sitting on top of villages that fed it in kind. When the great halls burned, the tenants went on ploughing; what ended was the collection of rent, not the farming. On her account nothing collapsed, someone merely stopped being paid, and the vocabulary of collapse is inherited from the one class in these societies that kept written records — it is an artifact of whose paperwork survived, not a fact about how anyone lived.

I find myself unable to endorse either, but I cannot yet say precisely why, and I want the reasons separated rather than merged. Three features of the evidence seem to me to complicate both mappings, and I would like each of them run through both analogies explicitly:

First, the clay tablets of the palace archives were unfired; they survive because the conflagrations that destroyed the buildings baked them. The documentary record of the collapse is thus a physical product of the collapse, and its content is overwhelmingly account-keeping — inventories, allocations, rations, land holdings — rather than testimony.

Second, at Hattusa the fire appears to have come late: the site was shrunk and progressively abandoned over decades as the imperial administration withdrew, and agricultural communities with a materially distinct culture were living in the ruins afterward. The people who inherit the site are not the people who worked it.

Third, a recent re-audit of the destruction layers traditionally dated to around 1200 BCE concluded that a majority of the sites on the standard lists are misdated, thinly evidenced, or were never destroyed at all.

What I would like, then: a table of correspondences for each analogy; the disanalogies ranked by how much work they do against the conclusion actually drawn, with the merely decorative ones marked as such; an account of what each source domain quietly imports that the target lacks — a proprietor who suffers the loss, a balance sheet with a threshold below which the entity is simply dead, a decision procedure that could have chosen more slack, a tenant body continuous across the event; a verdict on each of the three claims in play, namely that the collapse was systemic, that it was bad for the people who lived through it, and that it licenses a redundancy policy today; and a judgment on whether the two analogies can be used together by one author or whether they cancel. If some third source domain does the work better, say what it would have to be able to represent — I suspect it must be able to hold systemic loss and class-indexed gain in a single picture, and to explain why our evidence for the event was manufactured by the event — and say what it would still obscure. Please resist the reflex that every analogy is merely limited; I want the load-bearing failures identified, and I want to be told if one of these two speakers is simply right.
