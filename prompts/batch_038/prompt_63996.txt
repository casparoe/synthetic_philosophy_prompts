I spent four decades in experimental physics, much of it designing measurements for processes that could not be observed directly, and since retiring I have turned to the philosophy of history — and so, inevitably, to the argument over who was responsible for the war of 1914. Two camps have been talking past each other since Fritz Fischer, and I should like to know what they could actually sign together.

Camp A, in Fischer's tradition: the assurance Berlin gave Vienna on 5–6 July 1914 — that Germany would stand by Austria-Hungary whatever followed from a move against Serbia — was a calculated gamble that made a continental war likely rather than merely possible; the September memorandum of war aims, drawn up by the Chancellor's private secretary, shows where that gamble was heading; and it is proper to say that Germany, as a state, bears a distinctly larger share of the responsibility.

Camp B, the distributionists: the July crisis was a cascade generated in every capital by mobilization timetables, alliance commitments, and officer corps that had come to assume war was inevitable; Berlin's conduct was reckless but not different in kind from anyone else's; and the September memorandum, written more than a month after the war had begun and amid apparent victory, cannot be read backwards as evidence of prior intention.

Please draft the joint statement an adversarial collaboration between these camps ought to produce. I would want them to face three questions that strike me as inference problems rather than historical ones. First: under what conditions can a record produced after an outcome license inference about the dispositions that preceded it? Camp A must be relying on some stability assumption — name it, and say whether it is available in this case. Second: responsibility ascriptions ought to be counterfactually sensitive, in that the outcome should depend on the choice. For which of the great powers was the 1914 outcome genuinely dependent on that power's decisions, and for which was it so over-determined that a different choice would have changed little? Third: does it matter to the ascription whether Berlin intended a continental war, or merely chose a course whose likely consequence it accepted while hoping to localize the conflict?

Format it as a numbered section of claims both camps can sign — including claims about the evidential standing of those two documents, and about what holding a nation responsible can and cannot mean — followed by a table of the residual disagreements. Each row should give the crux; its statement in wording Camp A would accept as its own; its statement in wording Camp B would accept as its own; the evidence or argument both agree bears on it; and each camp's prediction, recorded in advance, of what that evidence will turn out to show. End by saying which of the disputes you judge to be merely verbal, and which single crux, if settled, would actually move one side.

No references or reading lists, please — I have the books. What I want is formulations neither camp could dismiss as a caricature.
