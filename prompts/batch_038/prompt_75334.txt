Last night I spent the better part of three hours in a pub back room arguing with a friend about whether Nozick's tracking account really contains two modal conditions or one, and we got nowhere. He maintains that "tracking" is simply sensitivity — that had the proposition been false, the subject would not have believed it — and that adherence, the converse requirement that had the proposition been true the subject would have believed it, is decorative scaffolding that does no independent work; I maintain that adherence is load-bearing, that there are ordinary cases in which sensitivity and adherence come apart, and that his version of the theory silently collapses into a single-condition view with different advertising. Somewhere in the middle of the argument we both realised we were also disagreeing about two further things without having noticed: whether the closure of knowledge under known entailment is a principle to be preserved at all costs or a prejudice to be diagnosed, and how finely a belief-forming method may be individuated before the modal conditions become trivially satisfiable. Neither of us could say which of these disputes was downstream of which, and that is precisely what I would like your help with.

I co-run an informal epistemology reading group and I want to build a sorting instrument — part decision tree, part diagnostic quiz — that establishes where each member actually stands on this cluster of questions, by asking them about cases rather than about labels. Ten questions, each presenting a concrete scenario in four or five sentences and offering two or three possible verdicts, phrased so that a reader who has never encountered the terms "sensitivity", "adherence", "closure" or "relevant alternatives" can answer honestly without guessing which position each answer feeds. Not every question should be reached by every respondent: the structure must be genuinely conditional, so that, for instance, the question about how the modal condition is to be formulated is only posed to those who have already said that knowledge survives the sceptical scenario, and you should tell me explicitly, for each question, which earlier answer makes it live. I don't mind if two or three of the questions are recognisable cousins of the canonical cases — the hands case, the painted mule, the county full of convincing façades, the stopped clock — provided they are re-parameterised so that a member who has memorised the standard verdicts cannot simply recite them, and provided that at least five are entirely new to the literature as far as you can tell. Please work through the following four axes, and make each of them the pivot of at least one question: whether closure under known entailment holds for knowledge; whether the operative modal condition is one of avoiding error in the counterfactual situation where the belief is false, of securing belief in the counterfactual situation where it is true, of both, or of neither; whether the set of alternatives the subject must be able to rule out is fixed by her objective situation or by what has been raised in conversation; and how finely methods are to be individuated before sensitivity is satisfied by anything.

Four of my own cases must appear, though you may polish the wording. First: Nia believes her son is in the house because she can hear him practising scales in the front room; had he in fact been out, her partner would have told her he was in to spare her a futile search, and she would have believed it all the same — does she know, and does the answer change if we describe her method narrowly as hearing him play rather than broadly as forming beliefs about his whereabouts? Second: Mared believes she has two hands; had she been a handless consciousness fed a simulation of a body, she would have believed it anyway, and yet in every nearby situation in which she forms that belief on that basis she does have two hands. Third: a conservation team has dated a panel by tree-ring analysis, the test is sound and the panel is genuine, and next month a rival laboratory will report a conflicting result that is in fact contaminated — is the team entitled, now and in advance, to discount that future report? Fourth: Sayed knows he is sitting in his kitchen, knows that if he is sitting in his kitchen he is not a disembodied consciousness being fed a simulated kitchen, and yet, when asked directly, denies that he knows he is not such a consciousness — which of those three attitudes, if any, is the mistake? The leaves of your tree should number at least eight and must include: outright scepticism about the external world; a closure-denying view requiring both modal conditions; a closure-denying view requiring only the first; a view that keeps closure by making the avoidance of easy error the condition and treating knowledge as too basic to be analysed into components; a view on which the standards for "knows" shift with the attributor's context while closure holds within a context; a view on which the relevant alternatives are set by the objective situation rather than by conversation; a view that dissolves the whole dispute by insisting that method individuation, properly handled, delivers everything; and a view that rejects modal conditions altogether in favour of the character of the cognitive performance. Flag honestly any pair of leaves your ten questions cannot in fact separate, and say what further case would separate them; and note any combination of answers that is logically available but left empty, with a diagnosis of whether the corresponding position is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Please don't give me a bibliography or a list of references, and attach philosophers' names to positions only where the attribution is wholly uncontroversial — I would rather have a descriptive label than a guess.

Return the whole thing as a single JSON object with the following fields. "bottom_line": one or two sentences stating which of the four axes is genuinely prior to the others and where my friend and I were actually talking past one another. "questions": an array of objects, each with "order" (integer), "vignette" (the case as the respondent will read it), "options" (array of objects with "key", "verdict" and "what_this_commits_you_to"), "axis_tested", and "live_only_if" (the earlier answer that makes this question reachable, or "always"). "leaves": an array of objects with "id", "position_name", "answer_pattern" (the sequence of option keys that terminates here), "central_claim", "stance_on_closure", "modal_condition", "method_individuation", and "most_awkward_commitment".
