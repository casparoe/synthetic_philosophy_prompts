I cover climate and science-culture stuff for a magazine, and I'm working on a piece about why we're all so bad at thinking about personal responsibility for collective problems — the carbon-footprint-calculator angle, the "stop buying burgers to save the planet" angle, the whole genre. For the piece I read a chapter in a new essay collection by a pretty well-known writer who argues, essentially, that the individual-obligation view is dead and that anyone still pushing it is doing the oil industry's PR for them. My editor says there's a whole philosophy literature here — he called it "the inefficacy something-or-other" — and that I'll get shredded if I quote this guy approvingly without knowing it. I'm not trying to fact-check his numbers so much as audit his reasoning, because the numbers are roughly fine (two tonnes of CO2 for a return flight, ~40 billion tonnes a year globally, all in the right ballpark as far as I can tell) and it's the inferential jumps I can't evaluate. Here's the passage that's doing all the work in the chapter, cleaned up a bit from my notes but I've kept his order of claims:

"Consequentialism is supposed to be the grown-up ethics — the one that cares about outcomes instead of rules and feelings. So put the grown-up question to it: does your flight make the outcome worse? It does not. Two tonnes of CO2 vanishing into forty billion is not a difference the atmosphere can register. The same goes for the burger, and there's an even neater version of the point here: there is no number of chicken purchases at which buying one more causes a single extra bird to be raised and killed, so the individual threshold that the guilt-mongers appeal to is a fiction, and the philosophers who insist it's real are just doing arithmetic on a story they made up. Now notice what follows. If my act can't make things worse, it can't be wrong, because wrongness just is making things worse. So there's nothing to sell. And note who benefits from the confusion: an oil company spent real money teaching all of us to calculate our personal carbon footprints, which turned a structural problem into a private sin and left the actual decision-makers unmolested. The honest response isn't 'abstain,' it's 'organize.' Individual abstinence is a hobby, not a duty."

Can you go through this claim by claim and tell me what an ethicist who actually works on collective action problems would sign off on, what they'd wince at, and what is straightforwardly wrong? A few specific things I'm worried about. First: he slides from "my act almost certainly changes nothing" to "my act has no moral significance" — is that slide legitimate, or does the tiny-probability-of-a-big-effect structure do something he's ignoring? Second: the chicken/threshold bit. My vague memory of a podcast about this is that the threshold problem is a real and hard thing, but that at least one well-known philosopher uses it to argue the opposite of what this guy says he uses it for — is that right, and if so how badly does he mangle it? Third: he treats "wrongness just is making things worse" as if it were what consequentialists say; I have a feeling that's a specific and contestable version of the view rather than the view, and that there are also non-consequentialist reasons to abstain (fairness to people who are abstaining, free-riding, what participating in a practice means) that his argument never touches. Fourth: is the supply-chain story he's implicitly relying on for chicken — the buffers, the oversupply, the rounding-up at the distributor — even the right model to import into aviation emissions, or is he moving a premise between two very different causal structures without saying so?

And then two follow-ons. The last section of the chapter sweeps voting in with the same logic, and I think that's where it's weakest — there was that Virginia statehouse race a few years back that ended in an exact tie and got settled by pulling a name out of a ceramic bowl, so "one vote never matters" seems obviously false in a way that "one flight never matters" isn't, and I suspect the disanalogy has something to do with how the probabilities and the decision-making are structured rather than with any moral difference. Is that intuition of mine any good? Finally, tell me what survives. I don't want to write a piece that says the whole essay is bogus, because I'm pretty sure the carbon-footprint-marketing point is correct and important and the "organize" conclusion might well be right even if the argument for it is bad. So: give me the strongest version of his argument in a few sentences — the one a sympathetic philosopher would actually defend — and tell me which of his conclusions still stand once the weak parts are removed, and which ones only looked supported because of them. I need to be able to defend whatever I print to a hostile reader who knows this literature better than I do.
