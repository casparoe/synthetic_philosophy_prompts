I'm a third-year doctoral student in biostatistics, and for the past two years I've rented a flat with Nadia, who works nights in a surgical ICU. Neither of us is a philosopher, though I audit a reading group in the philosophy department on the ethics of markets, and Nadia has been reading along with it — aggressively — since she got hold of a battered copy of *A Theory of Justice* during the pandemic. Last month her hospital gave out its annual excellence-in-patient-care award: a plaque, a mention in the staff newsletter, and a $2,000 bonus. Nadia was one of three recipients and turned it down in writing, attaching a two-page statement that has since been circulating among the nursing staff and has made her something of a local cause célèbre. When I asked her to explain herself over breakfast, she produced the argument below, which she says isn't originally hers — she takes its shape from Rawls, and from the reading group's session on Feinberg's account of desert bases — but which she maintains is airtight:

(1) If a person deserves Y on the basis of X, then she deserves X.
(2) Everything that actually serves as the basis of the desert claims we make — talent, intelligence, stamina, conscientiousness, the capacity to defer gratification, and the effort these make possible — is the joint product of a genetic endowment and an upbringing that the person neither chose nor did anything to earn.
(3) No one deserves what she did nothing to bring about and could not have avoided having.
(4) So no one deserves her own effort or her own character.
(5) So, by (1), no one deserves anything held in virtue of effort or character.
(6) Salaries, grades, promotions, fellowships, prizes, honors, and professional standing are all conferred in virtue of effort and character; that is exactly why we call them rewards rather than transfers.
(7) So no one deserves her salary, her degree, her fellowship, her prize, or her standing.
(8) When institutions distribute scarce benefits on a basis that nobody actually satisfies, and distribute them unequally on that basis, the resulting pattern is unjust.
(9) So essentially every reward institution in a society like ours is unjust, and a just distribution has to be governed by something other than desert — need, say, or the position of the worst off.

Two things bother me about her position, and neither is the sort of thing I know how to press. The first is that on the very evening she walked me through this, she spent twenty minutes describing a colleague who had presented her audit data at a regional conference under his own name, and she was still visibly angry; when I asked whether he deserved that anger, she said obviously he did, and that this was a different matter entirely because it concerned something he did rather than something he was born with. Given (2) and (3) I cannot see how that distinction is available to her, and she could not say why it is. The second is that her two-page refusal is, on her own account, an act that merits nothing, since she no more originated her own scrupulousness than her colleague originated his opportunism — and yet I have watched her check the nurses' group chat every morning for three weeks, and she plainly wants the admiration. There is also a smaller irritation I can't shake: my advisor's fellowship letter says that I am diligent, and since reading her statement I haven't been able to decide whether that sentence is now false, unjust, neither, or both.

So — which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost? I would most like to keep (3); without something like it I don't know what the word deserves could be doing at all. If it turns out that (9) can be preserved by giving up something cheaper than (3), I would take that deal, since I think she is roughly right about what the awards system looks like from the inside. But I want the diagnosis to be specific rather than a general lecture on moral luck. Tell me which step fails and why it looked entirely innocent in context, as it did to me on first reading; lay out the main repairs on offer and price each one, including what each repair would leave of Nadia's decision to turn down the plaque and the money — I suspect some repairs make her refusal admirable, some make it merely tasteful, and some make it confused; and say explicitly whether whatever fixes the distributive argument also fixes the blame case, or whether she needs a separate argument for why her colleague deserved her anger. If you think part of the dispute is over what deserve means, say which sense each numbered step is trading on, and tell me whether the surviving sense is one that can still do the work she wants it to do in a grievance meeting with hospital management.
