In three weeks I give a twenty-five-minute paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on state legibility, followed by twenty minutes of questions and no slides on the podium. I do not want a defense of the paper; I want a Q&A prep sheet built around the room I will actually be in.

The thesis, compressed: citizens do not merely enjoy a permission to resist state visibility. Many of them are under a qualified duty to be hard to see. The duty runs to co-citizens and to future citizens, not to the state, and it is discharged through unglamorous conduct — encrypting correspondence, declining convenience-driven data collection, transacting in cash while cash still circulates, refusing app-mediated and loyalty-card intermediation, using disposable addresses and blocking scripts where these are available. Three claims carry it. First, retroactivity: a government can act only on records that exist, so the archive assembled under a liberal administration determines what a less liberal one can reconstruct after the fact, and today's legibility is therefore a gift to an unknown future recipient. Second, externality: each citizen's legibility lowers the marginal cost of the surveillance infrastructure that will eventually be aimed at everyone, which is why the nothing-to-have argument misfires — not because I have something to hide, but because my hiding is partly other people's business. Third, standing: a polity whose members are visible by default forfeits the position from which it could complain when the state treats visibility as the normal condition of citizenship. On my account the German constitutional holding that the individual should in principle decide when and within what limits facts about their private life are disclosed states not only a right against the state but the content of an obligation citizens owe one another, and the GDPR requirement that personal data be adequate, relevant, and limited to what is necessary is a rule addressed to processors that citizens must make effective by refusing to supply the surplus in the first place.

I limit the duty explicitly: it does not reach tax records, criminal procedure, public-health reporting, or anything a court has ordered; it never requires law-breaking; and it binds hardest on those for whom opacity is cheap.

The room is about thirty people in three blocs that do not share a vocabulary. Roughly a third are German and Dutch data-protection practitioners and lawyers, several of them in or adjacent to supervisory authorities, who think in legal bases, purpose limitation, impact assessments, and enforceability. Another third are American scholars of transparency, sunshine, and open government — some former requesters or investigative journalists — for whom the check on power runs through compelled disclosure by the state, and who regard citizen opacity as at best a private luxury and at worst a dodge of democratic accountability. The remainder are comparative scholars of Chinese governance and social-credit-style compliance systems, who are allergic to having their field of study used as a scare image for Western data practice. There is also one criminologist who works on organized crime and one former prosecutor.

Please produce the eight or nine objections this particular room will raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the paper if it landed unanswered — not the objections a privacy-conference crowd would ask, which I could assemble myself. For each objection: name the bloc most likely to press it; phrase it in that bloc's register and at its native level of specificity (a Dutch authority lawyer will not ask whether the view is extreme, but where the legal basis sits, who bears the cost of the recommended measures, and how a duty to minimize data squares with statutory retention and audit obligations); give me a reply deliverable aloud in under two minutes; and then state candidly where that reply is soft — which premise it smuggles, which empirical claim it cannot support, and which bloc will simply refuse it.

After the list, add three short sections. Which two objections should I concede in part during the opening, before anyone asks, and what exactly should the concession say? Which single reply has to land for the paper to survive the session? And what is the question I am hoping nobody asks — with one or two sentences on why that one, rather than any of the others, is the objection I have no answer to.
