ok so I need help writing a document that should already exist and doesn't, and I think you're the only place I can get it.

Context, sorry it's long. I sit on the advisory panel for a mid-sized city's wellbeing-budgeting pilot. There's a fixed pot of money being split between roughly two things: (a) a community chronic-pain management program and (b) a program for moderate depression, and they commissioned a survey outfit to put the two populations on a common scale so the split isn't just vibes. The outfit's instrument is a 0-10 life-satisfaction ladder plus anchoring vignettes — little descriptions of hypothetical people, low/medium/high on the thing being measured, that respondents also place on the same ladder, so you can detect that group A is using the scale differently from group B and correct for it.

And there's a result that has the panel at each other's throats. After vignette correction, the chronic-pain group rates *higher* than the depression group, even though every panel member independently agrees the pain condition is worse to live with. The pain patients have had it about 8 years on average; the depression cohort about 14 months. So whether adaptation means those people are genuinely doing better, or means their scale has drifted in a way the vignettes can't catch, is now load bearing for actual funding.

Two of us have been talking past each other for months and I'm pretty sure we're the two camps in the old interpersonal utility comparison fight, wearing civilian clothes.

Me: the comparisons are legitimate if you do the measurement work — vignette anchoring, experience sampling of moment-to-moment affect, tradeoff and trade-up tasks, whatever it takes. I'll grant that this particular instrument is bad. I won't grant that no instrument could be good, and I think "you can't compare utilities across people" has been used for most of the last century as a respectable-sounding excuse not to try.

Her: not a positivist exactly, more structural. She thinks a self-report number is indexed to the respondent's local comparison set and their own history, so a corrected number still isn't the kind of thing you can add or average across people — and she thinks the whole enterprise quietly smuggles in a utilitarian aggregation ethic that's doing all the real work while the survey data supplies the legitimacy.

Write the joint statement the two of us could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Specifically:

1. A numbered list of claims we can both sign — about what the panel's data does and doesn't license, about what adaptation is and isn't evidence of, about what a 0-10 ladder actually measures. Non-vacuous only: if a claim is one we'd both sign because it says nothing, cut it.

2. A disagreements table. For each live disagreement, the statement of it in wording I'd accept as a fair description of my own position, and the same disagreement in wording she'd accept for hers. 3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move one of us. For each, a concrete case, dataset, or test we'd both agree in advance bears on it, with each side's prediction written down before the result comes in. Include at least one crux where we predict in opposite directions about the same finding.

4. A section on how much of this is verbal. I suspect "utility" is doing at least three jobs in our argument — moment-to-moment hedonic state, disposition to endorse a scale point, all-things-considered normative ranking of a life — and that once you taboo the word she and I agree on more than we think. Then for each of us, an explicit line stating what we give up by signing it, and what specific finding would make us retract the signature.

And if a crux is unresolvable with data a city could realistically collect, say that plainly instead of inventing a study.
