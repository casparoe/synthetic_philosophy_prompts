I am a fourth-year doctoral student in philosophy of cognitive science, currently drafting the central chapter of my dissertation on whether adopting the intentional stance toward a system commits one to the reality of its beliefs. My supervisor, who is broadly sympathetic to Dennett's framework but resolutely deflationary about folk psychology, keeps steering the chapter toward a single illustration that he says disposes of the question: a belief, he insists, is like the center of gravity of an object — a move he regards as being in the spirit of Dennett's own talk of abstracta. Nobody supposes that a brick contains, over and above its mass distribution, some further item sitting at the center of gravity; the center of gravity is a way of summarizing that distribution, a calculational convenience. And yet the summary is not up for grabs: it picks out something that constrains what happens next, and the predictions it licenses come out right or wrong. His conclusion is that the quarrel between realists and eliminativists about belief is therefore a pseudo-debate — beliefs are exactly this species of well-behaved abstraction, both sides are arguing about nothing, and my chapter should be restructured around that result rather than around any argument of my own. When I objected that the illustration is drawn from rigid bodies whereas the systems I care about are deformable and self-reorganizing (people, octopuses, a model that has been fine-tuned into a substantially different set of dispositions), he replied that this is no difficulty at all, since one can define an instantaneous center of gravity at each moment, which is precisely analogous to a belief that changes over time. Two friends in my department have been drafted into the dispute: one thinks the supervisor is right and my chapter is wasting its time, the other thinks the whole framing is a category error and that neither side would accept the analogy if it were stated plainly.

Could you audit the analogy for me? First, an explicit statement of the correspondence: what in the target domain plays the role of the mass distribution, of the point that summarizes it, of the equations that make the summary predictive, of the decision to use the summary instead of the full distribution, and of the rigid body. Second, the disanalogies, separated into two piles — those that actually damage the intended use, which is to dissolve the realism debate, and those that are merely cosmetic. My suspicion is that the damaging ones concern the fact that a center of gravity is a determinate function of an already fully specified lower-level description, whereas the step from physical description to belief attribution is precisely what is contested, and the fact that a center of gravity is not interest-relative in the way a real pattern is supposed to be; I would like that suspicion tested rather than echoed back to me. Third, tell me whether the analogy may be cutting against my supervisor: if beliefs genuinely are like centers of gravity, they would seem to be real in the same unproblematic sense that centers of gravity are, which would make the illustration an argument for realism rather than for quietism — or is that a cheap trick, since the two sides do not mean the same thing by real? Fourth, consider whether the design stance is the better home for this kind of abstraction (a thermostat's set point strikes me as closer to the intended target than a center of gravity does), and whether the instantaneous-center-of-gravity reply about change actually works or simply relocates the problem to the question of what fixes the center at each moment.

Finally, if the analogy fails, propose a better source domain and say which of the three stances it tracks, and assess the two candidates I have been playing with — temperature in statistical mechanics, and a Nash equilibrium of a repeated game — noting what each would let my supervisor keep and what each would cost him. Flag anything about attributing beliefs to a trained language model that the audit should treat differently from attributing them to a person, since that is the case study my chapter ends with. I am not looking for references or a reading list; I want the analysis, and I would like the verdict stated plainly rather than hedged on both sides. Please write the response in American English.
