For four decades I measured heat capacities for a living, and I have been reading philosophy only since my retirement five years ago. I now convene a small monthly discussion group — mostly former scientists and engineers of my acquaintance, plus one philosophy postgraduate who tolerates us — and for the last four months we have been arguing about whether pleasures and pains are commensurable in the sense that aggregative ethics requires. The argument has stopped moving entirely, and I would like a diagnosis of why.

Please adopt the role of a professional philosopher whose work sits at the intersection of population ethics and the formal foundations of welfare measurement, and think carefully before answering. I want an analysis of the structure of our disagreement, not a survey of the positions and not a verdict on who is right. I should also ask you not to give me a bibliography, reading list, or citations of any kind: I am working offline, and a list of works I cannot consult is worse than useless to me.

Here is the group, and here is what each party actually says.

The economist (a comparativist) holds that badness comes in degrees on a single cardinal scale, and that for any harm, however appalling, some sufficiently large number of sufficiently similar but milder harms is worse. His test case is the dentist: ten thousand people each spared a momentary twinge, he says, can outweigh one person's hour in the chair under a failing anaesthetic, and anyone who denies this has confused the vividness of a case with its magnitude. He also presses the vagueness objection: if there is a threshold in badness above which no aggregation of lesser bads can reach, then where exactly is it, how is it identified, and what could possibly make it one notch higher rather than one notch lower?

The physician, who spent her career in palliative care, holds that some suffering is lexically bad: past a certain point it cannot be traded off at all, and no number of sub-threshold sufferings, however vast, outweighs a single instance of it. Her test case is the torture case: she says the economist's position, applied consistently, licenses sacrificing one person's worst day for a sufficiently dispersed mass of trivial comforts, and that this is not a counterintuitive consequence to be swallowed but a reductio of the scale itself. When asked to locate the threshold, she declines, and says the refusal is the point — some things are not on a scale.

The mathematician says we are both wasting our time: 'worse than' is simply not transitive across a spectrum of pains running from a year of torture down to a pinprick through steps no one can feel the difference between, so there is no threshold to posit and no common scale to deny. The economist's reply is that if transitivity goes, the mathematician is left with no ordering at all and cannot even state what he refuses to weigh.

My own hypothesis, which I offer as a hypothesis and expect you to test rather than flatter, is that the economist and the physician are answering different questions under a single heading, and that this is why nothing either says lands. In the laboratory I would never have asked whether a small temperature rise in a million beakers could outweigh a large rise in one: temperature is an intensive quantity and does not sum, whereas energy is extensive and does, and the question simply has no content until one says which structure the measurement has. I suspect something similar is happening here. Within a single life, psychophysics from Fechner to Stevens gives us a great deal of structure — smooth intensity continua, power laws, arguably ratio scales — and the economist reads that structure as settling the ethical question. But what the physician is denying is a property of the *interpersonal* ordering, something like the Archimedean condition: that any positive quantity of one person's bad, multiplied enough times, exceeds any other person's bad. Lexical views are just non-Archimedean orderings, and no amount of evidence about how finely a single subject can discriminate two pressures bears on whether the social ordering satisfies that axiom. If this is right, the vagueness objection and the reductio objection are not even addressing each other: one is a complaint about how the individual scale is partitioned, the other about how individual scales are combined.

So: is my hypothesis right, right in part, or wrong? And please address the worry I cannot dismiss, which is that importing measurement-theoretic concepts from calorimetry into ethics is a category error that merely dresses up an ethical disagreement in formal vocabulary.

Then commit to the diagnosis you judge best, and support it by pointing to what each party in the group actually says rather than to what such views are usually said to involve. Then tell me what would count as progress on that diagnosis: a distinction, an impossibility result, a new kind of case, a reformulation of the question, or something else — or argue honestly that no progress should be expected and explain why. Say explicitly whether you think our question is ill-posed, hard, or merely contested. Finish with the single thing I could put to the group at its next meeting that would most effectively test your diagnosis, together with the reply you expect from the physician.
