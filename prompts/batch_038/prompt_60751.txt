I teach a 90-minute upper-level philosophy of mind seminar, 18 students, and we've already done Searle's Chinese Room to death in discussion — they can recite the systems reply, the robot reply and the brain-simulator reply in their sleep. I want an in-class activity that makes them run into a different problem on their own, one they haven't met: the gap between a room that follows compositional rules and a room that just has a big list of canned answers. The goal is that they (a) fail to tell the two apart behaviorally for a while, (b) then succeed, and (c) realize that the distinction they've found isn't syntax-vs-semantics at all and doesn't do the work Searle wants it to do. They should not hear the words "lookup table" from me until after the reveal.

Here's the shape I have in mind — tell me if it's unworkable and how you'd fix it. Two student teams run two "rooms" simultaneously. Neither team sees meaning: they work with a small set of abstract glyph cards I invent, so nobody can smuggle in comprehension of Chinese or whatever. One room gets a rulebook with compositional rules over the glyph set; the other gets a table of maybe 25 pre-written exchanges. The rest of the class are interrogators who ask questions in English, and someone holding the key translates each question into glyphs and each reply back into English. I want that translator to be a student rather than me — partly logistics, but mostly because I suspect it matters: after the reveal I want to ask where the understanding was sitting, and the translator is a live candidate answer.

What I need from you:

- The glyph inventory: how many symbols, what they should look like so a student can match them fast under time pressure, and how I can make the physical cards cheaply.
- The rulebook. Write out roughly the first ten rules concretely enough that I can judge whether an undergrad can execute one turn in under a minute. Keep the content dumb — arithmetic, colors, whether it's raining, that kind of thing — so the class can actually pose questions the rules cover.
- The table room's corpus of ~25 exchanges, chosen so that room survives as long as possible. Tell me which categories of question to seed it with and what to deliberately leave out, and why.
- Roles, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 90 minutes, and a betting/scoring scheme that doesn't need real money (candy at most).
- A "design a killer question" phase: I want the interrogators to spend ten minutes trying to construct a question that exposes the table room, and to hit the problem that a blank or off-target answer isn't evidence of no-understanding, because the rulebook has gaps too. How do I arrange things so they notice that themselves instead of me saying it?
- The debrief, mapping specific moments onto the issues: the systems reply, homunculus/level confusion, Block's finite-table case (I think that's the one people call Blockhead, and that Shannon and McCarthy had the idea before Block — correct me if I've got that backwards), and Dennett's speed-and-scale objection, since my rooms will be comically slow.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time and how to handle it. Two I already worry about: the rule room will be slow and the class will get bored; and the rule team will probably start half-learning the glyph language by the end, which seems to wreck the premise that the operator understands nothing. Is that second one a bug, or should I lean into it and how?
- Straight talk on what the activity can't reproduce — the unbounded generality and speed of an actual language user, for one — and what I'll have to supply verbally afterwards to keep the analogy from collapsing.

Also give me a fallback version where I have no TA and have to be the translator myself, and a 50-minute cut-down in case the semester slips. And tell me which single moment of the activity is doing the most philosophical work, so I know what to protect when we run over.

No citations or reading lists please — I'm building materials this weekend, not writing a paper, and I dont have time to chase anything down. Just the activity, concrete enough that I could actually make the cards and hand them out on Monday.
