I'm the only foresight analyst in a mid-sized city's capital planning office. I do the 30-year appraisals — sea defences, a new water intake, where the hospital extension goes — and I keep getting asked for probabilities I don't think exist. The finance committee wants one expected value per proposal and treats any number that ends up in the box as a fact about the world. We also have a house habit of rating "confidence" one to five, which everyone games, and I want to kill it.

I've been reading Keynes on probability — weight of argument as separate from the balance of it, the thought that some probabilities can't be compared at all, the later stuff about uncertainty you can't average away and about leaning on convention instead. Turn that into a procedure I can actually run: the questions I ask in order when a proposal lands, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, when I'm permitted to put a number in, a range, or nothing, what goes in the box instead when there's no number, and where the procedure stops and hands the thing to the committee becuase it's a question of values rather than facts. Constraints: I get a week per proposal, nobody who signs off has taken a stats course, and a consulting engineer countersigns my assumptions.

Then run it on three cases: a sea wall whose payoff depends on a 2070 storm-surge projection; a hospital extension whose main input is a population forecast everyone knows is wrong and nobody can improve; and early repayment of the city's variable-rate debt. Tell me which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure, and what the procedure can't do — above all whether refusing a number just lets the status quo win by default, which is what I suspect. One of the three answers should be the one I won't like.

Plain prose please, like an email: no headings, bullets, bold or tables, just number the steps inside the sentences if you have to.
