I am a second-year doctoral student in philosophy of biology, currently drafting a chapter on the classification debates that ran through systematics from the 1960s into the early 1980s: the Hennigian cladists against the evolutionary taxonomists who held that a good classification must register degree of adaptive divergence and not merely branching order. My tentative argument is that the cladists' central complaint — that groups such as invertebrates or the traditional Reptilia are picked out by who has been left out rather than by common ancestry — could have been pressed on grounds their opponents already accepted, so that the dispute was not beyond rational adjudication. When I floated a version of this at our work-in-progress seminar last week, my supervisor cut in to say that the two camps were incommensurable, and that asking who had the better reasons therefore misconceived the dispute. The room nodded, we moved on, and I did not push back, mainly because I could not say what precisely I was objecting to.

That single word was doing an enormous amount of work, and I suspect much of the work is being done by a metaphor that has come loose from its source: two magnitudes are incommensurable in the Euclidean sense when they share no common measure, as the side and the diagonal of a square do not, and I very much doubt that anything equally clean survives the transfer to whole research traditions. So I want a disambiguation rather than a survey. Please separate the distinct precise theses that the remark could have been asserting about the cladist and evolutionary-taxonomist camps, running from the near-trivial to the radical. My own rough hunch, which may well merge or miss distinctions, is that there is something about shared terms changing their meaning, and something quite different about the absence of neutral methodological standards; I have no idea how many further readings belong on the list or how to state any of them so that they could be true or false. For each candidate thesis, give it a formulation precise enough to have a truth value, and say whether it is a platitude, a substantive but defensible claim, or something false or incoherent — and if the latter, which familiar objection does the damage: the worry that a scheme resistant to translation cannot even be conceived, or the worry that reference outlives meaning change.

Then, for each reading, say whether it would actually license my supervisor's dismissal of my chapter's question, since the precisification I need is the one that bears on that move; I also want your view on which reading people most likely have in mind when they reach for the word casually in a seminar, and whether Kuhn's own later and considerably more restricted usage — which, as I read him, consistently insisted that incommensurability entails neither incomparability nor irrationality — sits closest to any of them. Please answer as exactly five numbered entries, no more and no fewer, each of two or three sentences: the first formulating one precise thesis, the second giving the verdict on it and whether it supports the dismissal, with any third sentence reserved for the objection that defeats it or for a note on which reading the casual use tracks. No introduction, no conclusion, no recap of my seminar, and nothing outside the five entries.
