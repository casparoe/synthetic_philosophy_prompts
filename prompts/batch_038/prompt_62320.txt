I am a junior at a high school whose student-run philosophy society meets on Wednesday evenings, and we have been working through material on grounding and fundamentality: partial ground and full ground, the claim that grounding is a strict partial order, the idea that some truths are fundamental in the sense that nothing grounds them, and the objection that a fundamental level is just an admission of brute facts. Last week the society held a discussion on whether the universe requires an ultimate explanation, and one of the older members — I will call him R — argued as follows. Every fact must be grounded in some further fact; a chain of grounds cannot run on forever without ever terminating; therefore something must sit at the bottom of the chain that is not grounded by anything else; and, he added, the only plausible candidate for such a terminus is a necessary being. When I asked what grounds the necessary being, he said, without hesitation, that the buck has to stop somewhere. When I suggested instead that perhaps the most fundamental physical facts are simply brute — they obtain, and there is no further answer to be had — he said that this was merely giving up, and repeated the same line. He has since said it twice more, including in the minutes he drafted for the society, so it is clearly load-bearing for him; I am writing up a short essay on the exchange for a regional student philosophy review, and I need to be able to say precisely what he is claiming.

My difficulty is that I think the slogan is concealing several different theses that come apart, and that R is trading on the fact that they sound alike. I could think of only two candidates myself — that there cannot be an infinite descending chain of grounds, and that every fact has to have a ground — but I suspect those are not the same claim and that neither one obviously delivers a necessary being. So please give me the full menu of precise claims that this one line could be expressing, ranging from the platitudes that no one could reasonably deny to the substantive and controversial ones. I would like you to consider, among whatever else belongs on the list: whether infinite descending chains of ground are forbidden, and if so by what; whether circularity or self-grounding is separately at issue; the difference between a demand that every fact be grounded and a demand that every fact be explained, and whether the slogan smuggles the second in under cover of the first; whether the terminus has to be a single item or could be many fundamental items at once; whether what stops the chain is supposed to be ungrounded, self-grounded, or grounded in something of an entirely different kind, and whether those options are equally available to R; whether the fundamental is concrete or a fact; and whether the insistence is metaphysical or merely epistemic — that is, whether the buck cannot keep moving, or whether we simply cannot keep asking. For each reading, tell me how defensible it is (trivially true, substantive but arguable, or false or at least unmotivated), what exactly one who accepts it is committed to, whether it is the reading people who repeat the line probably have in mind, and above all whether it would actually get R his conclusion. Where two readings look interchangeable, say what each permits that the other does not, and tell me whether my brute-facts suggestion is an objection to any of them or merely a refusal to play the game.

Please number the readings R1, R2, and so on, and under each give a short label, then the precisified thesis in one or two sentences, then a verdict on its plausibility, then a line stating whether it is sufficient for R's argument, and whether it is necessary for it. Keep the whole thing organized as a list I could bring to the next meeting, and do not feel obliged to endorse one reading over the others in the body of the answer; the disambiguation is what I need. At the very end, on its own line, write "Verdict:" followed by the label of the single reading that is both defensible and sufficient to establish R's conclusion, or, if there is no such reading, the single word none.
