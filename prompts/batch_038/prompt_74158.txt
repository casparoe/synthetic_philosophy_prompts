Here's the practice I want picked apart. Interface design routinely builds displays that are known, on purpose, not to report what's going on inside the machine. Progress bars are engineered so the wait feels shorter rather than so the percentage is true: they accelerate towards the end, and in plenty of systems they animate smoothly while the underlying job is stalled, or while there is no meaningful sense in which it is "40% done". Phone cameras make a shutter sound though there is no shutter, and in some markets that sound is built in so it cannot be switched off. Touch screens give you a haptic detent on a scrolling wheel that has no detents. Deleting something usually doesn't delete it. The desktop, the folder, the document, the bin — none of those objects exist anywhere in the system. And EU regulators now require quiet electric and hybrid cars to emit a continuous artificial noise when reversing and at speeds below 20 km/h: a sound no part of the vehicle actually makes, mandated so that a pedestrian can hear something that is doing nothing audible at all.

The puzzle I keep tripping over is that the same profession treats honesty as a cardinal virtue. It condemns dark patterns, insists affordances must be real, says a control should look like what it does. So there's obviously a line between a fake progress bar and a fake unsubscribe button, but I can't find the principle that draws it.

A distinction I've been playing with — stress-test it, don't just adopt it. Call it a *state-lie* when an interface misreports a quantity that genuinely exists underneath (bar says 80%, job is 40% done). Call it a *structure-lie* when the interface posits objects or events with no counterpart down there at all (the shutter, the folder, the little car-noise). My hunch is that design is comfortable with both but only ever justifies itself in state-lie terms — we're simplifying something real — and that the justification gets shakier wherever you look closely at the structure-lies.

Now the bit I actually care about. Suppose some version of the user-illusion picture of consciousness is right: introspection hands us a simplified, task-shaped model of our own processing, the way a desktop hands you a task-shaped model of a filesystem, and there's no inner screen and no watcher. The design practice above seems to license exactly that picture — designers assume a person's model of a system need not resemble the system, and that this is fine, even good. But check whether the licensing runs both ways. In every designed case there is a determinate truth underneath that the interface is a simplification *of*, and someone who could in principle be given access to it: you can open the bonnet, read the source, ask an engineer. So does the practice presuppose that the user is *able* but not *entitled* to the truth, or that they aren't able at all? And does the consciousness case meet either condition?

Then deal head-on with the two cases that break my taxonomy. The car noise isn't told to the user of the interface at all — it's addressed to a bystander, it's compulsory, it can't be turned off from inside, and it's imposed by a third party for the bystander's own safety. If our introspective interface is like that, who is the designer, and does the practice presuppose there has to be one — or can the same arrangement arise with nobody designing it? And the second: if the user-illusion view is right, the introspective reports the view is built out of are themselves interface output. Doesn't that make the theory self-undermining in precisely the way a design team would be if it argued that user complaints are unreliable because they come from the interface? Is that fatal, or is there an asymmetry?

I'm interested in whether transparency-maximalism is even coherent for the bystander case, and whether it costs us anything we definately shouldn't want to lose.
