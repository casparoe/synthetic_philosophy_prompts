Our patient-safety committee has roped me into running a 75-minute session for about 25 clinical staff — mostly nurses, a few pharmacists, two attendings, one risk manager — on moral luck, the Nagel/Williams thing. I'm the hospital ethicist and I did a philosophy degree a long time ago, so I know the material fine; what I can't figure out is how to sequence it for a room that has never heard the term and is bracing itself for a compliance lecture about blame. Don't explain moral luck to me. Plan how I explain it.

Context you should design around rather than ignore. The unit runs a just-culture and near-miss reporting program, so the framework they already have is: don't blame the person, look at the system, and report near misses precisely so that outcomes stop mattering. Two people in the room have personally been through a fatal error and what came after — one was disciplined, one wasn't. So this isn't hypothetical to them, and I need an arc that works whether or not someone in the room has that history. Also: no readings, no bibliography, no "for further thought" list. They won't read anything and I want a plan I can execute cold.

What I need:

**The dependency chain.** The order the pieces go in and what makes each one depend on the piece before it. Specifically I want to know what has to be in place before resultant luck is even intelligible to them, because my worry is that if I open with the two-drivers case they'll hear "sometimes you get unlucky, that's life" and I'll never get to the actual philosophical problem — which is that the principle they already believe seems to be in trouble. Flag any two steps I'll be tempted to flip and what breaks if I do. And tell me where, if anywhere, the distinction between what you're to blame *for* and how much you're to blame should land in 75 minutes, or whether it should land at all at this level.

**The carrying case.** One case, not a gallery of four. I've been assuming it's the pair of nurses who make an identical dispensing error where one patient dies and one doesn't. Tell me whether that's actually right. I suspect a fatal-error case may be actively bad in this room, because they'll slide straight into "so the one whose patient died must have been less careful," which is exactly the move I have to block before anything else works. Say whether a good-outcome case, or a case where the luckier agent is the worse person, would carry the load better — and state plainly what the case has to contain and what it has to be missing.

**The misconception map.** Which wrong ideas they arrive with and which they'll form in the room, in the order they surface, and the exact point in the sequence where I head each one off — with the sentence or the small move that does it, not a mini-lecture about it. I'm already expecting: "luck just means the negligence you didn't notice"; "if you blame the system the puzzle dissolves"; "this is really about lawsuits"; and the big one, "so basically nobody is ever responsible for anything," which I think is where I lose them if I let it run past a certain point. Also whether they'll treat the Williams-style agent-regret case (the faultless driver who kills a child) as just a fact about feelings rather than something with moral weight behind it, and how to stop that.

**What I refuse to cover.** The things a fuller treatment would include that I should visibly set aside here — determinism and causal luck for a start — with the one-sentence version of each refusal that doesn't sound evasive or like I'm dodging the hard part.

**The check.** One closing question, plus what a real answer sounds like versus a polished non-answer that just hands my vocabulary back to me. It has to be a question they can't pass by parroting.

Format: use those five headings in that order. Dependency chain gets the most room, roughly 350–450 words. Carrying case about 200. Misconception map as a table with columns misconception / where it surfaces / the move that defuses it, about 350 words total. Refusals about 120. The check about 150. And put a short section at the very top called "Read this first" — three or four lines max — only if you think my whole framing is wrong for this room, which is welcome but isn't what I asked for, so keep it tight.
