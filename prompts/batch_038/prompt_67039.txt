I'm putting together a small zine of philosophical fiction with some friends and I want to contribute one story, but I'm stuck and honestly I think the best way for me to work out what I want is to ask you to write it.

The subject is Taurek's argument that the numbers shouldn't count — the denial that five deaths add up to something worse than one, and the coin toss as the only thing a rescuer actually owes. Problem is everyone writes this as a trolley problem or a triage tent, and it always collapses into either a puzzle box or a lecture. I want something else.

Write a short story, roughly 1800–2500 words, first person, from the perspective of one of the five. She's trapped with four other people she has never met and will never meet again — a ferry hold, a mine adit, a stuck lift in a building that's being evacuated, whatever you like, but pick one and make it physical: air, temperature, sound, the specific indignity of not being able to sit up. Rescue can save her group or the other group, not both, and the decision is being made by someone outside who doesn't know any of them.

The constraint that should do the real work: the narrator keeps reaching for the word "we" and keeps finding nothing there for it to grab onto. Not as a stated thesis — as a felt, recurring failure, the way you'd notice a missing step. Meanwhile the rescuer, whose voice reaches her down a shaft or a radio or a phone, talks fluently and constantly in aggregates: the five, the many, the greater loss, the numbers. By the end his vocabulary should sound like a man describing a thing that isn't in the room.

Two more rules. First, nobody in the story gets to explain the argument. If a character starts laying out premises, cut it — the philosophy has to be carried by the grammar of the narration and by the mismatch between those two vocabularies. Don't name Taurek and don't title the thing after him. Second, and this is the one people always get wrong: don't let it resolve into partiality or special obligations. It's tempting to have her think *of course he'd save his own brother*, but that's not the point and it lets everybody off the hook. Nobody here is anybody's anything.

The coin should show up, because it should — an equal chance is the whole of what's on offer. But I don't want it reading as a device for sharing out the blame. End it before the coin gets read; I'd rather the last thing in the story is a hand closing over it.

Also make her a person and not a mouthpiece: give her something petty and irrelevant to think about in the middle of all this. And no uplift, no "and in the end we were all one after all" business — that's exactly backwards, thats the thing the story is supposed to refuse.
