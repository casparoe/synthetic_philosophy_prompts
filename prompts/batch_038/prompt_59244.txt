I have fifteen minutes in front of my school district's curriculum review committee in early August, and what I actually want help with is the half hour afterward, when they stop being polite. Some background on me, because it will shape what lands: I have never taught a class, I stopped formal education at a two-year certificate, and I work as an auto-insurance claims adjuster. What I do have is two years of reading on my own, which started after a board meeting here dissolved into shouting about whether our U.S. history text was patriotic or unpatriotic. Since then I have worked through the 1619 Project material, the report put out by the presidential 1776 Commission, several state textbook-adoption fights, and a fair amount on how Japanese middle-school texts are screened and what Seoul and Beijing have said about them over the years. Out of all that I arrived at a proposal of my own, and the committee has agreed to hear it as a public-comment item — which, I understand, is not the same as being invited.

The proposal, which I call the two-account method — the name is mine, I have not seen it anywhere — is that the district should stop trying to select the correct story about contested periods and instead build those units around the disagreement itself. Concretely: for each contested unit students receive two genuinely incompatible sources on the same event, and the unit's assessment asks them to adjudicate between the two using evidence. I would pair, for instance, an excerpt from the 1619 Project against the corresponding chapter of a text the state currently adopts; and — this is the part I have not tried on anyone yet — a passage from a Japanese middle-school text beside a passage from a Korean one describing the same wartime episode. There are four design commitments I intend to defend rather than fudge. The teacher is not permitted to announce which account is better. Grading is on the reasoning and never on the verdict a student reaches. We begin in eighth grade rather than eleventh, on my theory that a fourteen-year-old is more open than a seventeen-year-old who has already picked a side. And the two accounts rotate from year to year, so that no coalition of parents ever wins permanently. My argument is short: (1) district-level fights over which narrative to teach recur every adoption cycle and are unwinnable by anyone, because this committee has no method for settling them and never will; (2) adjudicating between conflicting accounts is a skill that can be taught and graded, and it survives whatever the next political fight turns out to be; (3) parents who would never agree on a narrative can agree on their children learning to check one, or so I believe; and (4) the alternative to teaching the disagreement is not a settled story but the disagreement itself, taught badly, at the dinner table.

Here is the room as best I can reconstruct it. The chair is a former middle-school principal whose first concern is alignment with state standards and whose second is staying out of the local paper. Two retired social studies teachers sit on it, one of whom taught AP U.S. History for thirty years and holds that source work is real but belongs in college. The district's assessment coordinator is there to make sure whatever gets taught maps onto the state test. One member is a parents'-rights activist who has organized two book challenges in this district and reads every curriculum document as ideology. One is the teachers' union building representative, whose questions will be about prep time, materials, and whether a teacher can be held accountable for what happens in her classroom. One is a board member who owns a car dealership and will ask what it costs. One is a retired Army lieutenant colonel who cares how the country comes across to the children. And one is a Korean-American parent of a seventh grader who moved here six years ago and is worried about what an eighth-grade classroom would do with the Japanese material.

Please give me a prep sheet rather than a defense of the idea, in these sections and this order. (1) "How they will hear it" — about 120 words restating my proposal in the skeptical terms each faction will actually use, so that I meet the caricatures before I walk in. (2) "The objections" — the eight or nine I will genuinely get, ordered by how much damage each does to the proposal if I answer it badly, not by the order in which they will arrive. For each: who raises it; the question in that person's own vocabulary, phrased the way a principal or a union rep or an assessment coordinator would phrase it rather than the way an academic would; why it has force against this proposal specifically rather than against curriculum reform in general; a reply I could deliver in about ninety seconds in a room where nobody wants a lecture; one line on where that reply is softest; and a severity tag — fatal if unanswered, serious, manageable, or softball. (3) "What I concede in the first two minutes" — four or five items, each written as an exact sentence I can read aloud, chosen so that conceding them costs the proposal nothing and buys me the room. (4) "The one reply that has to land" — roughly 150 words on the single point the whole thing rests on and how to put it to these nine people in particular. (5) "The question I am hoping nobody asks" — one question, why it would be so hard for me, and the least-bad answer available. (6) "Three sentences to strike from my own draft" — claims I am tempted to make that would sound convincing to a layperson like me and would be taken apart by the retired AP teacher or the assessment coordinator.

Two constraints. I do not want references, a reading list, or citations to studies: I could not chase them down, and I would rather you not invent any — where one of my claims is the sort that would normally need research behind it, just mark it in a clause so I know not to state it as settled fact. And I do not want a general essay on why teaching history is difficult; every line should be aimed at this committee, this proposal, and these nine people.
