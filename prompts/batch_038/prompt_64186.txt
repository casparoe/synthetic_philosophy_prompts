I've basically talked myself into a position in the genocide-label debate and I want you to try to break it. Not survey it — break it. For lack of a better name call it label quietism. Three parts:

One. The Convention's Article II definition isn't a botched attempt to capture a moral kind. It's an instrument built for a specific machinery — prosecution and state responsibility — and its famous narrowness is the price of the machinery working at all. Protected groups limited to national, ethnic, racial, religious; no political or social groups; cultural destruction stripped out in the drafting; and special intent stretched over the whole thing. Treat that as a failed moral definition and you'll be "fixing" it forever without ever getting a category you can actually charge anyone under.

Two. So moral and political argument should stop borrowing the legal term and just describe things: massacre, starvation run as policy, forced transfer of children, deliberate dismantling of a group's institutions. Same facts, no all-or-nothing threshold, no standard of proof smuggled into your sentences.

Three, and this is the load-bearing empirical bit. The politics of naming atrocities is mostly wasted effort because the label doesn't do the work people think it does. The Clinton administration's determination not to say the word during Rwanda, and then Powell finally saying it about Darfur a decade later with nothing much changing afterwards — for me those are the same datum, not two. Recognition changes parliaments' vocabulary and not their behavior.

So what would count against this? I want the map of where it's actually exposed. A few places I already suspect, but I don't want to steer you too hard:

- What does the view predict that its rivals don't, and is it coming out right? If recognition campaigns keep winning in legislatures while changing nothing on the ground, is that confirmation for me, or is there a reading of those campaigns where the goal was never intervention and my whole "wasted effort" framing is measuring the wrong thing?
- Lemkin. His own concept was much wider than what survived into the Convention — a coordinated plan against the foundations of a group's life, running in two phases, first tearing out the oppressed group's pattern and then imposing the oppressor's, covering institutions, language, culture, economic existence, and individual dignity, not just bodies. I've been treating the Convention as a betrayal of Lemkin that turned out to be fine. What would have to be true for that to be wrong — i.e. for there to be moral work that only the group-destruction concept can do and that my list of ordinary atrocity descriptions structurally can't?
- Is part two self-undermining? "Ethnic cleansing" isn't a standalone crime anywhere, "massacre" is newspaper language, and every substitute term I'm reaching for has its own contested extension and its own politics. Does the quietist actually exit the naming fight or just relocate it to terms nobody is policing?
- The case law. Akayesu's expansive reading against Krstić's narrowing of "destroy in part" to physical-biological destruction, and then the ICJ in 2007 finding that genocide happened at Srebrenica but wasn't attributable to Serbia, which was held to have failed at prevention instead. I read that last one as vindicating me: the legal category did its narrow job and delivered a verdict almost no one would call the moral truth of the matter. Is there a reading where those same cases count against the view?
- Insulation. I worry this thing can absorb anything — every counterexample where the label did move policy gets explained away as states having other reasons. Is that a genuine problem here, given that part three is supposed to be empirical, or is a claim of this shape allowed to be robust without being empty?

If it helps calibrate: what would move me most is anything showing the group-level concept doing explanatory or moral work that an aggregated list of individual crimes can't replicate. Show me that and I'll move.

Now be committed about it. Don't lay out the objections symmetrically and leave me to weigh them — tell me which one you think actually breaks the view, or if none of them do, say so plainly and tell me what the view has to be narrowed into to survive. And tell me which of these a sharp defender would already concede, because I'd rather hear it from you than in an argument. Rank the defeaters, fatal down to merely awkward. No bibliography and no reference list — I roughly know where this stuff comes from, and I'd much rather you leave out a specific you're unsure of than pad it with invented ones.
