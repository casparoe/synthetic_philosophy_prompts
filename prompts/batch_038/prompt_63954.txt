so I've gotten myself stuck on something and a couple of my friends have too, and honestly I want an essay and not another group chat about it. I've been reading around population ethics and suffering-focused stuff on and off — nothing systematic, I just keep coming back to the same puzzle. Here's the setup.

Three cases, all of them fully stipulated so there's no hidden facts to argue about:

A: ten million people, each with a long good life, plus exactly one person who spends their whole life in unrelievable agony that never ends and never gets any better.

B: a completely empty world. nobody, no animals, nothing conscious at all. no suffering anywhere.

C: a world with ten million people who are all deeply happy, and nobody at all who suffers.

My gut ranking is that A is better than B, and C is better than A. Almost everyone I've argued with agrees on both. But I can't find a principle that gets both without doing something that looks like cheating. A view that just says "minimize total suffering, full stop" puts B above A and can't explain why I think A is better. A view that lets you trade agony off against enough happy people at some rate gets A above B, but then it seems like it should also be able to buy off the agony in A by piling on more happiness, and I don't want to say that — at some point the pile of happiness feels like it's just noise next to that one life.

Two candidate accounts I've been kicking around, and I'd like to know which one survives.

The exchange-rate account: suffering and happiness are the same currency, there's some finite number of happy lives that outweighs one tortured life, and my verdict on A vs B just means ten million happens to be past that number. No lexicality, no tiers, one continuous scale.

The threshold account: agonies of that kind sit on a separate tier from anything good, so no finite amount of good can outbid even one of them, but the tier only kicks in above some intensity, and mild discomforts are still tradeable against ordinary happiness.

The essay I want: which of these better explains the whole pattern, A vs B and C vs A together. Take the pattern seriously as data rather than explaining one half and shrugging at the other. Then the part that actually matters — don't stop at "the threshold view explains it." Say what the exchange-rate view predicts for all three cases once you set its numbers right, and then decide whether tuning those numbers can rescue it. If tweaking the parameters gets you the same verdicts everywhere, is it still a different view, or have the two collapsed into one thing wearing different clothes? And if they really do come apart, name the case that separates them and say which way you'd bet on it.

Two more things. If you come down on the threshold side, you owe me an answer on where the line goes and what stops it from being arbitrary — my friend thinks any line you draw is obviously ad hoc, and I want that objection met rather than dodged. If you come down on the exchange-rate side, you owe me an answer on whether the view licenses trading off the one tortured life in A for enough extra happy people, and if it doesn't, what's doing the work besides something lexical sneaking in through the back door.

Oh and one small thing worth checking, because my friend reckons it flips the whole issue: would your verdict change if the numbers were tiny instead of ten million? Like, one very happy person plus one agonized person versus an empty world. If the answer is yes, say what that tells us about which of the two accounts we were really defending the whole time.

Please commit to a verdict at the end and argue for it. "It depends on your prior intuitions" or "both views have merits" is exactly the answer I'm trying to get out of, not the answer I want back.
