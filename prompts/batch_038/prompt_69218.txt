For eighteen months I have kept a file of remarks from readers, and although I write a fortnightly column on books and the sciences of mind, I have never managed to pin down what the file is actually about. The remarks all come after the fact. A woman tells me she wept through a harrowing chapter of a novel and then learned in an interview that the episode was lifted almost intact from the author's own life; she describes the discovery as curdling rather than deepening her grief, and cannot say why, since the suffering is now more real than it was before. A man describes finishing a memoir that destroyed him and then reading that substantial portions of it were invented, and reports a peculiar impulse to take back the tears, as though they had been paid to the wrong account. A third reader, whose favourite novel for a decade was a translation, found out that the death scene she had mourned over and over had been softened by the translator and simply does not occur in the original. In every case the person insists, when pressed, that they never for a moment supposed the characters existed; the distress is not about having been tricked, and it is not that the feeling was unpleasant at the time. It is the sense that an emotion they genuinely had has been orphaned by later information about the status of its object, and that they are now mildly ashamed of it on their own behalf. One of them said she felt she had grieved out of turn.

What I find most striking is an asymmetry. When people discover that the snake in a nature programme was a prop, or that the aircraft they braced for doom in was never in difficulty, the fear evaporates and they find the whole thing funny; nobody is embarrassed to have been afraid, because the feeling simply lapses. With the book the feeling does not lapse — it seems to persist while being retrospectively demoted, which is a strange combination, and it is why I doubt the label I am most often given. Several academics I put this to said I had rediscovered the paradox of fiction, but that puzzle concerns how we can be moved at all by people we know do not exist, and my correspondents have no trouble being moved; their difficulty is with having been moved. Nor is it the older puzzle about why we seek out distressing art, since the discomfort I am describing arrives later and attaches to the discovery rather than to the reading. I am looking for whatever the philosophy of emotion, or of fiction, calls this specific after-the-fact condition, and failing that, for a term I can defend in print.

I am not after a bare name; I want the carving. Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each of one or two sentences, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no bibliography. The first three items should each take one established concept that comes closest to what I have described — including any account that treats such responses as not full-blooded emotions, any account of emotions that persist against a person's own better judgment, and any account framed in terms of whether a feeling is appropriate to its object — and should state precisely where my phenomenon departs from it. The fourth item should give the term you would use for the phenomenon itself, with a definition sharp enough that I could tell a reader when it does and does not apply. The fifth item should settle two boundary cases for me: whether the same condition arises when the object was always real but the description of it turned out to be wrong, as with a eulogy delivered about a person the mourner had misremembered, and whether it arises when later information strengthens rather than weakens the claim on the feeling, as with my first correspondent.
