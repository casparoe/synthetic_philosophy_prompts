I write novels — three out, working on a fourth — and I've talked myself into a hole I can't climb out of, which is a bit of a problem since the hole is my job.

At a festival last month somebody asked me about when you're allowed to say something, and she gave me the strict version: the rule is that you may assert it only if you know it. Not "only if you sincerely believe it," not "only if it happens to be true" — you have to actually know. I said something evasive on stage and then went home and wrote the whole thing out applied to what I do, and it came out like this:

1. You may assert p only if you know p.
2. Writing a realist novel is asserting a great many propositions: that a woman called So-and-so walked out on her husband in the spring, that the works closed in the winter of 1984, that her brother couldn't look at her in the hospital.
3. I know almost none of them. Most aren't even candidates for knowledge because the people never existed, and the ones about the real strike I only "know" from three interviews and a local history book, which is a long way from knowing.
4. So nearly every sentence I publish violates the norm — and I can't plead carelessness, because I do it on purpose, for money, to readers who rely on me exactly as they rely on anyone else who tells them how things are.
5. A norm that condems a whole ordinary, long-running, mutually useful practice wholesale can't be the norm governing that practice. If 1 makes all of fiction one giant impropriety, then 1 goes.
6. But if I keep 1, the way out is to say a novel isn't asserting anything at all — the entire thing is a kind of agreed pretense, with the usual rules switched off.
7. And if nothing in a novel is asserted, then nothing in it is offered as true, and a novel can't tell its reader anything true about the world. It's decoration with a plot.
8. Novels obviously do tell readers true things; half the reason people read mine is that they come out understanding something they didnt before.
9. So either fiction is one enormous lie, or it says nothing.

I can't live with either horn. If I had to pick a premise to die I'd keep 8 — that's the one I'd fight a man in a pub over — but I don't trust my own judgement here, because I want the book to be worth something and I'd rather hear it from you than from me.

So: which step actually breaks? Tell me why it looked fine when I wrote it, because it looked extremely fine. And then price the exits for me, including the ones I haven't thought of. In particular — if the trouble is that I've got the wrong norm in 1, does switching to truth, or to honest belief, get me out, or does each one break in the same place with different words? Does the mongrel nature of this specific book matter: real strike, invented family, so some sentences are ones I could genuinely come to know and others can't even be true? And does it make a difference that I do it deliberately? I've been assuming a planned violation is worse than a sloppy one, but I'm not convinced that's obviously right, and a lot of my chain leans on it.
