I'm a software engineer on a reliability team, with no formal philosophy training, so I hope you'll forgive the informal framing of what is, I think, a real question. Over the holidays I went down a rabbit hole on Bertrand's paradox after noticing that our design reviews keep relitigating the same argument about when an engineer is entitled to assume something is "uniform at random." For orientation: Bertrand put the puzzle in his 1889 probability treatise as a warning about the principle of indifference — pick a chord of a circle at random, what is the chance it is longer than a side of the inscribed equilateral triangle — and three constructions that each look like an honest way of making "at random" precise give 1/3, 1/2, and 1/4. Jaynes, in a 1973 paper called "The Well-Posed Problem," argued that demanding scale and translation invariance makes the question well-posed after all and singles out the second construction. What struck me is that our internal review log is full of miniature versions of exactly this dispute, and that my team's staff engineer — call her Nadia, who has a statistics background I don't — has been ruling on them for two years with very terse written justifications. I have the page open. Her record so far:

1. Our write-corruption fuzzer had to pick which of 64 shards to target. A teammate proposed uniform over the 64 and she approved it with no conditions; her entire note was that relabeling the shards changes nothing about the system, so there is no competing proposal to defend.
2. A load-test analyst wrote up a regression as "somewhere between 1.2x and 3x slower" and proposed a uniform distribution over that interval of the ratio. She struck it and had them use uniform over the logarithm of the ratio across the same bounds, on the grounds that describing the result as a slowdown or as a speedup cannot be allowed to change the arithmetic. She then made them rerun the whole analysis with both bounds widened by a factor of two and report the two sets of numbers side by side.
3. A storage team wanted a log-uniform distribution over mean-time-between-failures for a disk model we had no field data on. She rejected it and made them fit the vendor's five-year fleet telemetry instead, which came out Weibull-shaped. Note: "we have measurements; this was never an ignorance problem."
4. For a defect-rate estimate on a brand-new component, someone asked her to bless a single "objective" prior. She refused to name one. She had them compute under three — the scale-invariant one, one deliberately hostile to the conclusion they wanted, and one concentrated near zero — and let the claim stand only because it survived all three.
5. A canary harness was sampling traffic in proportion to production volume, which is about 80 percent from three large tenants. She made them sample uniformly across tenants instead, arguing that proportional sampling means the small tenants effectively never get exercised and the entire point of the canary was tenant-specific breakage.

Thursday's review is the case I actually care about. We're building a chaos harness that kills a node after a randomly chosen delay d, and the proposal on the table is a log-uniform distribution over d between 1 millisecond and 10 seconds, defended as the scale-invariant choice for a positive quantity with hard physical bounds. Two further facts that I expect to matter: we do have production incident telemetry on when nodes actually die, and it is heavily clustered in the few hundred milliseconds after a garbage-collection pause or a deployment, i.e. almost nowhere in most of the 1 ms to 10 s range; and the specific bug class this harness exists to catch is a node dying while its peers still consider its lease valid, where the relevant protocol constants are a 250 ms heartbeat interval, a 2 s election timeout, and a 15 s lease. So invariance, telemetry, and the purpose of the test each seem to point somewhere different, and I genuinely cannot tell which one Nadia treats as authoritative.

Please take the role of a philosopher of probability who has also spent enough time in industrial test engineering to find my situation unsurprising, and think this through carefully rather than answering from first impressions.
