I am assembling a case study on natural kinds for a graduate seminar, and I want the 2006 decision by the International Astronomical Union to demote Pluto worked through the conceptual machinery rather than through the familiar story of astronomers overriding public sentiment. Take the following as given, so that nothing turns on the history: once a scattered-disk body comparable to Pluto in size and considerably more massive was announced in 2005, the union faced a choice between admitting a dozen or more new planets and drawing a line; the resolution adopted at Prague defined a planet as a body orbiting the Sun, massive enough for its own gravity to have made it nearly round, and having cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit; a new category of dwarf planet was created for round bodies failing the third condition; a proposal to keep dwarf planets as a subclass of planets was voted down; and the outcome was settled by a show of hands among the few hundred members who happened to be present, out of a union numbering many thousands.

Run that episode past three accounts. First, the homeostatic property cluster view associated with Boyd, on which a kind is a cluster of co-occurring properties held together by an underlying mechanism. Second, the pluralist view Dupré calls promiscuous realism, on which many cross-cutting classifications of the same domain are equally legitimate and none is uniquely correct. Third, the microstructural picture associated with Kripke and Putnam, on which a kind is fixed by the inner nature of its instances, transposed as best it can be from chemical elements to astronomical bodies. For each, say what the account predicts about whether the resolution tracked a joint or imposed one, and show where its machinery actually bites on the details — the third criterion above all, since it makes planet-hood depend on a body's contingent surroundings and dynamical history rather than on anything intrinsic to it, so that a body's status would change were it relocated elsewhere in the system.

Then two points I care about most. Would any of the three have delivered a different verdict had the union instead defined planet geophysically, by roundness alone, promoting Ceres and a dozen large moons along with Pluto? If all three turn out indifferent to a choice the practitioners experienced as momentous, what does that show about their grip on scientific practice? And does a vote have any metaphysical significance on any of these views, or must each of them treat the episode as a stipulation dressed as a discovery? Where an account falls silent, or forces the case into a shape it does not have, say so plainly; I would rather have an honest anomaly than a tidy mapping. Close with your judgement about which account, if any, earns the right to call this a joint in nature.
