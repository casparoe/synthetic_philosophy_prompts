I am marking a take-home question for a graduate seminar on modality, and I want an independent assessment of one submission before I settle on a grade. The question was worth 30 marks — 12 for accuracy of exposition, 12 for the argument developed, 6 for the defence of a conclusion — and read as follows.

State the counterpart-theoretic truth-conditions for a de re possibility claim of the form: Humphrey might have won the election. Then evaluate Kripke's complaint that the analysis answers the wrong question, since what matters to Humphrey is that he himself might have won, not that some other individual in another world does. Does taking that complaint seriously oblige the modal theorist to posit transworld identity, or does the counterpart theorist have an adequate reply? Argue to a conclusion.

The submission ran as follows.

> Counterpart theory analyses de re modal claims without ever allowing one individual to exist in more than one world. On Lewis's account, the claim that Humphrey might have won is true just in case, in some possible world, there is an individual who is a counterpart of Humphrey and who wins the election there. Humphrey himself is world-bound: he exists only in the actual world, and there he loses. What makes the possibility true is therefore the existence and character of a distinct individual elsewhere in logical space, related to Humphrey by resemblance in the respects that matter.
>
> The natural way to understand that relation is by analogy with Lewis's treatment of time. Humphrey persists through time by having distinct temporal parts at distinct times; on the same pattern he is spread across worlds by having counterparts, which function as his modal parts. Transworld identity is then not so much eliminated as replaced by transworld parthood, and that is the source of the view's appeal: it retains a reductive account of de re modality while refusing to say that one and the same individual is wholly present in two worlds at once. Because the counterpart relation is never a relation of numerical identity, Lewis also takes the view to remove any temptation to posit purely numerical, non-qualitative differences between worlds, which is why he pairs it with his rejection of haecceitism.
>
> Kripke's complaint is that all of this answers a question Humphrey never asked. Humphrey wants to know that he might have won; being told that someone very like him wins elsewhere gives him nothing to rejoice at or regret, since that person's victory is not his. The standard reply, which I think succeeds as far as it goes, is that the objection begs the question: it assumes that de re possibility must consist in the very same individual existing under other circumstances, which is precisely what is at issue. On the counterpart account the winner in the other world is not a stranger picked out of nowhere but the individual who is, in the respects the context makes salient, Humphrey under other circumstances. The complaint therefore tracks a difference in what we care about rather than a defect in the truth-conditions.
>
> Kripke's alternative is to dissolve the problem of transworld identification rather than solve it. Possible worlds are stipulated and not surveyed; we do not peer into other worlds and then ask which individual in them is Humphrey. Identity across worlds is fixed by origin: what makes an individual in another world Humphrey is that he comes from the same zygote, and so from the same parents, as the actual Humphrey. Origin thus does for Kripke the work that similarity does for Lewis, and no qualitative criterion of matching is needed.
>
> Where I think counterpart theory is in genuine trouble is over determinacy. The counterpart relation is fixed by comparative similarity, but similarity comes in degrees and varies with the respects one weights, so for almost any candidate counterpart there are others marginally more and marginally less similar. It follows that there is rarely a determinate fact about what is possible for Humphrey. Lewis's appeal to contextually salient respects looks like an attempt to repair this, but it is unclear that it can: if which respects are salient is settled by our interests rather than by the world, the analysis offers no determinate truth-conditions and has merely relocated the unclarity from the plurality of worlds into the context of utterance.
>
> I conclude that Kripke's objection does not by itself force the counterpart theorist to adopt transworld identity, but that the choice between the two theories is decided elsewhere — by whether one is prepared to live with a broadly indeterminate account of what is possible for an individual.
