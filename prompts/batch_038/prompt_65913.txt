I am in the second year of a PhD in early modern European history. My dissertation works with the paper trail left by inquisitorial tribunals over a rural population that produced almost nothing else — the standard-bearer for the genre being Carlo Ginzburg's reconstruction of the cosmology of a Friulian miller tried twice for heresy, the second trial ending in his execution in 1599. Last week our departmental methods seminar, which this term is being run jointly with a philosophy reading group, spent two hours on the epistemology of such records, and the sentence "history is written by the victors" was deployed three times, by three different people, in what I am fairly confident were three incompatible ways. The visiting scholar used it against my project: I cannot treat a defendant's testimony as evidence of what he believed, because the questions were the inquisitor's, the notary chose the summarising vocabulary, and the only words that survive verbatim are the ones the tribunal needed to be verbatim. A fellow student used it as the motivation for the opposite practice: precisely because the archive belongs to the victors, we are obliged to read it against the grain to recover the losing side. And someone from the philosophy group used it about historiography itself, to suggest that any narrative we now write, however sympathetic, is still a victor's narrative, leaving silence or permanent self-cancellation as the only honest options. Nobody offered an argument on any of these occasions; the sentence did all the work. Everyone attributed it to Churchill, and nobody could source it.

Could you produce something like a handout for me on what that sentence can actually be taken to assert? What I want is a menu rather than a single favoured interpretation: the distinct precise theses hidden in it, laid out along the axes that seem to matter to me, namely who is supposed to count as the "victor" (the winning party to the conflict described, the institution that generated the record, the literate classes generally, the archivists and editors who decided what survived, or the working historian), what "written" is supposed to cover (composed, selected, summarised, preserved, transmitted, canonised, narrated), and what the scope of the claim is (this document, this archive, the historical record as a whole, historical knowledge, or the discipline's output). For each reading I would like a one-sentence formulation precise enough to be argued with, followed by a verdict on whether it is a platitude no historian would deny, a substantive and defensible thesis, false, or so vague as to be unassailable — and where a reading is self-undermining or unfalsifiable, I would like to be shown how. Then I would like you to say which reading each of the three uses in my seminar actually requires, and whether the reading required is one that survives scrutiny; I suspect the visiting scholar's dismissal and my fellow student's imperative are leaning on different senses of the same words, and I would like to know whether either of them is entitled to it.

Please structure your answer in exactly these sections, in this order: (1) **What would have to be true for the sentence to be worth saying** — about 120 words, framing the question before the menu; (2) **The menu of readings** — roughly 700 words, numbered, each with its precise formulation, its implied sense of "victor" and "written", and its scope; (3) **Verdicts** — about 300 words, sorting the readings into platitude, substantive and defensible, false, and unassailable; (4) **What each of the three uses needed** — about 250 words, matching each seminar intervention to the readings that would license it and saying whether those readings hold up; (5) **What survives** — about 150 words, on whether any version of the sentence gives me a reason to change how I use these records; and (6) **Confidence** — about 120 words. In that final section, please be explicit about how confident you are in the verdicts you have handed down and which distinctions you think are genuinely load-bearing versus merely tidy, and single out the one claim in the handout you are least sure of, saying why and what would settle it.
