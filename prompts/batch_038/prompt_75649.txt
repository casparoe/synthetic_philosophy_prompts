I am the dean of students at a secondary school that hosts roughly forty exchange students, aged sixteen to seventeen, for a ten-month academic year. They live with local host families and take classes alongside local students. About eight times a term my office receives a complaint from a host parent, a local teacher, or a neighbor concerning a student's failure to observe some ordinary local practice: where to stand in a queue, whether to leave money at a restaurant, whether to cross against a red signal on an empty street, how to receive an object from an elder, which seat or doorway to use, whether to eat while walking. Less often the complaint runs the other direction, with a student objecting to being expected to do something at all.

Every case terminates in a written response that the host family and the student will both read, plus an entry in the student's file, and about two placements a year are genuinely at risk of ending. I have thirty minutes per case. The people who would run the procedure — myself and two teachers who cover for me — have no training in philosophy or law. Our present responses fail in both directions: to the family they read as a school excusing rudeness on the grounds that it happens elsewhere, and to the student they read as the enforcement of local custom under threat of a bad file entry.

We have been using an informal three-question test: was anyone harmed, could the student reasonably have known the rule, and is the rule the sort of thing reasonable people disagree about. I cannot defend its outputs. It tells me that a student who left a large tip at a small family restaurant did nothing wrong because nobody lost anything, even though the owner came to the school to say the student had treated his waiters as beggars. It also tells me that a student briefed on a norm during orientation is thereby bound by it, which makes the orientation itself the source of the obligation.

Please write a decision procedure my office can actually run: the questions asked in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the small set of terminal outcomes it can reach (no obligation and the complaint is answered as such; obligation with excuse; obligation to comply; comply while registering an objection privately; decline to comply; refer to me), and the points at which it must hand the decision to a person because what remains is a question of values rather than fact.

The procedure has to draw, in language a teacher can use with a sixteen-year-old or a host parent, at least the following distinctions, and I want you to tell me explicitly which of them must survive the simplification and which may be collapsed for lay use: norms whose force depends on a structure of mutual assurance, so that one person's opting out changes what others can count on; norms that bind because others relied on expectations the visitor themselves invited; norms that bind only through what non-compliance expresses to onlookers; and norms that are not moral in any sense but markers of local standing. For each kind it must output whether a student present for ten months is unbound, bound but excusable, or bound to the same degree as a local, since the wording of my letter differs sharply depending on which applies. It needs a step deciding whether a norm is one that binds members only — religious observance, forms of address within a family, criticism of the host community from inside — and whether ten months of residence makes a student a member for that purpose. It needs a step deciding whether to go by the rationale locals give for a norm or by what the norm observably does, including cases where the stated reason is plainly not the operative one. And it needs a branch for when compliance would require the student to do something they take to be wrong.

Two half-formed ideas of my own that I want either built in or demolished. First, norms that seem to collapse if a single person opts out appear to bind an individual student far more tightly than norms that only degrade once many people opt out, which would appear to bind mainly those already inside the practice; I cannot tell whether that distinction is doing real work or merely restating whether anyone noticed. Second, in almost every case that reaches me, compliance is invisible and non-compliance is conspicuous, and I suspect this is doing more work in the complaints than any question of harm.

Apply the procedure to three cases: a student who crosses a pedestrian signal at eleven at night on an empty street with three friends, is seen by a neighbor, and is reported to us by the host mother as a serious matter, though the student says they would not have stepped out had a car been coming; the restaurant tip described above, where the student's home practice makes withholding the tip the insult and leaving it the courtesy; and a student who, at a gathering of her host family, is expected to receive a cup from the grandmother with both hands and a slight bow and declines the gesture, explaining that in her upbringing that posture is reserved for prayer and that performing it toward a person would be something she cannot do sincerely, while the family says the grandmother has been insulted. One of these three must produce a result my office will resist. Tell me which and why without softening it; I would rather have a procedure that says the unwelcome thing than one that flatters us.

Then give me the honest part: what will predictably go wrong the first three times we run it; which single question carries most of the weight and is least reliable; how the procedure can be turned into an instrument for enforcing local hierarchy against a teenager who has no standing to object, and what safeguard against that can actually be written in; and what it cannot settle at all. Finish with a one-page version for students arriving in August, and the two or three sentences I can say to a student who insists that nobody was hurt without implying that their moral sense is naive.
