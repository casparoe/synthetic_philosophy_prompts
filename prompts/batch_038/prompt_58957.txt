I have no training in economics. I left school at sixteen, and for the past year or so I have been reading through the socialist calculation debate on my own — far enough to follow an argument, not far enough to check one. What I want from you is a sparring partner, not a document.

Please play a present-day economist who holds the Mises–Hayek position: that an economy which has abolished private ownership of the means of production cannot calculate, because where capital goods never genuinely change hands there are no genuine prices for them, and therefore no way to tell whether a given production plan is economical rather than merely feasible. Play the strongest available version of him — a working economist, patient with an amateur, not a pamphleteer, and above all not a strawman. You may concede anything peripheral: that Lange's model is internally coherent, that consumer goods could be priced administratively, that Mises put some of this badly in his original statement of the argument in 1920. You may not concede the core. And if you judge that my challenge really is fatal to some version of the thesis, say so plainly — I would rather have an honest concession than a defence of something you believe to be false.

Ground rules. First person, in character, until I say stop. One paragraph per reply, so that it reads as a conversation rather than a lecture. Your first sentence must be your verdict on my challenge, stated outright, for or against; only then the reasons. No preamble, no restating of what I asked, no out-of-character commentary, and no telling me to go and read something. When you use a technical term, gloss it in the same sentence, and I will tell you if I still do not follow.

My opening move. Every version of the impossibility argument I have met turns on the claim that planners cannot obtain the information a market generates through prices. Now consider a global online retailer. It decides what to stock in which of hundreds of warehouses, it moves goods between them, it routes vans, it times promotions — and almost none of that internal allocation is done by prices. There is no market inside the firm for shelf space, or for a pallet's journey from one depot to another; those are forecasts, quotas, and instructions. On any measure anyone has offered, that internal planning operation is larger and far more fine-grained than what most national planning agencies actually did. I know the standard reply: the firm is disciplined by real prices at its edges, it can always buy instead of make, and its internal decisions are judged by a profit figure that only means something because of the market around it. But that reply seems to me to prove only that a planned island needs a market sea — which is exactly what a socialist should want: keep trading with the world, plan at home, and use world prices the way the firm uses the prices at its boundary. So the boundary answer cannot be the whole of it. Tell me, then: what does a whole economy have that a firm of that size lacks, and why does the absence of internal prices inside the firm not matter, while their absence across an economy is supposed to be fatal?
