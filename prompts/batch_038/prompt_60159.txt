I've followed the flying-man thought experiment through years of rationalist-adjacent discussion, almost always in the same two-line gloss — "medieval precursor of the cogito; self-awareness without the senses" — and I want to stop accepting that gloss, because one clause in the text refuses to fit it. The passage is Avicenna's flying man, Kitab al-Shifa, De Anima I.1 (lines 49–60), in this English rendering (the translation varies between editions, so please work with the wording as given rather than with a version you remember):

"Suppose that one of us were to think as if he was suddenly, created and complete but with his view obscured so that he could not see outside. And suppose that he had been so created as if he were moved in the air of the void in such a way that he was not touched by the thickness of the air that he would be able to sense it and as if his limbs were separated so that they did not touch either him or each other. Thence he should see if he would affirm being of his essence. He will not hesitate to affirm himself to exist. He will not however affirm things exterior to his members nor the hidden things of his interiors nor his soul nor his brain nor anything else extrinsic. He will affirm himself to exist though, he will not affirm the length or the width or the thickness of himself. If however in that period of time it is possible for him to imagine a hand or another member, that would not be imagined to be part of him nor necessary to his essence."

The clause that stops me is the one where he will not affirm "his soul" alongside his brain. If what the flying man is directly aware of is his soul, why does the soul land on the list of things he declines to affirm? My own hypothesis: his awareness is bare first-person acquaintance with no sortal attached — he is aware *that* he is, but has no concept of *what* he is, and "soul" is precisely a theoretical kind-term with a definition, so denying it costs the argument nothing; the passage's real work is to block any identification of the self-affirmed item with a body or body part. Please assess that hypothesis: give me the strongest rival reading of that clause, and say whether my reading survives the obvious objection that a being created all at once with no prior experience has acquired no concepts whatsoever, so there is nothing determinate for its "affirmation" to be about.

Lead with a one-sentence verdict on whether my hypothesis survives, then keep the whole answer under 350 words. No bibliography or further reading — I can't look anything up.
