I hold a chair in a department where I am the only person who works on epistemology, and my own field is early modern philosophy; I have come to this material sideways, through a long-running argument with a colleague in the history department about whether a historian's record of past error is evidence bearing on her present manuscript. That argument has forced me into a fairly definite position on the lottery and the preface, which I am now writing up as a chapter for an edited volume on the norms of belief. The chapter is due to the editors in six weeks, and I would much rather find the cracks now than in the referees' reports. I have described the position to two colleagues: one assured me it was perfectly coherent without saying why, the other said it collapses at the first step without saying where, and neither response was any use to me. So I want a genuine audit rather than encouragement.

Here is the package, as I would state it in print:

1. High credence is a necessary condition on rational outright belief, but it is never by itself sufficient. I deliberately decline to fix a numerical threshold; I think the demand for a precise cut-off is itself a mistake. I also reject pragmatic encroachment in any form: what it is rational to believe does not vary with what happens to turn on believing it.

2. What high credence needs in order to amount to belief is what I call evidential specificity. One's evidence must speak in favour of the proposition in a way that it does not equally speak in favour of each of a range of rivals. Evidence that is indiscriminate between a proposition and its competitors cannot support outright belief in it, no matter how high it drives the probability.

3. I keep closure. If one rationally believes each of finitely many propositions, one cannot rationally believe that their conjunction is false. I want to hold onto this: a norm of belief that tolerates outright-believed inconsistency does not deserve the name of a norm.

4. In a large fair lottery it is not rational to believe that your ticket will lose, and the reason is (2) rather than the bare magnitude of the probability. I am equally unwilling, however, to extend that verdict to a lottery containing more tickets than there are atoms in the observable universe. There, I think, the odds do settle the matter, and at some point the numbers simply have to be allowed to count. I treat this as a fixed point: any account of belief that requires me to withhold assent even in that case is refuted by it.

5. The author who has checked her work as carefully as she can does rationally believe each of the individual claims in her book. This is precisely what (2) is for: her evidence for each claim — a document, an argument, a measurement — is specific to that claim in a way that lottery evidence never is. In the standard preface case, where her only ground for expecting error is an anonymous base rate about books like hers, I am content to say that she does not rationally believe that at least one of her claims is false. I regard the paradox in its textbook form as dissolved rather than solved, and I am prepared to pay that price.

6. Now the case my colleague and I have been fighting about, which is my own and which I most want pressure-tested. A historian keeps a private record: across nine previous monographs, once the reviews and the corrections came in, an average of three of her substantive claims turned out to be wrong. She has just finished a tenth book of comparable size and scope. Part of her confidence in each individual claim comes from this same record — she reads a difficult hand and feels sure of it because in nine books that instinct has very nearly never let her down. I want to say that here her belief that at least one claim in the new book is false is rational, because the evidence is not an anonymous base rate about a class of authors but a record of the reliability of the very process that produced these particular beliefs. I also want to say that the numerically identical belief, held on the anonymous base rate alone, is not rational.

7. Finally, a piece of practice I take to be obviously consistent with (1): I tell my doctoral students to write that their evidence suggests rather than shows, and to flag every speculative claim, and I tell them this is a requirement of intellectual honesty and not merely stylistic prudence. A colleague objected that this is stakes-sensitivity by another name. I think the objection misses, because hedging concerns what one asserts, whereas (1) concerns what one believes, and these are governed by different norms.

Two further points on which I believe I am on safe ground, and which I mention so that you can tell me whether I am. First, another colleague insists that (3) and (4) cannot coexist: since each individual proposition to the effect that some other ticket will lose is highly probable, closure will have the agent believe them all and hence believe that her own ticket wins. I think this is simply a mistake, because (2) blocks each of those beliefs from forming, so closure is never given anything to operate on. Second, I assume the standard diagnosis of the preface — that the author's two sets of beliefs are jointly incoherent — cannot be turned against me, since I have already surrendered one of the two sets.

Please act as an expert in the epistemology of rational belief, comfortable with the formal apparatus but writing as a professional philosopher addressing a colleague rather than as a textbook. Think carefully, and begin by working through the package pairwise — which commitments constrain which, and what follows from which — before you render any verdict. Then give me an audit, not a lecture: identify the genuine tensions, ordered by severity, and for each say exactly which commitment has to go or be modified, what the modification costs me elsewhere in the package, and whether the cost is bearable. Tell me separately which pairs only look as though they are in tension and dissolve under inspection, including whether my two dismissals above really do the work I think they do. Finish by naming the single revision that would repair the most damage, and say what the resulting position looks like. I am not asking you to guess which of these I would prefer to abandon; I have no antecedent attachment and would rather be told. Do not send me a survey of the literature, a reading list, or attributions to named philosophers — I can find those myself, and they are not what I need here.
