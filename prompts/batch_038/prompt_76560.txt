I have ninety minutes, one whiteboard, and no slides in which to teach Wittgenstein's remarks on religious belief to second-year undergraduates in a philosophy of religion course. They have just completed three weeks on verificationism, and their course reader frames Wittgenstein as the thinker who concedes the verificationist premise — that religious utterances do not state facts — and then softens the verdict. They therefore arrive intending to file him next to Ayer with a warmer tone, and my task is to break that filing without letting him look like an apologist. The group is mixed in a way that matters: two students are practising believers who resent being converted into a case study, one is a physics major who will keep demanding a yes-or-no answer on whether the relevant propositions are true, and the remainder have no theology and limited patience for exegesis. The session must leave all of them able to write a defensible essay next week on whether religious language is truth-apt, which is their assessed question.

I am not asking you to explain Wittgenstein to me. I want the plan for explaining him. Do not summarise the source material, do not supply a bibliography or secondary literature, and do not quote passages at me; assume I know the texts cold and am only short on sequencing.

**1. Dependency structure.** Lay out the order in which the components must be introduced, and for each, what it depends on and what it unlocks. In particular, I want to know where in the arc you separate three things these students will otherwise fuse: what a belief says about the world, the work it does in the believer's reasoning and conduct, and the grounds on which it is held. If Wittgenstein's claim cannot be reached until some prior distinction is in place, tell me which one, and how long that costs.

**2. The carrying case.** The example must not be any of the three that come to mind first — the Last Judgment discussion, the exchange with the Catholic priest who was also an electrical engineer, or the Austrian general's remark near the start of the first lecture. All three arrive pre-coded as religious material, which will start the believer-versus-sceptic fight in the room before the concept exists to settle it. They may reappear at the end as confirmation, once the students hold the idea from elsewhere. Instead, design a secular case for me, and justify it against the following constraints: it must be ordinary and non-pathological; held with a conviction visibly out of proportion to the evidence; a case in which an outsider's natural response is emphatically not "well, perhaps, after all it's not impossible"; and a case in which what would count as decisive counter-evidence can be stated by the holder, yet encountering it would not prompt revision — where you can then show that this is neither stubbornness nor self-deception, because the holding is doing something no weaker, updated version could do. It must contain no ritual, sacred text, religious community, or supernatural agent, so that nothing is smuggled in by the trappings; it must not be reducible to an emotion; and it must not be a stipulation or a rule of a game, or I lose half the room to "so it's make-believe."

**3. The misconception map.** Give me at least five, and for each: where in the sequence it arises, whether to head it off pre-emptively or let it surface and be answered, and the shortest move that defuses it. Include at minimum the readings that he is a verificationist in reverse, that religious believers do not really believe what they say; that his claim is an empirical-psychological thesis about what happens in believers' heads; that the view renders religious claims neither true nor false and thereby immune to criticism; that it collapses into relativism about whole ways of living, such that disagreement becomes impossible; and that "unshakeable by ordinary evidence" is a polite way of saying irrational. Also tell me which single objection I should deliberately refuse to answer at the moment it is raised, letting it sit until a later stage, and why deferral teaches better than rebuttal there.

**4. Board work.** At most four things should ever be written on the whiteboard. Tell me what they are, when each appears, and what each one is there to prevent.

**5. What I decline to cover, and say so.** Name the material this level cannot support — the private language and rule-following considerations, the technical weight of forms of life, the early work, the remarks on Frazer, the commentators' fideism dispute, the question of Wittgenstein's own religious sincerity — and for each give me the one honest sentence that marks the gap as a choice rather than an oversight.

**6. The most likely point of failure.** Identify where this plan is most likely to come apart with this particular group, and give me a fallback that costs no more than five minutes.

**7. The check.** Design the comprehension test as a short invented vignette rather than a request for a definition. Tell me the answer that shows the idea has landed, the specific wrong answer that shows they have psychologised it, and what I should do with that wrong answer in thirty seconds at the end of a ninety-minute class.
