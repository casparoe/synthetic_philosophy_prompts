**Question 2 (25 marks; word limit 1,200 words)**

Putnam asks us to imagine Twin Earth: a planet indistinguishable from Earth in every macroscopic respect, whose inhabitants are psychological duplicates of us and speak a language they call English, but where the clear, drinkable liquid in the rivers, lakes and rain is not H₂O but a chemically different compound, abbreviated XYZ. He sets the scene in the middle of the eighteenth century, when the chemistry of neither planet could have revealed the difference.

(a) [10 marks] Set out the argument Putnam draws from this case against the claim that meanings are states of the head. In your answer, explain the role played by the stereotype and the role played by what Putnam treats as the indexical element in the meaning of a natural kind term.

(b) [10 marks] Now consider a planet we shall call Dirth. Dirth is in every respect like Earth: its lakes and rivers contain H₂O, and the Dirthians are psychological duplicates of us. But the Dirthian word 'water' was introduced, some centuries ago, by a castaway who had spent her whole life on Twin Earth. She baptised the liquid in a Dirthian lake by holding up a flask of XYZ that she had brought with her and stipulating that whatever is the same liquid as this is to be called 'water'. Since then the Dirthians have used the word entirely in the ordinary way — swimming in it, drinking it, buying and selling it — and no sample of XYZ has survived anywhere on the planet. On Putnam's account, what does the Dirthians' word 'water' refer to? Does your answer show that reference is fixed once and for all by an original act of ostension, or that it depends on the causal contact a community keeps up with its environment? Explain the considerations that tell on each side.

(c) [5 marks] Oscar is transported in his sleep from Earth to Twin Earth and lives out the rest of his life there, never learning that anything has changed. Does his word 'water' come to refer to XYZ? Say what, if anything, in Putnam's framework settles the question.

**Submission (960 words)**

(a) Putnam's argument begins from the observation that Oscar and Twin Oscar are duplicates in every psychological respect. Each associates 'water' with the same body of belief — that it is a colourless, tasteless liquid, that it falls as rain, fills lakes, quenches thirst — and each is disposed to apply the word in the same circumstances. Yet, Putnam argues, the extension of Oscar's term is H₂O while the extension of Twin Oscar's term is XYZ. Since the two speakers are in the same psychological state while their words differ in extension, psychological state cannot determine extension; and since Putnam identifies the meaning of a natural kind term with that which determines its extension from world to world — an intension, construed as a function from possible worlds to extensions — the conclusion carries over from extension to meaning. What a speaker means is not fixed by what is in his head.

Two pieces of apparatus do the work. The first is the stereotype: the loose body of beliefs and expectations a speaker must associate with a term in order to count as using it competently. The stereotype for 'water' is much the same on both planets, which is precisely why it cannot account for the difference in extension. The same apparatus explains Putnam's other examples: a speaker who cannot tell an elm from a beech nonetheless uses 'elm' with a different extension from 'beech', because the difference is not carried by his own knowledge but by a division of linguistic labour, which routes the application of the term to those in the community who can tell them apart. Putnam reinforces the general point with a second case, that of the man who believes he has arthritis in his thigh and is told by his doctor that this cannot be so, since arthritis is an inflammation of the joints. Because the man's belief is false rather than merely differently worded, its content cannot have been settled by what was in his head; it was settled, as with 'water', by the environment in which he is causally embedded.

The second piece of apparatus is the indexical element. Putnam treats a natural kind term as carrying a condition to the effect that whatever falls under it must be the same liquid as a paradigm picked out ostensively in the speaker's environment. The paradigms differ across the two planets, so the extensions differ, even though the stereotypes coincide. This is also why Putnam does not treat Twin Earth as a possible world in which water is XYZ: because natural kind terms are rigid, our word 'water' picks out H₂O in every possible world, so Twin Earth is a different context of use of a word that sounds like ours rather than a counterfactual situation for ours.

The date at which the scenario is set matters more than is often allowed. In 1750 neither Oscar nor Twin Oscar can say what his word picks out, and it is natural to conclude that at that date 'water' had not acquired a determinate microstructural extension on either planet, so that both terms ranged over the same disjunctive kind, any clear drinkable liquid of either composition. On this reading the extensions come apart only once chemistry supplies a concept capable of distinguishing them, and Putnam's case is best understood as a claim about how scientific discovery progressively refines reference rather than as a standing difference between the twins. The reading is more economical, since it does not require us to say that ordinary speakers are already tacitly tracking microstructure, and it costs nothing: reference is still fixed by facts outside the head, only over a longer timescale.

(b) The baptism on Dirth pointed at XYZ. Since Putnam's indexical condition is supplied by an original ostension, the Dirthian term carries the requirement that its instances be the same liquid as that sample; the sample having perished, nothing on Dirth satisfies it. Dirthian 'water' therefore has an empty extension: their lakes contain something that is not water, and their ordinary claims — that water is drinkable, that water is what falls as rain — are false, however reasonable they sound to the speakers. The case resembles a term introduced for a planet that turns out not to exist: the ostension was performed, but it latched onto nothing durable.

This shows that reference is fixed once and for all by the original act of ostension. The causal contact a community subsequently maintains with its surroundings cannot alter what its terms refer to, because if ongoing contact could shift the reference, then reference would after all be settled by the states of the speakers who are in that contact, and Putnam's conclusion would collapse. The Dirthians' total ignorance of their own predicament is exactly what an externalist account should predict.

(c) Oscar's usage inherits the baptism performed on Earth, so on the account given in part (b) his word continues to refer to H₂O throughout his life on Twin Earth, even though every sample he drinks, swims in and boils is XYZ. His belief that water is wet remains true; only his beliefs about what is in his glass are false, and he could not discover as much without chemistry. Putnam's framework does not, however, settle the matter as easily as that. If reference were determined by ongoing causal contact rather than by the original ostension, then after decades of exclusively Twin Earth contact his term would have shifted to XYZ, and the theory supplies no criterion for how much contact, sustained over how long, would be required to displace a paradigm. The difficulty therefore arises only for the version of the account that makes contact matter. That is worth noticing, because it suggests that what is really driving the judgement that Oscar still means H₂O is our sense that his intentions and memories are continuous with his Earthly upbringing, rather than anything in Putnam's analysis of the term itself.
