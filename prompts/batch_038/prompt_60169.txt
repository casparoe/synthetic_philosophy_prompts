I write the ideas column for a weekly — normally science and cognition, but this month I've been reporting a piece on immigration and work, and one sentence has turned up in nearly every interview I've done: immigrants do the jobs the rest of us wont do. I've heard it from a nursing-home operator who means it approvingly, from a union organizer who means it as an indictment (the jobs are terrible, that's the whole point), and from an economist who told me the sentence is worthless until somebody names a wage. That last one is where I'm stuck, because I want to build my nut graf around the phrase and I have no idea which version of it I'd be signing up for.

Can you take the sentence apart for me? I want the different claims it could be making laid out as a menu, not as one settled meaning. My guess is that the load is on "won't," but there's probably stuff hiding in "the jobs" too, and in who counts as "the rest of us." For each reading: what it actually asserts, whether it's a claim about willingness or about ability or about legal permission, whether it's trivially true / substantively true / probably false, and what you'd have to go look at to settle it.

Three more things. Some people use the sentence to argue that immigration therefore can't be dragging native wages down — tell me which reading, if any, actually licenses that inference. There's also a chicken-and-egg worry I can't quite articulate, something about whether a job is badly paid because nobody wants it or unwanted because it's badly paid, and whether it changes what "the jobs" even refers to once people start filling them — if that's a separate reading, say so and put it on the list. And finally: which reading does each of my three sources need for their point to survive, are those readings compatible with each other, and is there any version of the sentence I can print without it being either empty or false?

Keep it to something I can read in ten minutes. No citations or reading list — I just need the distinctions and your verdicts on them.
