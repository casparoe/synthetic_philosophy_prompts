Quick request, and it's a slightly odd one. I'm co-teaching a grad seminar this term with a colleague over in CS — something grandiosely titled "Minds, Models, and Meaning," first half philosophy of mind, second half mechanistic interpretability — and week six is where the two halves are supposed to actually collide. What keeps happening instead is that everyone slides between registers without noticing. My students will say "the network represents that the input is sarcastic" as a loose manner of speaking, then two sentences later as a hypothesis about activation geometry, then as a claim about causal role, and then as a metaphysical commitment about content, and it's never clear which one they mean or whether they'd want all of them to come out true together. The CS students tend to think the question "but does it *really* represent that?" is either settled by their sparse-autoencoder plots or a verbal worry not worth an hour of seminar. The philosophy students split into people who think it's a hard question and people who think its a pseudo-question, and both camps use the same vocabulary. Before we argue about it I want everybody sorted by what they're actually committed to.

So: build me a decision tree that sorts someone by their commitments on whether neural networks have representations. Five or six branch nodes, eight to ten leaves, and write each node as a concrete question a student could answer without already knowing which position it leads to — nothing like "are you a realist about features?", because that just sorts people by the label they like. Give me the logical space first: the questions, and the combinations of answers they permit. Then locate the actual positions in that space. For any combination nobody occupies, tell me whether that's because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored, and one sentence on why. Flag any two leaves your tree can't genuinely separate rather than papering over it — I have a suspicion that at least one pair of views I've been treating as rivals will collapse into the same leaf, and I'd rather see that stated up front. Each leaf should get a name (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it) plus one sentence on the most awkward thing it has to say about one of the cases below.

The nodes should be driven by the cases that actually generate the disagreement, so I want these in there somewhere: (a) a linear probe recovers "the input is sarcastic" from a middle layer with high accuracy, but causal interventions on that direction change nothing downstream; (b) the classic polysemantic neuron that fires for cat faces and car fronts, and, after decomposition, a direction that lights up for pictures of bridges, the word "bridge," and engineering text about spans — plus those feature-clamping demos where a model just can't stop working one San Francisco landmark into every answer; (c) the fact that there seem to be more features than dimensions, so no clean one-feature-per-direction story is available; (d) a model that in one context treats the Eiffel Tower as being in Paris and in another treats it as being in Rome, and the question of whether it then has both representations, one confused representation, or whether "has a representation" just doesn't apply; (e) a state that reliably tracks something about the training environment which the model can't use for anything. For leaves I need, at minimum: a straight realism on which features are out there and our instruments find them; a view that makes representation depend on the state being consumed by the model's own downstream machinery; a teleosemantic option that treats training as a selection process; a Dennettian pragmatic option on which ascription is licensed by predictive success and there's no further fact of the matter; an indeterminacy view on which two equally adequate ascriptions can both fail to be uniquely correct; a minimalist or deflationary view; a biochauvinist view that denies genuine representation absent consciousness; and flat-out eliminativism about representation talk in favor of dispositions. My own hunch, for what it's worth, is that the real fault line isn't realism versus anti-realism but whether causal role or mere decodability is doing the work — but don't build the tree around that hunch if it isn't where the space actually splits, and if you think one of the leaves I listed is a fake leaf that belongs nested under another, say so and give me the honest tree instead.
