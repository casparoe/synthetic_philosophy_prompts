I am working through Hume's monetary essays in the Political Discourses of 1752 — the ones on money, on interest, and on the balance of trade — and I have hit a knot I cannot untangle. I would like you to explain the conceptual issue to me as precisely as you can.

Here is the knot. In the essay on money Hume says that money is "none of the wheels of trade," that if we consider any single kingdom by itself "the greater or less plenty of money is of no consequence" because the prices of commodities are always proportioned to the plenty of money, and that the only advantage of a greater plenty accrues to the public, and even then only in its wars and negotiations with foreign states. In the same body of essays he nonetheless tells the magistrate that his good policy consists in keeping the money, if possible, still increasing, because an inflow keeps a spirit of industry alive during the interval before prices catch up, and because the stock of labour is a nation's real power and riches. And in the essay on interest he denies that the quantity of money determines the rate of interest, locating it instead in the demand of borrowers, the riches available to answer that demand, and the profits of commerce. So money is a counter whose number does not matter; yet more of it arriving is a good thing; and its quantity is not even what makes credit cheap.

The part I find hardest is his proposal, in the essay on money, concerning a bank that "locked up all the money it received" and never returned any part of its treasure into circulation, which he calls the most advantageous bank of all, on the ground that the national advantage resulting from "the low price of labour and the destruction of paper-credit" would compensate the state for the salaries of its directors and tellers — while adding that so large a sum "lying ready at command" would be a convenience in times of public danger and distress. That recommendation seems to contradict both of the other strands at once. It deliberately withdraws circulating money in order to make labour cheap, which is exactly what the advice about keeping money increasing tells the magistrate not to do. It treats a low price level as a national advantage, which the comparative-static claim says cannot be an advantage for the internal economy at all, only for underselling foreigners. And the hoard is supposed to be valuable precisely in an emergency, though on Hume's own account the public's advantage in an emergency comes from money being in circulation and actually commanding labour and foreign supplies, not from sitting in a vault.

My own diagnosis, which I want you to test rather than merely report, is that the knot dissolves once the relata are separated: the "of no consequence" claim concerns the level of money in a settled state, the advice to the magistrate concerns the growth rate along the path toward such a state, and the two reasons Hume gives for wanting more money are of different kinds — the state-power reason is positional, depending on other countries' price levels being lower, while the industry reason is transitional, depending on wages and prices adjusting at different speeds and on the point at which the new money first enters. The flow mechanism he describes in the essay on the balance of trade, whereby money drains away from wherever it has become too plentiful relative to the commodities to be circulated, then makes the magistrate's target unstable in any case. If my separation is right, though, Hume owes three things he may well not supply: an account of what determines the speed of adjustment; a story about whether the awakened spirit of industry leaves a permanent residue once the inflow stops, since if it does then money is not neutral after all, and if it does not then the policy is a treadmill that his own attack on the jealousy of trade would condemn; and some reason for the entry channel of the new money to matter at all, given that he denies the quantity of money any effect on the rate of interest.

Please explain the issue along those lines. Tell me whether separating level from growth rate, and positional from transitional reasons, actually rescues the position or merely relocates the problem; work through the lock-up bank proposal in particular, deciding whether it is a genuine inconsistency or something that resolves once one distinguishes money in circulation from money as a dormant claim on other people's labour; and end by saying which strand, if any, has to be given up for the rest to cohere. If you think my diagnosis is wrong, say so plainly and give me the better one. Work entirely from your understanding of the essays — I do not want a bibliography, page numbers, or a list of further reading, and where you cannot recall the exact wording of a passage, paraphrase it in your own words rather than guessing at a quotation. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Somewhere around a thousand words would be right.
