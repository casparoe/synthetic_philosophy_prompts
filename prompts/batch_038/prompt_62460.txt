I am a fourth-year student double-majoring in philosophy and environmental studies, and this term I am taking a seminar on the ethics of food systems. Separately, I serve as the undergraduate representative on my university's Dining Sustainability Committee, which meets roughly once a month and is asked to rule on a steady stream of proposals that all sound morally urgent and are almost always under-specified: swapping the default protein at the main dining hall, replacing a share of the animal-protein line with a plant-based analogue, adopting a higher-welfare supplier at a substantial price premium, dropping a particular fish or a particular vegetable because of land conversion in another country. Our mandate is advisory. The contractor may decline any recommendation that raises its ingredient cost per meal by more than a stipulated margin, and the committee chair — an administrator with no philosophical training — has to be able to defend whatever we recommend in one page to the student newspaper. At present we have no framework whatsoever. Decisions are made by whoever speaks most confidently in the room, and the two of us with any background in animal ethics spend our allotted time re-litigating aggregation rather than evaluating proposals. What I would like from you is a decision procedure that a rotating student volunteer with no statistics training could run on a proposal in about two hours and hand to the committee as a completed worksheet, together with a short plain-language summary of its output.

The procedure should state, in the order the questions must be asked, what it is being compared against (the implicit baseline is usually the status quo menu, and half of our disputes dissolve or sharpen once it is named); which categories of harm are in scope; and how each category receives either a number or an explicitly bounded range rather than a false point estimate. Please keep three categories distinct that our committee routinely conflates: the farmed animals whose lives a proposal brings into or out of existence; the field animals — rodents, ground-nesting birds, invertebrates — killed in cultivating the crops the proposal requires, including crops destined for feed rather than for direct human consumption; and the wild animals displaced or supported by the land the proposal occupies or frees up. It must handle the feed-conversion entanglement explicitly, since the grain inside a chicken is grain that already carried a field-death burden, and it should tell the volunteer when a second count of that burden is double counting and when the two counts are genuinely independent. It must also take an explicit position on the creation problem: reducing demand means fewer animals are brought into lives that, on most accounts, are net negative, but those animals are not thereby harmed, and our committee has never agreed whether that counts as a benefit, a cost, or something outside the calculus altogether. Build in a sensitivity check for sentience uncertainty rather than burying a single invertebrate-weighting parameter, because the ranking of several of these proposals flips entirely on it, and tell me how to report a weight-dependent result honestly instead of laundering it into a single score. Finally, distinguish clearly which steps are matters of empirical input, which are matters of value that the volunteer cannot settle, and which must be escalated — and to whom.

Then run the procedure on three live proposals, using these stipulated figures so that the arithmetic has something to bite on: we serve approximately 4,000 dinners a night during term, and the incumbent default entrée is conventionally raised beef. Proposal one replaces that default with conventionally raised chicken at constant portion count, cutting ingredient cost per portion by roughly forty percent and, by the contractor's own estimate, the land embodied per portion by close to an order of magnitude, while multiplying the number of individual animals involved by a factor of several hundred. Proposal two replaces one third of the chicken portions with a soy-and-pea analogue at comparable cost, where the soy may originate partly from recently converted savanna in South America and the committee is split on whether harms outside our direct purchasing chain fall within scope at all. Proposal three sources pasture-raised poultry for half of the chicken portions at three times current ingredient cost; the contractor projects an eight percent decline in meal-plan participation as students eat off campus, so the proposal's local effect is partly offset by consumption we cannot observe, and I want the procedure to say whether such market-level and substitution effects are inside its competence or outside it. At least one of the three applications should return a verdict I will not like: I suspect that under a defensible weighting the chicken-for-beef swap is not the improvement my committee assumes, and I would far rather see the procedure say so plainly than discover later that it was quietly engineered to produce the conclusion we arrived with. Mark every point at which the worksheet asks the volunteer for a judgment dressed up as arithmetic, and close by stating what this procedure cannot do — which questions it should never be used for, including whether it can be turned around to advise individual students about their own plates rather than institutional procurement. Please do not give me a reading list and do not cite particular studies; for each parameter, say instead what kind of empirical input it needs, whether our purchasing records could supply it, and what to substitute when the input does not exist.
