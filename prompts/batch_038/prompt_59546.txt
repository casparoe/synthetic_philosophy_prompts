I am a pricing actuary in general insurance, and my working vocabulary is credibility theory: expected process variance, variance of hypothetical means, the credibility factor Z = n/(n + K) with K = EPV/VHM, Bühlmann–Straub weighting by exposure volume when the number of observations differs across risks, and the fact that what I am doing is best linear unbiased prediction in a random-effects model. I am fluent enough in it that I can no longer think about a variance partition in any other terms. I have read enough quantitative genetics to follow the algebra — additive genetic variance over phenotypic variance, response to selection as heritability times the selection differential — but I do not know the field's idioms, and I have noticed that its practitioners are unusually insistent that heritability means far less than journalists and policymakers take it to mean. I want to know whether that insistence is a substantive claim or a ritual disclaimer, and I think the fastest way to find out is to force it through my own framework.

So: take the deflationary position on heritability — the bundle of things a quantitative geneticist says when someone over-reads a published estimate — and restate it claim by claim in credibility vocabulary. For each claim, mark it as translating cleanly, translating only with distortion, or having no counterpart in credibility theory at all, and where you distort something, say exactly which assumption you bent. Please run through at least the following:

1. That a heritability estimate is a property of a population in a given range of environments rather than a property of a trait, so the same trait can carry very different estimates in two well-run studies and neither is wrong.
2. That heritability can be close to one while a difference in group means is entirely environmental — the illustration with one mixed seed lot sown into two trays of different soil, where nearly all variation within each tray is genetic and all of the difference between the trays is not.
3. That it licenses no statement about how any particular individual's phenotype was produced, and in particular that high heritability is compatible with the trait being wholly preventable or wholly curable by changing the environment, as with a metabolic disorder managed by diet.
4. That broad-sense and narrow-sense heritability come apart, and that only the narrow one earns its keep, because it is the quantity that predicts response to selection.
5. That the partition itself is not stable: genotype–environment interaction and correlation mean the components move when the environment distribution moves, including the much-discussed finding that the heritability of cognitive test performance differs sharply across socioeconomic strata.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want, in three pieces.

First, where the translation broke down, tell me whether that is a limitation of credibility theory or a sign that the geneticists' claim was less clear than it sounded. I want you to be blunt about item 1 in particular: does population-relativity do real work in their argument, or is it a caveat everyone mumbles before proceeding as though the number were a property of the trait?

Second, my own suspicion, which I would like tested rather than confirmed. Credibility theory is relentlessly individual-predictive: when I assign Z to a policyholder I am licensed to say something about that policyholder — this much weight to your own experience, this much to the manual rate. Nothing in the arithmetic stops me from reading heritability the same way, as the credibility attaching to your genotype in predicting your phenotype, and I suspect that reading is precisely the error the field is warning against. Show me where the two setups diverge structurally rather than rhetorically. If the divergence is only a matter of what may be inferred downstream from the same number, say so plainly; if my setup contains a feature — a random risk parameter that is fixed for the individual across periods, a stable environment distribution, a decision to be priced — with no genetic counterpart, name the feature and say what it does to the analogy.

Third, does the traffic run the other way? Credibility theory has resources that the heritability discussion seems to lack: heterogeneous credibility across risk classes, weighting by exposure, and a sharp distinction between the weight given to a group's own experience and the weight given to the collective rate. Is there anything in that toolkit that would let the genetics debate state one of its own points more precisely, or expose a place where its practice is sloppy — for instance in how it reports an estimate without reporting the environment distribution that generated it, which in my world would be like quoting a credibility factor without saying what collective you were credibilizing toward?

I am not asking for references or a bibliography; I want the translation and the accounting. Keep the mathematics at a level where I can check your algebra against mine, and do not flatter the analogy for its own sake. If the honest verdict is that credibility theory and heritability share a formula and a variance decomposition but not a subject matter, I would rather be told that in the first paragraph than after three pages of tidy mappings.
