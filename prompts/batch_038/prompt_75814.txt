I am an MA candidate in philosophy, currently enrolled in a graduate seminar on the later programmes of the Vienna Circle: physicalism, the project of unified science, and the visual-education work that grew out of Otto Neurath's Museum of Society and Economy in Vienna. Alongside the seminar I convene a peer-led revision workshop for the cohort, and for our closing session I have been asked to set a mock essay question of the kind that might appear on the seminar's own final. I would like your help drafting the question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The difficulty I am trying to solve is that this material has hardened into a set of stock summaries for us — the Viennese museum, Gerd Arntz's woodcut-derived pictograms, the team's dispersal to The Hague, the slogan "words divide, pictures unite" — and any question that can be answered by reproducing that narrative will tell me nothing about whether my colleagues have grasped the philosophical problem at the centre of the programme. I want a question that obliges them to decide something, and where the decision cannot be settled by recall.

So that the question is self-contained, please build the stem around the following two materials. The first is a passage from our reading packet, from *Anti-Spengler* (1921), in English translation:

"We are like sailors who on the open sea must reconstruct their ship but are never able to start afresh from the bottom. Where a beam is taken away a new one must at once be put there, and for this the rest of the ship is used as support. In this way, by using the old beams and driftwood the ship can be shaped entirely anew, but only by gradual reconstruction."

The second is a case I have constructed for the exercise. It is not a chart from the literature and must not be presented as one. Before the case, the stem should restate the design commitments our seminar isolated in Isotype (International System of Typographic Picture Education, originally the Vienna Method of Pictorial Statistics): that a greater quantity is shown not by enlarging a pictogram but by repeating an identical pictogram more times, each figure standing for a fixed value within that chart; that figures are drawn without perspective, so that what is compared is countable rather than apparent area; that each chart is produced from source data by a transformer, whose task is to decide what to group, what to omit, and what unit value to assign to a figure; that the charts were always accompanied by verbal titles and legends and were described by Neurath as a "helping language," indeed as a "language-like technique" rather than a language in its own right; that charts were produced in multiple copies for permanent and travelling exhibitions; and that the museum's stated trade-off was that "to remember simplified pictures is better than to forget accurate figures." The case is then this. A chart is to compare annual coal output across four countries, and the transformer has fixed the unit figure at one wagon per 100,000 tonnes. Three of the four outputs are integer multiples of the unit. The fourth is 1,350,000 tonnes. The transformer may show thirteen full wagons and a half-wagon, which destroys the identity of the repeated figure; or round the value, which makes the chart precisely wrong; or change the unit so that all four values come out as integers, which silently alters what every wagon in the series means and, given the re-use of charts across exhibitions, what previously published charts said.

The question you write should ask candidates to explain why this is a philosophical problem rather than a drafting inconvenience, and to use it to assess whether Neurath's picture language is a language in anything more than an honorific sense. In particular it should require them to state what a representation must be able to do in order to be the sort of thing that can be true or false, can be judged mistaken, and can stand in relations of contradiction or support to sentences — and then to say how far the design commitments above help or hinder that. It should also require them to bring in the first material and to say whether the sailor passage gives Neurath a resource against the objection that a standardized, institute-maintained symbol vocabulary is exactly the dry dock his own epistemology forbids. Please leave the direction of the better answer open: a candidate should be able to argue either that the concession to a helping language is a retreat that surrenders the philosophical ambition, or that it is the honest conclusion of the programme, and should be marked on the argument rather than on the verdict.

Constraints and deliverables. The question is to be answered in 45 minutes, in no more than 900 words, and is worth 25 marks; please state the word limit and the mark allocation on the face of the question. Then give me a model answer of roughly 900 words that would earn full marks. Then give a marking scheme totalling 25, broken into named moves with a mark value attached to each, followed separately by a short list of the specific mistakes that should cost marks — I have in mind errors such as reading the first material as a claim about a coherence theory of truth rather than about the revision of beliefs; concluding that because a picture cannot be false the programme collapses, without noticing that verbal titles and legends were built into the charts from the outset; crediting Neurath with a verificationist theory of meaning he did not hold; treating standardization of a symbol vocabulary as though it were automatically a foundationalist commitment; and answering only the technical question of how best to display 1,350,000 tonnes. Finally, in a closing paragraph, tell me what separates a first-class answer from a competent but unremarkable one, in terms of moves rather than coverage.

Two further constraints. Please do not require or supply citations, page references, or a reading list: the workshop is closed-book apart from the two materials in the stem, and I would rather the model answer not gesture at literature my colleagues cannot consult. And please keep the model answer's claims about Neurath within the materials supplied above; wherever the argument needs a further fact about the programme, formulate it as a conditional about what would have to be the case, rather than as an assertion about what Neurath did or said.
