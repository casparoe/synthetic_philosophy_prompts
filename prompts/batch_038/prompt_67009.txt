I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the past two years a disproportionate amount of my copy has involved how officials and commentators reason from historical precedent. In the course of that reporting I have become convinced that there is a specific failure mode at work, one I keep having to describe in clumsy circumlocutions, and I cannot tell whether I am laboriously reinventing a distinction that already exists somewhere in the literature on analogical reasoning in politics, or whether it genuinely lacks a name. I should say at the outset that I am not after a bibliography — assume I can verify nothing and will check nothing. What I want is the conceptual geography: the nearest established ideas, how my phenomenon differs from each of them, and, if nothing fits, a coinage with real boundaries.

Here is the pattern, as best I can describe it without smuggling in the vocabulary that would answer my own question. A comparison is offered, initially as a comparison: this situation resembles that earlier one in these respects, so we should expect a comparable outcome. It circulates, gets compressed into a short label, and after a few years the label is being used as the name of a *kind of situation* rather than as an assertion of likeness to a particular past — people write "we are in another Munich" or "this is a Sputnik moment" or "the two countries are in a Thucydides trap" with the same grammatical confidence as "we are in a recession." At that point the claim's exposure changes in a way I find hard to pin down. Objections that used to bite — the two cases differ in the number of great powers, in the technology, in what the actors believed at the time, in whether the earlier disaster was actually produced by the mechanism the label invokes — no longer register, because the speaker is no longer asserting a likeness that such objections could defeat; they are asserting a category membership. And yet the original case's outcome continues to travel with the label as an implicit forecast: to say that a situation *is* one of these is heard, by speaker and audience alike, as saying how it will end. So the term acquires the immunity of a definition while retaining the persuasive force of an empirical prediction. When I try to press the point I get caught in a small pincer: raise a disanalogy and I am told I am being literal-minded, since the phrase names a pattern rather than a case; suggest that the pattern then says nothing, and I am told that the historical record plainly supports it, with the original comparison wheeled back in as its evidence. The same career seems to have run, with variations, for "quagmire," for the rising-power-and-fearful-incumbent phrase built out of one line in an ancient history of the Peloponnesian War, and — outside foreign policy entirely — for "Sputnik moment," which now attaches to any jolt that reveals a country to be behind in something, whether or not anything about 1957 is being claimed.

So: does this have a name? I am aware that a good deal has been written about how the Munich lesson misled a generation of policymakers and about how analogies in statecraft get abused generally, and I have come across reification, dead metaphor, and concept creep in adjacent arguments — but each of those seems to me to describe something either broader or slightly different. Reification and dead metaphor cover the loss of figurative force in a term without, so far as I can see, capturing the specific double bind: that the label keeps the source case's *conclusion* as an unearned prediction precisely as it sheds the source case's *constraints* as defeaters. And the general complaint that political uses of history are unfalsifiable does not distinguish this from any other loose analogy. Please lay out which established concepts come closest, what each one covers that mine does not and vice versa, and which fits best. If the honest answer is that nothing fits, then define the thing for me and give me its edges: does it require that the label have begun life as a real comparison, or can a term be born as a category and acquire a spurious historical warrant afterward? Is there anything specifically wrong with the historical case, given that ordinary technical terms shed their origins harmlessly all the time — nobody thinks less of "atom" for no longer meaning indivisible? Where does this shade into mere vagueness? And is a disciplined version possible at all — can a diagnostic label drawn from a past episode remain a comparison, stay exposed to disanalogy, and still be usable in newspaper prose? Finally, if you think my carving is wrong — if the two halves of what I have described are really two phenomena, or if what I have circled is just a familiar sin wearing a new hat — say so rather than supplying me with a flattering term.
