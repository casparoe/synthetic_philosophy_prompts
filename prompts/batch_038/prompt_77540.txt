The two arguments run in opposite directions, and that's the main structural difference.

A Dutch book argument starts from the agent's degrees of belief. Suppose her credences violate the probability axioms — say she assigns 0.7 to it raining tomorrow and 0.6 to it not raining. A bookie who knows her fair betting prices can then construct a finite set of bets each of which she will accept individually but which together guarantee her a net loss, whatever the weather does. The conclusion is that incoherent credences amount, in effect, to inconsistent beliefs: the agent has committed herself to a set of claims that cannot all be true, and the book simply makes that contradiction visible in her wallet. Notice that this argument needs no assumptions at all about how the agent feels about ordinary options; it works directly on the numbers.

A representation theorem runs the other way. It starts with a preference ordering over acts, where an act is a function from states to consequences, and shows that provided the ordering is complete and transitive there is a probability function and a utility function such that the agent prefers A to B exactly when A has higher expected utility than B. The representation is unique up to positive linear transformation of the utility function. Savage's version of the theorem is stronger than Ramsey's in one respect worth flagging: Ramsey had to assume bounded utilities, so his construction breaks down for prospects with unbounded payoffs, whereas Savage's axioms allow unbounded utility and can therefore handle gambles of the St Petersburg type.

What both arguments establish is a conditional. They tell us that an agent who violates the norms is either practically self-defeating or not interpretable as having beliefs and desires at all, but they say nothing about whether her ends are worthwhile. Neither shows that the agent literally possesses numerically valued belief and desire states; at most she behaves as if she does. This is why coherence arguments are sometimes dismissed as only-Bayes results: they constrain the shape of rational agency while leaving its content untouched.

Both arguments also have a diachronic payoff, which is the part I think gets underappreciated. Because the representation theorem pins down a unique probability function, up to the transformations noted, an agent who satisfies Savage's axioms and then learns that E has occurred has thereby been shown to conditionalize on E: any other way of revising would leave her preferences unrepresentable in the same framework. So the coherence arguments establish not just probabilism and expected utility maximization but conditionalization too.

In short: Dutch book arguments work backwards from belief to choice and show that incoherence is self-defeating, while representation theorems work forwards from choice to belief and show that rational preference just is expected utility maximization. Together they support the standard claim that ideally rational agents are expected utility maximizers, while leaving entirely open which ends such an agent ought to have.
