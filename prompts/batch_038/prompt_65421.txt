I am two years into a novel whose central consciousness is a provincial collector of Lepidoptera in the 1860s — a man who begins the book certain that species are the fixed handiwork of a creator and ends it unable to say what it is he has been cataloguing all his adult life. To get the texture of that undoing right, I have been reading the first edition of *On the Origin of Species* slowly and aloud, and I have stalled not on the argument everyone quotes but on a few sentences at the head of Chapter II, "Variation under Nature." Darwin there declines to define his central term: "Nor shall I here discuss the various definitions which have been given of the term species. No one definition has as yet satisfied all naturalists; yet every naturalist knows vaguely what he means when he speaks of a species." Later in the same chapter, if I recall the drift of it correctly, he goes further and says that he regards the word as one given arbitrarily, for mere convenience, to a set of individuals closely resembling each other. What I actually need help with is a paragraph that falls between those two remarks, and that I find far harder to parse than anything in the famous fourth chapter:

> "There is one point connected with individual differences, which seems to me extremely perplexing: I refer to those genera which have sometimes been called 'protean' or 'polymorphic,' in which the species present an inordinate amount of variation; and hardly two naturalists can agree which forms to rank as species and which as varieties. We may instance Rubus, Rosa, and Hieracium amongst plants, several genera of insects, and several genera of Brachiopod shells. In most polymorphic genera some of the species have fixed and definite characters. Genera which are polymorphic in one country seem to be, with some few exceptions, polymorphic in other countries, and likewise, judging from Brachiopod shells, at former periods of time. These facts seem to be very perplexing, for they seem to show that this kind of variability is independent of the conditions of life. I am inclined to suspect that we see in these polymorphic genera variations in points of structure which are of no service or disservice to the species, and which consequently have not been seized on and rendered definite by natural selection, as hereafter will be explained."

Three things defeat me here, and I would rather have them untangled than be handed the state of play in twentieth-century philosophy of biology, which I can acquire for myself. First: when Darwin reports that "hardly two naturalists can agree which forms to rank as species and which as varieties," is he describing our failure to know a fact that is there anyway, or the absence of any fact to be known — and how much weight can that sentence bear, given that he then treats the disagreement among experts as evidence of something about the organisms rather than about the experts? Second: why should the persistence of polymorphism across countries and across geological time be perplexing at all? My guess is that it disposes of an explanation in terms of local conditions of life, but I cannot see what Darwin would otherwise have expected, or which rival account the fact was meant to favour. Third, and most important for my purposes: what does he mean by variations that "have not been seized on and rendered definite by natural selection"? Selection, as I understand him elsewhere, alters forms rather than sharpening categories, so the phrase implies something I cannot reconstruct — either that selection is what makes the joints of a genus visible to us, or that it is what converts indefinite variation into discrete, nameable kinds, so that the messiness of *Rubus* and *Rosa* is the standing residue of selection's absence. Tell me which of these readings the sentence supports, or propose a better one, and tell me whether the paragraph sits comfortably with the conventionalist remark about species later in the chapter or strains against it. My character's crisis turns on exactly that hinge: whether Darwin is saying that nature is jointed and we merely cut badly, or that in these genera nature is unjointed and our knife is honest.

Please argue from the sentences themselves and from the rest of the chapter as you know it; I want no bibliography, no reading list, and no names or dates of secondary literature, and I would rather you commit to a reading than hedge between several. End your reply with a single line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of the labels JOINTED-NATURE, UNJOINTED-NATURE, or HINGE-BY-GENUS, and then no more than fifteen words naming the reading of "rendered definite" you settled on.
