I am co-writing a paper on money pumps with a colleague whose verdicts on particular cases are firm and, so far as I can tell, mutually consistent. She says that: the textbook three-good pump, where the agent sees the whole cycle coming and could have declined the opening trade for free, is irrational; the same pump where the offers arrive one at a time from a trader whose pattern she had every opportunity to learn and did not, is irrational; the same pump where the only available precommitment device costs more than the pump extracts and she declines to buy it, is rational; an agent with cyclic preferences who is never offered a trade at any point in her life, is rational; an agent who travels all the way around her cycle by means of three costless swaps, ending exactly where she began with nothing paid and nothing forgone, is rational; and an agent with perfectly transitive preferences who takes out a subscription she foresaw she would cancel, paying twice when declining both was free, is irrational.

We are now stuck on this case: three unconnected traders each offer her one trade, each trade costs a cent and each is strictly preferable to keeping what she holds at the moment it is offered, there is no pattern she could have detected and no reason for her to expect further offers, and the third trade returns her to the good she began with — leaving her three cents poorer than if she had declined the first, which was free to decline.

What will she say? I want the rule or rules that her six verdicts are jointly consistent with, which of them decides this case and why, your best guess at her verdict with reasons, and one further case that would settle which rule she is running. My own suspicion is that the record leaves open whether she is assessing whole sequences or individual decision points, which is why we keep talking past each other.
