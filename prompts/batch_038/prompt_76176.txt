I spent forty-one years in analytical chemistry, most of them running a mass spectrometry group, and I have now been retired three years. Philosophy of mind has become my evening habit, and I have come upon a difficulty that I cannot set down, chiefly because it presents itself to me not as a metaphysical puzzle but as a broken experimental design. I should like a short story built around it.

The difficulty is this. Suppose the epiphenomenalist is correct: experience is caused by the brain and causes nothing in return — Huxley's old suggestion, in effect, that consciousness stands to the locomotive as the whistle does, making a noise and performing no work. Then my belief that I am conscious, and the sentences with which I express it, must have been caused entirely by neural events that would have occurred whether or not any experience accompanied them. A world in which I am lavishly conscious and a world in which nothing at all is felt should therefore generate, from my side of the bench, precisely the same testimony: the same confident descriptions of color, the same insistence that my pain is awful, the same claim to immediate certainty. My reports cannot serve as evidence for the thing they report. The more vividly I describe my inner life, the less that description is worth. I understand this has a name — the paradox of phenomenal judgment — and I find it harder to dismiss than any of the replies to it.

I want a story that enacts this rather than expounds it. The situation I have in mind: a phase II trial of a compound developed as an adjunct to general anaesthesia. The sponsor's hypothesis is that the compound suppresses a sustained posterior oscillation which the field has come to treat as the best available correlate of experience, while sparing everything the anaesthetist needs spared — discrimination, motor response, speech, memory consolidation. The pre-registered success criterion is that on every measure the trial collects (accuracy, latency, confidence ratings, the length and specificity of free verbal description, autonomic arousal, next-day recall) the dosed arm and the saline arm are statistically indistinguishable. The trial succeeds, and the sponsor intends to market it on the claim that patients remain insensate.

The narrator should be the study coordinator, who enrolled herself in a small single-blind crossover sub-study and, as she does in all her work, kept handwritten contemporaneous notes during each of her two sessions. In one of those notebooks she wrote something like "the light on the instrument housings is the yellow of a wasp's abdomen," and she cannot say which session produced it. Let the randomization code exist and be breakable, and let the breaking of it be the story's turn rather than its resolution: discovering which afternoon was empty does not make her notes from that afternoon any less vivid, and it makes the other notebook's identical confidence worse rather than better.

Please include one competent objector — a philosopher sitting on the ethics committee, say — who argues that the whole enterprise is question-begging, since the assay assumes that suppressing the oscillation suppresses experience, which no physical measure can establish, so that a null result was guaranteed by the design whichever way the metaphysics fell; and who notes that the sponsor's promotional claim rests on reports that patients would have given in either case. The narrator must not demolish him. His objection should survive the final page substantially intact.

On craft: between 1,800 and 2,800 words, first person, past tense. Give me at least two passages of concrete, particular sensory description that the reader is invited to enjoy, one of which must come from the unattributable notebook. Render at least one artifact as an artifact — a page of notes, an adverse-event line, a clause from the consent form. Invent whatever science you need; I would far rather you invent the assay than get it wrong, provided it stays internally consistent. Do not allow any character to deliver a lecture, and do not let the concept be named and explained as exposition; it must be carried by events, documents, and dialogue. Above all, do not resolve the metaphysics, and do not resolve it by revealing that the narrator was or was not a zombie. The ending should land on a particular thing done or seen, not on a reflection.

After the story, add a note of about a hundred and twenty words in your own voice identifying the single detail that is doing the most philosophical work, and stating what would have to be true for the story to constitute evidence on one side of the question rather than the other.
