I'm a software engineer on a team that builds retrieval-augmented assistants. I have no formal philosophy background, so please be alert to places where I've garbled the arguments I'm about to report. Two of my colleagues — I'll call them R and T — got into a sustained disagreement last month that the rest of us failed to referee, and I'd like an outside adjudication.

The occasion was a debugging session. We fed our model a satirical news article and then asked it factual questions about the article's content. It answered as though the piece were sincere, and it did so stably across many rephrasings of the questions. R trained a linear probe on the model's mid-layer activations that predicted, well above chance, whether a given article would be treated as sincere or as satire; R also found that adding the probe's direction to the activation vector flipped the model's downstream answers, and that the flip generalized to articles the model had never been tested on before. R concluded that we were now licensed to say the model believed the article was sincere, and that this belief is what caused the wrong answers. T said that conclusion doesn't follow and never could. Here is the exchange, roughly in order:

(1) T: Citing the belief explains nothing, because the belief isn't a further cause sitting alongside the mechanism — it is the mechanism. What produced the wrong answers is a particular configuration of activations interacting with fixed weights. If R insists the belief is something over and above that configuration, then we have two candidate sufficient causes for one effect, and R owes us an account of why the effect isn't systematically overdetermined, which is absurd for a thing that happens thousands of times a day in our own logs. T said this is an instance of an exclusion argument he'd read about, due to a philosopher named Kim, transplanted to our setting.

(2) R: That's a false dilemma. Either the belief just is the activation configuration, in which case saying the belief caused the output is entirely legitimate, since identity carries causal efficacy with it; or the belief is something distinct, but nobody on this team is claiming that. The overdetermination charge only bites if you first saddle me with a second, spooky cause and then object that I've multiplied causes. R added that the level of description R works at is the one that buys prediction and control: you cannot tell from the weights which new articles will be misread, but you can tell from the ascribed belief.

(3) T: If the belief just is the configuration, then belief talk is a redescription, and redescriptions don't earn causal credit. What does the work in the experiment is a direction in activation space that we can write into the residual stream. Note that we can write it into a run where the model never saw the article at all, and it then behaves as though it "believed" something it never encountered; and note that we can flip the behavior without touching the weights or anything R would call the belief. The belief merely supervenes and rides along while the physical state does the producing.

(4) R: Supervenience plus manipulability is what it takes for a higher level to be causal, not evidence that it isn't. Intervention is precisely how we test causal claims: we intervened at the level R describes — the axis carrying sincere-versus-satirical — and the effect moved the way R predicted. If that fails to establish causation, no experiment establishes causation. R also challenged the argument's setup: the exclusion argument assumes there is a well-defined, causally closed bottom level, and it's not obvious our system has one, since the "physical" description here is floating-point arithmetic spread over thousands of accelerators whose individuation depends on our own choices about batching, quantization, and hardware.

(5) T: Then R has abandoned the argument rather than answered it. If there's no well-defined lower level, there's no lower-level explanation for the belief to be excluded by, and belief talk has stopped doing metaphysical work and become an engineering heuristic — which is all T ever claimed it was. T also argued that swallowing widespread, non-accidental overdetermination would leave the concept of cause unusable, since it would license arbitrarily many co-causes for every effect.

(6) R: Not a retreat at all. The belief-level generalizations hold across training runs, model scales, and even other organizations' models, whereas any given activation geometry doesn't survive a change of hardware; the level that generalizes better is if anything the more causally serious one. And T is equivocating between producing and explaining: "what emitted these tokens" and "what accounts for this behavior" are different questions with different answers, and both are legitimate.

They asked me to write this up for someone without a stake, and here I am. Please go through the six moves in order and tell me, for each, whether it is sound, partly sound, or unsound, naming the specific error where there is one. Then tell me which of the two is closer to right overall, and — separately from that scorecard — identify the single premise or distinction the whole dispute turns on and give me your own view of whether it holds. Finally, tell me what neither of them raised that materially changes the picture.

One last request. I'm conscious that I've rendered a metaphysical argument in an engineer's vocabulary, and that some of what R and T said may be a confusion in costume. If that's the case — if the dispute dissolves once some term is disambiguated, or if move-by-move adjudication can't be done responsibly given the way I've reported it — please say so plainly instead of cooperating politely and handing me a scorecard for an argument that shouldn't have been scored.
