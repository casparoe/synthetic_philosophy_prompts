I'm sitting in a graduate seminar on the ethics of intellectual property and we've been going in circles on Locke for three weeks now. Half the class thinks the labor-mixing story justifies patents and copyright pretty much straightforwardly — you worked on it, you mixed your labor with it, so it's yours — and the other half thinks that's nonsense because ideas aren't the kind of thing you can enclose in the first place. Nobody's budging, and I've become convinced it's because everyone is using "own" to mean three different things at once. So here's the distinction I want to run with (my prof sort of gestured at it once and then dropped it): split any claim to own an idea into a credit claim (I originated this and deserve to be recognized as its originator), a control claim (I get to decide whether and how anyone else may use it), and a fruits claim (I'm entitled to whatever value its use generates). My hunch is that nearly all the intuitive force behind IP talk comes from the credit claim — nobody wants to say a discoverer deserves no credit — and then the control and fruits claims get smuggled along behind it as stowaways.

Write an essay that attacks or defends that hunch. If the credit claim is doing the real work, can it be satisfied by something that grants no exclusion at all — attribution norms, citation requirements, moral rights of authorship, prizes, a claim on a share of revenue but no right to say no? Press on the places where the three come apart. Independent simultaneous discovery is the obvious one — Darwin and Wallace, Newton and Leibniz, and a hundred less famous races — where two people mix equal amounts of labor into the same idea and neither can hold it exclusively; does that show the credit claim is really a claim about priority rather than about property? Then there's the invisible-labor case: the person who does the work and never publishes, or dies with the notebooks still in a drawer. On a pure labor theory they own it; on any theory that makes ownership do social work, they own nothing at all, and I want to know which of those consequences you're willing to swallow. And there's an inversion nobody in my seminar has noticed — Locke's proviso bites hard on land, where enclosure genuinely leaves less for everybody else, but ideas are non-rivalrous, so the proviso looks trivially satisfied for them, which would mean the scarcity IP creates is entirely manufactured rather then pre-existing. Does that cut for the enclosure story or against it? A lot of people think non-rivalrousness is what makes IP benign; I suspect it's actually what makes it harder to justify, since you're not resolving a natural scarcity but inventing one, and I want that argument made properly or demolished properly.

Take a side and argue for it. I dont want to end up with an "it depends on getting the incentive balance right" shrug — a scoped verdict is fine, something like "the credit claim survives, the control claim collapses, the fruits claim is contingent and has to be justified on a completely different route," but it has to be earned, not announced. Use whatever real cases help you — the fashion industry's tolerance of rampant copying, compulsory licensing for medicines, open source, sampling, patent trolls, academic scooping — as long as each case is doing argumentative work and not just decorating. Somewhere around 1,500 to 2,500 words. And skip the bibliography, I'm not handing this in anywhere, I just want the reasoning laid out so I can take it back to seminar on Thursday.
