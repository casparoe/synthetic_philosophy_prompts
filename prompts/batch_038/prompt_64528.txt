Here's the pile:

1. Beliefs are (ideally) degrees of confidence, and rationality is basically probabilistic coherence plus conditionalization. I don't think you need extra norms on top of that.
2. Self-deception is real and common, not just poetic talk — people genuinely end up holding things that contradict what the evidence available to them supports.
3. I reject intentionalism about it. The self-deceiver isn't running a scheme of the form "I will now cause myself to believe p." I think that's both incoherent and empirically unlikely.
4. I also reject Davidson-style partitioning into semi-autonomous sub-structures. It looks question-begging to me: it dissolves the paradox by stipulating two minds, and I want a single-mind story.
5. What I accept instead is bias in evidence handling — motivated recruitment of evidence, biased hypothesis testing, selective attention to confirming data, asymmetric memory. Automatic and sub-personal, no intention required.
6. Confabulation is pervasive. People are bad at knowing their own reasons, and introspective reports about one's own motives are barely better than guesses about a stranger's. So I put very little weight on self-report, including mine.
7. Even so, I think a self-deceived person is properly blamed, not merely pitied, for a good chunk of it. It's a character thing, not a disease.
8. Practically, I've been trying to fix this in myself with calibration practice: three-plus years of a prediction spreadsheet scored with a proper scoring rule, reviewed quarterly.
9. My own coinage, which I want you to stress-test rather than accept on faith: I call it the utterance floor. Any scoring procedure only reaches claims you actually uttered. If a motivated bias operates upstream — on which questions you bother to form a stated opinion about, which ones you file as "not my area," which ones you simply never get around to — then your calibration record can be genuinely excellent while you are still self-deceived about the thing you care about most, with no trace left in the data.
10. Corollary I believe: my own spreadsheet shows me well-calibrated on public events and on other people's projects, and noticeably worse on questions about my own career transition. I take that asymmetry as evidence the utterance floor is a real phenomenon and not just something I invented to feel better.
