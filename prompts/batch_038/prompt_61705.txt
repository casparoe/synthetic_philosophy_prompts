I maintain release tooling for a widely used open-source library, and I have been asked to give a talk to our engineering organization about what our compatibility policy actually assumes — not as folklore, but as a stated theory. Preparing it, I keep running into the fact that the software ecosystem already operates a de facto metric for how big a change is, and that metric looks a great deal like what people mean when they talk about building AI systems that avoid side effects. Semantic versioning sorts every change into three buckets: a fix that leaves the public interface untouched bumps the patch number, a backward-compatible addition — including the act of marking something deprecated — bumps the minor, and anything backward incompatible bumps the major. The specification defines a bug fix as an internal change that corrects incorrect behavior, which means a correction that alters what the software observably does cannot be a patch release at all; it is by definition breaking, even when it makes the software strictly better. Alongside this sits an observation named for the engineer who made it: "with a sufficient number of users of an API, it does not matter what you promise in the contract: all observable behaviors of your system will be depended on by somebody." The Linux kernel adopts the most extreme version of that stance as a rule of governance; when a subsystem maintainer suggested in 2012 that a regression was really the fault of the user-space program that broke, Linus Torvalds answered on the mailing list that "If a change results in user programs breaking, it's a bug in the kernel. We never EVER blame the user programs." Layered on top are deprecation windows — announce in a minor release, keep the thing working for months or years, remove it only at a major — plus one standing exception: security fixes ship immediately and are permitted to break whatever they break.

What interests me philosophically is that proposals for low-impact AI face the same design problem and solve it in incompatible ways. Some define impact as deviation from the starting state, so that any change to the world counts against the system. Some define it as deviation from what would have happened anyway had the system been switched off or never deployed, which makes inaction rather than the status quo the baseline. Some measure impact as a loss of reachability — how many states of the world the system can no longer bring about, whether or not anyone cares about them. Some measure it as the reduction in the system's ability to achieve a broad set of unrelated goals, on the theory that a change is serious to the degree that it forecloses options. Others give up on scoring the world at all and penalize change only to a designated list of variables that someone has declared to matter. Each faces the objection that you cannot say what a side effect is without already knowing what is valuable; each answers that objection differently, or claims not to need an answer.

So here is what I want from you. Reconstruct the theory of impact that these versioning practices presuppose: which baseline they measure against, what they treat as the locus of harm, and whether the appeal to user dependence is doing the work of a value specification — that is, whether the practice amounts to a bet that you can measure how much a change matters by what people actually rely on rather than by what is good for them. Tell me which of the candidate accounts above the practice is committed to, which it merely sits comfortably with, and which it rules out outright. Then audit the package for coherence: that an improvement counts as breaking while an addition does not, that announcing a removal is minor but performing it is major, that security patches bypass the whole scheme, and that everything below version 1.0.0 is exempt from it. Which of these are genuine tensions in the underlying theory, and which are pragmatic hedges that leave the theory intact? I also want the counterfactual worked out concretely: what would change in how we version, deprecate, and patch if we adopted a reachability-based metric instead, or one that scored changes by whether they serve users' actual interests rather than their present dependencies? Throughout, separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely permits, and mark the places where it is underdetermined enough that several incompatible metrics would license the same rules. I am not after a survey of options with the decision left to me — commit to the single best reconstruction of what we are doing, defend it against the obvious rival reading, and tell me plainly where you think the practice is philosophically indefensible rather than merely untidy.
