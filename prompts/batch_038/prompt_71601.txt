I work as a registrar in a mid-sized municipal museum, and on Wednesday evenings I play second cello in an amateur orchestra. Between those two lives I keep running into practices that seem to assume wildly different things about what a work of art is, and I would like help getting the assumptions out into the open rather than being told that this is all just market convention with no intellectual content. Start with my museum. Our treatment of an exposed forgery is total: a canvas produced ninety years ago by a skilled copyist working in the manner of a Dutch seventeenth-century master, and indistinguishable from the genuine article by every test we can apply — pigment chemistry, canvas weave, craquelure, and above all the naked eye of the visitor standing in the gallery — is not merely repriced when the attribution collapses. It comes off the wall, goes into a crate, and can thereafter only ever be exhibited as evidence of a deception, in a show about forgery. Nobody claims it has become worse to look at. At the same time we display posthumous bronze casts, made from the artist's own mould decades after the death, provided the estate authorized the edition and the number falls inside the declared limit; the identical object cast one time too many is a fake. And we label plaster casts of lost Greek bronzes as Roman copies and hang them in the main galleries, where a modern copy of the same statue would be a scandal. The operative property does not look like appearance, and it does not look like physical continuity with the artist's hand either, but something documentary.

Now the orchestra side. Nobody in my musical life thinks a performance can be a forgery. A period-instrument ensemble will commission brand-new copies of Baroque instruments from living makers, and critics will praise the resulting recording as more authentic than one made on modern instruments; a cellist playing a factory-made instrument from last decade is still, uncontroversially, playing Bach. Yet the physical instruments themselves command absurd sums: a fine cello by a living maker costs a small fraction of what an old Italian one costs, even though blind trials going back more than a century have repeatedly failed to establish that experienced players or listeners can reliably tell the two apart, and even though hardly any old instrument survives unaltered — necks reset, internal parts replaced, varnish retouched, geometry altered to suit modern pitch standards and modern halls. Within a single art form we treat the sound as freely reproducible and authenticable by ear, and the object as bearing a value that perception cannot detect.

So I would like a reconstruction of the theory of the artwork, and of value, that each of these practices presupposes. Does our treatment of the forgery presuppose that the work just is a particular historical object, such that a causal-historical property rather than any perceptible one carries the aesthetic weight — or something narrower, such as that the painting functions as testimony about what a person actually did, or that the market requires assets that are not interchangeable? Note that we do not destroy or exile the forgery for being bad; we exile it for being a lie, and I cannot tell whether that is an epistemic norm about an institution's truthfulness or an aesthetic one in disguise. Then: can any single theory simultaneously cover the authorized posthumous cast, where the paperwork seems to do the entire work, and the exposed forgery, where equally impeccable-looking paperwork fails to do it? Please distinguish carefully what these practices strictly presuppose from what they merely sit comfortably with, and tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where a causal-historical account, an account in terms of the artist's achievement and effort, a scarcity-and-asset account, and an account on which the art world's own conferral of status does the work would all license exactly the same behaviour in the galleries. Finally, tell me whether the museum and the concert hall can be governed by one account or genuinely require a disunified one, and what would concretely have to change — wall labels, acquisition and deaccession policy, insurance valuations, restoration practice, the pricing of instruments — if we replaced the presupposed view with the claim that a work's value is entirely a function of its perceptible properties.
