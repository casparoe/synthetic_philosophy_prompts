I run a small community foundation — six staff, about forty volunteers — and we administer a hardship fund whose donor deed says the money is for people whose difficulties arose without fault of their own. That's my paraphrase, the deed is worse. We get roughly 900 applications a year, grants run from 200 to 3,000 pounds, and each one is assessed by a volunteer with no legal or philosophical training, working from documents only: bank statements, a GP letter, a landlord's notice, a P45. Fifteen minutes a file. The written reason goes back to the applicant, and every refusal is appealable to a three-person panel.

Years ago a board member read something about luck egalitarianism — inequalities being unjust when they come from luck rather than choice, Dworkin's brute luck versus option luck — and it quietly became our unofficial theory. We've never managed to turn it into anything a volunteer can actually run. What we do instead has a phrase: going up the ladder. You ask whether the mess was a choice, then you ask what caused the choice, then what caused that, and we have no rule for how many rungs anyones allowed to climb before you stop and call the thing circumstance. Two volunteers will climb to different heights on files that look identical, and I have the appeal numbers to prove it.

So: can you write us the procedure itself? The questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each given only what's in the file, where the ladder stops and on what grounds, where the procedure terminates in a no, and where it has to stop and hand the file up to me or the panel because the question is one of values rather than fact. It has to be usable by someone who's never heard of any of this, and it can't assume we can investigate an applicant's character or intentions — file facts only. We've decided, I think rightly, that asking volunteers to judge someone's will is both impossible and demeaning, and I don't want that smuggled back in through a cleverer wording.

Then run it on three files we're arguing about right now. (a) A woman who borrowed to open a bakery two years ago; the business is dead, she has two kids and an eviction notice. (b) A man who gave up full-time work for fifteen years to look after his mother; she died in March, he has no pension and no recent work history at all. (c) A young man who rode without a helmet, crashed, and has a head injury that keeps him out of work. Be straight with me about (c) — I already suspect a strict procedure either refuses him or has to smuggle something in at step two, and I'd rather be told which.

And tell me where the thing will misfire. Which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, what a luck-egalitarian procedure structurally can't see, and — I've read enough of the criticism to know people say this whole approach humiliates applicants and abandons exactly the people a fund like ours exists for — whether your procedure can absorb that objection or whether you'd redesign around a different metric entirely. Finally: what would change your mind about the design you're recommending, and where is your reasoning most likely to fail?
