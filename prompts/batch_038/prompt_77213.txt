I have spent twelve years building storage and query systems for a company that sells analytics infrastructure, and I have no formal training in philosophy: two evening courses through a continuing-education program, and a good deal of reading on my own time, mostly seventeenth- and eighteenth-century material that I came to through puzzles about what it means for a system to have a state it is not currently consulting. I am applying for a one-year fellowship that funds independent scholars like me to write a substantial piece of work, and the application asks for a proposal of roughly fifteen hundred words. My project concerns Berkeley's master argument: the move from the observation that I cannot conceive of trees in a park or books in a closet with nobody by to perceive them, since in the very act of conceiving them I do perceive or think of them, to the conclusion that nothing can exist unconceived. I know the two objections that come up constantly in everything I have read. One says that conceiving is an act, and there is no reason why its object must itself stand in the relation of being perceived. The other says the argument slides between two readings, since what is supposedly inconceivable is the whole state of affairs of a thing's existing unconceived, not the thing's existing while unconceived. My hunch is that both objections, even where they are correct, are aimed at the wrong target, and I would like the proposal to convert that hunch into a question with a determinate answer.

Here is the hunch in more detail, and it comes out of my day job. In the systems I write, a thought about this particular row, this key, this shard is available only to a process that holds some channel to it; singular access is not something you can have at a distance, and everything that looks like it is either a description or a cached copy of an earlier contact. I want to read Berkeley's talk of conceiving along those lines: not as a modal or epistemic operator at all, but as expressing a grounding condition on singular thought, namely that to think of a particular thing as that thing requires acquaintance with it, and that the only form of acquaintance we have is perception. On that reading the master argument is not primarily a conceivability argument, and the reading-slippage objection never touches it; it is an argument about what it takes to refer to an object, and idealism becomes a thesis about reference rather than a thesis about what the world is made of. What I want the proposal to ask is whether that reading is faithful to the work the argument does for Berkeley, and if it is, whether it leaves the argument valid, question-begging against the materialist, or valid precisely in virtue of begging a question that the materialist cannot dispute without already assuming his side of it.

Please write the proposal itself, and in it I need the question stated once, precisely, in a form where a reader could tell whether it has been answered; an account of why the two standard objections do not already settle the matter, including a candid attempt to show that my acquaintance reading escapes the slippage objection without simply collapsing into the act-and-object objection, or an admission that it does collapse; the method, which is to say what I would actually do across the twelve months, roughly month by month, covering the formal reconstructions I would attempt and the internal evidence in Berkeley's own writings that I would test the reading against — one piece of evidence I already worry about is the dispositional gloss he offers when he says that to assert the table in his study exists while he is out of the study is just to say that were he in the study he would perceive it, or that some other spirit actually does perceive it, since that sounds like a claim about what would be experienced rather than about what it takes to think of a thing; a defence of this method against the obvious alternatives, which I take to be a close reading of the Dialogues undertaken for its own sake, a general defence or refutation of idealism, and a survey of the existing debate about the argument's validity; a description of what a successful result would look like, and of what would count as the project failing; and an honest assessment of whether this is achievable for someone with my background in the time available, together with what I should cut first if it is not. I do not want a bibliography or any list of references: describe positions and arguments in prose and I will supply citations myself before I submit. And please write it as continuous prose in paragraphs, the way one would write a letter or a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold or italicised emphasis, no tables — because I will be pasting it into a plain-text form and doing my own layout.
