I've been taking minutes for the governance council of a ~115-person intentional community for six years now (yes, six years of minutes), and we have a vote coming Thursday that I don't think our own track record cleanly settles. I want to know what consistency actually demands of us, not what vibe does.

First the record. Seven decisions since 2019, roughly in order:

- Tool library: after a year of hoarding and vanishing socket sets we adopted a strict sign-out sheet with escalating fines. Passed easily. Rationale in the minutes: the tools are a shared pool, and one person's "I'll bring it back later" is everyone else's missing tool.
- Noise: three straight years of complaints, and we refused to adopt quiet hours. Instead we designated one building the loud house and one the quiet house and let people move between them. Council's stated reason: any enforceable version would need somebody policing bedrooms, and sorting fixes it without that.
- Overnight guests: a cap of four nights a week was proposed and voted down, even though long-stay guests were visibly eating into the shared food budget. Reason in the minutes: unobservable in practice without a sheet at every door, i.e. you'd have to surveil who slept where.
- Work days: monthly Saturday chore days with chronic non-attendance from a handful of members. We did not make attendance mandatory; we created a pay-in-lieu option, $30 to the maintenance fund instead of showing up. Nobody on council objected that this let people buy their way out of community.
- Short-term sublets: banned outright, unanimously, including for a member who'd only ever done it once. Rationale: it removes a unit from our housing pool and we cannot replace that pool.
- Gossip and conflict: twice we declined to write anything down, on the explicit grounds that you can't legislate how people talk to each other, norms get cultivated by people modelling them.
- 2021 vax requirement for shared indoor meals, with a medical exemption: adopted, extremely contentious, four people left over it — and then we let it lapse last spring and didnt renew.

So some of those are tight and some are loose, and half the council has read enough of the tight/loose cultures stuff to argue about which kind of place we're supposed to be. That argument is useless. The question isn't which kind of place we are in the abstract, it's what our own seven decisions commit us to.

The new proposal, close to the proposer's wording: every adult member attends at least six of the twelve monthly all-hands meetings per year, compliance tracked by a sign-in sheet at the door. No payment in lieu — deliberately. The proposer says if you can write a check to skip it, the mandate is just a tax and it defeats the whole point. Their stated reason: the same fifteen-ish people have made every consequential decision for three years, the non-attenders still complain loudly about outcomes, and the council's legitimacy is quietly eroding.

Facts you'll probably want. Nobody can name an individual who has been harmed by someone else's non-attendance. The sign-in sheet is cheap and non-intrusive — it records presence and nothing else. There is no sorting option here, unlike with noise, because there's only one all-hands and it decides for everybody. The problem is a slow burn, not an acute threat. And the buy-out escape hatch is off the table by design, unlike work days.

What I want: which way should we vote if we want to cohere with ourselves? Specifically — (a) lay out the candidate rules our seven precedents are jointly consistent with; I can see three and I suspect there are five; (b) say which of those rules the attendance mandate passes and which it fails; (c) give me your best single guess at the rule the council has actually been running, and why you prefer it over the others that fit the record equally well; (d) name the closest of the seven to this case and say exactly where the analogy breaks; (e) if it genuinely comes out underdetermined, tell me straight what principle we'd be adopting by voting yes and what we'd be adopting by voting no, including which future proposals each of those would then force us to decide a particular way.

Please don't resolve it by proposing a watered-down amendment unless you can show the watered-down version is what the precedents point to. I get three compromise amendments a year and most of them are just people avoiding the actual question.
