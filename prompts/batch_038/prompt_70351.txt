I'm putting together a handout for a reading group on Lewis's *Convention* and I've hit a wall with the examples. Everyone in the group already knows the analysis cold — regularity, recurrence, the conditional preference clause, the mutual expectations, the near-indifference between equilibria, precedent supplying salience — so I don't want any of that recapped back at me, and I definitely don't want a survey of what the secondary literature says about whether he gets the definition right. What I want is one real episode run through it hard, in both directions. I've settled on Dagen H, the day Sweden switched from driving on the left to driving on the right, and I want to know whether Lewis's machinery can actually chew on it or whether it just gums it up.

The facts, so you don't have to guess at them: Sweden had driven on the left for most of the twentieth century, but nearly everyone drove left-hand-drive cars imported from the continent, most of the neighbours drove on the right, and head-on collisions on narrow two-lane roads were a real problem. In a 1955 referendum about 83 percent voted to keep driving on the left. The Riksdag passed Tage Erlander's proposal anyway in May 1963, setting the change for 1967, and a state commission ran a four-year public information campaign — a logo printed on milk cartons, a TV song contest, reminder items handed out to drivers. On the night of the switch, non-essential traffic was banned from 1am to 6am; any vehicle still on the road had to come to a complete stop at 4:50, move over to the right, stop again so others could cross, and only proceed at 5:00. Around 350,000 road signs were replaced, the new lane markings were painted in advance and covered with black tape, spare signal heads sat wrapped in plastic at every intersection. Only 157 minor accidents were reported on the day itself and motor insurance claims dropped something like 40 percent — but the accident rate was back to its pre-change level by 1969, apparently because the hyper-cautious behaviour was driven by a surge in perceived risk that decayed. Trams were scrapped in central Stockholm, Helsingborg and most of Malmö, the old left-door buses were sold off to Pakistan and Kenya, and the railways and the Stockholm metro never switched and still run on the left.

So: (a) Is this a Lewisian convention at all? Run the conditions one by one, but do it separately for 1955 and for 1967, because my hunch is they come out differently and that the difference is where all the interest is — in particular whether the near-indifference clause survives contact with the fact that there were first-order safety and economic reasons to prefer the right. (b) The referendum. Explain to me how 83 percent of a population can vote to keep the left and then comply almost perfectly four years later, without anyone's underlying preferences having changed — what does the conditional-preference clause predict here, and was the question the referendum asked even well-posed given that structure? Does Lewis's account make the Riksdag's override look undemocratic or look like the only way to get an accurate read on what people actually wanted? (c) The 4:50 stop. What does suspending play for five hours do to the coordination problem — is the state just deleting the game for one night so that no one has to coordinate while the equilibrium is in flux? And is the four-year campaign best described as the artificial manufacture of common knowledge, which would make this a strange vindication of Lewis's core insight via a route he explicitly rules out, since his picture is of conventions arising and persisting without anyone designing them? (d) Tell me where the theory is silent rather than making it speak: both the old rule and the new one were backed by statute and by sanctions, which is exactly what Lewis wants to keep separate from convention; the transition costs were enormous and landed very unevenly (bus fleets, tram systems, headlamps, signposts) and there's no place in the framework for who pays them; the salient precedent came from neighbouring populations rather than from the population's own history of solving this problem; and the railways and the metro staying on the left raises a question about how you individuate a convention in the first place. (e) Then give me a verdict. Does Dagen H confirm, strain, or refute Lewis? If he wanted to absorb it, what would have to be bolted on — some account of convention revision as a second-order coordination problem, where the coordination is over which equilibrium to move to and when? And separately: I vaguely remember Samoa flipping its driving side in 2009 largely for vehicle-import reasons, which sounds like a case where the indifference clause fails outright. Say whether that would be a better or a worse test of the theory, but don't worry about getting the details right, I may be misremembering it entirely.
