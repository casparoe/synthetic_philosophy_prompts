I grew up in a river town and work part-time in the county surveyor's office my grandfather ran for forty years. I have no philosophy training, though I have been reading on my own for a couple of years and have picked up labels like epistemicism and supervaluationism without being certain I have earned the right to use them. What makes me want a story rather than an argument is that the puzzle reached us as a piece of paperwork. Our river forms the boundary between two counties, and the rule this office still works under — a rule I gather is common in river jurisdictions generally — is that if the channel creeps, the boundary creeps with it, but if the channel jumps all at once in a flood, the boundary stays where it was, running down the middle of the abandoned channel. So a family keeps or loses a piece of bottomland depending on something that looks like a question of grammar about the weather: was the change gradual, or was it sudden? Eleven years ago our river began walking sideways without ever doing anything dramatic. There was no single night. The ledger the office keeps — weekly readings of the channel against a fixed range line, taken since 1947 — shows a slow creep interrupted by three storm weeks, each of which moved the water further than any ordinary week, and none of which moved it as far as the quiet weeks put together. Now the county across the water wants a certified date. A church cemetery that used to sit on our side is across the channel, the road to it is unmaintained, the parcel it stands on has been taxed twice or not at all, and somebody with a stamp has to say which of the eleven years did the deciding. Stranger than the case, to my mind, is a standing practice in the office: whenever a certification of this kind is prepared, two surveyors work from separate copies of the ledger and each marks, independently and without conferring, the day he or she would name as the crossing. In eighty years, on this river and on the others in the district, those two marks have never disagreed by more than one reading. My grandfather had a phrase for it. He said the line keeps itself.

Please write a short story of roughly 1,500 to 2,500 words out of that material, told from the point of view of the junior surveyor who has to draft the certificate by the end of the month. Set her against the retired director of the office, and let the disagreement be the engine of the plot rather than a topic the characters debate in the abstract. The junior surveyor has come to think the eighty years of agreement is evidence that there is a real date in the history of that river, one day and not the next, sitting there in the silt whether or not anyone can point to it, and that the office's habit of writing "on or about" on its certificates is a failure of nerve dressed up as caution. The retired director thinks the date is not in the river at all: that sudden and gradual are words that admit more than one equally acceptable way of being made precise, that a certificate is defensible only if it comes out the same on every acceptable way of sharpening them, and that eight decades of converging marks show how tightly the words hold the people using them, not that the water contains a fact. She should also be the one who explains why two signatures are required — so that no individual ever has to claim knowledge of the line. Give me three concrete things and no more than three: the ledger itself, including a predecessor's unexplained habit of pencilling a small X in the margin beside certain weeks for reasons nobody recorded; the certificate form with its two signature blocks; and the river in flood season as a physical presence, not a symbol. I want one scene in which the director asks the junior surveyor a single question — if your date were off by one reading, would you have any way of noticing? — and the junior surveyor cannot answer it. Nobody in the story should use the vocabulary of a philosophy seminar, or of my paragraph above; the ideas have to be carried entirely by the ledger, the pens, the form, the weather, and the road to the cemetery. And do not end by declaring a winner: end in a way that lets the reader feel what it would be like to live in a world where one of the two is right, whatever else remains unsettled.

After the story, add a short note rather than leaving me to guess. Tell me which of the two positions the construction of the story quietly favors, and by what means — whether, for instance, letting the two marks converge at all already smuggles in a claim the narrative has not paid for, or whether the ending's restraint is itself a way of siding with the director. Point out where you took liberties: I know that the view my grandfather's phrase gestures at is not simply the claim that there is a fact we happen to be ignorant of, and that the director's view is not simply that anything goes so long as it sounds reasonable, and I would rather hear you say where the story flattened either one than read something smooth. Most importantly, tell me what would have changed your mind about how to write it — which argument, objection, or consideration would have made you end the story the other way around, or made you cut the scene with the question in it — and where in your handling of the dispute you think you are most likely to be wrong or to strike someone who knows this literature as having committed a straightforward confusion. I would like that flagged plainly, in your own assessment, not hedged on both sides.
