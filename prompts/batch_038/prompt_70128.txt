I'm building a system that infers what an agent wants from what it does — inverse RL, basically — and I've been writing down my own gut verdicts on when the inference is legitimate, and they don't add up. Here they are:

- Delivery drone flies 400m out of its way to dodge a corridor with a ceiling fan. Infer it dislikes fans: fine.
- Robot attempts a grasp eleven times, fails every time, stops trying. Infer it didn't much want the object: not fine, that's obviously a competence failure, not indifference.
- Office worker buys the same dull sandwich every day though the good place is two doors down. Infer she prefers dull sandwiches: not fine — she's skint — but I'm loath to just call the budget a preference either.
- Researcher stops submitting papers after years of rejections. Infer she no longer wants to publish: I want to say not fine, but I can't say how this differs from the sandwich case. The shape looks identical to me.
- Same researcher, given three quiet years with no rejections, still doesn't submit. Now I do infer she doesn't want to.
- Rat presses a lever for a solution that makes it ill, and keeps pressing. Infer it wants the solution: fine, oddly, even though it's clearly worse off.

What principle sits behind all this? I'd like it stated tightly enough that I could implement it as a decision rule, and I want to be told which of these verdicts it forces me to give up rather than having all six saved by special pleading. Also what does it say about a case I haven't listed — an agent acting perfectly sensibly on a badly wrong model of the world? And does the sandwich/researcher difficulty have an established name somewhere else, since it smells like a known trap in economics as well.
