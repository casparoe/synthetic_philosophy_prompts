Bit of context first: I'm a history grad student, early modern mostly, so I'm out of my depth here, but I volunteered to present on Mill and India at a reading group on empire and political theory and I now mildly regret it, because Mill turns out to be much better at stating principles than at holding onto them when the principles get expensive. I want to work through one concrete episode and have the analysis run in both directions — what his theory says about what happened, and what what happened does to the theory.

The episode as I have it. Mill spent his whole working life, something like thirty-five years, at East India House, ending up as Examiner of Indian Correspondence. After the 1857 rebellion, when Parliament decided to abolish the Company and put India directly under the Crown, Mill wrote the Company's defence — the memorandum on the improvements in Indian administration, and the petition to Parliament submitted in February 1858. The Company was abolished that summer and he lost his post. In the petition he complains the Company is being destroyed "on the expressed ground that it has been too forbearing and considerate towards the natives," he defends its hands-off policy toward religious practice except where that practice was, in his phrase, abhorrent to humanity, and then — this is the part my group kept tripping over — he offers the rebellion itself as proof the policy worked, arguing that during the mutiny, which he says was caused by unfounded apprehensions of danger to religion, "the heads of native states, and the masses of the population, have remained faithful to the British Government." So the same event is treated by his opponents as the verdict on Company rule and by Mill as an exoneration of it.

Now set that against the theory. In On Liberty he allows that despotism can be a legitimate mode of government when dealing with peoples he considered not yet capable of governing themselves, provided the end is their improvement and the means are justified by actually bringing that improvement about. In Representative Government he argues that a dependency like India should be run by a professional body with genuine local knowledge, deliberately insulated from metropolitan opinion and from the churn of parliamentary politics. And in his essay on non-intervention he distinguishes cases where outside interference genuinely helps a people from cases where it just substitutes one foreign will for another, holding that a people can't be made free by someone else's arms. Here's what I want from you, in continuous prose only please — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just write it the way you'd write a long email, because I'll be reading it aloud. First, do the mapping carefully: who counts as the moral patient and who as the agent in Mill's framework, what exactly does the improvement proviso require, and what does his theory of dependencies imply the Company was for. Second, sort out whether his 1858 defence is even an argument from that theory. My own hunch, and please push on it rather than just endorsing it, is that there are two different justifications of foreign rule tangled together in Mill and that he slides between them: a tutelary one, where rule is legitimate because of what it does to the capacities of the ruled over time, and a custodial one, where rule is legitimate because it shields the ruled from other predators, including the ruler's own countrymen and their press and missionaries and merchants. The petition reads to me almost entirely custodial — the Company as a buffer against the British public — while On Liberty reads entirely tutelary. If that split is right, does the theory contain resources to connect them, or is the 1857 crisis what exposed the gap? Third, on the improvement proviso specifically: is there any state of the world that would have counted for Mill as showing the proviso unmet, and does his reading of the rebellion as a mutiny of sepoys stirred up by baseless religious panic, rather than as a popular revolt, function as a way of keeping the proviso unfalsifiable? If classifying the event is doing that much theoretical work, is that classification available to him without begging the question, and what would his own non-intervention principle say about a people trying to free themselves from a tutor who claims to be improving them? And fourth, the thing that I think is the most interesting and that nobody in my group could settle: the Crown took over and then kept essentially the same administrative machinery, the same civil service, and much of the same policy. Does that show Mill's theory was really about who administers, a caste of experts, rather than about legitimacy at all — in which case he lost in 1858 and nothing changed, so his alarm was misplaced? Or did the transfer alter something his theory can actually name, like accountability to an electorate that has no knowledge of India? And where is the theory simply silent — I keep feeling it has no way to describe what happens when one tutor is replaced by another tutor with the same structure and worse incentives, which is precisely what 1858 looks like from the Indian side. If you think the episode is a confirmation of his view rather than an anomaly, say so and argue it, but don't force the theory to speak where it has nothing to say.
