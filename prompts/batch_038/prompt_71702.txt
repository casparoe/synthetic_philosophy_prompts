You're a historian of 19th-century French political thought who also does a fair bit of work on contemporary memory politics — think of yourself as the person who gets asked to referee arguments between the archive people and the "let it go" people. Think carefully about this one and do some actual brainstorming before you settle on an answer.

Here's the setup. Let's say Ernest Renan is resurrected, spends a year reading up on everything that happened after 1882, and then has to give a position paper. I want you to predict where he lands on the modern duty-to-remember debate.

The anchor I'm working from is his famous Sorbonne lecture on what a nation is. As I read it, his central claim is that forgetting — and not just forgetting but a certain amount of shared historical error — is a condition of possibility for a nation, not an obstacle to one. Every citizen, on his account, has to have forgotten the sectarian slaughter that went into making the national community; unity was purchased with violence, and a nation survives only insofar as its members no longer have to know which side their ancestors were on. Nations for him are a kind of solidarity founded on a shared past that has been made non-obligatory. He's also explicitly rejecting race, language, religion, geography, and dynastic interest as the glue, and substituting something like a continuing collective act of consent — a plebscite renewed daily, as he puts the idea.

Now: the thing Renan could not have anticipated is that a bunch of modern states built their political identity on the exact opposite principle — a legally and institutionally enforced duty to remember. Germany is the obvious case, with its memorial landscape in the middle of the capital, its school curricula, and its criminalization of denial. Add to that the whole post-Cold War memory boom: truth and reconciliation commissions, reparations debates, museum mandates, the "never again" idiom as a live justificatory resource for military interventions, and the general expectation that groups get recognition via the acknowledgment of past wrongs.

So, concretely, what I want:

1. Start with a brainstorm — a rough, messy list of candidate positions Renan could plausibly take, including ones you think are wrong, and why. Don't tidy this up too much; I want to see the option space.

2. Then argue your way to an actual verdict on this question: would Renan regard a nation constituted around mandated remembrance as a viable nation, a pathological one, or something genuinely new that his framework doesn't have vocabulary for? Is remembering-the-crime, on his logic, just the old sectarian memory problem wearing a moral costume — i.e., do German schoolchildren being taught to identify with the perpetrators' descendants reintroduce exactly the "you have to know which side your ancestors were on" structure he thought had to be abolished? Or is there a version of remembrance that does what he wants forgetting to do, namely produce solidarity rather than grievance? I want you to pick a side and defend it, not hedge.

3. Place him in the current Margalit/Rieff dispute. Margalit argues in The Ethics of Memory that memory is a matter of ethics rather than morality — that thick relations, the ones constituted by a shared past, carry an actual obligation to remember, and that this obligation runs to particular people rather than to humanity in general. David Rieff argues the near-opposite in In Praise of Forgetting — that there is no such collective duty, that memory politics is usually ordinary politics using the past as ammunition, and that the "remember so it doesn't recur" rationale doesn't survive contact with the historical record. Which of the two would Renan find himself closer to, and — this matters more — on what grounds? I suspect the interesting answer is that he'd agree with Rieff's diagnosis but for reasons Rieff wouldn't accept, and agree with Margalit's premise about thick relations while denying his conclusion. Either confirm that or show me I'm wrong. Reason it out from Renan's own commitments: his anti-clericalism, his liberalism, his hostility to ethnic and religious foundations, his consent-based account of belonging.

4. Give me five specific predictions, each with a short justification:
   - Holocaust denial laws. Does he read these as the state taking sides in memory, and therefore as a threat to the plebiscite, or as legitimate self-defense of a political order?
   - Statue removal and renaming fights in the US and UK. What does he say about a country relitigating its founding violence in real time?
   - Permanent digital archives. This one interests me most: does his argument secretly depend on forgetting being a live technical option? If nothing can actually be lost anymore, does the theory survive, or does it turn into a theory about attention and salience rather than about retention?
   - Secession referendums driven substantially by memory politics — Catalonia, Quebec, Scotland. His daily-plebiscite idea looks vindicated by the form of these events; is it vindicated by their content?
   - Truth and reconciliation commissions, which are designed to produce a shared official record before asking for reconciliation. Is that, for Renan, a machine for converting forgetting into remembering, or a machine for making forgetting finally possible on honest terms?

5. Tell me one thing in the lecture he'd now disavow or heavily qualify, and one place where he'd say contemporary memory culture has the logic exactly backwards — something that looks like a moral advance to us and would look to him like a regression to pre-national forms of belonging.

I don't want a bibliography or a reading list, and I don't want invented page references. I want the reasoning.
