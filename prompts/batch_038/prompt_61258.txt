I am trying to work out what, if anything, unifies my judgments about actual infinities, and what I want is a principle rather than a survey of positions. Below are the cases together with my verdicts. I have tried to report them accurately, including where they conflict with one another.

1. **The natural numbers.** That there are infinitely many primes, and that they form a single completed collection, I accept without reservation. Whatever scruples I have about the infinite do not reach here.

2. **Hilbert's hotel, one arrival.** A hotel with countably many rooms, all occupied, admits one further guest by moving the occupant of room n to room n+1. I accept this as genuinely possible, not merely as a consistent description.

3. **Hilbert's hotel, countably many arrivals.** The old guests move into the even-numbered rooms and the new guests take the odd-numbered ones. Also accepted.

4. **Hilbert's hotel, countably many coaches,** each carrying countably many passengers, all accommodated by assigning rooms according to powers of distinct primes. Here I hesitate. I can follow the assignment and verify that it is one-to-one, but I suspect that what I am accepting is the adequacy of a notation rather than the possibility of a situation. My verdict: admissible, though I am not confident that I have conceived anything.

5. **Thomson's lamp.** A lamp is switched on and off at ever-shortening intervals so that infinitely many switchings fall within a finite period, and we ask what its state is at the moment the sequence completes. I reject this as impossible — not because I can exhibit a contradiction in the description, but because I have no idea what the state at the limit is, and I do not think the description settles it.

6. **An infinite plurality existing all at once.** A universe containing infinitely many concrete objects, particles spread through unbounded space, never assembled and simply there. I accept this.

7. **An infinite past.** Every event preceded by another, with no first event. I reject this. Something has to have brought us to the present, and an endless run of predecessors does not seem to do it.

8. **The immortal counter.** Someone has been counting down through the negative integers and reaches zero today. I reject this — but only just, and for a reason I distrust, namely that infinitely many days would have had to elapse one after another. That reason appears to apply with equal force to case 2, in which infinitely many guests each perform a move, and yet I accept case 2 without a qualm. And case 6 contains just as many objects as case 7 contains events.

9. **The collection of all sets, or of all ordinals.** I reject these as well, but I believe the objection differs in kind: what troubles me is comprehensiveness rather than infinity, since the sheer size does not bother me in the way it does in cases 5 and 7.

10. **The case I keep returning to.** A shelf in a library that has always existed, on which one new volume has been placed each day for as long as there have been days. The volumes now on the shelf form a collection that exists all at once, after the manner of case 6, yet it was produced step by step, after the manner of case 7. I have no settled verdict here.

My tentative principle is that a totality is admissible when it is given all at once and inadmissible when it must be formed by successive addition. I expect this is wrong, or at least incomplete: case 5 seems to be given all at once by its own description and I still reject it, and the moves in case 2 are themselves ordered in a sequence, so I cannot tell what "all at once" is doing there.

What I would like from you:

- A principle, or a short list of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I had never met before.
- For each clause, the case above that motivates it.
- The principle's ruling on case 10, together with its rulings on at least three further cases of your own choosing — cases I have not listed and that discriminate among the candidates you offer.
- A candid statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot preserve, and which of them you think I should give up, with the argument for giving them up.
- An assessment of whether case 9 belongs in the same theory at all, or whether it is a separate problem that my principle may be allowed to ignore.

I would rather be given one principle that costs me a verdict than a clause-by-clause device engineered to reproduce all ten.
