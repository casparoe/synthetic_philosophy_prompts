Three of us decide, once a year, whether our field reports will use the word "genocide." We are a small documentation group working in a region you have probably never read about: we interview survivors, verify casualty figures, and publish. None of us is a lawyer or an academic — I came to this work from freight logistics, and I have thought about the naming question for fifteen years on my own. Please act as an expert on genocide as both a legal and a moral category, and on the politics of naming atrocities, and think carefully before answering. Begin by brainstorming the rules our past verdicts could be an expression of, and only then judge the new case.

Here is our record.

- A militia campaign against a minority: we used the word six weeks in, on the strength of radio broadcasts and the shape of the killing, years before any court had established a plan.
- A government's requisition and blockade policy that killed roughly four hundred thousand members of one distinct ethnic group. The documents showed the government knew and did not care, but wanted the grain, not the deaths. We said crimes against humanity and refused "genocide."
- A regime's killing of about a million people defined by schooling, landholding, and party membership, in a country where the killing fell across all ethnic groups roughly in proportion to their size. We declined the word, reasoning that it protects groups people are born into rather than categories a regime invents.
- A state's thirty-year program against a small indigenous people: every child removed to boarding schools, the language banned in public life, places of worship demolished — no killings, no starvation, and the population grew. In a retrospective report we called that genocide.

The case in front of us now. An occupying power has been besieging a region inhabited almost entirely by one ethnic group for eleven months. About sixty thousand have died, overwhelmingly from starvation and untreated disease rather than from direct attack; aid convoys are admitted, but by our counts at a level that cannot sustain the population. A leaked directive from the regional military command orders the closure of the group's religious schools and the transfer of some four thousand children to households in the occupier's own ethnic group, justifying this as draining the insurgency's future support. Our document examiner puts the directive at perhaps seventy-forty authentic. We have seen no central policy document, the group is never named as such in anything official, and the stated aim throughout is counter-insurgency. Our two field staff have reached opposite conclusions and both think the other is being dishonest.

Tell me what we should decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves. Which of our four precedents is the closest analog, and precisely where does the analogy break? Set out each candidate rule that fits our record — I suspect at least three do, including one on which intent is inferred from the pattern of acts and one on which dismantling a group's institutions is sufficient on its own — say what each implies here, and then give me your best single answer with your reasons for preferring that rule. If our precedents genuinely underdetermine the case, say so plainly and tell me what principle we would be committing ourselves to by each available choice, and describe one further hypothetical case that would reveal which rule we have actually been applying all along. Finally, tell me where our record is already in tension with itself: I half suspect the second and fourth verdicts cannot both be defended, and I would much rather hear that from you now than from a critic later.
