I chair the rules committee for a seven-member appellate panel at a national medicines regulator, and before we select a voting procedure I want the requirements written down. The panel's output is not a bare verdict. We publish a single determination that records findings on an ordered sequence of questions — whether the pivotal evidence is methodologically adequate to the claim, whether the benefit–risk profile is favorable in the target population, whether the manufacturing and stability data suffice, whether the proposed labeling is adequate — and then derives the disposition from those findings by a rule fixed in advance. The determination has legal force and is treated as authority in later matters, so the reasoning is the product rather than a gloss on the result. Our present practice is an independent majority vote on each question followed by mechanical application of the rule, and two incidents have persuaded me that this is not obviously defensible.

Incident A. On a renewal application the panel split four–three in the applicant's favor on each of the first three questions and four–three against on the fourth. The derived disposition was refusal. No member held the combination of findings the determination attributes to the panel, and six of the seven would have voted to grant had the disposition been put to them directly. Applicant's counsel now argues, in unrelated proceedings, that our determinations record views the panel does not hold.

Incident B. On a safety suspension the sequence in which the questions were taken decided the case: addressing the adequacy of monitoring before causation produced one pattern of majorities, and reversing the order produced another, with identical votes by identical members on identical questions. One member has also stated on the record that she voted against a finding she accepted in order to secure a disposition she preferred.

What I want is the specification, not a recommendation. Write the desiderata any aggregation procedure for a body of this kind must meet to count as adequate for our purpose, stating for each the case or consideration that motivates it and what would count as violating it. Be precise about the distinctions the list must not collapse: aggregating judgments rather than preferences; a collectively consistent set of findings as against findings attributable to some actual member or coalition; the procedure that fixes the disposition as against the procedure that fixes the published reasons; stability of the outcome as against stability of the reasoning when membership rotates or a matter is reheard. Then mark which desiderata are non-negotiable for a panel that issues binding reasoning and which are preferences of particular camps — deductive closure of the collective set, anonymity, independence across questions, unanimity preservation, responsiveness, non-manipulability, order-invariance — saying in each case what institutional commitment makes it mandatory or dispensable here.

Where a subset cannot be jointly satisfied, that is the part I most want to see. State the clashing conditions exactly, identify what has to be given up, and say whether the surrender is forced by the logical structure of the agenda or only by our insistence on a single authored determination. Tell me also which single desideratum does the most work in ruling candidates out, and which of the things routinely demanded of such procedures are genuine requirements rather than artifacts of modeling this panel as a court rather than as a committee.

Finally, briefly and without belaboring it: how do a premise-based procedure, a conclusion-based procedure, and a procedure that publishes several reasoned opinions while taking the disposition by simple majority fare against the list? No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I want the reasoning, not the references.
