I'm teaching a philosophy of mind survey — 26 students, one 75-minute block, and I cant assume anyone did the reading. Design an in-class activity that makes them feel how underdetermined the question of which animals are conscious actually is, by having them build the criterion themselves out of cases rather than being handed one. The trap I want them to fall into: whatever test they land on should either sweep in things they don't want in (a sea anemone, their own gut neurons, the semi-autonomous arms of an octopus) or throw out things they do want in (honeybees, which run on under a million neurons and still learn from watching each other).

Give me the case cards — at least ten, each stating only the biological facts and no hint of an answer — plus the round structure, the timings, and a payoff mechanic with something genuinely at stake: a limited pool of sentience tokens each group has to commit before the next card is revealed, with a scoring rule that punishes both over- and under-inclusion. One card has to be an octopus whose severed arm keeps solving problems on its own, and that card should split the room between "one subject" and "several subjects," with the split actually costing or earning points rather than being a fun tangent. No lecture up front, and no ism gets named until the debrief.

Then a debrief that maps the moments they got stuck onto the main families of answer — behavioral criteria, neural-convergence criteria, evolutionary or functional ones, higher-order ones — and what each costs to hold. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to steer through it, and be straight with me about which feature of the problem the activity just cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply myself.
