For the past term my comparative ethics seminar has been split down the middle between two students who are each committed, far more firmly than I think the material warrants, to one side of the Mencius–Xunzi dispute about human nature. One defends Mencius: nature is good in the way a sprout is good, since everyone has the four incipient responses — alarm and commiseration at the sight of a child about to fall into a well, felt for no advantage and no reputation; shame and dislike; deference; and a sense of what is and is not so — and these are the beginnings of benevolence, righteousness, ritual propriety, and wisdom. They are fragile and need nourishing, and the parable of the stripped hillside (a mountain once well wooded, hewn down near the capital and then grazed bare by cattle, so that it looks barren though barrenness is not what it was meant to be) explains moral failure without conceding that nature is bad. The other defends Xunzi: what is inborn is desire for gain, envy and hatred, and the appetites of the ear and eye, and simply following nature yields contention, injury, and license; goodness is *wei*, deliberate activity, the way bent wood becomes straight only after the pressing-frame and steaming and blunt metal only after the whetstone; ritual is produced by the accumulated deliberate activity of sages rather than born in anyone, and if nature were already good there would be no work left for teachers, ritual, and law to do. Notably they agree that cultivation, models, and environment are indispensable, and that sagehood is within the reach of anyone — Xunzi's version being that a passerby on the road could become a Yu.

They have argued in circles for months, so I want the document they should have produced instead: the joint statement a committed Mencian and a committed Xunzian could both sign after a good-faith adversarial collaboration. Include the claims both accept — about their own texts, about what moral failure consists in, about the role of ritual and instruction — and the disagreements stated precisely, each phrased so that its own side would accept the wording as its own. Then the cruxes, and for each one the interpretive or empirical test both would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. My own working hypotheses, which you should test rather than adopt: first, that a large part of the dispute is verbal about the extension of *xing*, since Xunzi defines nature as whatever requires no learning or effort while Mencius counts cultivated potentials as native, so the two theses never actually contradict; second, that the substantive residue is a claim in moral psychology about whether untrained spontaneous response is prosocial or self-interested, which developmental findings about infant helping and consolation and, going the other way, about adult bystander non-intervention might speak to; third, that the hardest crux for Xunzi is the origin of the first sage's project of ritual, and whether his concession that humans have a native capacity to know and to approve of ritual and righteousness is a quiet retreat toward Mencius; fourth, that they may simply be answering different questions under the same heading — Mencius asking what makes moral demands internally motivating and authoritative, Xunzi asking how stable social order is causally possible. Tell me explicitly which parts of the disagreement are verbal and which are not, and do not let the verbal diagnosis swallow the real one.

Begin directly with the document itself, with no preface, no framing note, and no commentary afterward. Structure it as a numbered section of agreed claims, then a disagreements table with columns for the topic, the Mencian formulation, the Xunzian formulation, and whether that row is verbal or substantive, then the cruxes each with its test and both sides' recorded predictions, and close with a short paragraph stating what, if anything, both parties would concede could actually move the dispute. Give Chinese terms with an English gloss at first mention, and please do not append references, page numbers, or chapter-and-verse citations — I have both texts in translation — and describe passages rather than quoting them.
