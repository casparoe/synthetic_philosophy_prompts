So, weird request, but you're the only person I know who reads philosophy for pleasure rather than for a deadline, and I've hit a wall on a novel I've been living inside for about four years. The narrator is a wine critic who lost his sense of smell in a cycling accident at fifty-one and has gone on publishing for three more years anyway, working entirely off what his assistant tastes and reports back to him. Half the book is him arguing with himself about whether he's a fraud or whether he's stumbled into something nobody's bothered to name. To get the voice right I invented two labels for what he thinks he might be doing, and now I can't stop thinking about them — and, worse, I suspect they're not actually two different views, just one view wearing two hats.

The first I call the courier view: when someone whose palette you trust absolutely tells you a wine is magnificent, the verdict itself travels across sealed and intact, exactly the way an ordinary fact does. You end up knowing the wine is magnificent on their say-so; what you miss out on is the pleasure, not the warrant. The second I call the signpost view: testimony about beauty or deliciousness can never hand you a verdict, it can only hand you coordinates — where to look, what to notice, which bottle to buy, which paragraph to reread. The value part stays permanently untransferable, so the best anyone's report can do is position you to have your own response, and if you never have it, you simply never get the thing at all.

What I want from you is a real comparison of those two, but grounded in the actual debate rather than in my coinages. My sense is there's a whole literature on testimony about aesthetic value — something about acquaintance, and whether you can judge a thing beautiful on anyone's word, going back to Kant's insistence that nobody can be argued into a judgment of taste and to Hume's ideal critics — plus a more recent worry that the problem isn't just about knowledge but about a kind of failure of self in letting someone else's taste stand in for yours. So: which of my two labels lines up with which actual position, where do they caricature those positions, and what does each one have to say about the cases I actually need to write? Specifically — does it matter whether he'll ever taste anything again, i.e. is the courier view any different for someone who can never verify versus someone who's just trusting a review before opening night? What happens when his assistant reports that a wine he himself once loved is mediocre — does better taste defeat your own memory of an experience, or can't it? And what if the "courier" is not one person but a consensus, a panel, an aggregate score? Then the part I care most about: is there a third option my two labels jointly miss, something that would let my narrator be honest without letting him off the hook? I'd rather you pick a side and defend it than give me a balanced survey. No bibliography please, and don't feel obliged to name-drop — I'll never look any of it up. Just lay out the argument so clearly that I can tell whether my narrator is deluding himself.
