You are a philosopher of history whose secondary specialty is the metaphysics of social entities and the logic of vagueness. Please think this through carefully and visibly — I want a diagnosis, not a survey, and not a hedge between equally weighted options.

I have reconstructed the following argument out of a seminar on late antiquity, and it appears to establish a conclusion I cannot accept. Here it is in full.

(1) For a polity P to fall is for there to be some time t such that P exists immediately before t and does not exist at or after t.
(2) Whether P exists at a given time depends on whether enough of the features that constitute P's identity are still in place: its fiscal apparatus, its standing army, its law and its administrative language, its territorial control, and the self-understanding of those who rule and those who are ruled.
(3) In the Roman case these features came apart gradually and independently of one another. Territorial control in the West unraveled province by province across the fifth century; the city of Rome's population and its imported food supply declined over a stretch of two centuries; the tax machine atrophied as successor kingdoms collected in kind, commuted obligations, or let them lapse; while the imperial title, the Senate, Roman private law, the Church's diocesan geography, and the name "Roman" persisted somewhere or other for centuries afterward — and in the East every one of these persisted without interruption until 1453.
(4) So for any candidate date t at which Rome is said to have fallen, there is some nearby date t′ at which the case for saying that Rome still existed is not appreciably weaker than the case at t.
(5) If (4) is true, there is no determinate time at which Rome ceased to exist.
(6) Therefore, by (1), Rome did not fall.
(7) Nothing in (1) through (6) turns on anything specific to Rome; the same reasoning applies to any large, complex polity whose identity-constituting features come apart gradually. So no such polity ever falls, and "collapse" names a way of talking rather than a way things are.

What I cannot accept is (7), and the slide into it. Here is what I am confident of and what the argument seems to jeopardize. The Western Roman state lost, at some point in the fifth century, its capacity to tax, to raise armies, and to make its writ run; that loss was not a redescription but a catastrophe for the people who lived through it, and it is legible in the material record — coarser pottery, smaller livestock, tile roofs giving way to thatch, mass-produced goods vanishing from ordinary sites. Separately, we say without the slightest hesitation that the Soviet Union collapsed, and that it did so over a handful of years rather than at an instant. If (7) is right, both of those ways of speaking are on all fours with saying that Rome fell, and I do not believe that.

For what it is worth, I would most like to keep the empirical content of (3) — I find the gradualist picture of the fifth and sixth centuries convincing, and I do not want to escape the argument by reasserting that everything happened abruptly. Treat (3) as granted while you diagnose (1) through (6), and then tell me afterward whether granting it was itself the cheapest concession available.
