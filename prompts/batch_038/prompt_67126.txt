I am drafting a short paper on the therapy/enhancement distinction for a departmental workshop, and I would like critical comments on the argument below. This is a working draft rather than polished prose; please read it charitably on matters of style and uncharitably on matters of substance.

**Uptake and the Baseline: Why the Therapy/Enhancement Distinction Is Dynamically Unstable**

1. The distinction is standardly drawn in terms of normal functioning: therapy treats a departure from species-typical functioning, while enhancement improves functioning beyond it. On the account associated with Norman Daniels, this baseline is what makes health care a matter of social obligation rather than consumer preference, since departures from normal functioning impair opportunity in a way that mere sub-optimal traits do not. Two criticisms are familiar. First, normal functioning is statistical, and statistical baselines are reference-class relative: typical for whom, at what age, in what environment? Second, some apparent enhancements remedy deficits produced by social arrangement rather than biology, so the classification tracks institutions rather than nature.

2. Both criticisms are usually, and I think rightly, answered by an appeal to administrability. An institution need not settle borderline cases in principle in order to draw workable lines — treat disease, do not fund trait augmentation. Vagueness at the margins is compatible with a serviceable core. The difficulty I want to press is not that the baseline is blurred but that it moves in response to the practice the distinction governs. Call this the uptake objection.

3. On a statistical reading, the baseline is a fact about the distribution of a trait across a population at a time. Widespread uptake of an intervention changes that distribution. If uptake is extensive enough, the previously enhanced level becomes the new typical level, and states that were once ordinary come to count as subnormal, hence as deficits, hence as candidates for treatment. Whether an intervention is therapy is therefore partly a function of how many people have already had it. The classification is endogenous to the practice it classifies.

4. Corrective lenses are the obvious case: moderate uncorrected refractive error once sat comfortably inside the typical range, and is now treated as a defect with educational and occupational consequences. Iodized salt and water fluoridation were, for the many individuals who would never have developed the relevant deficiency, strictly improvements on the typical baseline; their spread nonetheless changed what counted as normal dentition and thyroid function. Vaccination may be the strongest instance, since once coverage is high, susceptibility is treated as a preventable defect and non-immunity as a matter of medical and sometimes legal concern.

5. The consequence is that a policy admitting therapy while barring enhancement is not the conservative position it appears to be. Each permitted therapy nudges the baseline, and a shifted baseline reclassifies former normalities as deficits, enlarging the therapy category from within. Conversely, if one genuinely wanted to hold the line, the variable that matters is aggregate uptake, not the individual clinical decision — and clinical and insurance practice operates case by case, with no purchase on uptake. The distinction may survive as a snapshot rationing device within a fixed budget and a fixed distribution, but it cannot do the work its defenders assign to it, namely marking the principled boundary of medicine.

6. The objection I expect is that I have confused a diachronic with a synchronic question. At any given time the distribution is determinate and so the classification is determinate; that the baseline drifts does not make it indeterminate now, any more than a ship's position is indeterminate because the ship is moving. Case-by-case application remains coherent.

7. My sketch of a reply is that this objection concedes the substantive point: it grants that the content of normal functioning is fixed by contingent facts about uptake rather than by facts about human nature, and the appeal to nature is what allows the line to be presented as principled rather than arbitrary. If the line's content is set by how much of an intervention has already been used, the distinction records past decisions rather than constraining future ones.

My questions:

- Is section 4 doing the work I need? I suspect the vaccination and fluoridation cases may be better explained by harm to others or population-level prevention than by shifts in trait distribution, which would leave the argument resting on the weaker optical example. Tell me if that is right, and whether anything survives it.
- Is the objection in section 6 stronger than my reply in section 7? My worry is that the reply trades on an equivocation between arbitrary and not-fixed-by-nature, since a defender could accept uptake-dependence while insisting the line is principled: it is anchored to an objective such as protecting opportunity, which is indifferent to how the baseline came to be where it is. Should I concede and reframe, or is there a reply I am missing?
- Does the argument collapse if the statistical account is abandoned? Suppose a defender holds that function is fixed by what a part is for, independently of how the trait is distributed. Would the uptake objection still bite, and can section 5's conclusion be reached without statistics?
- Is section 5 consistent with section 7? I claim the distinction can still serve as a snapshot rationing device, yet I also claim it is merely a record of past decisions; I am not sure I can have both.

Finally, a blunt verdict on whether the central idea is worth developing at all, or whether it is a repackaging of the familiar complaint that the line is vague. Please argue the points rather than appending a list of references or suggested reading; I want your reasoning, not a bibliography.
