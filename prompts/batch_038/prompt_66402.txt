I am a high school junior, and for an independent study I have spent the last several months reading large-scale accounts of the whole human past. Three of them have stayed with me: one arguing that the distribution of domesticable plants and animals across the continents decided which regions of the world ended up dominant; one arguing that a new capacity for believing in shared fictions is what allowed strangers to cooperate at a scale larger than the village; and one treating the shift to farming as the point after which most of what I would call a problem enters the record. Across all three I keep meeting the same argumentative shape, and I have no word for it.

Each book's central claim is extremely general. When a case fits, the general claim is credited with it. When a case does not fit, the book neither denies the case nor retreats from the claim; it produces a further, much more specific statement about that particular case — this valley had no domesticable grain, this stretch of coast had enough fish that farming was unnecessary, this people borrowed the technique from a neighbor rather than inventing it — and the specific statement is usually checkable, and is sometimes actually checked against botany or excavation. What strikes me is that the general claim never seems to lose anything in the exchange. It emerges exactly as broad and as confident as it went in, while the specific statement absorbs the difficulty. I also noticed a second version of the same thing: where the predicted development occurred, the explanation offered is the general force, and where it failed to occur, the explanation offered is a local shortage — so both outcomes are accounted for, and the general claim is never told what would count against it.

My history teacher's response was that this is simply how historical explanation works, and that adding detail as evidence accumulates is the normal business of refining a theory. That did not satisfy me, though I cannot yet say why. I do not think this is just a theory being unfalsifiable, and I do not think it is just a theory being rescued by an arbitrary stipulation, because the added statements are not arbitrary: they are empirical, specific, and often defensible on their own terms. But neither does it look like honest refinement, because refinement ought to change something.

Is there an established concept for this — a general claim that is surrounded by an ever-growing set of individually reasonable specific claims, none of which ever costs the general claim anything? I would like to know the nearest established ideas, exactly how what I am describing differs from each of them, and where the line falls between this and legitimate refinement of a theory in light of new evidence. If nothing established covers it, please propose a term and supply boundary cases that fall on both sides of it. The label matters much less to me than the carving.
