I'm a philosophy PhD student working on identity under fission, and I've been invited to contribute an essay to a collection on film and the philosophy of physics. I want to read Christopher Nolan's *The Prestige* through the quantum suicide and quantum immortality argument, and I need help making the reading hold up rather than merely sound suggestive.

The fit is tempting. Angier's machine does not transport him; it produces a duplicate, so each performance branches into one Angier taking his bow at the far end of the theatre and one Angier drowning in a water tank beneath the stage — and Angier says it took courage to climb into the machine every night precisely because he did not know which of the two men he would be. That is structurally the Everettian set-up: indexical uncertainty before a branching event, plus a survivor on every branch who is, from the inside, the man who stepped in.

Tell me where the analogy genuinely holds and where it collapses. Two worries I can't resolve on my own. First, the film supplies no branch weights: exactly one survivor and exactly one corpse, with no amplitude to render the drowning case negligible, whereas the quantum immortality argument seems to depend on there being some such weighting. Second, the drowning copy is at home in his branch and experiences dying, while the first-person argument works only because nobody experiences the death branch — so does the film dramatize a cost the argument suppresses, or is the comparison a category error, since duplication is not measurement? I'd also like the film's rival model of branching taken seriously: Borden's secret is a twin who alternates with him, two continuous people sharing one public identity, which is fission without duplication.

Say which scenes to build the argument on, whether the film endorses the Everettian attitude toward one's own death or merely stages it, and where an honest critic should concede the reading breaks down. No citations or survey of the interpretations debate — I want the criticism.
