I teach an upper-level philosophy of biology seminar — 18 students, 75 minutes, one meeting a week. They've all had at least one evolution course, they know what fitness means, they can read a payoff table, but not a single one of them will do algebra if they can avoid it. Next week is the units of selection. I've run the usual version of this (everybody reads a chapter of Dawkins and a chapter of Sober and Wilson, then we argue in a circle for an hour) and it's dead on arrival. They leave having sided with whoever talked loudest, and not one of them could tell you what evidence would actually settle anything.

So: build me an in-class simulation that makes them feel the level-of-selection problem rather than hear about it. Nobody hears the phrase group selection until the debrief — I want them to run into it.

Here's the piece I actually care about, so please don't sand it off. I want the whole thing to turn on a distinction from population genetics: hard versus soft selection. Roughly, hard selection means a group's absolute productivity is what determines how many members it contributes to the next generation — a group that does well really does send more descendants into the pool. Soft selection means every group contributes the same fixed number of members regardless of how it did, because the pool size is capped, so the only thing that matters is who beat whom *inside* the group. I want them to play two rounds under hard selection where a costly cooperative strategy visibly takes over — they should be surprised and a little smug about it — and then, without announcing anything, have round three run under soft selection, with the exact same payoffs, and watch cooperation get eaten from the inside. The flip is the lesson. If they can see the switch coming mid-round, the activity is ruined, so tell me how to disguise it (a new regulation card, a change in how I read out the tally, whatever) in a way that doesn't feel like I lied to them afterwards.

What I need from you:

- the roles, the group structure, and how many groups of what size. Also how migration or defecting-out-of-your-group works, if it works at all.
- a token-or-card payoff scheme with concrete numbers. No real money. I've got poker chips, index cards, and a whiteboard, and I have no TA this term, so one person has to be able to run the whole thing.
- the arithmetic, shown, that your numbers genuinely produce the intended flip. Don't hand me a scheme that only works if everyone plays sanely — tell me what happens to the dynamics if a third of the room is screwing around or refusing to defect when they should.
- a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75.
- a debrief that maps specific moments of the game onto the real conceptual issues: the within-group versus between-group decomposition of variance, whether the multi-level description is a rival explanation to a gene's-eye one or just a repackaging of the same facts, and why soft selection guts the group-level story even though nothing about the individuals' payoffs changed. Here you can finally name everything.
- what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and the cheap fix for each.
- which feature of the debate the activity flatly cannot reproduce, and what I have to supply verbally so they don't walk out with a false picture. I already suspect the answer has something to do with the fact that my students are choosing and organisms aren't, but work out the full list.

One last thing, and please take it seriously. In the debrief, keep a visible line between what's settled and what's genuinely contested among people who work on this. The bookkeeping results I regard as unarguable, but the interpretive questions — whether group selection is a distinct explanatory level or a redescription, whether anything like it has ever mattered in the history of life, whether the human-cultural case is a special one — are live and people who know the material disagree sharply. I've read far too many summaries that flatten that into a single take. Don't hand my students a consensus that doesn't exist, and say outright where you're giving me your own view versus where the field actually splits.
