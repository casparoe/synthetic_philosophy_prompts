I sit on my university's facilities safety committee, and last week two other members had a disagreement they asked me to settle. I couldn't, and I'm not sure where I would even begin.

Background: Cora is the structural engineer who personally inspected and signed off on a fifty-year-old pedestrian bridge on campus. In March the student paper ran a story arguing that the bridge is unsafe, drawing on maintenance records it had obtained. Cora has not read the story and says she will not.

The exchange went roughly like this.

1. Cora: I inspected that bridge myself and I know it is sound. If I know it is sound, then I also know that whatever the article alleges is a mistake — someone misread a corrosion log, or confused our span with a similar one. So the article cannot teach me anything true about the bridge; the only thing it can do is unsettle a belief I already know to be correct. Declining to read it is therefore the rational choice, not the lazy one.

2. Elias: That is word for word the reasoning of every crank who has ever refused to look at anything. If it were acceptable, nobody could ever be corrected, since anyone who happened to be wrong would say exactly the same thing about the evidence against them.

3. Cora: A fool and an expert can produce the same sentence from different epistemic positions. What matters is not whether my words sound like something a fool would say, but whether I know. I do.

4. Elias: Then you are claiming knowledge about a document you have not opened — that it is mistaken, and specifically how.

5. Cora: I do not need that. Knowing the conclusion is false suffices for knowing that every route leading to it is defective; I need no more of a catalogue of those routes than a man who knows his wife is faithful needs a catalogue of every way a compromising photograph of her could be staged, misdated, or misidentified. Refusing to look at such photographs is not closed-mindedness; it is what knowledge calls for.

6. Elias: Notice what that concedes. If you looked and the photographs were genuine, you would have to revise. So refusing to look is not a consequence of your knowing — it is a device for ensuring you never have to find out whether you do.

Please rate each of the six moves from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence of justification per rating. Then tell me: is Cora's inference in move 1 valid, and if it is, what exactly has to be given up for its conclusion to stop looking so uncomfortable? Do moves 3 and 5 actually answer the objection in move 2, or does it still bite? And is there something both of them are taking for granted that neither defends, or a question they should be having instead of the one they are having?
