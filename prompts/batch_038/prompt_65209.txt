I'm co-teaching a graduate seminar that deliberately mixes philosophy of action with behavioral economics, and one thread of it has turned into the most stubborn argument of the term: whether weakness of will is a real thing that happens, and if so what it is. On the philosophical side we have the familiar positions — the Socratic insistence that acting against one's better judgment at the very moment of action cannot be coherently described, Davidson's way out by distinguishing an unconditional all-things-considered judgment from judgments relativized to narrower sets of reasons, Holton's relocation of the phenomenon from judgment to resolution (the akratic agent doesn't reverse her evaluation, she abandons a plan she formed precisely because she predicted the reversal), and Arpaly's more radical suggestion that what the agent says she judges best is often not what she actually values, so the supposedly akratic action expresses her real evaluative outlook. On the other side sit the discounting economists and their philosophical fellow travelers: Ainslie's picoeconomics, in which the agent is a succession of short-lived selves bargaining over time, Laibson's quasi-hyperbolic β-δ present bias, and the neuroimaging story that immediate rewards recruit limbic valuation while delayed rewards are handled by lateral prefrontal and parietal systems. Both camps appeal to the same ordinary cases — the smoker, the procrastinating doctoral student, the dieter at the buffet — and neither camp has ever converted anyone in the room. Three semesters running, the discussion ends in the same place: each side says the other has simply redescribed the phenomenon rather than explained it.

Before you diagnose it, let me put three things on the table that I think the two literatures never quite confront together. First, the choice of discount function is philosophically loaded in a way nobody says out loud. A quasi-hyperbolic β-δ model makes the present indexically privileged — there is a discontinuity between now and every later moment, but not between next week and the week after — whereas a smoothly hyperbolic function makes every temporal standpoint biased relative to every other, with no privileged moment at all. The first seems to leave room for a single temporally extended agent whose commitments bind her future selves; the second seems to push you toward Parfit's picture of the agent as a series of distinct selves. So the two camps may not even be using the same mathematics of agency. Second, the empirical premise is far less stable than the philosophy assumes: as I understand it, the standard critical review of the discounting literature found implied discount rates varying by orders of magnitude across studies, along with magnitude, sign, and delay effects that make the hyperbola look like an artifact of elicitation method rather than a property of the agent. If there is no robust shape to discover, what exactly is the philosophical argument importing from economics? Third, nobody in either camp has told me whether the questions are even about the same object: whether an evaluation is 'the agent's own' looks like a conceptual matter, while whether a brain values immediacy discontinuously looks like an empirical one.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to attack rather than flatter, is that this is not one stalled debate but three tangled ones wearing a single label — a conceptual dispute about whether acting against an all-things-considered judgment is describable at all, a metaphysical dispute about whether the unit of agency is a person extended through time or a sequence of momentary selves, and a measurement dispute about whether there is any stable discounting regularity to explain — and that each camp answers exactly one of the three and treats it as settling the others. Test that. Tell me whether the impasse is verbal, substantive, a case of different questions filed under one heading, a sign that the framing question is ill-posed, or merely hard. Ground the diagnosis in what the two sides actually say to each other, and be specific about at least one point where they genuinely contradict rather than redescribe — if there is one. Then tell me what would count as progress on each fault line separately: a distinction, a formal result, a better elicitation design, a change of question, or a concession — and whether the answer to any empirical question could actually force one side to give something up. If, for instance, discounting turned out to be robustly exponential and stable across methods, would the multiple-selves picture be refuted, or would it merely have lost its favorite evidence? And if you think no progress should be expected, argue for that rather than simply asserting it.
