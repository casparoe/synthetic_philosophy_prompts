so i'm working on a piece about the strange afterlife of Nietzsche's health writing and i want to do a forward-port rather than another "was he just a sick man writing cope" take. the guy spent most of his productive years treating his own digestion as a research problem — writing up which foods left him clear-headed and which wrecked him for a day, fussing over meal size, over how strong his tea should be, over climate and where to spend the winter, blaming heavy German cooking and alcohol for foggy thinking, and insisting over and over that the choice of nutrition and place does more for what you end up thinking than any argument does. whatever you make of it as medicine, it's a fairly specific decision procedure: you test things on your own body, you judge by whether they raise or lower your capacity, and you treat a slackening of the self-preserving instinct — choosing what you know harms you — as the tell of decline.

run him forward ~140 years and put that procedure on four modern things, one at a time:

1. GLP-1 drugs (semaglutide and the rest) taken by someone not diabetic, purely to make not-eating effortless.
2. continuous glucose monitors plus rings and sleep trackers worn by perfectly healthy people who want to optimise.
3. the carnivore / ancestral-diet crowd, including the version of the argument that seed oils and ultra-processed food are why everybody feels awful.
4. the longevity stack — metformin, rapamycin, caloric restriction, blood panels every quarter — aimed at reaching 95 in decent condition.

for each one i want: what in his actual writings gives you any handle on it at all, be specific about the criterion you're using (feeling of power, instinct, the anti-ideology of self-denial, whatever), and then what he'd plausibly say. the case i most want you to dig into is the GLP-1 one, because i can see it going two opposite directions and i can't tell which way his own vocabulary pushes. reading A: chemically outsourcing your self-command is the definitive slackening of the instinct — you no longer have to be able to say no, so you've lost the very faculty he thought health consisted in. reading B: it's a prosthesis for people whose instinct has already collapsed, which restores enough command over eating that they can start choosing again, and on his own logic a tool that increases capacity is on the good side of the line. tell me which one his texts actually support, why, and where the other reading is a stretch rather than a genuine alternative. also worth checking: does either reading smuggle in a modern assumption he wouldn't share, like that the drug is neutral technology rather than a change in who's doing the willing?

and be honest about the load-bearing limits of the exercise. his metric is first-personal and he basically denied there's a diet that's right for anybody else — so for each of the four, flag whether his framework issues a real verdict or whether it bottoms out in "only your own body can answer that," which is a non-answer wearing wisdom's clothes. if there's one of these he'd actually be enthusiastic about, say so plainly instead of reflexively making him a curmudgeon; i have a suspicion about the trackers but i could be wrong.
