I write a weekly column on science and ideas, and I am finishing a long piece about whether it makes any sense to call DNA a code. I want to close it with a commissioned poem, and I would like you to write it. Please treat the following as the brief, and hold to it closely; the poem only works if the constraints are obeyed rather than gestured at.

**The situation.** The poem is an address from the reading machinery to the thing being read: a collective first-person plural — adapters, synthetases, ribosome, whatever you like, but never named — speaking to a sequence it has been decoding since before there were cells. That framing is the argument. In this transaction there is no sender, no one for whom the message was intended, only a reader that manufactured its own key and then called the key the message's meaning.

**The form.** Twelve tercets plus one orphan line inserted somewhere between the sixth and ninth tercet, thirty-seven lines in all; you may add or subtract one tercet if the sense demands it. Each tercet rhymes only on its third line, and those third lines chain stanza to stanza. The links may be slant, near, or vowel rhymes, and near rhymes are to be treated as equivalent to exact ones: that is the wobble, the degeneracy, several different spellings arriving at the same residue. The orphan line is a frameshift. After it, the chain re-pairs so that words which have already appeared land in different positions and carry different weight — a reader who knows nothing about genetics should simply feel the ground move. No punctuation inside the tercets; the code is read three letters at a time, without overlaps and without commas, and the reader must know where to begin. The first word of the poem must recur later, mid-sequence, as an ordinary word, so that one token serves both as *begin here* and as just another residue. And the poem must stop on a line whose final word has already appeared twice earlier doing stop work, so that it is clear the stopping is read, not written.

**The content, in the weeds but never lectured.** Synonyms that are not quite synonyms: two codons for one residue that cease to be interchangeable once the cell's tempo, its folding, and the shape of its messenger are in play. The dialects: the same triplet read as a full stop in one lineage and as an amino acid in another, so that there is no single universal key, only mutually intelligible accents. The frozen accident: no evident reason for this pairing rather than that one, and no way back now, because everything downstream leans on it. And the reader's complicity — that the key is a chemistry, that nothing was sent to anybody, and that *information* may be our name for a regularity we learned to exploit.

**Register.** Use only technical words that are also ordinary English words doing ordinary work in the line: frame, stop, silent, adapter, degenerate, wobble, read. Name no person, cite nothing, quote no real text; if a line needs quotation marks, invent what goes inside them. Do not resolve whether DNA is genuinely a code or a metaphor that hardened into an ontology — the final tercet should leave both readings live. Give it a title that works in both registers.

The poem will be read aloud at the end of a three-thousand-word essay, so it has to land on first hearing with the technical structure as an optional second layer. Before you send it, check two things: that the frameshift genuinely re-groups the recurring words rather than merely arriving as an extra line, and that the last three lines would still be true of a sequence with no interpreter anywhere near it.
