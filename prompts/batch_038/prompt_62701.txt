I am assembling a handout for a graduate seminar on the unity of consciousness, in which James's 1890 Preface is read against Dennett's multiple drafts model. The passage below is from William James, *The Principles of Psychology*, Volume I (1890), Preface. Please write in American English.

> I have kept close to the point of view of natural science throughout the book. Every natural science assumes certain data uncritically, and declines to challenge the elements between which its own 'laws' obtain, and from which its own deductions are carried on. Psychology, the science of finite individual minds, assumes as its data (1) thoughts and feelings, and (2) a physical world in time and space with which they coexist and which (3) they know. Of course these data themselves are discussable; but the discussion of them (as of other elements) is called metaphysics and falls outside the province of this book. This book, assuming that thoughts and feelings exist and are vehicles of knowledge, thereupon contends that psychology when she has ascertained the empirical correlation of our phenomenally given thoughts with definite conditions of the brain, can go no farther—can go no farther, that is, as a natural science. If she goes farther she becomes metaphysical. All attempts to explain our phenomenally given thoughts as products of deeper-lying entities (whether the latter be named 'Soul,' 'Transcendental Ego,' 'Ideas,' or 'Elementary Units of Consciousness') are metaphysical. This book consequently rejects both the associationist and the spiritualist theories; and in this strictly positivistic point of view consists the only feature of it for which I feel tempted to claim originality. Of course this point of view is anything but ultimate. Men must keep thinking; and the data assumed by psychology, just like those assumed by physics and all the other natural sciences, must some time be overhauled. The effort to overhaul them clearly and thoroughly is metaphysics; and metaphysics can only perform her task well when distinctly conscious of its great extent. Metaphysics fragmentary, irresponsible, half-awake, and unconscious that she is metaphysical, spoils two good things when she injects herself into a natural science. And it seems to me that the theories both of a spiritual agent and of associated 'ideas' are, as they figure in the psychology-books, just such metaphysics as this. Even if their results be true, it would be as well to keep them, as thus presented, out of psychology as it is to keep the results of idealism out of physics.
>
> I have therefore treated our passing thoughts as integers, and regarded the mere laws of their coexistence with brain-states as the ultimate laws for our science. The reader will in vain seek for any closed system in the book. It is mainly a mass of descriptive details, running out into queries which only a metaphysics alive to the weight of her task can hope successfully to deal with. That will perhaps be centuries hence; and meanwhile the best mark of health that a science can show is this unfinished-seeming front.

Please address the following, in order, in roughly 800 to 1,000 words. No bibliography and no citations to secondary literature are wanted; you may name familiar positions and phenomena, but the analysis should be driven by the quoted text.

1. **The four-item list.** State the single feature that, on James's own stated criterion, makes "Soul," "Transcendental Ego," "Ideas," and "Elementary Units of Consciousness" alike count as metaphysical. Two of the four are the targets one expects from a passage whose punchline is the rejection of spiritualism; explain why the fourth belongs on the list at all, and what its inclusion implies about how James wants his view separated from the psychology of his day. Your account should show why the list is unified rather than merely assorted, and why the criterion is about the *direction* of explanation (the given explained by the not-given) rather than about the ontological respectability of any particular posited item.

2. **"Treated our passing thoughts as integers."** Distinguish two readings: (i) a substantive claim that thoughts are simple, indivisible units of mental reality, and (ii) a merely methodological or individuative claim — that thoughts are the items psychology counts, describes, and correlates with brain-states, with no commitment to their internal simplicity. Argue for one of them, and identify the specific clause or clauses in the passage that decide the matter. Then resolve the apparent tension: if James rejects associationism while treating thoughts as integers, what exactly has he kept from the associationist and what has he dropped? Say what work the second sentence of the second paragraph does in that resolution.

3. **Two words that carry weight.** Explain what "mere" contributes in "the mere laws of their coexistence with brain-states," and what is gained or lost by James's choice of "coexistence" rather than a causal verb. Does the passage commit him to a view about whether brain-states cause thoughts, or does it deliberately decline that question? Also gloss "unfinished-seeming front" and note whether it is a concession about the book's failure to be systematic or a claim about the epistemic virtues of a young science.

4. **The appropriation problem.** A colleague wishes to cite this passage as a nineteenth-century anticipation of the multiple drafts model: on both views, there is no deep entity behind the goings-on, and so no inner venue at which contents are assembled for a spectator. I want to know whether the passage licenses that. Please distinguish carefully between a claim about *explanatory ontology* — what a science is entitled to posit behind its data — and a claim about the *temporal and structural organization of experience itself* — whether there is a determinate order and a determinate moment at which contents become conscious. Say which kind of claim the passage makes, and then specify the additional premise that would be required to get from James's refusal to explain the given by the not-given to the thesis that there is no fact of the matter about the sequence in which contents are fixed.

5. **Where the passage cuts the other way.** Address directly whether "integers" is the right vocabulary for a theorist who denies that experience arrives as a countable succession of finished items. If individuation into discrete, bounded, countable thoughts is itself something the multiple drafts model treats as a retrospective imposition rather than a datum, does James's methodological starting point support or undermine the parallel your colleague wants? Conclude with one sentence stating what James would have to add or withdraw to become a multiple-drafts theorist, and one sentence stating a respect in which this passage commits him in the opposite direction.

Throughout, keep separate what the passage says from what you are reconstructing on its behalf, and flag any point where the text underdetermines the reading you prefer rather than smoothing the difficulty over.
