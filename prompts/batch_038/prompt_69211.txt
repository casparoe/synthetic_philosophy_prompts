Quick context dump, sorry — I'll try to be brief-ish.

I write novels. For reasons nobody can explain I've also sat on the editorial board of a literary quarterly for about six years, and we run a section — call it the Witness pages — of first-person accounts: people writing up the worst or strangest thing that happened to them, sometimes under a pseudonym. Our standing policy is one sentence long and I voted for it twice: 'we do not fact-check these pieces, the teller owns their account.' Which is fine when the piece is about your mother's funeral.

Now we're stuck. Submission from a former deckhand on a Gulf shrimper about a night in the early nineties when the boat's engineer died in the engine room. The death itself is on the record — Coast Guard report, ruled accidental. His claim is about what happened after: that the captain left the man down there for something like forty minutes before raising the alarm, and that the time in the log was written later, to fit. Two complications. (a) The official report has the alarm raised within minutes and says nothing about any delay. (b) Four years ago we ran a piece in this same section by another crewman off that boat about the same night, and he puts the captain on the bridge the entire time. The captain is alive, in his eighties, and we have no intention of contacting him — no right of reply on these pages is part of the policy too, also my vote.

Board's split three-three and the editor wants something in writing before Friday's meeting.

One side says: the entire point of the section is that we take people at their word, and demanding corroboration from a man who has no documents and no reachable witnesses is just an elegant way of not beleiving him, and the conflict with the report proves nothing because reports like that get written by the people who need them to be survivable. The other side says: testimony isn't evidence until you have some independent reason to trust it, 'no reason to doubt' is not the same thing as 'a reason to believe,' and we're about to print an accusation against an identifiable old man on the word of one person whose memory has had thirty years and a very satisfying narrative arc to work with.

I'm the swing vote and I'm the fiction writer, which means everyone assumes I'll go soft, and honestly I don't know that I will. I build people out of nothing for a living — I know exactly how much tidying a story does to a life — and I also know that 'what did he have to gain?' is a rotten question to ask a man writing about a night at sea.

Could you draft the memo I'll circulate? Things I want in it:

- Bottom line in the first paragraph, and make it an actual decision — print as submitted, print with a specific named editorial note, hold for one issue while we do something concrete, or decline. With a time frame.
- Keep two questions seperate that I think half the board is running together: what we're entitled to believe, and what we're entitled to print. I suspect people are answering the first one and voting on the second.
- A philosopher friend once told me there's the position where testimony is innocent until proven guilty as a source, the position where you need positive reasons each time, and a middle one where the source in general is fine but the individual teller always gets checked. If any of that maps onto our fight, use it — I'd rather the memo had machinery in it than vibes. But no footnotes, no reading list, no name-dropping; nobody on this board is going to look anything up.
- Somebody should say something about whether two accounts agreeing is corroboration or just two testimonies, and whether a disagreement between them is any more informative than an agreement is.
- Answer whether a note at the top of the piece ('this account could not be independently verified') changes anything for the reader, or whether it's just the board keeping its hands clean.
- Criteria for the section going forward that a junior editor could apply without us. Not a restatement of the split.
- A fair, steelmanned paragraph on the strongest thing the losing side has. I mean it, don't sandbag it — if this reads like a prosecution it won't survive the room.

Seven to nine hundred words.

Then after the memo, a table — markdown is fine — of the features of this particular submission that are actually doing epistemic work. One row each, aim for six to eight, don't pad it with the same feature wearing a different hat. Columns exactly these:

- feature: the thing itself (no obvious gain from lying, the conflict with the Coast Guard report, the earlier piece by the other crewman, the thirty-year gap, the pseudonym, whatever else you think matters)
- counts_for: what it gives us a reason to think, if anything — 'nothing' is an acceptable entry
- counts_against: what it gives us a reason to doubt, if anything
- independent: whether it's genuinely independent of this teller's word or is just more testimony — yes / no / partial, plus one clause saying why
- net: pushes toward printing, against printing, or a wash
- handling: what we do with it — keep, cut, note it, try to check it, ask the writer about it
