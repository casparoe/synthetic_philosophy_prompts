I am a fourth-year doctoral student in history and philosophy of science, and through a combination of bad luck and a supervisor's enthusiasm I have become the de facto triage editor for a small interdisciplinary journal on computation, information, and mind, as well as the person who coordinates reviewing for an annual workshop on unconventional models of computation. Between the two venues I see roughly a dozen submissions a year whose central claim is that the Church–Turing thesis has been refuted, overturned, or superseded: analog circuits, relativistic spacetimes, quantum devices, real-number machines, adaptive reservoir systems, and the occasional privately printed supertask. My own preparation for judging these is one term of recursion theory and a seminar on the philosophy of computation; my referees are typically graduate students with less than that. Our advisory board includes two working computability theorists and one relativist, all of whom have made it unmistakably clear that they will not look at a manuscript that has not first survived a serious initial assessment.

What I need from you is a first-pass decision procedure that I can run on a single submission in forty-five minutes to an hour, that a non-specialist referee could also run without misusing it, and that terminates in one of four outcomes: desk rejection with a written justification that would withstand being quoted back by an indignant author; rejection coupled with an invitation to restate the claim and resubmit; ordinary external review; or escalation to a named specialist, accompanied by a one-page brief explaining what specifically the specialist is being asked to adjudicate. I would like the tests to be derived from the conceptual terrain rather than assembled as a list of generic red flags, so please begin with a short taxonomy and only then turn to the procedure. Lay out how many genuinely different claims are routinely collapsed under the single label Church–Turing thesis — a claim about what can be effectively calculated by a human being following rules without insight, a modest physical claim that whatever function a physical device used as a computing instrument delivers is Turing-computable, a bold physical claim that any physical process whatsoever is computable or simulable to arbitrary precision by a Turing machine, and a further claim about tractability and polynomial time — and, for each, say what would and would not count as evidence against it and why their epistemic statuses differ, since one of them is close to a stipulation fixing the meaning of a term, another is an empirical claim about the world that is permanently hostage to the idealizations built into the model of the measuring device, and a third is a complexity conjecture. The procedure should force the referee to identify which of these a submission is actually addressing before evaluating its argument, and it should be ordered so that the cheap and disqualifying checks come first. Please build in the checks I keep needing and keep fumbling: whether the non-computability is smuggled into the input, the initial condition, or the machine's parameters rather than generated by the dynamics; whether the claimed output is available to an ordinary observer in finite time and at finite precision; whether the model tells us what happens under perturbation and noise, or only under exact real arithmetic; whether the paper's claim is about computability at all or merely about speed; whether computable is being used in a BSS or real-RAM sense and the author has not noticed the substitution; and whether, if the argument succeeded, anyone should care. I would also like a short list of dismissals that referees must not deploy. I once rejected a manuscript with a line to the effect that no physical system can store a quantity to infinite precision, the author replied showing that this simply assumes the conclusion, and the exchange cost me a year of standing with the board.

Then please run the procedure end to end on four submissions I will describe. First: an electrical engineer argues that after a finite settling time the exact voltage across a node in an analog circuit, set by a physical parameter, contains the successive bits of Chaitin's halting probability; the author concedes that noise limits any actual reading to about forty bits but maintains that the underlying value is nonetheless physically present. Second: a philosophy-of-physics paper argues that in a Malament–Hogarth spacetime, one in which an unboundedly long worldline lies entirely in the past of an event that a second observer reaches in finite proper time, an observer can decide the halting problem, and that this falsifies the bold physical formulation as a matter of what general relativity permits; the mathematics is explicit and, so far as I can judge, correct. Third: a computer science manuscript whose abstract announces that quantum computing refutes the Church–Turing thesis while the body establishes only a polynomial-time separation. Fourth: a flawless short paper observing that the three-dimensional wave equation admits computable initial data whose unique solution is not computable, in the manner of the Pour-El and Richards result, presented without further argument as a refutation of the physical thesis. For each of the four, give me the verdict, the one-paragraph justification I would send the author, and an explicit marking of the places where the verdict rests on a judgment call rather than on a checkable condition. Finally, tell me honestly which of the four the procedure will handle badly and why; I suspect it will be too generous to the second, since that submission seems likely to pass every test I can think of and still cost me six months and the permanent good will of a very reluctant relativist.

For this task, adopt the standpoint of a philosopher of computation who has actually refereed in this literature and has watched submissions of this kind go wrong in both directions. Think carefully, work through the taxonomy before writing anything procedural, and where the procedure pretends to mechanical precision it does not possess, say so plainly at that step rather than in a general disclaimer at the end. Please do not supply a reference list, a reading list, or a bibliography of any kind: I am asking for a tool I can hand to a first-year referee, not a syllabus, and where you mention a result, describe what it establishes rather than citing where. I have no objection to your flagging, in a closing section, what the procedure cannot decide at all, including whether any version of the physical thesis is genuinely empirical or whether the debate over the thesis has been vitiated by people using the word computable to mean incompatible things.
