So our small foundation (about $4M a year in grants) just brought on a consultant who's pushing something he calls impact-per-dollar triage. Every program we fund has to get scored on one comparable number — estimated lives-improved-equivalent per dollar — and anything that can't produce a defensible number doesn't get penalized, it gets zeroed out by default, treated as unscoreable and therefore unfundable. His pitch is that this is perfectly neutral: "we're just not paying for things we can't measure." On top of it, the board is also considering a fellowship that would pay a handful of very smart grads to go into quant trading on the theory that their future donations beat anything they'd do with their hands directly.

I run our grants to prisoner reentry legal aid and to a couple of really idiosyncratic history-of-science archival projects, and I have a strong gut feeling this quietly guts precisely the stuff that's hardest to score while leaving the trading fellowship untouched. But I want to argue it properly at the board meeting instead of just asserting it.

Give me the most important implications of a scoring rule like this for what kinds of causes survive triage in the long run. I mean the structural, predictable effects, not just "some worthy things get cut." Specifically: does the rule systematically favor certain shapes of intervention over others, and if so which shapes and why; what does it do to what grantees choose to measure, study, and report back to us over a decade; how does it interact with time horizon; is there a self-undermining dynamic where our own credibility as a funder depends on things the rule excludes; and does the trading fellowship actually get treated differently by the math or am I just motivated-reasoning about that one.

And please flag where I'm overreaching. I want you to be explicit about which parts of this are settled among people who actually work on measurement in philanthropy and altruism, and which parts are genuinely live disputes — don't hand me a contested position dressed up as consensus, and tell me if my gut feeling is just the standard complaint with no teeth.
