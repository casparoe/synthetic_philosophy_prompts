I'm writing a seminar paper on the Dennett–Caruso dispute over just deserts and punishment, and I want a calibrated assessment of an argument I constructed myself. It is not in the literature as far as I know, so please do not hunt for a source for it, do not supply citations, and do not tell me which published author says what; I want your own judgment of the argument exactly as I state it below.

**Setup.** Take the free-will skeptic's preferred alternative to retributivism — the public-health/quarantine model in the form its advocates defend it: basic desert is unjustified, so incapacitation is legitimate only insofar as it is genuinely needed to protect others, and it is constrained by a least-infringement principle (use the mildest intervention that achieves the protective aim) plus some proportionality-to-danger requirement.

**The Trigger Argument.** An institutional version of that model has to say when detention may begin. Two triggers are available: a past offence, or a sufficiently high assessment of dangerousness.

1. On this model, the only consideration that can justify depriving someone of liberty is that doing so reduces expected harm to others.
2. If (1), then the justification for detaining a particular person is exhausted by that person's forward-looking risk profile.
3. A *risk-only* trigger commits the model to detaining non-offenders whom any plausible risk metric ranks above the median convicted offender, which the least-infringement principle does not license and which the model's advocates unanimously wish to disown.
4. An *offence* trigger avoids (3), but only by making the past act load-bearing: it permits against the offender a deprivation that identical forward-looking considerations would not permit against an equally dangerous non-offender.
5. A principle on which a past act changes what may permissibly be done to a person, independently of any change in that person's risk profile, is backward-looking in structure — regardless of the label attached to it.
6. Therefore the model either over-detains the innocent (3) or reinstates, under a new name, the structural feature of retributivism it was designed to eliminate (4)–(5).

**The proposition I want a number on.** Let **P** be: *a free-will skeptic can coherently defend a system of criminal detention that both (i) triggers on a past offence rather than on risk alone, and (ii) accounts for the normative force of that trigger without relying on any consideration whose justificatory work is backward-looking in the sense of (5).*
