Camp A — I'll call them the dissolutionists. Their view is that there's no paradox worth the name. The switching argument (the other envelope holds either half or double, so its expected value is 1.25x) treats the observed amount as a free parameter held fixed across two counterfactual states of the world, which is just a conditioning error; and it can only deliver a positive gain for *every* possible x if you smuggle in something inadmissible, namely an improper scale-invariant prior over the amounts or a prior with infinite expectation. Hand them any proper prior over the smaller amount and the conditional calculation becomes determinate: switch for small x, keep for large x, with a crossover wherever the tail forces one, and the unconditional gain from an always-switch policy is exactly zero by symmetry. Camp B — the residualists — grant all of that algebra and say it misses the point. Their claim is that the dissolution relocates the puzzle rather than answering it: an agent actually standing there with two envelopes has to decide before she can pin down a prior over the organizer's secret generation procedure, so "just specify a proper prior" is doing all the substantive work while being presented as a solution. They add that the class of distributions under which naive switching looks favorable isn't a curiosity, it's what you reach for whenever you genuinely don't know the scale, and in those cases you can have a policy that is never beaten pointwise and yet is dominated in aggregate — which for them is a real decision-theoretic pathology, not a slip in conditioning. Their slogan is that the two-envelope problem is a St. Petersburg problem wearing a disguise, and that dissolving it by conditioning hides the only thing worth learning from it.
