Friedman's F-twist — judge a theory only by the accuracy of its predictions about the phenomena it's meant to explain, and treat the realism of its assumptions as beside the point — ought to have a clean verdict on the Phillips curve. The arc as I've got it: Phillips found an inverse relation between UK unemployment and the rate of change of money wages over roughly a century of data; Samuelson and Solow turned that into a US policy menu by swapping wages for prices, while noting in passing that the shape of the menu might itself shift over time; it looked like it worked for fifteen years or so; then Friedman and Phelps argued the relation depended on workers mistaking nominal for real wages, called the long-run curve vertical, and stagflation showed up on schedule. Lucas then ran the same move on Friedman's own adaptive expectations.

So go through it and say which idealizations were harmless and which were load bearing by the F-twist's own standards — in particular whether the nominal/real confusion is a convenient fiction or a substantive psychological claim, since that seems to decide whether Lucas was continuing Friedman's method or overthrowing it. Does the F-twist actually vindicate Friedman's call, or could it only have issued that verdict after the fact? And what does the curve looking dead through the 2010s and then apparently waking up in 2022 do to any of this? One wrinkle: I've seen it argued (Forder) that the whole "everyone in the 60s believed in a stable exploitable trade-off" story is a myth written up in the 70s — if that's right, does the episode still test the F-twist, or has it lost its subject? Be explicit about where the F-twist just has nothing to say.
