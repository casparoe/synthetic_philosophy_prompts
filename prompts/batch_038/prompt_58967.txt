I'm auditing a graduate seminar on the foundations of decision theory this term, and for my final paper I want to do something the syllabus never does: treat the Allais paradox not as a thought experiment handed down from on high but as a piece of fieldwork with a date and a participant list, and then ask what that changes philosophically. The episode as I have it, which you should feel free to correct where I've mangled it — in the relevant numbered item, not in a preamble: in 1952 Allais circulated two short questionnaires to economists gathered in Paris for a conference on decision under uncertainty, a room that happened to contain several of the Americans then building the expected-utility program, and he published the responses the following year in Econometrica alongside his critique of the very axioms those people had proposed. The choice problems are the familiar ones — a million for certain against a gamble yielding a million with probability .89, nothing with probability .01, and five million with probability .10; then an eleven percent chance of a million against a ten percent chance of five million — and by Allais's own account a substantial share of his respondents took the certain million in the first pair and the ten percent shot at five million in the second, which is the pattern no utility function over money can generate once the independence axiom is imposed. Savage took the example up in The Foundations of Statistics two years later, and on my reading his reply was not to concede the datum but to relocate it: what a subject picks when the problem is first sprung on him is not the same object as the preference he has once he has thought the problem through. Half my seminar thinks the paradox was settled in 1979 and half thinks it was never a problem; I want the paper to adjudicate that by going back to the room in Paris.

Please give me exactly five numbered items, one per question below, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion.

1. Reconstruct the episode as a test rather than as an anecdote: what would have to hold for the 1952 responses to count as evidence against the independence axiom rather than as evidence about a roomful of jet-lagged economists being asked to introspect about hypothetical millions, and which of those conditions the episode actually meets.
2. Say what the axiom is being asked to be — a descriptive generalization about choices, a constitutive criterion for when a pattern of choice counts as a preference at all, or a norm that real agents only approximate — and then say which of those readings makes the paradox a paradox, which dissolves it, and what each reading gives up.
3. Assess Savage's reflective move on its own terms: is a reversal on reflection a discovery about what one already preferred or a revision of the preference, and could the identical move be run in the opposite direction to vindicate the respondents rather than correct them?
4. Identify two features of the episode that the independence-axiom framing misses or misdescribes — for instance the fact that one option is certain rather than merely high-probability, the presentation of the two questionnaires as a matched pair to be answered in sequence, or the gap between a stated choice and anything an agent would pay for — and say what each costs the standard reconstruction.
5. Render a verdict in both directions: whether the episode confirms the expected-utility program's self-understanding, is an anomaly it successfully absorbed, or is a case that program never anticipated, and whether the theories subsequently built to accommodate the pattern — rank-dependent utility, regret theory, risk-weighted expected utility — together with the later experimental results showing some of those fixes fail other patterns, tell us which side of 1952 was actually right.
