I'm three years into a novel about a woman who runs the research arm of one of those big strategic-giving foundations — longtermist in spirit, though I never use the word in the book because it dates the thing — and I've built her worldview by sort of accidentally building my own, over alot of late nights with a stack of library books I only half understood. Now I'm worried the thing I've built doesn't stand up. My agent keeps telling me to just write the scenes and let the philosophy sort itself out, but I can't, because there's a four-page argument in chapter 14 where her sister accuses her of caring about hypothetical people instead of real ones, and I have no idea what she can coherently say back until I know whether her position actually holds together or whether I've written her a smart-sounding speech that's hollow in the middle. So here's the package, stated as honestly as I can:

(1) I'm a straight expected-value maximizer — right act is the one with the best expected value, no side-constraints, no "some things you just don't do." (2) I reject the repugnant conclusion: a world containing a hundred billion lives each barely worth living is not better than a world of a few billion flourishing ones, and any arithmetic that says otherwise has gone wrong somewhere, because numbers alone shouldn't be able to win. (3) I don't discount low probabilities as such — if the payoff is big enough, a one-in-a-billion shot outweighs near-certainty of something modest, and I think people who wave away the mugging-type cases purely because the odds are tiny are dodging the argument rather than answering it. (4) What I do discount is speculative modelling: an estimate resting on a heroic chain of assumptions gets a haircut proportional to how heroic the chain is, so my character isn't a sucker for any large number someone waves at her. (5) I think the far future counts and counts heavily — there could be an astronomical number of people who don't exist yet and their interests aren't second-class. (6) A concrete verdict I'd give without hedging: a friend deciding between selling her house to fund a one-in-a-billion shot at averting a civilisation-ending catastrophe, and funding a boring well-evidenced health programme that saves a known number of children this decade, should fund the health programme, and she is not making a moral error by doing so. (7) My character is warm and particular and adores her daughter and thinks about her constantly, and I want that to be fully compatible with (1) rather than a failure of nerve or a seperate compartment she hasn't examined. (8) She also doesn't buy lottery tickets or earthquake insurance, which I don't think is a problem for her.

So: does this hang together? I want an audit, not a lecture. Go through it and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do — I suspect I've got at least one of each and I can't tell them apart from the inside. Rank the real tensions by severity rather than giving me a flat list, because I need to know what's fatal versus what's cosmetic; for each one say what has to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what it costs me in terms of the character and that chapter 14 argument. I'm not going to pretend I have a favourite plank or a preferred escape hatch, so don't assume I'm attached to any of them — including the ones that sound most like me. The speech leans hardest on her being able to say both that she isn't a fanatic and that she isn't a pushover, so please tell me straight whether this set can actually deliver both of those at once, or whether one of them is a load-bearing wall and the other is a curtain.
