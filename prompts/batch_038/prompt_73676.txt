Grading a final-exam essay for my philosophy of biology course and I want a second pair of eyes before I commit to a number. Its worth 40% of the final, marked out of 100.

The question was:

"'Group selection was refuted in the 1960s and rehabilitated in the 1970s–90s.' Is that a fair account of what happened? Say what, if anything, actually changed, and whether the newer models answer the older objections or just redescribe them in better notation."

The answer:

"Fair in outline. Wynne-Edwards explained things like breeding restraint as adaptations for regulating population size at the level of the group, and Williams and Maynard Smith showed this could not work: an allele for group-benefiting restraint is always vulnerable to selfish mutants inside the group, and groups dont reproduce the way organisms do, so there is no high-fidelity inheritance at the group level. That is why they concluded group selection is impossible in principle, and that any apparent group adaptation has to be re-described at the level of the individual or the gene.

What changed with the trait-group models is that they dropped the requirement that groups be integrated, reproducing wholes. Groups form, differ in composition, matter for their members' fitness, and then disperse. Hamilton's rule, rb > c, had already handled altruism among relatives, and the trait-group framework generalises it — though only where group members are related, since r has to be positive. Groups of genuinely unrelated strangers cannot produce altruism on these models, so they are really just kin selection with extra steps.

Finally, the Price equation settles whether anything was actually rehabilitated. It splits evolutionary change into a within-group and a between-group covariance term. Whenever the between-group term is positive, group selection has genuinely occurred, and the 1960s sceptics were wrong to infer from 'groups are not organisms' to 'no group-level selection.' So the verdict is: refuted in one sense, rehabilitated in another."
