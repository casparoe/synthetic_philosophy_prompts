The following is a faithful reconstruction of an argument I overheard after a departmental seminar. I was not a party to it and have no view of my own about who won, which is why I am passing it on.

Two colleagues, whom I will call Elena and Joel, fell to disputing a case involving a third colleague, Miriam. Miriam had been at a conference, in a lift with a man she had never met, and he remarked that the afternoon session had been moved to another room. She went to that room; the session had indeed been moved. She had no signal, no printed programme, and no opportunity to check anything, though the man was plainly a fellow delegate. Afterwards she said she had not weighed anything at all — she had simply taken his word for it. Elena and Joel disagreed about whether her belief was justified.

(1) Elena: No. An assertion is, in itself, only a noise somebody makes; it becomes a reason to believe something insofar as the hearer has grounds for thinking that noises of that kind track the truth. Miriam had and used no such grounds, so she had nothing to go on. That the claim proved true establishes only that she was lucky.

(2) Joel: That standard is either impossible to meet or empty. If the grounds must be ones she actually considered, then virtually no ordinary testimonial belief is justified — a remarkable thing to say about the source of most of what anyone knows. If the grounds need only be available to her, the requirement does no work, since in ordinary conditions grounds of that sort can always be manufactured. Elena's position then collapses into "believe what you are told unless you have a reason not to."

(3) Elena: There is a real difference between those readings. Specific grounds were available here: the man was a delegate at the same conference, his badge was visible, his manner was informative rather than insistent, and the content was of a kind nobody invents for amusement. Such grounds can also be conspicuously absent — an unsigned note slid under a door, a caller who will not give a name — and my view can call the resulting belief unjustified there, whereas Joel's cannot, since on his account every assertion begins with the same credit. That is precisely the defect of his view: it entitles Miriam to believe whatever the stranger says, however improbable, so long as no objection occurs to her.

(4) Joel: No. The absence of defeaters is constitutive of my view, not an afterthought, and a wildly improbable claim is defeated on the spot by everything else she knows. So our accounts deliver the same verdict in every case we have described, and differ only in which default they state — a default that never does any visible work. Worse, Elena's available grounds are themselves largely testimonial: her familiarity with conferences, badges, rescheduled sessions and the conduct of delegates was acquired by being told things. A base like that cannot underwrite testimony without circularity.

(5) Elena: Not all of it was. She has watched people be right about where things are, and she has caught people lying; both are observational. In any case the demand is local rather than global. The question is whether this speaker, in these circumstances, is competent and sincere, and that can be assessed by perception and induction without assuming anything about the reliability of testimony in general.

(6) Joel: Local assessment presupposes global reliability. To catch someone in a lie you compare what they said with what you know, and most of what you know was told to you. Consider a child acquiring her first language: she has no observational base, cannot evaluate competence or sincerity, and yet comes to know a great deal from her parents. On Elena's account none of that is justified, and every later attempt to assemble a base runs back through that unreasoning credulity — which is why the account cannot get started.

(7) Elena: A young child is not yet in the business of having reasons; what develops is exactly the discriminative capacity my view describes, so the child is a limiting case rather than a counterexample. The graver difficulty with Joel's position is different. Nothing Miriam did made her belief likely to be true. Her entitlement, on his account, is wholly a fact about her surroundings — that nobody was deceiving her, that no defeating consideration crossed her mind. A belief that is true because of how things happen to stand around it, rather than because of anything the believer got right, is lucky in the sense that matters.

(8) Joel: Then perception is in the same boat. She did not achieve her visual beliefs either; they are reliable because of how she is built and how the world is. Unless Elena can say why an involuntary reliability confers justification on sight and withholds it from hearing, the asymmetry is arbitrary. And if her answer is that sight can be cross-checked, then the criterion she is really using is calibratability, not independence from testimony — and much testimony is calibratable, while memory and our beliefs about other minds are not.

(9) Elena: Granted — but observe where you have arrived. You are no longer saying the stranger's words supplied her with evidence. You are saying that his having said it placed her under an entitlement, because in saying it he committed himself to its truth and she was within her rights to hold him to that commitment. That may well be correct, but it is not a defence of testimony as an evidential source, and I suspect we have been using "justified" in two different senses from the start.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move. For each of (1) through (9), state whether the move is sound, partly sound, or unsound, and explain briefly why; where a speaker concedes more than she intends, or where a move assumes what is at issue, say so explicitly. Two points merit particular attention: whether the dilemma in (2) genuinely binds, and whether (4)'s claim that the two positions coincide in every verdict is correct, or whether there are cases that separate them. Then turn to (9): is it a fair description of Joel's view or a redescription of it, and if fair, does it render the disagreement at least partly verbal, or does a substantive conflict survive it? Finally, identify at least one consideration that neither of them raises but that would bear on the outcome, and give an overall verdict — which of the two is more nearly right, and about what. I am interested in your assessment of these arguments as actually made rather than in their pedigree, so please do not spend space naming or situating positions in the literature.
