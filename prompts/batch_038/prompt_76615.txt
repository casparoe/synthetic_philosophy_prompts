I've gotten weirdly obsessed with the voting paradox after an argument with my roommate, and I want to find out what principle, if any, is hiding behind my own gut calls. Context: I sit on my condo associations board, nine of us, and last month a vote came out 4-4 with one abstention — the guy who abstained said he just "didn't have a view" — and I was furious at him in a way I have never once been about a national election. So here are my verdicts and I want you to tell me what they add up to, or whether they add up to nothing.

(1) Voting in a presidential election in a state where it's close: not irrational, mildly praiseworthy, and I'd feel a bit ashamed skipping it. (2) Voting in my town's council race, 30,000 voters, my guy wins by 4,000: totally routine, nothing to be proud of, no shame at all in skipping. (3) Somebody who drives three hours through a blizzard to cast a ballot in a national election that's near-certain to go one way anyway: admirable. (4) Somebody who drives three hours through a blizzard to vote in the town council race, where by my back-of-envelope they're maybe ten thousand times more likely to be decisive: kind of sad, like over-investment in a hobby. Same cost, wildly better odds of actually mattering, opposite verdict — I know thats incoherent, but its what I feel. (5) The condo board abstainer: genuinely blameworthy, worse than the national-election skipper, even though the stakes are a pool resurfacing budget. (6) Voting in a landslide for the side that was always going to win: not pointless exactly, more like signing a guestbook — but I wouldnt blame anyone who stayed home, whereas I do blame (5). (7) The purely uninformed vote, cast only to dodge a penalty — Australia fines you for not turning up but from what I understand doesn't care whether your ballot is a real choice or a scribble: I think that vote is worse than no vote, and also think it's none of the state's business to prevent. (8) An expat who's lived abroad twenty years, can still vote by mail, has no idea what's going on and no stake in the outcome: should abstain, even though their odds of being decisive are exactly the same as mine at home. (9) The three-hour blizzard drive is admirable and the two-minute mail ballot isn't, so my admiration seems to be tracking cost rather than causal effect, which I find embarrassing.

So: is there one principle behind all of this, or two incompatible ones fighting? A friend who does political science said the standard repair is to bolt a "duty" term onto the expected-value formula so it always pays to vote — but I've noticed that a duty term would be the same number in every single election, so I don't see how it could generate the fact that I feel so differently about (3) and (4), or why (5) and (8) come out the way they do. Give me the principle, or the two or three real candidates, state each one precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case, and say which item in my list is doing the work for each clause of it. Then tell me what your favorite one predicts about two cases I havent listed: someone who abstains on purpose because both candidates are awful, and someone voting in a party primary with 6% turnout where they're 100x more likely to be decisive but where I honestly feel no extra pull. And be straight with me about which of my verdicts your principle has to overturn — I'd rather lose a verdict than keep something baroque.
