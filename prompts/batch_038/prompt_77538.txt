Everyone who writes about Christopher Nolan's Memento seems to treat Leonard as the poster boy for epistemic akrasia — a guy who believes against his own better judgment about what his evidence supports — and I want to push back on that in a talk I'm giving, but I'm not sure what the right verdict is supposed to be instead. Help me work it out.

Set the film against the higher-order evidence debate. The standard higher-order defeat case goes like this: you get evidence not about the world but about your own reliability — you've been slipped something that scrambles your reasoning, you've been awake thirty hours, everyone whose judgment you trust disagrees with you — and that undercuts your belief even though nothing in your first-order evidence changed. Teddy does exactly that to Leonard in the final stretch: he tells him the Sammy Jankis story might be Leonard's own story, that the real attacker is already dead, that Leonard has been manufacturing fresh John G's for himself. That looks like a paradigm defeater. The catch is that Leonard cant hold onto it. By the time his next self forms the belief again the defeater has evaporated, and what's left is the notes, the Polaroids, the tattoos — all of which are things he controls the content of. So I want this question pressed hard: when the time-slice that has the higher-order evidence and the time-slice that forms the belief it defeats arent the same subject in any epistemically relevant sense, what should we say? Is Leonard rational, irrational, or is he sitting in a gap that even the level-splitting literature — the view that you can be rational in believing p while also being rational in believing that your belief that p is irrational — doesnt have a slot for?

Second thing, the ending. He burns the photograph, writes the warning note on Teddy's picture, and decides the lie is load-bearing because otherwise he has no reason to get up in the morning. Is that akrasia at all, or is it better described as deliberate management of your future self's evidence — same family as someone who plans ahead to read only one side of an argument, or who schedules themselves a confidence drug? Are those moves ever epistemically permissible, and what does it cost us to say Leonard's is?

I want the talk to be honest in both directions, so tell me where the film oversimplifies the philosophy or where the philosophy just doesn't get a grip on it, and which three or four scenes I should build the argument around. Finish with what Memento actually contributes to this debate that a journal article trading in pills and disagreement cases can't — the shape of the reasoning, not further reading.
