I am a junior at a public high school, and I want to write this carefully, because the last time I tried to make this case out loud I was shut down in about ten seconds.

Last spring our district enabled monitoring software on every school-issued laptop and on every school email and cloud-storage account. It scans students' documents, messages, and searches for keywords associated with self-harm, violence, and drugs, and routes alerts to administrators and counselors, including in the evenings and on weekends. I spoke against the policy at a board meeting. My argument had three parts: that the alerts are overwhelmingly false alarms, so the system mostly generates investigations of students who did nothing; that the burden falls unevenly, since the students most likely to be flagged are the ones writing honestly in private drafts about difficult things happening at home; and that knowing a folder might be read changes what people are willing to write down in the first place, which is a loss even for students who are never flagged at all.

A board member replied with the familiar line that if you have nothing to hide, you have nothing to fear. A number of parents nodded, the discussion moved on, and I never got anywhere.

I have since become convinced that the line was doing several different jobs at once, and I cannot yet separate them. Could you lay out the distinct precise claims someone might be making when they say it? I am thinking of questions like: whether "nothing to hide" means nothing illegal, nothing shameful, or nothing you would mind anyone seeing; whether "nothing to fear" means no punishment, no embarrassment, or no being misunderstood; whose judgment of what counts as something to hide the claim assumes; how much the claim depends on the watcher's inferences being accurate; and over what time span it is supposed to hold, since things that are harmless now may not stay harmless. Please go further than those if there are other readings I have not thought of.

For each reading, tell me whether it amounts to a platitude nobody would dispute, a substantive claim that could actually be argued for, or something false — and then tell me which readings, if any, would genuinely defeat the three points I made, and which ones leave them completely untouched. I would also like a closing judgment: which single reading do you think the board member most likely had in mind, given how the line is normally used, and what I would have to show to establish that even that reading does not answer me.

One further request, and it matters to me. Please be explicit about which parts of this are settled and which are genuinely disputed among people who study privacy seriously. I would much rather be told that the value of privacy for people who have done nothing wrong is contested terrain, with several competing accounts of what privacy even is, than be handed one such account as though it were the agreed-upon answer. And if any of my own three points is weaker or more loaded than I think it is, say so plainly rather than taking my side by default.
