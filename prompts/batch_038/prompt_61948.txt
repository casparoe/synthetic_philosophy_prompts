In the 2016 EU referendum the franchise extended to Irish and qualifying Commonwealth citizens resident in the UK, while excluding other EU nationals resident here — people who vote in British local and European elections and who had voted in the Scottish independence referendum — and excluding British citizens who had been off the UK electoral register for more than fifteen years. The question put, however, concerned the UK's future relation to a supranational polity whose membership rules themselves determine which foreign nationals may live and work here, and what rights British nationals retain abroad; so the referendum settled a question about the boundaries of the demos using a demos that the question would itself alter.

What theory of political legitimacy does that franchise presuppose — an all-citizens principle, an all-subjected principle, an all-affected principle, or no principle at all but administrative path dependence on the general-election roll? Reconstruct the implicit theory, and distinguish what the practice strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit. Then say whether its parts presuppose consistent things: is the inclusion of resident Commonwealth and Irish nationals reconcilable with the exclusion of resident French or Polish nationals, and does the fifteen-year rule express a view about how long a stake persists, or only a bright line that had to be drawn somewhere? Finally, if the all-affected principle were taken seriously, what would have to change about referendums of this kind — and could such a referendum be constituted at all, given that those affected include non-residents, future citizens and foreign nationals with no say in the result?

Please write your answer in British English.
