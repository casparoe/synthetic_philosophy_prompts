For about a year I have been keeping a notebook of trade-off verdicts about pleasure and pain — most of them surfacing in arguments with friends about charity, about whether to have children, and about whether a world with more people in it would be a better world — and I would like to know whether they add up to a principle I could actually state, or whether some of them have to go. Two friends insist that I am a negative utilitarian of a particular stripe: someone who believes there is a threshold of suffering above which pain is lexically prior to pleasure, so that no quantity of the latter can outweigh the former. I have not resisted the description, but I am no longer sure it fits, because my other verdicts run the opposite way. Here they are, roughly in the order I wrote them down. One stranger spared a year of untreated, disabling agony, versus a hundred thousand strangers each given the best year of their lives: save the one, and I do not think a million in place of the hundred thousand would move me. One stranger spared a month of migraine, versus ten million strangers each given a genuinely pleasant afternoon: the ten million, easily, even though a migraine is a dreadful thing while it lasts. One stranger's stubbed toe versus a billion strangers' mild pleasures: the billion. An hour of the agony from the first case versus a mild ache lasting ten thousand years: the ten thousand years is worse — I flatly refuse to let duration buy down intensity. And one stranger spared a year of acute grief after a bereavement, versus the same hundred thousand given their best years: here I go over to the hundred thousand, which sits badly beside the first case, since the grief was at least as awful to live through as the agony, and in my own worst year I would not have traded places with anyone.

Then there is the pair I cannot hold in my head at the same time. A life containing one month of severe pain and eighty years of intense joy: worth living, better than never existing, and a life I would want for my child rather than no child at all. A billion such lives versus a billion lives with the same eighty years of joy and no such month: the billion without the month — and I will add that no conceivable enlargement of the joy, eighty years of it or eight hundred or eight million, would make me switch. But if the month is never made up for in the sense I just insisted on, the first life is worse than nonexistence, and the second verdict is only the first one applied a billion times over. If the month is made up for, as the first verdict says, then the second one must be trading off after all, merely at a punishing rate. I suspect that I am running two different comparisons together and reporting both of them as claims about what is worse, but I cannot say what the two comparisons are.

So: what principle, if any, delivers these verdicts? I would like two or three candidates, stated precisely enough to settle cases I have not thought of, each clause tied to the verdict that motivates it, and an unflinching account of what each one has to surrender — I would rather be told that the honest price of simplicity is losing the grief case than be handed a machine with four unexplained dials. Two things I especially want pressed on. First, what my threshold is a threshold *of*: it cannot be felt intensity, since agony and grief come out level there and I judge them differently, and I keep circling the thought that what separates them is whether the suffering hollows out a life or is merely endured inside one — but I do not want the weight of a pain to depend on how much its sufferer had to lose, and I cannot see what else is doing the work. Tell me whether the view that emerges from my verdicts is still about pleasure and pain at all, or whether it has quietly become a view about agency and what a person remains able to do. Second, what the principle says about cases I have deliberately left out: short-lived animals whose whole existence may consist of states I would place below my threshold; whether it is a gain to add a vast number of lives that sit just barely on the worthwhile side of the line rather than a smaller number of very good ones; whether the threshold shifts when I would be causing the pain rather than merely failing to prevent it, or when the sufferer is me; and whether a pain that its sufferer afterwards reports being glad to have had counts differently from one they wish had never occurred. Finally, the case I keep returning to and cannot decide. My sister is carrying a fetus that, on the clinicians' estimate, will have a life with real joys and real attachments and something like a third of its years given over to moderate chronic pain — pain I can place neither above nor below my threshold, because I have never had it and she cannot tell me what it is like from the inside. Does my principle say that bringing this child into existence benefits the child, harms it, or does neither, and does it matter whether the alternative is that this particular child never exists or that a different, healthy child is conceived a year later? I should say that I have no training in any of this; I am a structural engineer, and the whole notebook began when a friend observed that I will drive across town at midnight for my dog's anti-inflammatory and have never once bought a ticket for anything I would call a pleasure.
