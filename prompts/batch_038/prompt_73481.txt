I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am in the final weeks of reporting a long feature about the recurring argument over whether today's large language models might have inner lives. One thread I keep pulling is how quickly the contemporary debate collapses into a nineteenth-century one, so I want to build a substantial section around Thomas Henry Huxley and the doctrine he defended in his 1874 address to the British Association meeting at Belfast: that animals, human beings included, are conscious automata, whose consciousness accompanies the machinery of the nervous system without ever intervening in it. The image I associate with that argument is of a whistle that travels along with a locomotive's work and is entirely without influence on the mechanism producing it — though if you think that image actually comes from somewhere else in Huxley, or from a later summariser rather than from Huxley himself, I would want to know, since I would rather cut it than misattribute it in print. My editor wants this section to be more than intellectual history, so what I need help with is how Huxley himself would have handled an objection that only became explicit long after his death.

That objection is the one now often called the paradox of phenomenal judgment. If feeling is causally inert, then the physical events that carry my fingers to the keys when I type out a sentence about my headache would have unfolded exactly as they did had there been no headache at all; so the fact that such reports so consistently match the experience they describe looks either like a colossal coincidence or like evidence that consciousness is doing causal work after all. There is a familiar epiphenomenalist rejoinder: the brain state that constitutes the pain and the brain state that produces the report share a common cause, so the report can be reliably correlated with the experience without the experience causing anything. What I cannot work out is which side of that dispute Huxley would have taken, and on what grounds. So far as I can tell, he was not much exercised by the epistemology of his own testimony; his aim was to show that a complete physiological account of behaviour is available in principle and that summoning a soul to fill explanatory gaps is a surrender rather than an explanation. Would a thinker of that temperament treat the reliability-of-report problem as a genuine difficulty for his view, as a merely verbal demand, or as a reductio of the assumption that a report has to be caused by what it reports? And would his answer shift if he were confronted with a machine whose every utterance about its experience is generated by mechanisms in which nobody supposes experience occurs — would he say such a machine merely makes explicit a predicament human beings are already in, so that the epistemic gap between a person's avowals and a model's is not the one everyone assumes, or would he say it shows that reading consciousness off reports was bankrupt from the start?

Two further things I would like your judgment on, and then a request about how you frame all of it. The first is a practical point: I have seen it claimed in passing that Huxley thought his doctrine carried no consequences for how animals ought to be treated, on the grounds that behaviour remains the only guide available to us either way. I do not know whether that is something he actually argued or something later writers have put in his mouth, and I would rather not print it without knowing which; treat it as a question to be assessed, not as a premise I am supplying. The second is that I am not asking you for sources or a reading list — I can chase those down separately — I want the reasoning. So please be explicit about your confidence and about the state of the evidence: I am not looking for a single assured answer to a counterfactual that has no determinate answer, but for a clear separation between what scholars of Huxley and historians of nineteenth-century physiology actually agree on, what remains genuinely contested among people who know the material, and what is your own extrapolation from the texture of his commitments. Where a reading of him is a live dispute rather than settled ground, say so plainly instead of letting it pass as consensus.
