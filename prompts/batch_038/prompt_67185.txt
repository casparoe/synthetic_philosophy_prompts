I'm a senior, and for two years now my older sister Rania — third year of a philosophy PhD — has run what my family calls the dinner-table seminar: she poses a decision problem, we argue, and then she tells us what she would actually do. Her framework is a version of superrationality, which she says she took from Hofstadter's essays, though she insists her version departs from his in places and I have no way to check that either way, since I've only managed to find the original material and not her amendments. She has a name for the group of agents whose reasoning she takes to match her own: she calls it her mirror set. For my school's philosophy club I'm giving a talk on whether a person's track record of verdicts really pins down the rule they're following, and her record is my case study, because I suspect it doesn't.

Here are the verdicts I've written down. (1) A one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a stranger she knows nothing about and expects to be an ordinary payoff-maximizer: she defects. (2) The same game against someone she's confident reasons as she does — both have read the same material, both know the other has, no communication, no second round, no reputation at stake: she cooperates. (3) Newcomb's problem where the predictor has simulated her reasoning: she takes only the opaque box. (4) A one-shot prisoner's dilemma in which the organizers announce beforehand that the other player's choice has already been fixed by an algorithm that looked only at demographic data and matches her eventual choice about eighty-five percent of the time, with no attempt to model anyone's reasoning: she defects, and she maintains this has nothing in common with case (3) even though the correlation looks the same from the outside. (5) Two people in her mirror set, one internal grant, both eligible: if both apply the funder is so annoyed by duplicate applications that it funds neither; if both decline the money goes to another department and neither gets anything; if one applies and the other declines, the applicant gets it and the other gets nothing — she says she would flip a fair coin, and she adds that in any such case the two people must use separate coins, because if they both flip the same coin in their heads nothing has been gained. (6) She votes in every election, including ones where she is certain her vote won't be decisive and where she cannot say how many other voters are in her mirror set; when I pressed her she conceded this one doesn't follow neatly from the rest. (7) On the edges of the mirror set: someone at least as clever as she is who has read the same material but cares only about winning, with no regard for the joint outcome, is not in it, and she plays them as an ordinary opponent — whereas someone she considers a considerably better reasoner than herself, who shares her framework and her ranking of outcomes, is in it, on the grounds that a better reasoner will see everything she sees, including what she expects them to see.

Next month she faces a real instance of case (5), except that the numbers are different. Her department may put forward exactly one nominee for a national fellowship, and the two plausible nominees are she and a fellow student she has co-authored with, who is in her mirror set in the fullest sense she requires: same framework, same information, and — she has checked — effectively the same stakes, since both are in their third year with the same funding left. The chair has told them both explicitly what will happen. If each puts their own name forward, the committee will deadlock and let the slot lapse rather than choose between them. If one puts their name forward and the other steps aside, the nominee gets a realistic shot at the fellowship. If both step aside, the committee will submit a joint nomination naming the two of them, which is worth considerably less than a sole nomination but is not nothing. Rating outcomes for herself, she says: sole nomination three, joint nomination one, lapsed slot zero, and stepping aside while the other takes the sole nomination also zero, since she would get nothing this cycle and no credit. Her colleague faces exactly the same numbers. What I want from you is this: lay out the candidate rules that are jointly consistent with verdicts (1) through (7), and I expect there are at least two or three genuinely different ones rather than one rule with different words; say what each of them tells her to do about the fellowship, and if any of them tells her to randomize, with what probability; give me your single best prediction of her answer together with your reasons for preferring that rule to the others, paying attention to which specific details of the earlier record each rule can and cannot explain, since I suspect the separate-coins remark, the case (4) insistence, and the voting admission each do a lot of work here; and finally name one further case I could put to her at dinner that would settle which rule she is running, saying what each candidate rule would answer in it.
