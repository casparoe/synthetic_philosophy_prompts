My friend and I spent most of last night on the Lucas–Penrose argument and arrived somewhere neither of us wanted. She is a logician and I am not, so I tried to settle it by writing the reasoning out as an explicit chain. The chain now appears to run straight into Gödel's second theorem, and I would like it taken apart step by step rather than reassured.

Here is what I wrote down. Let *me* stand for my own arithmetical reasoning, and let F be a formal system in the language of arithmetic.

(1) To be a machine of the relevant kind is for one's arithmetical reasoning to be reproduced by some F whose axioms and inference rules are effectively specifiable — specifiable, that is, in a form that could in principle be laid out in front of me for inspection.

(2) If F reproduces my reasoning, then every axiom and every rule of F is one I would accept; otherwise F would be deriving things I reject. So I am in a position to recognize each axiom of F as true and each rule as truth-preserving. Call this the surveyability condition.

(3) If I can recognize every axiom of F as true and every rule as truth-preserving, then I can recognize that F is sound; and soundness entails consistency.

(4) If F is consistent and sufficiently strong, then F's Gödel sentence — the sentence that, under the standard coding, says of itself that F does not prove it — is true and unprovable in F.

(5) So if F reproduces my reasoning, I can see the truth of an arithmetical sentence F cannot prove, and therefore cannot prove either, if F really does reproduce my reasoning. Contradiction. Hence no such F reproduces my reasoning.

That is the conclusion I expected. The trouble is what (3) licenses next.

(6) Nothing in (2)–(5) was special to a system that happens to reproduce me. (3) licenses me to see the soundness of *any* system meeting the surveyability condition — and the reasoning I have just carried out is itself a public exercise with its axioms on the table.

(7) Form F₁ = F together with F's Gödel sentence. F₁ is again effectively specifiable, since there is a computable procedure taking an index for F to an index for F₁, and it meets the surveyability condition, since I inspected the axiom I added.

(8) Reapply (3)–(4) to F₁: I can see that F₁ is sound, hence see its Gödel sentence, hence form F₂, and so on. The iteration is uniform, so at the limit I can take in the whole ascending sequence and see the soundness of the union F_ω.

(9) F_ω is effectively specifiable as the range of a computable function, and by construction it contains everything I have seen up to this point, including each recognition of each predecessor's soundness.

(10) Applying (3) again, I can see that F_ω is sound and so consistent. But F_ω contains my arithmetical reasoning, since at each stage I added precisely what I had come to see. Therefore I can see that I am consistent.

(11) Gödel's second theorem says a consistent, sufficiently strong, effectively axiomatized system cannot prove its own consistency. If F_ω reproduces my reasoning, then my seeing my own consistency is a proof of my own consistency available inside F_ω — which the second theorem forbids.

(12) So one of three things holds: F_ω does not reproduce my reasoning, in which case (9) is false and there is reasoning of mine outside it; or F_ω is inconsistent, in which case so am I, and (5) along with the entire argument collapses, since it needed my consistency at (2) and (3); or I can prove my own consistency, and the second theorem is false.

Her reading is that (12) makes the Lucasian position self-undermining: it either fails to get started or terminates in the claim that I am inconsistent, which nobody wants to accept. My reading is that something slipped, probably between (8) and (10), but I cannot name it and I refuse to just assert that it slipped.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look innocent when I wrote it? I take (2), (8), (9), (10), and (11) to be the live candidates, though I may have the list wrong. For each, say what it costs the Lucasian to give it up. In particular:

- Does the move in (3) from recognizing axioms to recognizing soundness actually go through when the axioms are delivered by a schema rather than listed finitely? I half suspect this is where the trouble lives, but I cannot see why a schema should behave differently, since I can still see that each of its instances is true.
- Is the uniformity in (7)–(8) carrying more weight than it appears to? What exactly has to be computable — and known by me to be computable — for the iteration to be something I can perform rather than something a bystander could describe about me?
- At (10), is "F_ω contains my reasoning" the same claim as "F_ω reproduces my reasoning," or have I traded one for the other? And if I can see the consistency of each Fₙ separately, what would it take to turn that into a single seeing of the consistency of their union?
- If the correct answer is that the second theorem simply does not apply to me because I am not an effectively axiomatized system, has my friend not already won the point she thinks the argument was supposed to establish — namely that the Lucasian has no non-question-begging route to her own consistency?

If I have to choose, I would rather keep (5) than (12). The intuition that we can see the truth of the Gödel sentence of a system we can survey is the one thing here that feels secure to me, and I want to know precisely what I am paying to keep it. I am not after the history of the debate or a list of who said what; I want this chain diagnosed.
