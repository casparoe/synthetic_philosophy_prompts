I'm revising a dissertation chapter that sits at the seam between social choice theory and political philosophy, and I've hit a point where I need an interlocutor willing to commit to a number. The chapter's target is a line of argument I've come to call, for want of a better label, the *negligibility defense*: since the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem guarantees that every non-dictatorial deterministic rule over three or more alternatives is manipulable, manipulability cannot do any work in choosing among such rules, and what remains to rank them by is everything else — majority consistency, monotonicity, resistance to vote splitting and clone effects, hand-countability, public intelligibility. On that view the theorem's moral for institutional design is not "keep hunting for a strategy-proof rule" but "strategy-proofness has ceased to be a desideratum," and a theorist who keeps worrying about manipulation is paying a cost for nothing.

To state the question precisely I need a magnitude notion rather than a binary one, so let me stipulate a coinage of my own. For a rule R and a distribution D over preference profiles, call the *manipulation surface* of R under D the expected product of (i) the fraction of voters who could, by some misreport, change the winner and (ii) the distance, in those voters' own ordinal rankings, between what sincere voting yields and what their best available misreport yields. Note a wrinkle I can't resolve: two rules can share the same aggregate manipulation surface while differing completely in how that surface is distributed — thinly spread over nearly every profile in one case, concentrated in a small set of near-tie profiles in the other — and it is not obvious to me that the aggregate figure is the politically relevant one. Relatedly, there is what I'll call the *composition objection* to the standard escapes from the theorem. Randomization buys strategy-proofness at the price of the determinacy that lets an outcome be publicly justified to those who lose it; the quasi-linear transfer route in the Vickrey–Clarke–Groves family presupposes a numeraire and a willingness to pay that political elections conspicuously lack; domain restriction — single-peaked preferences and the generalized-median rules that are strategy-proof on them — requires that the restriction actually obtain and stay stable, which is a political fact rather than an institutional choice, and is plausibly itself the object of manipulation; and the computational-hardness-of-manipulation argument shields only voters who would have to compute a beneficial misreport, whereas actual strategic voting looks heuristic and imitative rather than optimizing. My hunch is that these escapes do not compose into a design that is actually available for elections.
