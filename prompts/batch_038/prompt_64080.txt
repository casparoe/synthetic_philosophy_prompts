THE QUESTION (20 points, ~400 words expected):

"Take a sorites series for 'bald' running from a man with a full head of hair down to a completely hairless one, one hair at a time. Call a man in the middle of the series, in the borderline region, Harry. Explain how a supervaluationist and an epistemicist would each handle this case. For each view, say: (i) whether there is a sharp boundary in the series and what the status of that boundary is; (ii) what the view says about 'Harry is bald or Harry is not bald'; and (iii) what role ignorance or indeterminacy plays in the account, plus what you take to be the view's main outstanding problem."

Rubric our seminar uses: 8 points for accurately stating each view's semantics, 6 for the treatment of ignorance/indeterminacy, 6 for the comparison and for identifying each view's main problem.

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER:

"Both views start from the same diagnosis: the sorites only goes through if the tolerance principle is true — that one hair can never make the difference between bald and not bald — and both deny tolerance. Where they differ is in what they put in its place.

Supervaluationism, in Fine's version, says 'bald' is not fully precise but can be made precise in many different ways. A precisification is a way of sharpening the extension of 'bald' into a sharp one. A sentence is supertrue if it comes out true on every precisification, superfalse if it comes out false on every precisification, and neither if it comes out true on some and false on others. So there is no single cutoff in the series, though relative to any given precisification there is a last man who isn't bald. Because different precisifications put that boundary in different places, 'there is a last non-bald man in the series' is neither supertrue nor superfalse: a truth-value gap. But 'Harry is bald or Harry is not bald' is supertrue, since on each precisification one of the disjuncts holds. That's the selling point — the supervaluationist gives up bivalence but keeps excluded middle and every other classical tautology, so she doesn't have to do anything exotic to the logic.

Epistemicism keeps bivalence and instead says tolerance is simply false. There is a last non-bald man, a precise cutoff, and 'Harry is bald' is either true or false; we just don't know which. Williamson's argument here is basically an application of the KK principle: if anyone knew where the cutoff was, they'd know that they knew it, and we clearly don't know that we know it, so nobody knows it. On this view our ignorance is an evidential limit — like not knowing how many grains are in the heap sitting in front of you, the fact is perfectly determinate, it's just that our perceptual and linguistic evidence isn't fine-grained enough to pin it down. If we had a complete record of every use of 'bald' by every speaker, we could work out exactly where the cutoff falls.

So the two views share a picture — lots of candidate sharp boundaries — and disagree about whether one of them is the right one. The supervaluationist pays with truth-value gaps and a problem about higher-order vagueness, since 'definitely bald' looks vague too; the epistemicist pays with a boundary that looks arbitrary, and owes an account of how use could ever fix it."
