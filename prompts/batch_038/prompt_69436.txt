I sit on a small working group attached to our university's technology-transfer office, and we have been asked to redraft the policy that governs who holds what when a graduate student's algorithm, a postdoc's assay, or a lab notebook full of negative results turns into something licensable. Six months in, my conviction is that we are not actually disagreeing about policy: we are disagreeing because nobody in the room can say what "owning an idea" is supposed to amount to, and each of us is silently importing a different account whenever the phrase does work in an argument. Before I draft anything further, I want the specification rather than another candidate theory. Write out the desiderata: what any account of when, and in what sense, an idea can be owned has to deliver in order to count as adequate. Since adequacy is relative to purpose, let the purpose be this — a theory good enough to settle institutional allocation questions of the kind my office faces every week, not merely good enough to survive a metaphysics seminar.

I would like the list to grow out of pressure points like the following, though I do not want it confined to them, and I would rather you add requirements I have not thought of than pad the ones I have. (a) Non-rivalry: my holding an account of some factorization method costs you nothing, so the theory has to say what survives this as a ground for ownership, and — this is my own formulation, which you may reject or rename — it has to say where the genuine rivalry actually sits, since what is contested in practice is not the idea itself but positions in a market for its use. Call this the rivalry-relocation requirement. (b) The boundary problem: ideas do not come with natural joints, so a theory must say what individuates the owned item — the claim as written, the embodiment, the equivalence class of solutions, the problem solved — and cannot simply gesture at "the idea." (c) Independent arrival: two groups with no contact produce the same result weeks apart, one publishes and one files; copyright treats independent creation as a complete answer, patent law does not, and a theory has to explain why the two domains should differ, or else say they should not. (d) Foreclosure of downstream improvement: whatever grounds the first owner's claim seems present in even greater degree in the person who improves on it, yet improvement is often exactly what the original right blocks. (e) Credit without control: attribution, reputation, and citation are interests inventors plainly have, and a theory must say whether they are ownership interests, adjacent interests, or nothing the theory owes. (f) Disclosure as consideration: in the patent domain ownership is granted on condition that the idea be made public, which is strange for property and needs explaining rather than assuming. (g) Epistemic opacity: priority is frequently undeterminable — oral transmission, cumulative folk technique, plant varieties refined over generations by no identifiable hand, code assembled from unattributable fragments — and a theory that only works when the provenance ledger is clean is not yet a theory.

Alongside the list itself, I want three further things. First, for each requirement, mark it as non-negotiable, negotiable, or a prejudice of a particular camp, and be blunt about the third category: I suspect that demands such as "ownership must be exclusionary rather than compensatory," "there must be a single determinate owner," and "the right must be grounded in the owner's labor" are doing partisan work while masquerading as conceptual requirements. Second, identify any subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and give the argument; if the list turns out to be an impossibility result in disguise, that is the most valuable thing you could tell me. Third, say which single requirement eliminates the most contenders, and then — briefly, and only after the criteria are fully laid out — score the main families against the list: Lockean labor-based accounts, personality or self-expression accounts, incentive-and-welfare accounts, and the deflationary position on which there is no ownership of ideas at all but only a regulated bundle of entitlements to exclude or to be paid.

Please organize the response under these headings, in this order: **Purpose and Scope** (about 150 words, stating what the theory is being asked to be good for and how that constrains the list); **The Desiderata** (the core of the answer, 1,000–1,400 words, as a numbered list in which each item has a short name, a statement of the requirement, the case or consideration that motivates it, and its non-negotiable / negotiable / camp-prejudice tag); **Distinctions the Theory May Not Collapse** (about 300 words — expression versus thought, exclusion versus remuneration, attribution versus control, discovery versus invention, and any others you think we routinely run together); **Incompatibilities** (about 300 words); **The Excluder** (about 150 words, naming the one requirement that does the most work and what it kills); **Brief Scorecard** (about 300 words, no more); and **Bottom Line** (five sentences, including your honest view of whether my office should be looking for a theory at all or merely for a defensible bundle of rules). Where a requirement is contested, state the requirement in the form its proponents actually need rather than a straw version.
