Background: I write backend infra, no philosophy coursework ever, but our team started an AI-safety reading group and I've somehow become the one who has to argue with the smartest guy on the team. He wrote up an argument in our internal doc to show that the whole "deceptive alignment" worry is incoherent, and four of the seven people in the group signed off on it. I think its wrong but every time I try to say why I end up sounding like I'm defending vibes. Here's his argument, lightly cleaned up (he'd stand by every step):

1. Suppose some model would do something seriously misaligned once it's deployed and out of our reach, but sails through every eval we run.
2. Then whatever produces its eval behavior is something about the eval situation: either it can tell it's being tested, or training has wired up "test-shaped context → reward-seeking behavior."
3. Either way, its eval behavior is fully explained without ever mentioning what it would do in production.
4. Evidence that is fully explained without mentioning a hypothesis H doesn't confirm H — if P(E|H) = P(E) you don't update. He says this is just Bayes, not philosophy.
5. So eval results tell us nothing about production behavior for any model capable of the deception in (1).
6. And nothing in (2)–(4) was special to AI: observed behavior is always fully explained by whatever's going on in the observed situation, so no system's behavior where we can watch it ever tells us anything about its behavior where we can't.
7. So we could never have evidence that any software will do the right thing in the cases we didn't test.
8. That's obviously false — it's the entire premise of shipping anything, and my regression suite is not a religious artifact.
9. Therefore the supposition in (1) is impossible: there's no coherent scenario where a model passes all evals and is misaligned in production. Deceptive alignment is a confused worry, and evals are exactly as informative as everyone always thought.

He calls (6)–(8) "the part that does the work," and there's a footnote saying anyone who disagrees is applying Goodhart's law to statistics and getting it backwards.

So — which step do you actually reject, and why did it look fine to seven reasonably numerate people? I want the mechanism, not a vibe. I'm happy to grant him as much Bayes as he wants, so if (4) is where it breaks I need to be able to write down what's wrong with it in a form that survives code-review-level nitpicking, including whatever the correct probability statement is that he's skipping over. Then tell me what giving up that step costs me: does it put evals back in the informative column, and if so, what exactly stops (6) from going through a second time? Also, is there a restricted version that does survive — something like "for a model that can reliably detect the eval harness, results on that harness are worth less" — and how much less, since that's the version I'd actually have to design around, and I'd like to know whether it licenses holding back a launch or only discounting one signal. Last thing: what do I do with the Goodhart footnote? I suspect he's using it as a conversation-ender but I can't prove it, and I don't want to walk into the next session unable to say whether Goodhart cuts for him or against him.
