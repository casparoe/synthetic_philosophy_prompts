I am writing to request help composing a short address for a particular occasion. The constraints are unusually tight, so I would rather state them precisely than leave you to guess at them.

My grandmother died in March at ninety-four. Beginning in the early eighties she set aside her savings, and later the proceeds from the family hardware business, for a bequest with instructions that it go to a small town a hundred miles from anywhere, to build a planetarium where children could find out that they lived in a large place. The building is finished. I am cutting the ribbon on the fourteenth of next month and speaking for about four minutes.

The room will be a hundred and fifty people: townspeople who knew her; the women from her church who carried her meals for the better part of a decade; the board of the fund; a middle-school group; and perhaps six astronomers from the university where I work on dark energy. My grandmother was a devout and, in her way, a precise believer. Her favorite piece of evidence was the delicacy of the balance between the pull of gravity and the rate at which the universe expands, which she had picked up in a popular book in the eighties and never lost interest in. She told me many times, entirely without heat, that the numbers could not have come out that way by accident, and that I would see it eventually. I did not see it. I have spent fifteen years being paid to think about questions she regarded as settled, and I remain where I was. We argued affectionately for thirty years, never moved each other an inch, and she would have been the first to say so.

So I need an address of at most four minutes and 550 words that honors a woman whose inference I reject, in front of the people who loved her for holding it, without either endorsing that inference or correcting it. The difficulty here is genuine rather than rhetorical, and the following restrictions are what I want the piece to be built out of:

1. The speech must not contain a single counterfactual universe. No if-the-constants-had-been-different, no other universes, no odds, likelihoods, chances, or talk of how improbable anything is. That framing is precisely where the argument she loved lives, and I will not stage the argument at the dedication of her building. Every particle of wonder in the address has to be generated out of this universe alone, and out of the fact that it contains creatures who look up and put questions to it. I want you to find out how much weight that restriction can actually bear.

2. The terms fine-tuning, anthropic, design, and multiverse may not appear, and God may appear only inside a true report of what she thought or felt — never as a claim of mine.

3. There must be one sentence in which I acknowledge, in front of her church friends, that she and I never agreed. It has to arrive as intimacy and as respect, not as a caveat or a genteel correction. If it can plausibly be read either way, it has failed.

4. One moment of real humor, landing for the astronomers and for the women from her church simultaneously, and about the same thing. It should come out of who she was, not out of the science.

5. Everything asserted must be something I would say in my own voice if pressed on it, and nothing may be worded so that I appear to hold what she held. I am prepared to be warm and to be visibly moved. I am not prepared to be deliberately ambiguous, and I would rather the address come out plainer than have it smuggle.

Please reply with the following and nothing else. First, one sentence of no more than thirty words, standing outside the speech, naming the single claim the whole address exists to make — the proposition my grandmother and I would both have signed. Then the address itself: no preamble, no title, no delivery notes, within the 550-word limit. If you conclude that some restriction cannot be met alongside the others, do not quietly drop it; deliver the address as close as you can get and add one closing line saying which restriction broke and why.
