Write a short story of roughly 1,500–2,500 words in which the special composition question — under what conditions do many things add up to one further thing? — is a live practical problem rather than a seminar topic.

Suggested setup, which you may modify: a survey biologist on an ice moon has found a kilometre-wide mat of semi-autonomous modules that exchange chemical signals, share nutrients through a common vascular lattice, and reproduce only as a whole. The colony charter's protected-lifeform clause turns on whether the mat is one organism or a population of thousands, and that answer decides whether the site may be strip-mined to feed the habitat's heat budget.

The story should do more than stage the dilemma. Give real dramatic weight to at least three candidate answers: that cohesion and functional integration are enough for composition; that composition occurs only where the parts constitute a single life, with one metabolic and reproductive destiny; and that there are no composite objects at all, only simples arranged matwise, so the clause is asking after nothing. Include a scene where the boundary problem bites — the peripheral modules are only half-integrated, and there is no principled place to stop — and let the protagonist's treatment of them reveal which answer she has actually adopted. Close without resolving the metaphysics by announcement; the ending should carry the cost of her choice.
