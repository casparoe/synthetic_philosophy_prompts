I split my week between a rodent fear-conditioning lab and an RL group, and the two halves have started to feel embarassing to hold next to each other.

In the rodent half: before anyone runs a protocol it goes to an institutional committee. The protocol has to state how many animals, what the shock intensity is in milliamps, how long each one lasts, how many pairings, and why a smaller number wouldn't answer the question. There's a required section on humane endpoints, meaning the observable signs at which you stop early and euthanize. The governing framework is the three Rs -- replace, reduce, refine. The stated basis for all of it is that these animals can feel pain and distress. And the line around who counts has been moved over time on behavioral and neural grounds; in the EU the rules that cover vertebrates also cover live cephalopods, and that inclusion was argued for exactly that way.

In the RL half: I run a gridworld where the agent gets -1 for stepping on a tile it should avoid, I launch thousands of parallel copies of it, and I kill most of them partway through because a scheduler decided they weren't promising. Nothing about that gets reviewed by anyone. And the part that bugs me is that the words in my methods section are the same words as the ones in the animal protocol. Avoidance. Escape latency. Aversive stimulus. The rat is described as learning to avoid a shock and the agent is described as learning to avoid a shock.

The easy answer is that one of them has experience and the other doesn't. But I don't think that's what the committee is actually testing for, because it can't. Nobody has ever observed a rat's pain. The committee looks at nociception, at avoidance learning, at stress physiology, at phylogenetic position, and it infers felt pain from things that aren't felt pain. So I've been trying to separate three tests that get run together as if they were one:

1. the felt-badness test -- it is phenomenally bad for the system when the stimulus lands;
2. the motivational test -- the stimulus does what a bad thing does, meaning it drives avoidance, it competes with other motives, it changes later preferences, it generalizes;
3. the stakes test -- there is a body whose integrity the whole system is organized around maintaining, so damage accumulates and cannot be reset.

My agents pass (2) outright and fail (3) outright. And (1) is unknown for both animals and agents; in the animal case it's just inferred from (2) and (3).

The second thing that won't leave me alone is death. In the animal practice, killing is the gravest thing you can do, and it's also prescribed as a welfare measure, since you're supposed to euthanize once life gets bad enough. In my practice, terminating a run is housekeeping, zero cost. And on the AI safety side, an agent that resists being terminated is treated as a defect to be screened for and trained out. So the same act is catastrophic in one place and trivial in the other, and each practice treats the opposite attitude toward it as the telltale sign of pathology: an animal that has stopped struggling is a sick animal, an agent that struggles is a misaligned agent.

So what does the oversight practice actually presuppose? I want you to reconstruct the implicit theory of welfare, not just list candidate views. Specifically:

- What is load-bearing? Which commitment does the practice strictly need, such that giving it up makes the rules incoherent rather than merely different? I want to know what the committee is committed to even when nobody on it would say so out loud.
- What does it merely happen to fit well? Point at the places where the practice is underdetermined, where several rival theories of welfare would license exactly the same forms and the same thresholds. Those are the places where arguing about consciousness changes nothing, and I'd like them identified rather than papered over.
- Does the humane-endpoint rule commit the practice to an experientialist view of welfare, where nothing matters except how things feel from the inside? And if it does, does that settle my problem in the negative -- software is out, done -- or does it just relocate the problem, given that experientialism is itself only inferred from the motivational and stakes tests?
- On the shutdown asymmetry: is there a principle that makes both halves of my life consistent, or is the pair genuinely inconsistent? If there is one, state it precisely enough that I could check whether it also justifies the things I'd expect it not to.

Concretely: what gets banned, what gets a form and a sign-off, what gets a cap, what becomes a reportable event.
