I'm a third-year PhD student in economic history and I'vebasically staked my thesis chapter on a strong version of the path-dependence claim: when several alternatives are roughly equivalent at the outset, tiny early accidents plus increasing returns and switching costs pick the winner, and once it's picked the outcome is locked in for decades or centuries — so replaying history would land you somewhere meaningfully different, not just cosmetically different. My advisor thinks that's close to unfalsifiable, and I'm presenting the chapter at a workshop in a few weeks, so I'd rather find out from you than from the room.

So what would actually count against my view? I want the defeaters, not the friendly caveats. Start with the ones from inside the tradition: the worry that positive-feedback urn models are path dependent in a sense so weak that nearly any stochastic process qualifies, so I haven't said anything interesting; the fact that our flagship exhibit — the keyboard layout everyone cites — rests its inefficiency case on typing-speed comparisons of pretty dubious provenance, and that it's not even clear the incumbent is worse than the alternative; the sheer density of near-simultaneous independent invention in the history of science and technology, which reads much more like ideas being "in the air" than like accidents deciding anything; and the paleobiology argument about whether replaying life's tape converges on similar outcomes anyway. Then the outside ones. How would I distinguish lock-in from ordinary equilibrium selection in a coordination game, from a technology that was simply better and stayed better, or from a functional-convergence picture where the arbitrary surface details are contingent but the function they serve isn't? And what could in principle be observed that would force me to give ground — a real displacement event against big switching costs, a measured welfare loss from a demonstrably dominated incumbent, an estimate rather than an assumption about how steep the increasing returns actually are?

Please rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a committed lock-in theorist would concede it as genuinely damaging or wave it off, and why they'd be entitled to. I also want to know what my view predicts that the necessity/convergence rival doesn't, because if both predict the same observable history then my claim isn't doing any work. And finish with a blunt verdict on whether I've insulated the position with qualifications — if "locked in" ends up meaning "hard to switch, unless somebody switches," is that a fatal problem for a claim of this kind or just the normal condition of a historical thesis? Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — one sentence saying how exposed you think the view actually is — then the reasoning, ranked. No preamble and don't restate my question back at me.
