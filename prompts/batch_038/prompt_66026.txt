So the seminar I'm TAing hits easy knowledge this week — whether you can know something from a source before you've got any reason to trust the source — and I want to hand out a poem at the end of Friday's session instead of my usual bullet-point recap. Twelve students, and one of them spent six years as a diesel mechanic, which made the gas gauge case land way harder than I expected (we call the driver Roxanne, because everyone's version of the case does). I've decided the form should be a pantoum, because the whole shape of the problem is a reasoning process feeding its own output back in as its next input: a reading becomes evidence that the reading was accurate, and a string of those becomes evidence the instrument was trustworthy all along. A pantoum does that to lines, mechanically, whether you want it to or not.

Here's what I'm after. Five quatrains, strict recycling — lines 2 and 4 of each stanza come back as lines 1 and 3 of the next — and then the usual closing, where the two leftover lines from the opening stanza finally come home and the poem's first line lands as its last line. Speaker: the fuel gauge itself. I think it's the only voice that can carry this without winking, since it's the thing narrating its own readings getting promoted into credentials for itself. Plain concrete diction, no rhyme required, lines roughly the same length so the repeats don't clang. The real work is in the recycled lines: I want "the needle sits on F" to mean one thing in stanza one (a report), something else by stanza three (a datum in a track record), and something else again when it finally closes the poem (a proof). Same words, shifting punctuation and context doing the drift. The genuinely new lines are where the apparently independent details get slipped in — the tank really was full, she watched the pump, she filled it herself twice — and by the fourth stanza it should be palpable that none of that checking is actually independent, which is the engine of the whole objection.

Please don't let the last stanza resolve into a slogan, and ban these words outright: bootstrapping, circular, question-begging, reliabilism, closure, easy knowledge. If the poem needs to name what's happening it's failed. What I do want the closing loop to keep open is the two things my students spent an hour fighting over: that each individual step looks like a perfectly good inference from a perfectly good premise, with no single spot you can point at and call the mistake; and that the whole run nevertheless manufactures a warrant for the gauge that nobody thinks was there at the start. So whether the final line reads as triumphant — see, it worked — or as a trapdoor dropping shut should be genuinely undecidable on a first read, which is roughly where our discussion ended up too. No footnotes, no explanatory title, and no gloss afterwards; a title is fine as long as it isn't a subtitle in disguise. If the mechanic in my section smells the interior of the thing, bonus, but don't turn it into a car advert.
