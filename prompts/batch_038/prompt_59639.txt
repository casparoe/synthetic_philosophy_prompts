The answer, which came in at about 650 words:

Popper's demarcation criterion is falsifiability, and his picture of growth is conjecture and refutation: put forward a bold theory, try to knock it down, keep the survivors. Lakatos's complaint is that this cannot work even in principle, because a theory is never confronted with experience on its own. To extract a prediction you must add auxiliary assumptions, idealisations and initial conditions, and when the prediction fails all you know is that the whole conjunction has gone wrong somewhere. Falsification in Popper's sense therefore never locates a culprit. Worse, naive falsificationism would kill science at birth, since every theory in history was born refuted: there has never been a moment when a theory faced no counterexample at all. So the unit that can be appraised has to be bigger than the isolated theory.

That unit is the research programme: not a single theory but a sequence of theories held together by a common core. The hard core is the set of fundamental assumptions its practitioners have agreed not to give up — for the Newtonian programme, something like the laws of motion together with universal gravitation. Lakatos is careful that this is not a claim about truth, or even about evidence. The core is not shown to be right; it is made irrefutable by the methodological decision of the people working inside the programme. Around it sits the protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses, and that is where the adjustments happen. When Uranus would not stay on its computed orbit, nobody concluded that gravitation was false. The belt was modified instead, and the modification was that some further planet must be perturbing it.

The negative heuristic is what keeps such a programme alive as a research enterprise rather than a dead dogma. It is the partly articulated set of suggestions and hunches about how to develop the theory: which idealisations to relax next, which auxiliary hypotheses are worth trying, which puzzles repay the effort, roughly what the programme's model of the world looks like. It is the generative side, and a programme with a rich negative heuristic keeps producing new variants of itself. The positive heuristic does the defensive work. It is the prohibition at the centre of the programme: modus tollens must not be turned on the hard core, and refutations are to be redirected into the belt. This is why a programme can absorb a refuting observation and carry on.

It follows that a programme with a well worked-out negative heuristic is a progressive programme, since the heuristic is precisely the machinery that produces fruitful modifications to the belt. Progressiveness, on this account, is built into the structure of the programme: a healthy heuristic guarantees that the changes it generates will be changes for the better.

What then separates progressive from degenerating problemshifts? A shift is theoretically progressive when the new version has excess empirical content over its predecessor — when it implies something the earlier version could neither accommodate nor imply — and empirically progressive when some of that content gets corroborated. Neptune is the textbook instance: the Newtonian programme did not merely absorb the anomaly, it specified where an unknown planet had to be, and Galle found it within a degree or so of the predicted position. A shift is degenerating when the belt is altered only so as to swallow the anomaly and nothing new comes out — adjustments made for the occasion, which rescue the theory without predicting anything further. The distinction matters to Lakatos because it is meant to be an objective one. Against Kuhn he insists that the choice between rival programmes is not a conversion or a leap of faith, and that there is a fact of the matter about which programme is doing better.

But the criterion can only be applied in retrospect. Whether a shift was progressive is something you learn decades afterwards, once the new content has been corroborated and the rival has either flourished or petered out. So the methodology of scientific research programmes is not a decision procedure for a working scientist; it is a rational reconstruction of how an episode looks once the outcome is known. At the coalface, the scientist deciding whether to abandon a programme is guided by judgement, loyalty and temperament, exactly as Kuhn describes. Lakatos's disagreement with Kuhn is therefore narrower than it first appears: he offers a rational standard for the historian of science, not a rule the practitioner can use.
