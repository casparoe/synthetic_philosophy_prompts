I'm auditing a seminar on ancient history this term and something in the methodology keeps nagging at me, so I figured I'd ask someone who thinks about evidence for a living rather than my classmates. Whenever the question is whether some famous story actually happened, the profession lands on the same two-tier verdict. Troy: the mound at Hisarlik is real, it has destruction layers, and Hittite tablets from the Late Bronze Age mention a place called Wilusa and a power called Ahhiyawa, which scholars generally take to be Ilion and the Achaeans — but no, we can't say there was a war like Homer's. Exodus: the Egyptian records are silent about any mass departure, and an Egyptian victory inscription from around 1208 BCE already mentions a people called Israel in Canaan, so the story as told can't be right — but there's probably a kernel, people point at the Egyptian-sounding names in the priestly line. Arthur: there may have been a war-leader in the fifth or sixth century, but the Round Table is romance. So in all three cases we're allowed a kernel and never the story. What I can't figure out is what epistemology licenses that split, and why its applied so unevenly between the cases.

Three specific things bug me. (1) In the Troy case, matching Wilusa to Ilion and Ahhiyawa to Achaeans is treated as real corroboration that the Homeric world maps onto actual Bronze Age geography — even though the tablets themselves describe Wilusa as a Hittite vassal and never mention a Greek attack on it. Meanwhile the same kind of name-matching gets treated as worthless almost everywhere else. What's the rule, and is there one? (2) The silence of Egyptian records counts as positive evidence against the Exodus, on the reasoning that a bureaucracy like that would have written it down. But the total absence of British records from the fifth and sixth centuries is treated as just a gap that proves nothing either way about Arthur. Same shape of evidence — no documents — opposite evidential weight. (3) People regularly say the Arthur material is multiply attested, pointing at a ninth-century Latin compilation that lists twelve battles fought by a war-leader named Arthur and a tenth-century set of Welsh annals that dates one of those battles to around 516. But the annal's version has Arthur carrying the cross on his shoulders for three days and nights, which looks a lot like a garbling of the compilation's story about him carrying an image of the Virgin on his shoulders into a different battle — the old Welsh words for shield and shoulder are nearly identical. So the two "independent" witnesses might be one witness with a typo.

Here's what I want. Reconstruct the implicit theory of evidence this kernel/shell practice is running on. What does it presuppose about how traditions transmit — which parts of a story are supposed to survive intact and which decay (names? places? the fact that a battle happened? the numbers, the motives, the speeches, the gods)? What does it presuppose about when an absence of records counts as evidence, and therefore about which societies we assume would have written things down? What does it presuppose about the evidential value of a name, and about what makes two sources independent rather than two copies? Then seperate out what the practice strictly needs from what it merely happens to sit comfortably with — I suspect at least one of my three complaints above is a genuine internal contradiction rather than just different weighting of the same rule, and I'd like you to say which one and why the other two aren't. Also flag anywhere the practice is philosophically underdetermined, i.e. where several incompatible theories of testimony or memory would license the same verdict, because that's the part my seminar leader keeps waving away. Finally, tell me what the guild would actually have to change if it replaced all this with an explicit likelihood-based account where you state your priors and your likelihoods out loud: which of the three verdicts would move, which would stay put for reasons that aren't really evidential, and what would become impossible to say in public.

Do the main reconstruction as prose, but then give me a table of the presuppositions with these columns: `presupposition` (one sentence, stated as a claim) | `forced by` (which bit of the practice makes it unavoidable) | `rules out` (the rival view of evidence or testimony it quietly excludes) | `status` (strictly required / merely convenient / possibly contradictory) | `if dropped` (what verdict or habit changes). Mark any row where you think the practice doesn't really presuppose the thing and just happens to be compatible with it — I'd rather see five honest rows than fifteen speculative ones.
