I'm a fourth-year PhD student in a joint cognitive science and philosophy of mind program, and I have to hand in the methods-and-interpretation chapter of my dissertation proposal in three weeks. My lab runs temporal-order and simultaneity judgments on brief audiovisual pairs — which stimulus came first, or whether they were simultaneous — and we keep colliding with the fact that the two tasks disagree about the very same stimulus pairs, that order judgments for two taps reverse when the subject crosses their arms, that the perceived order shifts when we direct attention to one modality rather than the other, and that what a subject says depends on what we ask them to do with the stimulus after it appears. My PI holds that all of this is ordinary psychophysics and that the philosophical literature on when experience happens is a distraction from it; my philosophy co-advisor holds that we cannot interpret a single one of our figures without it. I want to settle the matter for myself before the defense rather than adopting either of their positions by osmosis.

What I need is the specification, not the contenders. Before I read another account of the timing of experience, tell me what any such account must deliver to count as adequate for a purpose I'll fix narrowly: it has to license the inferences my experiments actually make, which run from a subject's report about the order, timing, or duration of stimuli to a claim about the temporal properties of the experience those stimuli produced. I want genuine requirements only, and I want you to be ruthless about separating genuine requirements from commitments that belong to one camp and have been passing as neutral. My own pile is a mess and I trust none of it: that experience must have determinate temporal properties at all; that a theory must not locate consciousness at a single place-and-time finish line — or, if it does, must say what makes that line the line and why it does not merely push the question back a stage; that it must explain how one experience can carry temporal content, as in the color phi illusion (two differently colored lights flashed in quick succession a few degrees apart, reported as a single light that travels and changes color in mid-flight, a report containing information that was not available when the first light was on), or the felt row of evenly spaced taps between two widely separated real ones, or the flash seen as trailing an object it was physically aligned with — without requiring the subject to compare two static snapshots; that it must permit reports to be wrong about timing, but not wrong in a way that leaves nothing measurable; that it must say which probes fix what was experienced and which merely disclose it after the fact; that whatever it says about vision must extend, or fail to extend on principle, to touch and hearing; and that it must be commensurable with neural latencies, including the fact that what a stimulus is experienced as can depend on what arrives after it. I suspect several of these are one requirement wearing different clothes, that at least one is empty, and that at least one is a prejudice I inherited from a paper I read too early.

Give me exactly five numbered requirements and nothing else: no preamble, no closing summary, no separate scorecard, no references. Each item is to be two sentences at most — the first naming the requirement together with the particular case or consideration that makes it a requirement rather than a taste, the second giving its status (non-negotiable, or relative to a camp, and if so which) plus the one existing family of accounts it most threatens, chosen from the multiple-drafts or first-person-operationalist line, global-workspace-style broadcast theories, specious-present and retention accounts, postdiction and discrete perceptual-moment accounts, or whatever further family you think belongs on the list. Fold the hard part into the fifth item: state the inconsistency you believe my pile actually contains. My suspicion is a triad — that there is a determinate fact about when an experience occurs; that no probe has a privileged claim to reveal that fact; and that the difference between an experience that was backdated and an experience that was misremembered is a real difference — cannot be held together, and that this is what makes the Orwellian and Stalinesque readings of color phi undecidable rather than merely unsettled. Tell me whether that triad is the right one or repair it, and tell me which single one of the five requirements, once fixed, eliminates the largest number of otherwise plausible accounts.
