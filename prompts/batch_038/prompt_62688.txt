I have no academic training in philosophy — this is something I have been working out for myself over a couple of years, mostly while walking — and I have reached the point where I cannot tell whether I have found a real puzzle or merely talked myself into one. Below is a draft essay. I would like comments on the reasoning rather than encouragement, and I have some specific questions at the end.

**Draft: Introspection Feels Like Looking but Works Like Guessing**

1. The default picture. When I try to find out what I feel, the process feels like looking: there is an object, an inner scene, and I attend to it. On this picture an introspective mistake is like a perceptual mistake — the light is bad, I am distracted, the object is faint — which suggests a remedy: attend harder and you will do better.

2. The rival picture. Almost nothing I report about my own mind seems to be available by looking. Try to catch the moment at which you decide to reach for the salt and you find no such moment; you find only the reaching. So perhaps self-knowledge is not perception but inference: I work out what I must be feeling from the same materials I would use on somebody else — what I just did, what I just said, what the situation calls for, what a person like me would feel here. The inference is fast and silent, and fast silent inferences feel like direct acquaintance. That is why introspection feels like looking.

3. Evidence pointing this way, as I understand it from popular accounts. Shoppers asked to choose the best of four identical pairs of stockings showed a strong preference for the pair on the right, then explained their choice by the weave and the colour, and grew irritated when the arrangement was mentioned. Insomniacs given a sugar pill described to them as arousing fell asleep faster, and when asked why reported that they had simply had less on their minds that night. Patients whose hemispheres were surgically separated will produce fluent reasons for actions cued from the side of the brain that cannot speak about them. In each case the report is confident, plausible, and wrong about its cause.

4. The puzzle I actually want to solve. If self-knowledge is inference from evidence, confidence ought to move with the evidence, the way it does when I infer something about a stranger: poor evidence, weak confidence. But it does not move. People are as certain about why they chose as they are about the colour of the wall in front of them, and the certainty looks much the same whether the report turns out to be right or to have been invented. Call this the flat-confidence problem. For me it decides between the two pictures, and neither picture explains it as it stands.

5. My proposal. Confidence is not a read-out of how well supported the story is; it is a read-out of how easily the story came. When I say I am sure, what I am reporting is the fluency of narrative generation, not its warrant. That explains the flatness: stories about myself are always easy to generate, because I carry a rich standing theory of my own character and a strong motive to keep a coherent one running. It also makes predictions. Anything that makes a self-story easier to produce — a prime, a plausible explanation handed to me, a mood that happens to fit — should raise confidence without raising accuracy; and people prevented from generating any story at all should be uncharacteristically unsure rather than merely wrong.

6. A test I cannot run but which seems decisive. If introspection works off public traces, then reports about the causes of my own judgments should be more accurate when the cause left a trace others could see — what I read, who I was with, what I did — than when it did not: hunger, poor sleep, background noise, a mood carried over from an hour earlier. And confidence should be identical across the two conditions. An accuracy gap with no matching confidence gap would be the signature. If accuracy and confidence fall together, my proposal is wrong and the plain inference picture needs no repair.

7. Where I am stuck. Pain seems to defeat the whole thing. I do not infer that my hand hurts from my wincing; the hurting is there before any story, and no evidence about my situation could make me wrong about it. My only reply is that there are two kinds of self-report: detection, for raw present states that announce themselves, and interpretation, for everything else. The concession worries me, because if detection covers pain, itch, dizziness and a handful of similar things while interpretation covers what I want, why I decided, and whether I am angry or only embarrassed, then the second bucket holds every question I care about and the first is a stub. The thesis survives, but stops being interesting.

**What I would like comments on**

1. Section 7: does the detection/interpretation split really leave me with a stub, or is there a principled way to hold the line? And if the line holds, does the flat-confidence problem come back inside detection — am I confident about a pain for the same reason I am confident about a story?

2. Is section 5 circular? Tell me how one would distinguish the story came easily from the story is good without already assuming which reports are accurate, and sketch what a workable measure would look like.

3. Does the test in section 6 actually discriminate? Please give me at least two rival accounts that also predict an accuracy gap with no confidence gap. I have thought of one — confidence as a social signal, since you must sound certain about your own mind or nobody will coordinate with you — but I cannot tell whether that is a rival to mine or just the same idea wearing different clothes.

4. Identify the step where I move fastest. I suspect it is the slide from reports about causes being wrong to reports about the state itself being inferential, but I cannot see how to block it.

5. Give me the single most damaging objection to the essay as a whole, and then say whether the essay is worth writing at all — that is, whether the flat-confidence problem is a genuine puzzle or something that dissolves once described carefully.

Please keep your comments keyed to the numbered sections, and argue in your own words. Do not send me references, citations, or a reading list: I have no way of looking anything up, so a name and a date would be useless to me. What I need is your judgment of the reasoning.
