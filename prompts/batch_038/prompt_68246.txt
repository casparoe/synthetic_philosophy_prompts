I am a data architect at a company that builds a temporal data platform for environmental records — species observations, emissions inventories, monitoring-station series, and a growing volume of paleo and sediment-core data contributed by research partners. Everything we store is bitemporal, and I am thoroughly fluent in that vocabulary: valid time and transaction time, as-of queries, retroactive restatement, slowly changing dimensions, partition boundaries, enum domains enforced by check constraints, schema migrations and their rollback semantics, and the governance layer — who owns a table, who may merge a change to the schema, and what happens to downstream consumers when a definition is revised rather than corrected.

The Anthropocene debate has recently stopped being abstract for me. In a schema review, a colleague proposed adding a geologic-epoch attribute to our core fact tables so that analysts can slice deep-time records against the historical ones. That immediately produced an argument I was not equipped to referee: one engineer wanted every row dated from the mid-twentieth century onward labelled Anthropocene, another pointed out that the official chart still places us inside the Holocene, a third wanted the boundary pushed back centuries, and someone from the product team asked whether it mattered at all as long as the enum was stable. A downstream group now wants to filter on the field and has asked us to pick a value. So a dispute about when a geological interval began and who has standing to say has arrived on my desk as a data-modeling ticket, and I would rather understand the original argument than guess at it.

Please write as a philosopher of science with genuine working knowledge of stratigraphy who has followed the Anthropocene discussions closely, and treat me as an expert in the target vocabulary and a novice in the source one.
