Third-year cog sci PhD student here — I spend my weekdays running perturbation batteries on LLM-based agents in a robotics-adjacent lab and my weekends reading about free will, and the two halves of my life have never once talked to each other. I want to force them to.

Everyone in my lab uses "sphexish" as a slur. An agent that retries the same dead API call four hundred times, a policy that walks into a corner and jitters there until the episode times out — sphexish. The word comes from the digger wasp anecdote that Hofstadter and Dennett between them made famous: Fabre reporting, back in the first volume of his Souvenirs Entomologiques in 1879, a wasp he could get to repeat her provisioning run as many as forty times. Worth knowing before anyone builds a whole research program on it: Fred Keijzer dug into the provenance of that story and argues it's an old and questionable anecdote that the cognitive sciences have kept repeating uncritically ever since, and that the wasp in the original report isn't the mindless automaton of the retellings. So the founding case for sphexishness is itself wobbly.

That's partly why I want what I'm asking for. I need a triage instrument — a decision tree — that takes a stuck-agent case and lands you on a verdict about (a) which kind of rigidity it actually displays and (b) whether that kind of rigidity has any bearing on the agent's freedom or agency at all, or is just an engineering defect with no philosophical interest.

The thing I most want is for the tree to stop lumping together failures that are obviously different. My rough list: a routine triggered by a sign stimulus and then run to completion regardless; failure to re-evaluate mid-routine when the situation changes under you; inability to hold the goal apart from the means currently serving it; brittleness the moment you step off the training distribution; and habit or compulsion capture that the agent itself disowns. Build the logical space out of diagnostic questions about the failure first, and only then hang named positions at the leaves — not the other way round.

Use these cases, all of which have come up in my lab or my reading: the wasp itself; a Roomba re-bumping the same chair leg; an LLM agent looping on a failing tool call; a chess engine that trades a queen for a pawn it can't use; a pianist who cannot play bar 40 except by starting the piece from bar 1; an OCD checker who judges the checking pointless and does it anyway; a scrub jay that re-caches its food after noticing it was watched; and the expert who does the thing beautifully but cannot state the rule she's following — which I honestly can't tell whether belongs in my taxonomy as rigidity or as its opposite.

Leaves I need: Dennett's flexibility-as-freedom line and his point that sphexishness is also a *fear* people have about determinism; Frankfurt-style second-order accounts; the skeptical position that every one of these agents including the flexible ones is sphexish once you look hard enough; agent-causal libertarianism; a no-threshold view on which flexibility comes in degrees and "free" is just vague; and the deflationary view that sphexishness is a rhetorical device rather than a property of anything. I'd also like a branch that handles the Keijzer problem explicitly — how much of an inflexibility verdict rests on one unreplicated historical report rather than on the behavior — and I want you to tell me whether that node is genuinely philosophical or just methodology smuggling itself into a philosophy tree.

Give me eight to ten branch questions and six to eight leaves, as an indented tree, plus a table that maps each of the cases above onto its path. Then: list the cells of the logical space nothing occupies and diagnose each — incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored? — and flag any pair of leaves my probes can't actually separate. I suspect Frankfurt and Dennett are going to collapse into one another on the pianist case and I'd rather you say so than paper over it.

Audience is our lab reading group, mixed CS and philosophy, several people who've never read a word of Dennett, so no question can presuppose knowing which position it leads to. And please be explicit throughout about which claims are settled and which are live disputes among people who work on this — I especially don't want a contested view handed to me as the consensus, and I'd like to know where the philosophy-of-action people and the AI-robustness people simply disagree about what flexibility even is.
