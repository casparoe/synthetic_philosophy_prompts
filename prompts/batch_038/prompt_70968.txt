In Denis Villeneuve's Arrival, the opening montage of Louise Banks's daughter Hannah — birth, childhood, a terminal illness — is presented as memory and is only later revealed as prolepsis: Louise has not yet had a child, and the images belong to a future she goes on to choose with her eyes open. I would like a reading of the film through Dennett's distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque revisions of the record of experience, as it is deployed in the colour-phi case: a subject reports a spot travelling and changing colour midway across a gap where nothing of the sort was ever presented, and the two rival explanations — that the experience was had and then edited, or that it was never had until the relevant information was in — are empirically indistinguishable, which Dennett takes to show that the framing presupposes a definite moment of "presentation" there is no reason to believe in. Does the film put its viewer in structurally the same position, holding a determinate deliverance (that was a flashback) whose content cannot be assigned to any time? Please say where the parallel genuinely illuminates — the closing choice, the acquisition of a non-linear written language, Louise's own reports of what she is experiencing — and where it is a category mistake, given that a film is an authored artefact and a viewer's comprehension of a plot is not a perceptual present at all. I would also like your view on whether the film itself appears aware of the difference between revising what one saw and never having seen it, and whether its device offers the debate anything the laboratory case cannot: here the reporter can be told, and can go back through the sequence in the new order, in a way the colour-phi subject cannot. I am teaching the film in a philosophy of mind unit on the timing of consciousness and want criticism rather than plot summary, including a candid verdict on where the reading has to be abandoned.
