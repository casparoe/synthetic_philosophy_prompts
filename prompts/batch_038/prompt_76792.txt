For the past eight months I have been cycling between two career paths — remaining in a secure analytical role at a public agency, or moving to an applied research group in the private sector — and the cycling has cost me real money and real time. Tracing it backwards there is an embarrassingly clean sequence: I paid for a certification on the assumption I was staying, then declined a promotion in order to keep my options open for the move, then paid a second time to re-qualify for the kind of work I had just walked away from, then turned down the private offer after all. Each individual step looked, at the moment I took it, like the best of the options in front of me. A friend who works on decision theory glanced at my spreadsheet, said I had constructed a textbook money pump, and then told me not to worry about it — for reasons I have not been able to shake.

His argument was that a person extended over time is not really a single decision-maker at all, but something closer to a firm whose departments act in sequence: the department running things in January, the one that takes over in April, each with its own information, its own priorities, and no authority over the others, all drawing on a common treasury. Firms of that kind get pumped all the time — sales accepts a contract that engineering later pays to renegotiate, and every department can honestly say it chose rationally given what it knew — yet nobody concludes that the departments were irrational, because there was never a single mind whose preferences the sequence had to cohere with. On this picture my successive selves are just departments, the pump is a property of the organization rather than of any member of it, and the only genuine remedy would be to install a chief executive with binding authority over all of them, which he described as one optional institutional arrangement among others rather than a requirement of rationality. He also pointed me toward the older version of the same move in Parfit's Reasons and Persons, where a single life is treated as a succession of distinct persons linked only by memory and intention rather than as one continuing agent.

I would like an audit of that analogy, not a defence or a refutation of my friend. Please set out the correspondences explicitly — a table is fine — identifying what plays the role of each successive department, the shared treasury, the absence of authority between departments, the differing information, the firm's overall objective and whose objective it is supposed to be, and the executive whose installation is treated as optional. Then tell me which disanalogies actually bear on the use he is making of it and which are cosmetic. I am especially interested in whether the analogy quietly assumes that the successive members differ in evidence or in values: in my case nothing about the two options changed between steps, only my mood and my patience. And I want to know whether it assumes there is no single subject whose welfare the sequence adds up to — which seems hard to maintain for one person who remembers every step and can bind herself in advance. Does his conclusion survive once those assumptions are exposed: was my cycling genuinely not irrational, and is self-binding genuinely optional? If the analogy fails, what would be a better one that fits a temporally extended agent who retains memory of her earlier choices and has access to precommitment, and does the replacement support the same relaxed verdict or was the firm picture carrying that weight all along? I know the basics of money pumps, transitivity, and sophisticated choice, so please don't reintroduce them, and I'm not looking for references or a reading list — just the reasoning.
