I need a tiebreaker, because my friend and I spent three hours last night going in circles over one paragraph in a pop-sci book about consciousness I'd lent him, and we ended up properly annoyed at each other, which is dumb since neither of us is a philospher.

The chapter's thesis is that your experience of the world isn't live — it gets produced after the fact. To make the case it walks through the color phi thing: two small colored lights, say green then red, a few degrees apart and only tens of milliseconds apart in time, and instead of two separate flashes you see one dot traveling from the first spot to the second and changing color somewhere along the way. The book's puzzle is that you can't have seen the change where you saw it, since at that point in the apparent motion the second light hadn't come on yet. Then it draws its lesson, roughly like this:

> So the brain must write the experience into memory after the fact — the Ministry of Truth quietly reprinting yesterday's paper. There's no single instant at which you see anything at all: your conscious life is a bulletin issued about half a second after the events it reports on. Dennett calls this the Orwellian account, as against the Stalinesque one, where the brain simply holds the experience back until it knows how the story ends. Better brain imaging may eventually let us watch the editing happen.

That's my paraphrase of the gist rather than the wording, but I'd stand behind every sentence in it as being what the book says, and it's the bit we fought about.

My reading: neat, so we're all on a slight delay and consciousness is a reconstruction, and the Orwellian/Stalinesque thing is just two flavors of the same insight. His reading: I've missed it and so has the book, because the two labels are supposed to be rivals and the whole point is that you *can't* pick between them, so settling on Orwellian like that is the error. He also said the half-second figure belongs to a completely different experiment but wouldn't say which one, and I think he was bluffing. He kept muttering that the paragraph denies something it's simultaneously assuming, which I couldn't decode.

So who's right? Go through that block sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who actually works on the timing of experience would accept, what they'd wince at, and what runs directly against the point being made. Specifically:

1. What do the Orwellian and Stalinesque stories each say about the two lights, and what exactly gets lost when they're turned into two flavors of one insight? Does the book's version of them even keep them distinct?
2. Is "better imaging will let us watch the editing happen" a coherent hope, or does it misunderstand what the disagreement between the two stories is about? Be concrete about what any such experiment would have to show.
3. Where does the half-second come from, and is there any real sense in which my experience lags the world by that much? Does the number do any work in the color phi case at all, given the timescales involved?
4. Does "you never see the present, only a reconstruction of it" survive once the paragraph is cleaned up? And what's the honest alternative — is there some version of the deflationary claim that's actually more radical than the book's, or less?

Don't just tell me the book is sloppy. I want to know whether the sloppiness undercuts the conclusion or whether the conclusion could be true anyway, and what my friend's "denies something it's assuming" complaint was actually pointing at.
