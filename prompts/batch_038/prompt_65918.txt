I am a second-year law student auditing a medical-ethics seminar, and I have come at the material with more than academic interest: my mother was diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's in the spring, she drafted a directive refusing life-sustaining treatment years before that, and my father has been asking me, in effect, whether the piece of paper in his desk still speaks for her. The seminar's reading list is mostly primary sources, but a friend lent me a trade-press book on autonomy and aging, and its central chapter on dementia directives summarizes the debate in a single long paragraph that I have been turning over for days. It runs roughly as follows:

"Consider Margo, the Alzheimer's patient whom a young physician described decades ago as cheerful, painting and listening to music, and whose signed directive said that she did not want to be kept alive in that condition. Ronald Dworkin argued that her directive should be honored, and his argument turns on a distinction between experiential interests, which are interests in how one's life feels from the inside, and critical interests, which are interests in the overall shape and integrity of a life taken as a whole. The whole edifice, however, presupposes that the woman who signed the directive and the woman who now paints are numerically the same person. That is why the reductionist account of personal identity associated with Derek Parfit — on which identity consists in nothing beyond psychological continuity and connectedness — is fatal to it. In advanced dementia those connections have lapsed, so there is no longer a person there whose autonomy the directive expresses, and carrying it out would amount to killing somebody else. Rebecca Dresser's objection, the author of the chapter continues, is the mirror image of Dworkin's: Margo is happy, her happiness is all that is left to weigh, and the directive should therefore be disregarded. But that objection was already answered by Nozick's experience machine, which showed that hedonism is false, so a claim about how life feels from the inside cannot defeat a claim about what a life amounts to. The debate is therefore settled in favour of precedent autonomy, and the reason it drags on is sentimentality. In any case the question is largely academic: American law requires a physician to follow a validly executed living will."

I found that paragraph convincing, which is precisely what worries me, because I suspect it is convincing for the wrong reasons and because it seems to hand me a conclusion I would rather not have handed to me. So I would like an accuracy audit rather than a general survey. Could you tell me which claims in it a careful participant in this debate would accept more or less as stated, which are misrepresentations, and of whom; whether the chapter's treatment of the personal-identity objection gets both its direction and its target right, given what Parfit's reductionism actually says and given what Dworkin's own argument depends on; whether the chapter's characterisation of Dresser is fair, including whatever in her case is not simply an appeal to pleasant experience; whether the step from Nozick's machine to the conclusion that experiential considerations cannot defeat a directive is legitimate; and what survives of the legal claim. Then say what is genuinely still contested once the errors are stripped out, and whether the author's confident verdict on precedent autonomy follows from anything in the accurate portions. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Begin with a one- or two-sentence verdict on how far the paragraph can be trusted, work through it claim by claim in the order the claims appear, and close with what a reader in my position should regard as unsettled.
