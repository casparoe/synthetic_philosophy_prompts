I cover decision theory and behavioral econ for a science-and-ideas magazine, and honestly I've come around to risk-weighted expected utility — the view that a rational person can be allowed to weight probabilities nonlinearly over ranked outcomes, so the classic Allais picks come out rational rather than as a bias. What would count against that? I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, plus which ones a committed risk-weight theorist would themselves concede as counting against them versus swat away.

Three leads I'd rather you actually engage with than wave off:

1. The sequential choice worry. A behavioral economist I talked to says her lab ran a sequence version of the Allais pairs with the chance to revise after each round, and the subjects who picked the sure thing kept loosing a bit of money and, debriefed afterwards, said they'd rather have taken the other option. Does that hurt the theory, or only the small-world way the problem was posed — and what would the "commit to a plan up front" reply have to be true of for it to work?

2. Someone told me there's a formal result to the effect that once you embed a gamble in the agent's full real background situation, risk-weighted rankings converge back to plain expected-utility rankings. Don't cite anything — just tell me whether an argument of that shape exists and how much it bites.

3. If the theory only says risk attitudes are permitted, not required, does it risk anything at all? And what would show it's just a descriptive probability-weighting curve with a normative label stuck on?

Finally, what does the view predict that its rivals don't, so I know what to look for? Please keep what's settled among people who work on this separate from what's genuinely live, and don't hand me a contested reading as the consensus.
