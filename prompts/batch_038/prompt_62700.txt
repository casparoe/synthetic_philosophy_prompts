I lead a weekly section of nine students attached to a second-year module on perception and knowledge. Two weeks ago they were walked through the argument from illusion in lecture and in the set textbook chapter, and the section has since settled into the view that it is decisive: asked to explain why the oar looks bent, six of the nine now produce something along the lines of "because what I'm directly aware of is a bent something-or-other," and they say it with the confidence of people who believe the hard work is already behind them. My job on Wednesday is to teach disjunctivism in twenty-five minutes at a blackboard. The aim is not to convert them but to get them to see precisely what the view denies, such that by the end they can state the common-factor assumption in their own words and explain why the argument from illusion is supposed to depend on it. Please do not explain disjunctivism to me: I have read around it and I am reasonably confident I understand the position. What I need is a plan for how to explain it.

Three constraints shape the plan. First, I cannot simply reopen the argument from illusion by re-running it and disputing a premise. They will experience that as a stalemate of intuitions against intuitions, and I do not have the time, so the sequence needs a way of exposing the load-bearing assumption from inside an argument they already accept. Second, the case that carries the indistinguishability intuition may not be a hallucination case. One of the nine has been open with the group about a recent brief psychotic episode, and I do not want the session's central device to be "you could not have told your hallucination from a real thing," nor anything else built on psychopathology; equally I want to avoid the three cases they have already chewed through — the bent stick, the tomato seen in good light, the pink elephant. Whatever example I choose has to make subjective indistinguishability vivid enough that they feel the pull toward a common factor, while being one in which they cannot comfortably say that the object itself really was partly as it appeared. Third, I have a blackboard and no slides, so anything I write down has to earn its space.

Format the answer as exactly five numbered items, in this order: the sequence of ideas with what each step depends on and which one of them does the load-bearing work; the example and what it must have and must lack to do the job; the misconceptions they will arrive with and the point in the sequence at which each should be headed off; the misconceptions they will generate on the way, of which I expect "so disjunctivism is claiming that hallucinations have nothing whatever in common with perceptions" to be the largest; and finally one question I can put to them at the end that will show whether the idea has landed, together with what I should say aloud that I am deliberately leaving out at this level. Each item should run to one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion.
