The candidate's answer is reproduced in full below.

"Harsanyi's theorem starts from the point that ordinal preference orderings are not enough once social choice is made under uncertainty, so the domain is shifted from outcomes to prospects. The assumptions are: (i) each individual's preferences over prospects satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms and are therefore representable by an expected-utility function u_i, unique up to positive affine transformation; (ii) society's preferences over the same prospects satisfy those axioms as well, so that there is a social utility function u_s over prospects, likewise unique up to positive affine transformation; and (iii) a Pareto condition, in the form of Pareto indifference: whenever every individual is indifferent between two prospects, society is indifferent between them too.

The conclusion is that u_s is an increasing weighted sum of the individual utilities, u_s = a_1u_1 + ... + a_nu_n with every a_i positive. Social welfare is thus the average of individual utilities, which is exactly the utilitarian formula, and the theorem has earned its reputation.

The Pareto condition is where the philosophical interest lies, and it is doing far more work than its modest statement suggests. It is what makes the social utility a well-defined function of the vector of individual utilities rather than of anything else: if two prospects look the same to everyone, they look the same to society, so social evaluation can only be tracking the profile of individual utilities. Since both sides of that tracking are linear in probabilities, the function connecting them must be affine, and that is the weighted sum.

The deeper point concerns comparability. The ordinalists held that interpersonal comparisons of utility are meaningless, because nothing fixes whether one person's unit of utility is the same size as another's: each u_i can be rescaled at will. Harsanyi's insight is that this indeterminacy is harmless. Although each u_i is fixed only up to positive affine transformation, the linear representation shows that the social ranking is invariant to the choice of origin and of scale for each individual utility function; any admissible normalization yields the same ordering of prospects. So the theorem is best understood as having dissolved the interpersonal comparison problem rather than as having presupposed a solution to it, which is why it is often said to have vindicated cardinal welfare economics.

The linearity also disposes of a worry about timing. Because a weighted sum of expectations equals the expectation of a weighted sum, and because the prospects in the theorem are defined over a common set of states with agreed probabilities, it makes no difference whether society first aggregates utilities and then takes expectations across states, or first evaluates each state and then aggregates. The ex ante and ex post routes coincide, which is not true of every framework for social evaluation under uncertainty.

Critics who accept the mathematics generally attack the interpretation of the u_i rather than the derivation. The objection pressed by Sen and by Weymark is that a von Neumann–Morgenstern function is only a numerical representation of an individual's preferences over prospects and carries no independent claim to measure that individual's well-being; if the theorem is read as an aggregation of well-being, the interpretation has been supplied by the reader and not by the proof, and what is established is at most a utilitarianism of preference-satisfaction. Harsanyi's own response was to ground comparability in the extended-sympathy or impersonal-observer idea: an evaluator who could equally be any of the persons involved would assess a prospect by the utility it yields averaged over those possibilities.

Given all this, the assumption a critic should target is the Pareto condition, since it is the only one with obvious moral content. Anyone who wants to resist utilitarianism while keeping the expected-utility framework must be prepared to allow that society can rank two prospects even when everyone is indifferent between them, or must accept a rule that ignores unanimous indifference. Rawls's maximin is the standard example of the second route: because it ranks prospects by the position of the worst off, it can rank two prospects differently even when every individual is indifferent between them, and it therefore violates Pareto. This is the price of abandoning utilitarianism."
