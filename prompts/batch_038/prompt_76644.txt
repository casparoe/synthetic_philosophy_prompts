**Dominance, mixtures, and beliefs**

**1. "Dominated" doesn't mean "beaten by another row."**
Textbooks say a strategy is strictly dominated if some other strategy does strictly better against everything the opponents might do. Students read "some other strategy" as another row of the matrix, and then they get stuck on games where no row beats the row in question. The point is that the dominating strategy is allowed to be a lottery over rows. In this game (row player's payoffs only):

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;L&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;R<br>
A&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;3&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;0<br>
B&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;0&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;3<br>
C&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;1&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;1

C is not beaten by A (A is worse at R) and not beaten by B (B is worse at L), but the 50-50 mixture of A and B yields (1.5, 1.5), which beats C's (1, 1) at both columns. So C goes. This isn't a curiosity: it's the reason the iterated-deletion procedure has to be stated over mixed strategies, or it stops too early.

**2. Domination is the same thing as never being a best response.**
Here's the fact I want to lean on, usually called Pearce's lemma: a pure strategy is strictly dominated by a mixed strategy iff there is no belief about the opponents' play to which it is a best response. So the dominance test and the epistemic best-response test are the same test, and you may use whichever is convenient. That is why "rationalizable" and "survives iterated strict dominance" name the same set of strategies in a two-player game.

But the equivalence has a hypothesis hiding in it: "belief" has to mean a probability distribution over the opponents' *joint* profiles, not a list of independent distributions over each opponent's strategies. With two players those coincide, since there's only one opponent. With three or more they don't, and then the two tests come apart.

**3. Two notions that only look the same.**
Allow correlated beliefs — I may think the other two will coordinate, with no signalling device — and you get one notion of rationalizability. Insist that my beliefs about each opponent are independent and you get a smaller one, because every product belief is a correlated belief, so the correlated set contains the independent one, and in three-player games it's strictly bigger. Only the correlated notion coincides with iterated strict dominance; the independent one doesn't. (I'm fuzzy on the history: I think the independent version is Brandenburger–Dekel's and the correlated one is Bernheim's, but I might have that backwards, and I've seen the bare word "rationalizability" used for both.)

Is that a problem? I want to say no. A correlated belief is just a way of encoding the possibility that the others heard something from a common source, which is hardly crazy even without an explicit correlating device.

**4. What exactly survives?**
Two clarifications that get skipped. First, deleting mixed strategies never matters for which *pure* strategies survive: payoffs are linear in the mixture, so if τ beats s against every pure profile of the others it beats s against every mixture too. Second, the converse claim people assume — that mixtures of undominated strategies are undominated — is false:

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;L&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;R<br>
A&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;1&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;-1<br>
B&nbsp;&nbsp;-1&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;1<br>
C&nbsp;&nbsp;.5&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;.5

Neither A nor B nor C is strictly dominated, but the 50-50 mixture of A and B yields (0, 0) and is strictly dominated by C. So "the strategies that survive" has to say whether we're counting pure strategies or all strategies, because those are different questions with different answers.

**5. Strict versus weak, briefly.**
Order doesn't matter for strict dominance: you can delete everything dominated in a single pass at each round and always land in the same place, and since a strictly dominated strategy is never used with positive probability in equilibrium the procedure keeps every Nash equilibrium. Weak dominance is the opposite on both counts:

&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;L&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;R<br>
U&nbsp;&nbsp;1,1&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;0,0<br>
D&nbsp;&nbsp;0,0&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;0,0

U weakly dominates D, but (D, R) is a Nash equilibrium, so the deletion just destroyed an equilibrium. Deleting everything weakly dominated at once is also not the same as deleting one strategy at a time.

**6. Conclusion.**
Given Pearce's lemma the dominance test is redundant — it's the same test in different clothes — so the only version worth teaching is "is this ever a best response?". Mixed strategies as dominance devices, order independence, the whole apparatus falls out of that one question.
