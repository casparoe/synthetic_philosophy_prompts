I've been reading the Zhuangzi in James Legge's 1890s translation, which I realise is a weird choice, but it's the one my local library had on the shelf. Book XIX, the one called "The Full Understanding of Life", starts like this and I've read it maybe fifteen times and I still can't say what it's arguing:

"He who understands the conditions of Life does not strive after what is of no use to life; and he who understands the conditions of Destiny does not strive after what is beyond the reach of knowledge. In nourishing the body it is necessary to have beforehand the things (appropriate to its support); but there are cases where there is a superabundance of such things, and yet the body is not nourished. In order to have life it is necessary that it do not have left the body; but there are cases when the body has not been left by it, and yet the life has perished.

When life comes, it cannot be declined; when it goes, it cannot be detained. Alas! the men of the world think that to nourish the body is sufficient to preserve life; and when such nourishment is not sufficient to preserve the life, what can be done in the world that will be sufficient? Though (all that men can do) will be insufficient, yet there are things which they feel they ought to do, and they do not try to avoid doing them. For those who wish to avoid caring for the body, their best plan is to abandon the world. Abandoning the world, they are free from its entanglements. Free from its entanglements, their (minds) are correct and their (temperament) is equable. Thus correct and equable, they succeed in securing a renewal of life, as some have done. In securing a renewal of life, they are not far from the True (Secret of their being). But how is it sufficient to abandon worldly affairs? and how is it sufficient to forget the (business of) life? Through the renouncing of (worldly) affairs, the body has no more toil; through forgetting the (business of) life, the vital power suffers no diminution. When the body is completed and the vital power is restored (to its original vigour), the man is one with Heaven. Heaven and Earth are the father and mother of all things. It is by their union that the body is formed; it is by their separation that a (new) beginning is brought about. When the body and vital power suffer no diminution, we have what may be called the transference of power. From the vital force there comes another more vital, and man returns to be the assistant of Heaven."

Can you walk me through it? A few things in particular are doing my head in.

First, the opening pair. Why is "conditions of Life" matched with "no use to life" but "conditions of Destiny" matched with "beyond the reach of knowledge"? The two halves look parallel but the second terms don't obviously correspond, and I can't tell whether that's the Chinese or Legge.

Second, and this is the bit I most want sorted out: the whole passage seems to argue against looking after your body ("the men of the world think that to nourish the body is sufficient to preserve life") and then a few lines later it says that if you abandon the world you get "a renewal of life", your body gets "completed", your "vital power" is restored, and you end up "one with Heaven". Isn't that just the body-cultivation programme it started by mocking? My hunch is that the chapter is having a go at the longevity people — the ones doing breathing exercises and diets to live for ever — and that "renewal of life" isn't meant to be about lasting longer at all. But then what is it about, and what's doing the work in the argument that gets you from giving up on the world to being correct and equable?

Third, the death bit. "It is by their union that the body is formed; it is by their separation that a (new) beginning is brought about." Heaven and earth being the father and mother, presumably the separation is death. But a new beginning for whom? Is there any subject left over who benefits from the new beginning, or is the "beginning" just the start of some other thing's existence while the person who died is simply gone? This matters to me because I keep seeing Zhuangzi described as someone who was serene about death, and I can't tell whether the serenity is supposed to come from thinking you survive in some form or from thinking survival is the wrong thing to want.

Fourth, those last two sentences. "Transference of power", "from the vital force there comes another more vital", "man returns to be the assistant of Heaven" — I have no idea what's being claimed here. Someone who reads Chinese told me the word Legge puts as "transference" is a fairly ordinary one about moving or shifting something from here to there, and that Legge's noun-y "transference of power" might be making it sound more like a doctrine than it is. If that's right, how much of the obscurity is in the original and how much is Victorian translation glue? And do the brackets mean Legge is supplying a lot of the connective tissue, i.e. is the sequence of claims here even his to make?

Lastly, how does this sit with the famous stuff later on — drumming on a basin and singing after his wife dies, and the idea that life and death are just the alternation of day and night? Same doctrine, or is Book XIX doing something different, maybe more technical, than the anecdotes? I'm not after a reading list or references to the secondary literature, I just want the passage actually unpacked.
