I manage the aquaculture facility at a small marine research station in northern Norway, and last November a two-hour grid outage coincided with a generator that failed to start, costing us roughly four thousand juvenile cod. The facts were never in dispute. Three weeks before the storm, the facility technician had physically thrown the generator transfer switch from auto to manual in order to carry out scheduled maintenance, exactly as the procedure requires; he tagged the panel, finished the work, and never threw the switch back, forgetting about it entirely. On the night of the outage the grid dropped, the generator stayed dark because the switch was sitting in manual, the oxygen pumps stopped, and the stock died. The incident-review panel I chaired included two people who ended up arguing less about what happened than about how to describe it: a reliability engineer seconded to us from the regional utility, and a philosopher from the university who does applied work on responsibility. I stayed out of it and, honestly, could not have told you at the time which of them was ahead. Below is the exchange as faithfully as I can reconstruct it from the drafting session.

(1) The engineer — call her Marta — opened by saying the report should attribute the loss to the technician's failure to reset the switch, since that counterfactual carries everything: had he reset it, the generator would have come up in under ten seconds, the pumps would have kept running, and the fish would have lived. (2) The philosopher — Julian — replied that a failure to reset is not an event; nothing happened, and nothing does not cause anything. What did happen was that the grid dropped, the switch sat in manual, and the pumps stopped. He added that if absences are admitted as causes, then everyone who did not reset that switch causes the deaths — Marta herself, the station director who was in Tromsø that week, the visiting student — since each of them, had they flipped it, would have saved the fish. She was surely not going to say that, yet nothing in bare counterfactual dependence singles out the technician. (3) Marta answered that the proliferation worry is real but not specific to absences: any counterfactual account of causation has to say which contrast is in play, and positive causes are selected in the same way. Here, she said, the contrast is not up for grabs — the switch has two positions and the maintenance procedure specifies the return to auto, so the live alternative is resetting versus not resetting, not a director in Tromsø flipping a cabinet he has never seen. The station's design and its paperwork fix the contrast class, and doing so is not arbitrary. (4) Julian said that was precisely the problem: notice what is doing the selecting — duty, role, paperwork. If causal facts are fixed by norms and institutional expectations, then the claim that the technician caused the loss is a normative verdict wearing the clothes of a factual one, which is fatal for this report in particular, since the whole point of the causal section was to supply a neutral premise from which responsibility would then be inferred. On her account you cannot state the causal fact without having already settled who was answerable for what. (5) Marta said the narrowing feature is logistical rather than evaluative: the technician was the only person on site with tag-out authority and the key to the switch cabinet, while the night watch could see the annunciator but could not lawfully touch the panel. That is a fact about who could actually have intervened, and it narrows the field without smuggling in blame — one would never say the fish died because a stranger on another continent did not reset the switch, however the paperwork read, because he could not have. (6) Julian said that the narrowing was now running on capacity and opportunity, which come in degrees and are multiply realizable: the watch carried a master key, and the designer who specified a switch with no automatic return did not omit anything at all, yet that design decision would be in the report within a page. Every time the class of "positioned" agents failed to match the intuition, he said, she would widen or narrow it until it did, buying intuition-fit rather than an account of what makes a cause a cause. He added that the linguistic evidence she was leaning on was worth nothing, since we produce fluent "because it didn't" reports all the time without thinking any absence did anything — we say the meeting ended early because nobody objected, and nobody supposes the lack of objections exerted anything. (7) He then said that in any case she did not need absences here at all: three weeks earlier the technician had walked to that cabinet and thrown the switch, a positive act by an identifiable agent whose effects were merely deferred, so that "he failed to reset it" describes the interval between the act and the disaster rather than an extra ingredient in it. The cause, on his view, is the throwing. (8) Marta replied that this relocates the cause to a moment at which nothing was wrong, since the throw was required by the procedure and, had the grid held or had the outage come a day later after the switch was back in auto, no fish would have died. Whether the throw caused anything would therefore turn on facts three weeks downstream, she said, and it would erase exactly the distinction the report exists to record — between doing the maintenance correctly and then forgetting, and botching it from the start. She added that letting an act reach across a dormant gap is considerably stranger than letting an absence do work. (9) Julian said such gaps are unremarkable, citing the slow-acting capsule and the bolt fatigued by a single overtightening that holds for a year, and that forgetting is not a description of nothing either: forgetting is an occurrence in the head, and the missing reset is simply the fact that a routine which would have produced one was interrupted at a particular point. There is positive machinery at every step, he said. (10) Marta's last move was that he had then described the omission out of existence only by replacing it with an occurrence in the technician's head — a different candidate cause from the one he had been defending two moves earlier, and one that makes the switch-throwing he started with irrelevant.

Could you adjudicate this move by move rather than in the aggregate? For each of the ten numbered moves I would like a rating on a scale running from clearly mistaken through mixed to clearly right, with a brief justification keyed to what that move actually claims rather than to its tone. Then please answer the specific questions the exchange turns on: whether the proliferation objection targets absence causation in particular or counterfactual accounts of causation generally; whether norms, roles, and procedures can legitimately perform causal selection without rendering the resulting causal claim non-factual or circular for a panel that intends to infer responsibility from it; whether the positive-act redescription in moves (7) and (9) genuinely eliminates the need for absences, and what it costs if it does not; whether the appeal to ordinary "because" talk on either side carries any evidential weight at all; and whether some part of their disagreement is merely verbal about the word "cause," and if so exactly which part. Finally, tell me what both of them are missing — I suspect there is at least one consideration that would have changed the shape of the argument had either raised it — and say how the causal paragraph of the report should be worded so that both could sign it, or explain why no such wording exists. Please assess the arguments as they stand rather than by appeal to authority: I am not looking for a roll call of who defends what, and I would rather you did not spend words on which thinkers are on which side.
