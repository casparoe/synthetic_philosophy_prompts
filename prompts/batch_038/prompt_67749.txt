I am the teaching assistant for a second-year course on Locke, Berkeley and Hume — about 85 students, two markers plus me. The midterm was sat in week 7, closed book, 45 minutes. Students had been working from an edition of the Principles that keeps Berkeley's own section numbers, and the conceivability challenge had been flagged for them in lecture as falling around sections 22 and 23. Question 4 read as follows:

> **Question 4 (20 marks).** In the *Principles of Human Knowledge*, Berkeley challenges his opponents to conceive of a sensible object — a tree in a park, for instance — existing unconceived and unperceived.
> (a) Set out this argument, identifying its premises and its conclusion. **(8 marks)**
> (b) What work does it do that Berkeley's earlier claims — that the objects of the senses are ideas, and that ideas exist only in a mind — do not already do? **(5 marks)**
> (c) It has been objected that the argument depends on running together the act of conceiving something with the thing conceived. Explain this objection, and say whether in your view it defeats the argument. **(7 marks)**
> Suggested length: 700 words.

The marking meeting on this question produced an unusually wide spread, and the course director has asked each of us to write comments specific enough to survive an appeal, which students here may lodge within two weeks of getting scripts back. Below is one answer. It is anonymised, and I have left the student's wording exactly as written, including the clumsy bits. I have deliberately said nothing about how I read it myself, and I would rather you did not try to infer a view from the way I have set things up.

> Berkeley's starting point is that what we perceive by the senses are ideas, and that an idea cannot exist except in a mind that has it. A colour, a sound, a shape as we experience it: these are ideas, and it makes no sense to speak of an idea existing outside the mind perceiving it. From this he draws the conclusion that the being of a sensible thing consists in its being perceived. This is the master argument, and it is his central move against material substance.
>
> The argument is put in the form of a challenge. Suppose the materialist says that a tree in a park exists even when nobody is there to see it. Berkeley asks him to try to conceive this: to frame the conception of the tree existing absolutely, unperceived and unconceived. But in performing the task the materialist has conceived the tree, and what he conceives he has in his mind; the tree stands before his thought as the object of it, and so it is not unconceived after all. He has not conceived an unperceived tree, he has only produced further ideas in his own head, which Berkeley says misses entirely what was being asked of him. The conclusion is that a sensible thing cannot exist unperceived, since its existence just is its being perceived. And because the materialist cannot even form the conception of matter existing absolutely, he has no intelligible notion on offer, and the hypothesis of an unperceived substratum collapses.
>
> As to whether this adds anything to what came before: on my reading it does not add a new premise so much as complete the first one. Berkeley has already said that the objects of perception are ideas and that ideas exist only in minds, but the materialist might grant all of that about ideas and still insist that behind them there is something which is not an idea — matter, which supports the qualities and exists whether or not they are perceived. The challenge to conceive is how Berkeley closes off that route: he shows that the residual posit cannot be conceived, and so cannot be believed, and therefore cannot be true. In that sense the master argument is not a supplement to the principle that to be is to be perceived but the argument for it. The earlier sections state the principle; sections 22 and 23 prove it, by showing that its denial cannot even be conceived.
>
> The objection says that Berkeley runs together the act of conceiving and the object conceived. When I conceive of a tree, my conception is certainly in my mind, but that does not make the tree I conceive of something in my mind. That X is conceived by me describes a relation between an act and an object, and nothing about where the act is located transfers to the object. So the materialist has not failed after all: he conceived of a tree, and conceived of it as unperceived, and there is no contradiction in that pair of claims — only in the further claim that his conceiving of the tree brought the tree into his mind.
>
> I think the objection has real force but does not defeat the argument. Berkeley can reply that on his account there is no such thing as conceiving of an object except by having an idea of it, and ideas are the immediate objects of the mind, so the gap between act and object that the objector needs is one Berkeley's metaphysics simply refuses to supply. The objector will say that this restates the point at issue, since the materialist is not talking about ideas, and that is fair enough. But the challenge was never meant to settle the question on its own. What it shows is that the materialist cannot exhibit any content for his claim, and a claim whose content cannot be exhibited is not one that can be true. The upshot is that the tree, the park and the mountain are not really there in the way we suppose: they are constructions out of ideas, and their apparent independence of us is an illusion which Berkeley's argument dissolves. On this reading Berkeley is a sceptic about the external world in roughly the sense his contemporaries took him to be. He agrees that we have no access to anything beyond our own ideas; his only disagreement with the sceptic is about whether that is a loss.
