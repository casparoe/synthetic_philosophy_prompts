So I've been working for about four months on a small digitization project at a county historical society, and I keep getting into arguments with our consultant that I don't think are really about records at all, they're about something more basic that I can't quite name. Background: we inherited roughly 90 boxes from a county infirmary that doubled as the poor farm, in operation from around 1888 to 1961. Admission ledgers, indigency files, a burial book, some correspondence. The thing is, the boxes as they arrived are NOT how the infirmary kept anything. In 1974 a state records management survey crew went through the whole collection, pulled out what they called non-record material, and refiled everything into a new series arrangement by record type, and that's the arrangement the existing finding aid describes. Then there was a fire in the east wing in 1981 that took part of the storage room, and a microfilming outfit in 1998 filmed some series but not others. So basically every layer of intervention is stacked on top of the last one.

Our consultant's proposal is to pull everything out of the 1974 arrangement and refile the surviving indigency papers by resident surname, plus build a name index, on the grounds that these were people and the state's filing logic treated them as caseload, and the least we can do is give them back their names. The state archivist who signs off on our grant says absolutely not, that the 1974 arrangement is the arrangement of record, that we have an obligation to preserve the order the materials came to us in, and that re-filing would be an irreversible act of our own. But here's what I can't get past: the 1974 arrangement isn't the infirmary's order either. It's the order left behind by the exact people who did the discarding. So when the archivist tells us we're preserving provenance, whose provenance are we preserving? Are we protecting the creator's logic or a later triage crew's logic, and is there even a principled way to tell the difference once the earlier order is gone? It feels like both sides are using the same word to mean two different things and neither has noticed.

Second thing, and this one is the weirdest. The 1974 surveyors left a typewritten "disposition schedule" that lists, by category, roughly how many cubic feet they removed and why. So we can sometimes tell that a name in the intake ledger index has no file behind it, and why. Our consultant wants those names to appear in the new catalog with a note explaining that the file is gone and which cull probably took it. The archivist says you can't catalog a record that doesn't exist, that a catalog is a description of holdings, and that entries for absent items will contaminate the description. I genuinely don't know who's right. Is an entry for a missing file a record about the infirmary, or a record about the 1974 crew, or a record about us? And if it's the last one, does that make it a fake, or does it make it the most honest thing in the whole catalog?

Third, smaller but it keeps coming up. Back in the seventies a graduate student photographed and photocopied a run of 1930s case files for her dissertation; those originals were destroyed in the 1981 fire, and her photocopies, covered in her pencil annotations, are now sitting in one of our boxes. The archivist says they're not part of the infirmary's materials because the infirmary didn't make them, and they should be described as a separate small collection. Our consultant says they ARE the destroyed files, in the only form they still exist, and splitting them off is a technicality that will make them invisible to anyone searching for a resident's name. Also the student died in 1993, so her pencil notes are themselves forty-year-old historical evidence now, and nobody knows whose voice a given page is in.

What I actually need is a recommendation memo by Friday, so: can you explain what is really at stake in each of these three fights, in plain terms, and then tell me what I should argue for? I don't want a hedge that lists considerations on both sides and leaves me holding the decision. Take a position on each one, and tell me where you're least sure. Specifically, I want you to say what would change your mind, like which argument or which fact about our collection would push you to the other side, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to break down, because I'm going to get grilled on this by people who do this for a living. And if you're leaning on a debatable reading of any archival principle to get to your answer, flag it rather than presenting it as settled. American English please.
