You are a philosopher who works in action theory and experimental philosophy, with a serious command of the side-effect effect — the Knobe effect — and of the explanatory literature around it: the accounts that appeal to blame or to affective response, to the agent's normative reasons, to skill, to the trade-off between a goal and its foreseen side effects, and the deflationary view that the asymmetry is an artifact of how the question is put. I am not asking for a survey of that debate. I am asking what my own pattern of verdicts commits me to, and where that pattern has to be revised.

Here is the battery. In every case an agent acts for a primary goal, is certain of a side effect, and brings it about. The verdicts concern whether the agent produced the side effect *intentionally*.

1. The familiar pair. A chairman wants only to raise profits; he is told a new program will harm the environment and says he does not care about that at all, he just wants profit; the program harms the environment. My verdict: intentional. The mirror case, where the program will help the environment and he is equally indifferent: not intentional.
2. As in the helping case, except that the chairman is glad about the help and calls it a bonus, though not what he is aiming at. Still not intentional.
3. As in the harming case, except that the chairman is delighted by the damage and would have gone ahead regardless. Intentional, and more clearly so than in 1.
4. A pilot bombs a munitions plant. His aim is the plant; he knows some of the workers will die; he regrets this and raids at night precisely to reduce it, but their deaths cannot be avoided given the strike. Not intentional.
5. A homeowner pumps water from his well at night, knowing it will draw down the water table and kill his neighbor's orchard. He is genuinely sorry, has had an engineer confirm that no setting would avoid it, and keeps pumping because he wants a green lawn and a full pool. Intentional.

Cases 4 and 5 are the pair I cannot reconcile: same structure — foreseen, regretted, minimized, unavoidable — differing only in the worth of the goal and the standing of the victims.

6. An unskilled darts player aims at the bullseye and hits it by luck, after missing every previous throw. Not intentional. A professional who hits it: intentional.
7. An incompetent would-be killer fires from too far off, misjudges badly, and the bullet glances off a railing into the victim. I say he killed him intentionally. Structurally this is case 6 with a bad outcome, and my verdict flips.
8. A man takes a course of medication knowing it will certainly make him miserable for a week; he cares only about clearing the infection and is indifferent to the misery. I find myself saying he made himself miserable intentionally, which unsettles me, since there is nothing to blame him for.
9. The case I keep going back and forth on: a tenant turns the heat up in winter knowing it will kill the landlord's houseplants in the spare room; he does not care about the plants, he wants to be warm. This is case 1 with a trivial harm, and I lean toward *not* intentional, which feels arbitrary.

Work through this carefully before you answer, and start with some brainstorming rather than a thesis: lay out the structural features that vary across the nine cases — valence of the side effect, the agent's attitude toward it, its avoidability, whether it is a byproduct or a means, the agent's competence, the moral standing of the primary goal, who bears the cost, the seriousness of the harm — and note for each case which features are present. A compact table is welcome.

Then give me the following.

First, a principle, or at most two competing candidates, stated precisely enough to be falsifiable: conditions, not slogans, with each clause tied to the verdicts that motivate it. It should deliver my verdicts on 1 through 9, or explain exactly which it does not.

Second, a committed answer to the question behind the question: are these judgments about intentionality at all, or is the concept doing double duty for something else — blameworthiness, an assessment of character, a claim about what the agent was prepared to accept? Pick a side and argue for it. Case 8 is where a purely blame-based reading should break down, and I want to know whether yours survives it.

Third, predictions for at least three cases I have not listed, chosen by you to be genuinely diagnostic, with your reasoning. I would particularly like to know what your principle says about a corporate decision made by a committee rather than a person, and about a foreseen harm that the agent would have welcomed had it occurred but that does not.

Fourth, an honest reckoning: which of my verdicts does your principle force me to give up? Say so plainly, and say why the principle earns that cost rather than being patched to accommodate it. If your answer is that 4 and 5 cannot both stand, tell me which one goes.

Fifth, one vignette study — describe the paired vignettes, the response measure, and the predicted difference — that would discriminate your principle from its strongest rival, and say what result would count against yours.

Finally, a note on my reliability as a respondent: I have years of training in exactly these distinctions, so tell me whether you think my pattern is atypical of ordinary speakers, and whether that matters for what the principle is supposed to explain.

Please take a definite position throughout. I would rather be argued out of three of my verdicts by a view you will stand behind than be handed a balanced menu of options with the choice left to me.
