I'm prepping a section for an intro ethics seminar where the students will have just been walked through the standard impossibility argument against genuine moral dilemmas — the one that leans on obligations agglomerating, on "ought implies can," and on the claim that all-things-considered moral requirements can't conflict with each other. They're supposed to leave the room thinking a dilemma is just a hard choice in disguise. I want a case that makes them not so sure.

So: construct me a scenario that is a genuine moral dilemma in the strict sense — whichever way the agent goes, she fails to discharge an all-things-considered requirement, not merely a weaker reason that got outvoted. It has to satisfy all of the following:

- realistic and small-scale: an office, a family, a classroom, a small business. no trolleys, no burning buildings, no organ transplants, no lifeboats, no war, no sci-fi technology.
- no villain. Nobody coerces, blackmails or manipulates her into the choice, and the conflict isn't something she brought on herself through earlier wrongdoing, a reckless pile-up of promises, or ignorance she should have shaken off. It should come from the world suprising her.
- exactly two options, and no compromise course that partially honors both — no splitting the difference, no doing one now and the other next week, no coin flip or lottery or other fair randomizer that would let her discharge both duties at once.
- both requirements have to be owed to someone: there's a specific person who'd have a legitimate complaint against her, not just an impersonally worse outcome.
- neither duty may come from a professional code or job description that already ranks them. I dont want the case settled by an employee handbook.

Keep the scenario itself to about 150 words. Then show your work:

1. Go down the list and say how the case meets each bullet. Be honest if you had to bend one.
2. Name the two all-things-considered requirements and say which ordinary moral fact in the story generates each one — just stipulating that she's required to do both doesn't count, it has to fall out of the details.
3. Block the three escapes explicitly: why this isn't a mere pro tanto conflict with a unique verdict; why it isn't a dead heat where either option is simply permissible (symmetry alone doesn't make a dilemma — she has to be doing wrong whichever way she goes); and why there really is no third option.
4. Tell me which premise of the impossibility argument the case forces you to give up — agglomeration, ought-implies-can, or the consistency of all-things-considered requirements — and why that's the cheapest one to sacrifice.
5. Which single feature is doing all the work? Then say what the case collapses into if you delete that feature.
6. Finally, give the best attack a dilemma-denier has on your case. Not a strawman — the move I'd actually have to worry about if I were defending it in the room.
