I have no academic background in any of this — I have worked as a pharmacy technician for nineteen years — but I keep a notebook of arguments I have overheard and could not settle in my own head, and I would like to put one of them to you. It happened at a dinner in my house in October, between my sister Margit and a man named Des, a retired structural engineer who writes a small music blog that perhaps two hundred people read. The occasion was a reissue of a privately pressed record from the middle of the seventies by a woman who apparently never recorded anything else; three weeks earlier Des had sent Margit a link to his own review of it, in which he called it one of the most beautiful things he had ever heard. She listened, and now she plays it constantly. When she said so, Des told her he was glad, but that he did not think her liking counted for very much, and that is where it started.

(1) Des said that Margit had not arrived at her opinion; it had been installed in her beforehand, and that finding something beautiful because you were told it is beautiful is like laughing at a joke because you were told it was funny — the laugh is genuine, but it is not a response to the joke. (2) Margit said that if that is illegitimate, then a great deal of ordinary life is illegitimate: she chose the restaurant we were eating in from a review, and she reads novels because friends insist they are masterpieces, and nobody thinks less of her enjoyment of either. (3) Des said those cases are different, because there the review only tells you where to send your own senses, and the verdict comes from your tongue or your eyes: testimony is a search engine, not a judge. With the record, he said, she had reached a verdict before hearing a single note, so her listening was an inspection of a conclusion rather than a search. (4) Margit said the review pointed her senses too — it told her to listen for the way the drummer sits slightly behind the beat, and once she heard that she could not un-hear it. Des agreed that this part was legitimate, and said that directing attention is one thing while supplying the verdict is another. (5) Margit answered that she cannot tell which of the two happened, because both arrived in the same paragraph of the same review, and his distinction only works if you can watch the two operating separately. Des said you can: suppose she had read only the sentence about the drummer and never the sentence calling the record a masterpiece; her listening would have been the same, minus the confidence that she was right to like it. (6) Margit said that confidence is no trinket. Half of what she enjoys, she said, is the feeling of being in the presence of something that matters, and if the review is what supplies that feeling, then the review is doing real aesthetic work and not counterfeit work. (7) Margit then volunteered something against herself: she had in fact played the record once before Des's review arrived and found it dull, and she can no longer reproduce that reaction, so she has no way of knowing whether her present love is a change of taste or a surrender. She asked whether that makes her judgment worthless or merely unverifiable. Des said it makes it non-independent, and that non-independent evidence does not accumulate — two photocopies of the same review are not two reviews.

Then a third guest, Theo, joined in and opened a second front. He said that he had never heard the record and had no intention of listening to it, but that he would simply take Des's word that it is a masterpiece. (8) Des said this was entirely reasonable and that Theo would lose no belief by not listening: he would know the record is great in just the way he knows things about Arctic terns from reading. Des added that he himself believes his house's foundation is sound purely on an engineer's word, and that it would be absurd to say he does not really believe it. (9) Margit said Theo does not believe it at all — he believes that Des believes it — and that if you asked him why the record is great he would have nothing to offer but Des's say-so, so a belief he cannot use is not a belief about the thing. Des's last move was that what Theo is missing is not a belief but an acquaintance, and that the whole argument began because those two were confused with each other: you can be handed a verdict, and you can be handed the reasons for a verdict, but you cannot, he said, be handed the warmth of a voice.

I could not referee this and I have not stopped thinking about it since. Please go through it move by move — the numbered points, including Margit's move against herself at (7) and Des's closing line — and rate each of them on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for every rating. Then tell me which single point the whole dispute actually turns on, and whether one of the two comes out more right overall; I would rather be told that plainly than have the difference split evenly down the middle. I am especially interested in whether Des's separation of directing attention from supplying a verdict survives Margit's objection at (5) that the two arrive in the same sentence, and whether the case of Theo, who never listens, is genuinely different from trusting an engineer about a foundation. Finally, say what both of them missed or assumed without arguing for it. Please reason from the exchange as it actually went rather than from general remarks about art criticism, and do not send me off to books or articles — I have no access to a library, and I would rather have your own working-through.
