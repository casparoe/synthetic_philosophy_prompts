I'm in a decision-theory reading group and we've been going around in circles on Good's theorem about the value of information for the better part of two years, and at this point I'd rather have a diagnosis of the argument than another round of it. Please write in American English.

Here's the shape of the dispute as I see it.

Camp A's line: take an agent with a settled set of acts, a credence function over states, who conditionalizes on what she learns and doesn't forget it. Then a costless observation can never lower her expected utility, because in the worst case she just does what she'd have done anyway. The ignore-it move makes the result practically free, so what's left of "information can hurt you" is really about price, or about sloppy updating, or about weakness of will. On this view the interesting cases are engineering problems, not counterexamples.

Camp B keeps producing cases they think break it, and their general complaint is that the ignore-it move quietly assumes the agent can still perform the act she'd have performed uninformed — which is exactly what's in doubt, becuase either her tastes have shifted, or the act is off the table now that other people condition on the fact that she knows, or she can't stop herself acting on what she's seen. The three they lean on hardest:

1. A parent weighing a predictive test for an untreatable late-onset condition in her kid. A positive result changes no medical decision but changes how she experiences every birthday for thirty years. Camp A absorbs this by making the parent's own beliefs part of the consequence space — belief-dependent utility, so nothing is violated. Camp B says that absorption is the whole problem.
2. A hiring committee that scores candidates after seeing their names. More information can't hurt them if the goal is picking the best player, but the institution blinds the review anyway, not because names are costly but because the reviewers can't be trusted to conditionalize on names the way the institution wants. Camp A calls this a principal-agent problem in decision-theory clothing; Camp B calls it a case where "the agent's expected utility" is the wrong thing to be maximizing and the theorem is answering a question nobody asked.
3. A prosecutor who declines a cheap DNA test, or a buyer who skips the inspection, because a deal is alive only while both sides are uninformed. Learning is free but being known-to-have-learned isn't.

My own hypothesis, which I want you to attack rather than accept: this isn't really a disagreement about whether information can make someone worse off. It's a disagreement about which object gets held fixed across the learning event — a snapshot problem with a frozen act set and a conditionalizing updater, where Camp A is right and nearly trivially so, versus an extended situation where the act set, the tastes, the other players' strategies, or whose utility counts are all moving, where the theorem simply doesn't get a grip. And a good chunk of it may be verbal, since economists mean a willingness-to-pay number by "value of information," philosophers mean a requirement to gather your total evidence, and the ML people in my group mean instrumental usefulness for hitting a fixed goal, and all three camps are talking past each other under one label.

What I want from you:

- Tell me whether that's the right diagnosis, a wrong one, or only half of it. Is this substantive, verbal, two questions sharing a name, a hidden formal question doing all the work, or an ill-posed question? Argue for your reading using what each camp actually says, not using generalities about how decision theory is complicated.
- Engage seriously with the absorption worry in case 1. Camp A can always enlarge the state space, endogenize the preference change, or bring in Kreps–Porteus-style preferences over when uncertainty resolves rather than what it resolves to. If literally any apparent counterexample can be re-modeled until the theorem applies again, is the theorem unfalsifiable and therefore empty? Or is there a principled line between legitimate re-modeling and a dodge — some constraint on what counts as the same decision problem — and if so, state it.
- Say whether there's a case, or a kind of case, that would force Camp A to actually concede something rather than re-describe, and if there isn't, say that plainly.
- Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis: a theorem, a distinction, a change of question, a piece of data, or nothing at all — and if nothing, explain why the argument kept feeling substantive to everyone in the room even so.

Don't send me a bibliography, a reading list, or names-and-dates of papers — I'm offline and can't check any of it. I want the reasoning, and I want you to commit to a diagnosis instead of hedging across all of them.
