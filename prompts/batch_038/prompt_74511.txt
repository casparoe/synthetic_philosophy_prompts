I'm a high school senior and the student member of my district's Algorithmic Review Panel, which has to approve or reject any predictive tool before the district uses it on students. Our record so far:

1. **Approved** an early-warning score that assigned students to optional mentoring and bus passes. Chronic-absence rates were nearly identical across the two groups we track; the score was calibrated within groups, and its false positive and false negative rates came out within a point of each other across groups. It clearly beat the counselor-referral process it replaced.
2. **Approved** a summer reading screen with open enrollment, so every student it flagged got a place. Same pattern: base rates close, calibrated, error rates close across groups, and it reached families the old sign-up system had missed.
3. **Rejected** a behavior-risk score for assigning mandatory study hall and stripping extracurricular eligibility. The vendor's own validation showed predicted probabilities running too high for one group, that group's false positive rate was roughly double, and one input — a household-contact field — tracked closely with family income.
4. **Approved with conditions** a course-recommendation score, only after we made the district drop an input that functioned as an income proxy; with that variable removed it was calibrated and its error rates were close across groups.

The proposal in front of us now: a score predicting who will pass the state exit exam, used to allocate 120 seats in a capped dual-enrollment program. Base rates differ sharply between our two tracked groups (roughly 85% vs. 55%). The tool is well calibrated within groups — our consultant confirmed that predicted pass probabilities match observed frequencies in every score band for both groups — and she also told us that once base rates differ this much, calibration mathematically forces unequal error rates, with the lower-base-rate group ending up with the higher false negative rate. That is what we see: about twice as many of that group's students who would have passed are left out. Its inputs are only course grades and prior test scores, and our proxy screen found nothing, though one member argues grades are themselves downstream of unequal schooling. Because the program is capped, a false positive for one student is a seat lost to another. Under the teacher-nomination process it would replace, the lower-base-rate group received more seats than this tool would give them.

I think several different rules fit all four precedents — that calibration is decisive; that the *costly* error type must not fall unevenly, where which error is costly depends on whether the tool confers a benefit or imposes a burden; that no group may end up worse off than under the process being replaced; that inputs must survive a proxy screen; and that we only tolerated error disparities before because those benefits weren't rationed. Please lay out the candidate rules, predict our vote, and say what we would be committing ourselves to.
