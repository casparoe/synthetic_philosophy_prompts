You are to work as an expert in the semantics and pragmatics of derogatory language — someone equally at home with formal accounts of expressive meaning and with the day-to-day work of appellate content adjudication. Please think the material through carefully before writing; I would rather have a rigorous analysis than a fast one.

**Background.** I clerk for the independent appeals panel of a large online platform. The panel has five members and hears appeals from first-instance moderation decisions under Rule 7, which prohibits "the use of a slur to demean, degrade, or incite hatred against a person or group." That sentence is the panel's entire textual authority: "use," "slur," and "demean" are undefined in the rulebook. Its dispositions form an ordered set, from most to least restrictive: (a) uphold removal; (b) restore with a sensitivity notice; (c) restore with a sensitivity notice and strip the post from algorithmic recommendation and trending surfaces; (d) restore outright.

Slurs are not spelled out anywhere in this prompt and should not be spelled out in your answer; refer to them as "the word."

**The record so far.** Each entry gives the facts and the disposition; a ground is reproduced only where the panel stated one.

1. A member of the targeted group used the word self-referentially and affectionately inside a membership-restricted group of co-members. Restored outright. No ground stated.
2. The same speaker, in the same group, used the word about another member of the group; that member objected. Removal upheld. No ground stated.
3. A user posted a verbatim, quotation-marked copy of a private message in which an anonymous sender had directed the word at them, submitted as evidence in a harassment report. Restored outright. Ground: "quotation, not use."
4. An uninvolved third party posted a verbatim quotation of the identical message inside a long argument about the sender's ideology; the passage could have been paraphrased without loss to the argument. Removal upheld. Ground: "the derogatory content entered the public record without being needed."
5. An instructor posted, in a closed course group, the word with interior characters replaced by asterisks, in order to explain its history and semantics. Restored outright.
6. A user posted the word with interior characters replaced by digits, aimed at a named individual in an open thread. Removal upheld. Ground: "obfuscation does not convert a use into a mention."
7. A comedian posted his own recorded routine in full; he voices a racist character, and the word occurs inside the character's speech, with the performative frame unmistakable. First instance split; on appeal, restored with a sensitivity notice and removed from recommendation surfaces. No ground stated.
8. In a small invitation-only group containing no member of the targeted group other than the person described, an out-group member posted "K is a [word]," insisting he meant it descriptively; there was no evidence of animus. Removal upheld. Ground: "animus is not an element of the rule."
9. A syndicated newspaper article whose headline quotes a public official's use of the word, where the official's utterance is itself the news. Restored with a sensitivity notice.

**The appeal now before us.** A journalist who is herself a member of the targeted group posts, on her own widely followed account, about the pending prosecution of a member of an organized hate group. She quotes verbatim and unredacted two paragraphs of the group's manifesto in which the word occurs seven times, once in a slogan addressed to the reader in the second person. She explains in the post that paraphrase would have sanitized the record and that her readers are entitled to see what the group actually wrote. The post was picked up by the platform's trending module and is now reaching a very large general audience, including minors and members of the targeted group who did not seek it out. First instance removed it under Rule 7; she has appealed. The syndicate that carries her column has proposed, as a compromise, disposition (c).

**What I need from you.** Put your bottom-line prediction and your recommended disposition at the very top, then organize the rest under the following headings.

A. *Candidate rules.* Before delivering any verdict, brainstorm at least four decision rules a panel could be running — rules keyed to speaker standing, to the use/mention distinction, to the necessity of the echo, to the expressive force the speaker manifests, to the conditions of reception, to what the word contributes to the truth conditions of the sentence, or to something else you think fits the data better. State each rule precisely enough that a single precedent could falsify it, then test each against all nine precedents, marking for each rule which precedents it fits cleanly, which it strains, and which it contradicts.

B. *Survivors.* Identify the rules that survive the whole record, and say what each of them selects for the pending appeal, down to the lettered disposition.

C. *Prediction.* Give your best prediction of what this panel will actually decide, with a rough confidence, and your reasons for preferring the rule you rely on to the others.

D. *Recommendation.* Separately from prediction: if the panel's aim is coherence rather than anything else, which disposition best follows from its own precedents? Name the closest analog among the nine. Say honestly where the record genuinely underdeterminates the outcome, and for each live option state what principle the panel would be committing itself to by choosing it — including any commitment it could not disown in later cases without reversing itself.

E. *The theoretical tension.* There is a strain in the record I cannot resolve on my own: precedents 3 and 5 exempt a verbatim copy and a partially masked form alike as "mentions," precedent 6 treats a masked form as a full-blooded use, and precedent 8 upholds removal where no contempt was expressed at all. Explain how a coherent account of what a slur contributes to an utterance — at-issue content, presupposition, expressive force, a use-condition, an echo, a way of presenting a referent, or whatever you prefer — can accommodate 3, 5, 6, and 8 simultaneously. If it cannot, say which of the four the panel most likely decided wrongly by the lights of its own implicit theory, and why. Then tell me which family of accounts the record as a whole best fits, and whether that fit is stable or an artifact of how the nine cases happen to be distributed.

F. *The discriminating case.* Propose the single further appeal that would most sharply separate the surviving rules — described in enough detail that its disposition would be forced one way or the other — and explain the mechanism by which it discriminates.

**Constraints.** Do not survey the literature on slurs and do not provide a bibliography, reading list, or citations; you may use standard labels for theoretical positions where you are confident of them, but invent no attributions, titles, or dates. Argue from the record I have given you rather than from what such panels usually do. If you think my framing of the problem is mistaken in some respect — for instance, if you judge that the nine precedents cannot be rationalized at all, or that the pending case is not genuinely underdetermined — say so plainly and argue for it instead of working around it silently.
