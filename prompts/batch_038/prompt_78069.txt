DRAFT: The Idealization Schedule — Why Indirect Normativity May Be Answering a Question Nobody Asked

1. The target. Indirect normativity, as I use the term, is the strategy of answering a normative question by specifying a procedure rather than a result. Instead of asserting which outcomes are good or which acts are permitted, one describes an idealized deliberation — actual agents transformed along certain dimensions — and declares that whatever that deliberation converges on is the answer. The strategy is old in metaethics and recent in the design of artificial systems. In metaethics it appears as the family of ideal-advisor accounts: Brandt's proposal that a desire is rational to the extent that it would survive a process of cognitive psychotherapy, meaning maximal exposure to vivid and accurate representation of the relevant non-moral facts; Railton's account of a person's good as what a fully informed and fully rational version of that person would want her to want; Smith's analysis of a normative reason for an agent as what a fully rational counterpart of that agent would want the agent to want; Hare's archangel, combining perfect information with perfect sympathetic imagination. On the applied side it appears in proposals to specify a system's aims as the extrapolated product of what humans would want on extended reflection, rather than as a list of values.

All of these share a form: an input plus a transformation. The input fixes what is held constant about the actual agent — her desires, her cares, her evaluative starting points, her capacities, her situation. The transformation is a list of idealizations applied to that input: full information, perfect instrumental rationality, vivid imagination, unlimited reflection time, absence of cognitive defects, and sometimes impartiality or sympathy. Call the list a schedule.

2. The schedule problem. Two claims. First, the output is not invariant under the schedule. Holding a desire fixed while transforming the agent's information and rationality can extinguish the desire; this is precisely what Brandt's procedure is designed to accomplish. Holding desires fixed while transforming only how they are weighed yields a different answer again. Holding fixed nothing but the capacity to respond to reasons, and adding perfect sympathy, yields something closer to an impartial observer who may not be recognizably the agent who began. These are not marginal variations, and they can disagree in direction: an account that idealizes information alone may license a preference that an account idealizing sympathy alone condemns.

Second, there is no non-question-begging way to choose a schedule. Every reason offered for including an idealization already embeds a normative commitment. Vividness is included on the grounds that a dimly represented fact distorts deliberation — but which facts are reason-giving, and in what weight, is exactly what the procedure was supposed to determine. Impartiality is included on the grounds that partiality is a defect — again, the conclusion installed in the machine. The schedule is therefore not a neutral implementation detail. It is where the entire normative content of an indirect account resides, and it is defended, if at all, by intuitions the account was meant to systematize.

3. Three replies, and why I am not satisfied. Convergence: suppose that as idealization increases, outputs from different schedules converge, so the choice matters only where it ceases to matter. This assumes that further idealization removes disagreement rather than removing the constraints that were generating agreement, and there is no independent reason to think the former holds. It is also self-sealing: when two schedules disagree, the convergence theorist says one has been under-idealized, but under-idealization can be diagnosed only against an independent standard the account does not supply.

Endorsement on return, my own attempted repair: a schedule is admissible only if the actual agent, restored to ordinary capacity and fully informed of the idealized agent's verdicts, endorses those verdicts; the normative output is whatever survives idealize-then-return, making the account a fixed-point condition. This makes input and output answerable to each other, but I think it fails. The returning agent is not the agent who left, since the report has changed her, so any fixed point is neither obviously unique nor obviously stable. The return leg also requires its own schedule — how much disclosure, over what period, with what opportunity to revise — which reintroduces the problem one level up. To be non-regressive the return condition must be governed by the same schedule as the idealization leg, and I cannot see why that pairing is anything but arbitrary.

Deliberation as constitutive: the constructivist reply that the idealized process does not track independently existing norms but makes them, so no schedule is correct, only adopted. This dissolves the schedule problem only by dissolving the ambition. If the process constitutes the norms, the choice of process is a bare normative choice, and indirect normativity has not avoided first-order ethics; it has hidden it in a list of parameters.

4. A deflationary suggestion. Perhaps the schedule problem is not a defect. Perhaps indirect normativity was never an answer to what is good, and has been misread as one. Read instead as an answer to who is entitled to settle the question, the schedule is exactly what it should be: a proposal for allocating authority over the specification of values. Its acceptability condition is then recognitional rather than truth-tracking — those bound by the output must be able to see the idealized deliberator's verdicts as answers to their own questions rather than to someone else's. This is weaker than endorsement on return, since it does not require agreement, and stronger than causal continuity, since it constrains which transformations of an agent count as improvements. It also moves the hard work from a thin literature to a thick one: the question becomes whether the idealized agent is still me, which has been examined carefully before, though usually in connection with fission, teleportation, and dementia rather than idealization. On this reading the standard complaint that indirect normativity postpones the difficult questions is correct but misplaced: it postpones them to a domain where they are better understood.
