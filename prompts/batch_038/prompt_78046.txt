The question, as the examiner set it, with the mark allocation:

**Question 3 (25 marks).**
(a) [6] State the KK principle, and state what it is for a condition to be luminous. Be precise about the epistemic strength of the definition: what is required of the subject, and at what time?
(b) [12] Reconstruct Williamson's anti-luminosity argument for the claim that the condition of feeling cold is not luminous. Identify each premise, explain what licenses the premise that carries knowledge in one case over to the obtaining of the condition in the next case, and state the conclusion exactly.
(c) [7] If the argument is sound, does it establish that KK is false? Does it presuppose KK? Explain the relation between the two claims.

Here is the answer, transcribed as written:

> (a) KK is the principle that knowledge iterates: if S knows that p, then S knows that S knows that p. In epistemic logic it is axiom 4 read with K rather than B, so it is the knowledge analogue of positive introspection, and it is not to be confused with axiom 5, which concerns the introspection of ignorance. A condition C is luminous iff necessarily, whenever C obtains in a case, S is in a position to know in that case that C obtains. Two things are worth stressing. The requirement is that knowledge be available, not that S bother to form the belief; and the availability holds at the time the condition obtains, not later on reflection. Luminosity is therefore a completeness thesis about knowledge of C rather than a claim that C is incorrigible.
>
> (b) Williamson's target is the condition of feeling cold, which the Cartesian tradition treats as a paradigm case of a luminous condition. Construct a series of cases α0 … αn, running from a moment at dawn at which you feel cold to a moment at noon at which you feel warm, changing slowly enough that no case is discriminable from its immediate neighbour with respect to how you feel. The condition obtains at α0 and fails at αn.
>
> Assume for reductio that the condition is luminous. Working with the cases as described, in which you are awake, attentive and in command of the concept, luminosity gives: if you feel cold at αi, you know at αi that you feel cold. The premise that does the real work is the margin-for-error principle: if you know at αi that you feel cold, then you feel cold at αi+1. This is not stipulated. It follows from the requirement that knowledge be safe: since αi+1 is indiscriminable from αi, you would have formed the same belief there, so if the condition failed at αi+1 your belief at αi would easily have been false and would not have amounted to knowledge.
>
> Combining the two: if the condition obtains at αi, it obtains at αi+1. By induction over the series it obtains at αn. It does not. So the reductio succeeds: feeling cold is not luminous, and there is some case in the series in which you feel cold without being in a position to know that you feel cold. Structurally the argument resembles a sorites, but its premises concern knowledge and its reliability rather than borderline cases, and nothing in it turns on the predicate feels cold being vague. The schema is perfectly general, so no condition whatever is luminous.
>
> (c) Yes, and the interesting feature of the argument is that it refutes KK by using it. The margin-for-error premise is KK displaced by one case: KK says that knowing carries knowing that one knows, and the margin for error says that knowing carries the obtaining of the condition in the case next along, which is what you get when knowing that one feels cold at αi involves knowing that αi is indiscriminable from αi+1. KK is therefore doing the work in (b), and the failure of luminosity is just the failure of knowledge to propagate along the series. This makes the argument look circular with respect to (c): KK cannot be both a premise of the refutation and its conclusion, unless the margin for error can be supported independently, in which case the refutation is merely indirect. What is lost either way is the Cartesian picture. Luminosity was what made the mental a realm in which we could not go wrong, and its failure means that in the dark case you feel cold and are prepared to deny it; so we can be mistaken about our own present feelings.
