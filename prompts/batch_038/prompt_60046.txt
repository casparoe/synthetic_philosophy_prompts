ok so I've been asked to contribute a short "where I stand" box to a working group comparing the no-fee model (Germany, which scrapped tuition in 2014, plus the Nordics — though Norway started charging non-EU students from autumn 2023) against the loan model (US federal debt, and England's Plan 2 / Plan 5 system). Before I send it I want someone to try and break the package, because I can't see my own blind spots anymore. Here it is:

(a) Most of the earnings premium from a degree lands on the graduate, so the benefit principle says the graduate should carry most of the cost of producing it.

(b) But not all of it — there's a genuine social slice (health, civic participation, innovation spillovers, an electorate that can follow a budget), and that slice should be funded up front out of general taxation. That's basically the German/Nordic logic and I think its right in principle.

(c) The correct instrument for the graduate's share is income-contingent: a fixed percentage of earnings above a threshold, written off after a set number of years, no credit check, no collections, no co-signer, no impact on your ability to rent a flat.

(d) Charging above-inflation interest on that is indefensible. England has cohorts paying RPI plus up to 3% who can make payments for years and watch the balance go up rather than down, and the newest cohort trades that for RPI-only but waits 40 years for write-off. A loan where the arithmetic can run forever isn't a loan, it's a lifetime graduate tax in a loan costume.

(e) And yet I think universal free tuition is regressive in incidence. Graduates still come disproportionately from families with more money and more schooling, so the German model moves money from the whole tax base — including people who never went and never will — to a group that was already ahead. That seems to me an argument for delivering (b) through something like (c) rather than through zero fees.

(f) I'm flatly against the US arrangement where your price depends on your parents' tax return: two students, same institution, different net price, keyed to family finances. Intrusive, opaque, humiliating. The sticker price should be public and identical for everyone in the cohort.

(g) Norway charging non-EU students full fees doesn't bother me at all, since a lot of their spillovers go home with them after graduation.

(h) On method: you evaluate these systems ex ante — from a position where you don't know your own ability, your parents, or which way the labour market breaks — not by totting up who actually ended up paying what.

So: which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I'd much rather get two real cracks than have all eight flagged as suspicious, so please say explicitly when a pair is fine on inspection and why. Then rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one say which commitment has to move, what the move costs me downstream, and whether there's a rewording that keeps most of the content. Finally, tell me which single revision buys the most consistency for the least damage. Don't assume I'd sacrifice any particular item — I honestly don't know which one I'd drop, that's the whole reason I'm asking.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence: is this package tenable as stated, and if not, which item is the load-bearing problem. Then the reasoning. No preamble and no restating my list back at me.
