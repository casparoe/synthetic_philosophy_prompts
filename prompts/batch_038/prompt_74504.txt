A friend and I spent three hours last night on a federal regulatory practice, and we could not resolve it. When certain agencies propose a rule touching energy, they publish a single dollar figure per ton of carbon dioxide, representing the present discounted value of the damages that ton is expected to cause over the coming centuries, and they then multiply that figure by the rule's projected emissions reduction and set the product against compliance costs. The number is not observed anywhere; it is computed out of a climate model, a damages function, a discount rate, and an assumption about how much extra weight to give a poor person's welfare relative to a rich person's, and the published value swings by an order of magnitude depending on which of those inputs is chosen, with some administrations publishing a high figure, others a near-zero one, and at least one directing agencies to use no figure at all.

My friend insists this settles the old calculation debate against the impossibility side: the state is plainly setting a price where no market exists and revising it as evidence accumulates, and decisions get made. I insisted the number is parasitic on actual market prices and on surveys of what people say they would pay, and so cannot be the independent calculation it pretends to be. Reconstruct the theory of what a price is, and of where the knowledge in it comes from, that this practice presupposes; say whether my friend's reading is the best one or whether some weaker claim is what the practice actually needs; identify what it silently rules out; say whether its parts are mutually consistent; and tell me what the practice would have to become if its presupposition were false.
