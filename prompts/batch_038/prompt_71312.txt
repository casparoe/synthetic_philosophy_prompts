I am a third-year doctoral student in philosophy, working at the intersection of decision theory and the philosophy of action. Next month I am presenting a paper at our departmental workshop on the normative status of sunk costs. What follows is a draft of the central section — roughly the middle third, where I stop surveying the terrain and make my own positive claim. I have stripped the footnote apparatus for legibility, and I am emphatically not looking for references, a reading list, or pointers to further literature; I want argument-level comments from someone reading the reasoning cold. Please answer in British English.

---

**1.** The standard treatment holds that a rational agent's choice should be governed entirely by the prospective consequences of the acts available to her. Costs already incurred appear in every branch of the decision tree alike; being common to all branches, they cancel, and so they cannot affect which act is preferred. On this picture the disposition to honour sunk costs is a mistake, and the mistake has a name. I want to resist the generality of this verdict — but not by denying the theorem. My claim is that the theorem governs a narrower range of cases than its advocates assume, and that its apparent universality is an artefact of how the cases are written down.

**2.** The distinction I propose is this. Call an agent's response to a sunk cost *narrow* when she treats the past outlay as itself a term in the calculation of which available act yields the better outcome — when she reasons as though the money spent, the years given, or the pain endured adds to or subtracts from expected value. Call it *wide* when she treats the past outlay as bearing not on the value of outcomes but on the description of the act now before her, or on the identity of the project she is inside. The narrow variety is unambiguously an error, and nothing I say here defends it. The wide variety, I shall argue, is not a reasoning error at all, and the standard critique never reaches it.

**3.** First argument, from act description. The claim that sunk costs cancel is a claim about a particular way of formulating a decision problem. Under the description {continue, abandon}, the outlay sits in both branches and drops out of the comparison. But "abandon" is not a self-describing act. Abandoning a venture one has funded and defended for four years is not the same act as declining to fund it at the outset: the two have different objects, different audiences, and different positions in a history that is still being made. Once acts are individuated at this finer grain, the cancellation ceases to look like a discovery about the world and begins to look like a dividend of the coarser description — a description chosen, I suggest, precisely because it makes the past vanish. I do not claim that every description is as admissible as every other. I claim only that the coarse description is not neutral, and that its neutrality has been assumed rather than argued for.

**4.** Second argument, from temporally extended projects. The decisions that matter most to us are rarely one-off selections by a fixed agent with fixed preferences; they are episodes in projects whose continuation depends on the agent being the sort of person who continues them. Here the disposition to treat prior investment as reason-giving is itself part of the situation the decision is about. An agent known to discount her own past commitments is one whose collaborators cannot rely on her — and that, unlike the sunk cost, is a prospective feature of the branches. This is where I concede ground: the reason to persist is prospective, concerning the value of being trustworthy rather than the value of recovering what is gone. What I deny is that the concession collapses the wide category into the narrow one, because a wide reason is indexed to the agent's standing within the project and not to the project's expected yield.

**5.** The obvious objection, which I take seriously. The whole edifice looks like a rationalisation engine. The familiar escalation-of-commitment findings suggest that people who keep funding failing ventures reliably produce exactly the elevated talk of loyalty, integrity and fidelity to a shared history that I have just dignified with a technical label. If the same words come from the agent who is plainly throwing good money after bad, the words cannot be doing the justificatory work I assign to them.

**6.** My reply, in the form of a test I want to defend. Call it the *counterfactual transparency test*. A putative wide reason survives if the agent would still endorse it under two suppositions: (i) that the investment had been made not by her but by someone else occupying her position, and (ii) that the investment were wholly unobservable — so that nobody, including her future self and including any collaborator, could tell whether she had honoured it. A reason that evaporates under (ii) was a concern with appearances or with a flattering self-portrait, and is not a wide reason at all. A reason that evaporates under (i) is special pleading on behalf of one's own past. I concede that neither supposition is easy to reason under, and that a test which is hard to apply is a weaker test than I would like.

**7.** A second test, which I raise only to reject. It is sometimes said that sunk-cost irrationality can be exposed by the reversal question: would you, if you had invested nothing so far, invest this much now? As a diagnostic for the narrow case it is excellent. As a test of the wide case it misfires, because in the paradigm instances of wide reasoning — a marriage, a vocation, a movement one joined in one's twenties — the question "would you enter this now, from a standing start?" is simply not the question that governs whether to go on, and answering it negatively does not thereby answer the practical question at all.

**8.** Conclusion. Responsiveness to sunk costs is not one phenomenon with one normative status; it partitions. The partition runs between treating the past as a term in an expected-value calculation, which is always an error, and treating the past as fixing which act one is now performing or which project one is now inside, which sometimes is not. The confidence we rightly have in the first proposition has been borrowed, without warrant, to establish the second.

---

My requests for comment, in order of priority:

1. **The redescription worry.** The most damaging objection I can construct against myself is that (2) is not a distinction but a relabelling: any reason I call "wide", once stated with full precision, turns out to be a prospective consideration — about reliability, about future standing, about the value of a completed project — and so was already inside the standard model, which was never committed to a coarse act description in any case. Please press this as hard as you can, and tell me specifically whether the concession in (4) gives the game away. If the distinction is doing no independent work, say so bluntly; that is the single most useful thing you could tell me.

2. **The status of (3).** Is the act-description argument substantive, or is it an instance of the general point that decision problems are description-sensitive, which therefore proves nothing about sunk costs in particular? Do you think I need a positive criterion for which act descriptions are admissible, and could such a criterion be stated without begging the question against my opponent?

3. **The test in (6).** Is it really a test, or does it encode its own verdict? In particular, would an opponent fairly respond that any reason surviving (ii) is by construction a reason about the agent's own character rather than about the project, and hence still not a reason *about sunk costs*? I would also like to know whether (i) and (ii) do distinct work, or whether one would carry the whole load.

4. **On interest rather than correctness.** Does (7) earn its place? The claim that the reversal question misfires in certain projects strikes me as the most suggestive thing in the draft and also the least developed, and I cannot yet tell whether it is a genuine insight or a rhetorical flourish. If it is worth keeping, tell me what would have to be added to make it an argument.

5. **Overstatement and unshared intuitions.** Please identify any paragraph where I overclaim, and any point where the argument depends on an intuition that a reader who lacks it will not be moved by. I would much rather hear this now than at the workshop.

6. **Terminology.** The labels in (2) and (6) are my own coinages. If you think either is misleading, or liable to be confused with something already in circulation, propose a better one and explain what the replacement gets right.

A response of somewhere between 900 and 1,400 words would be ideal. Please key your comments to the paragraph numbers, and close with a two- or three-sentence verdict on whether the overall thesis of the draft — the partition asserted in (8) — can be defended at all.
