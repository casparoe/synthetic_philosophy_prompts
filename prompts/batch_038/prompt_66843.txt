I am applying for a twelve-month residential fellowship at an institute devoted to the study of collective behaviour. The selection committee is deliberately mixed: two philosophers, two behavioural ecologists, a statistical physicist who works on swarming, and an engineer who builds multi-robot systems. The proposal must run to about two pages and must survive all six of them.

My half-formed project concerns Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary — the ant colony that holds up its end of a conversation with Achilles and the Tortoise, where everything the colony contributes is produced by the movements of thousands of ants, not one of which is doing anything that could be described as conversing. Four decades on, the philosophical debate that this image provoked has settled into a predictable shape: one side runs down a checklist (does the colony integrate information, store memories, learn, act as a unified agent?) and the other replies that the checklist is either not jointly satisfiable or is satisfied only in a loose, derivative sense that amounts to metaphor.

My working thesis is that both sides are stuck on the wrong question. The obstacle is not integration but individuation. Before we can ask whether a colony has a mind, we have to say which system the predicate would be true of, and in the myrmecological case the boundaries are not fixed by anything: workers turn over on the scale of weeks to months; many species are polydomous, with one colony spread across several nests; some Argentine ant populations form supercolonies spanning thousands of kilometres with no aggression between distant nests; and colonies routinely bud, split, or merge. The same colony-level capacities can therefore be attributed to systems of wildly different extent depending on where the line is drawn, and the checklist debate silently assumes a boundary that nobody has argued for.

What I want to develop is a criterion I am calling interventional adequacy: a candidate boundary marks a genuine subject of mental predicates to the extent that interventions and predictions framed in terms of that boundary outperform both finer-grained and coarser-grained ones, and remain stable when the parts inside the boundary are perturbed, removed, or replaced. On this account boundaries are not discovered by metaphysical argument but earned by comparative performance, and mental attribution is boundary-relative without being arbitrary or merely conventional. There is a second strand I want the proposal to carry, and it is the one I find most promising: fission and fusion, which function in the personal identity literature as admittedly imaginary cases that critics dismiss as science fiction, are ordinary observable events in ant biology. The colony case may therefore be able to do something that thought experiments cannot — supply a naturalised testbed for reductionist accounts of what makes a subject the same subject over time, with results that run back toward the human case rather than only away from it.

Please turn this into the proposal. I need the following:

1. A precise statement of the question — one with a determinate answer, not a restatement of the slogan about whether colonies can have minds.
2. An account of why the existing positions leave it open: the checklist approach, the deflationary reply that colony talk is metaphor, strong-emergence treatments of levels of description, information-integration style measures of collective mentality, and group-agent or extended-mind accounts. Describe these positions and their arguments in your own terms.
3. The method, month by month across the twelve months: what I would read, what conceptual machinery I would build, which concrete boundary cases I would test the criterion against (single nest, polydomous colony, supercolony, a colony mid-fission, a merged pair), and what a fellow with no guaranteed laboratory access actually does with this material.
4. Why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives — in particular, why not simply adopt an existing information-theoretic measure of collective processing, and why not accept the biologist's standard superorganism criterion as settling the boundary question.
5. What a result would look like, and what would count as failure. Address at least the two failure modes that worry me most: that interventional adequacy becomes trivially satisfiable, since any boundary whatever will outperform its neighbours on some measure chosen after the fact; and that biology has already answered the boundary question, leaving the philosophical work with nothing to do.
6. A short paragraph in plain language, addressed to the physicist and the engineer, explaining why this is not an empirical measurement problem wearing a philosophical coat.
7. An honest statement of scope: what fits in one year for one person, what I should cut, and where the proposal should hedge rather than overclaim.

Please sharpen the thesis rather than summarise it back to me, and close with the two or three points at which you think it is most vulnerable — I would rather hear them now than from the committee. I will supply all citations myself, so include no bibliography and no literature review; and do not invent studies, data sets, or attributions to named researchers. Around a thousand words, in prose, with headings only where they earn their place.
