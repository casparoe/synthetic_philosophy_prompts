I am in my third year of a philosophy of science PhD, with a background in archaeological method, and I am applying for a two-year dissertation fellowship. My pitch is on the concept of societal collapse: whether the long-running dispute between those who think societies genuinely fail and those who think they merely reorganise is a substantive disagreement at all, or an artefact of an unexamined concept doing too much work. I do not want a proposal that surveys that literature. Everyone knows the shape of it: a neo-Malthusian account on which a society overshoots its resource base and disintegrates, a Tainter-style account on which complexity is a problem-solving strategy whose marginal returns can fall until it is no longer worth sustaining, and a critique from the other side holding that collapse talk is really a story told by and about elites, and that the people on the ground usually experience reorganisation, relocation, or liberation. The dispute has stalled, I think, because both sides keep trading the same cases — the eastern Mediterranean at the end of the Late Bronze Age, the Classic Maya lowlands, Rapa Nui, Angkor, the Chacoan system, Egypt's First Intermediate Period — and every case can be described in either vocabulary without strain. What I want funded is an attempt to force the question by making both sides operationalise.

My half-formed idea is this. Ask each party to commit to a decision rule: a function from a vector of indicator values (settlement density and hierarchy, the production and distribution of durable inscriptions and administrative records, the reach and intensity of long-distance exchange, the degree of craft specialisation, the scale of coordinated labour, nutritional and demographic proxies from skeletal and archaeological evidence) to a verdict about whether a given society in a given region over a given window collapsed. Then notice three things that I suspect are real and tractable rather than merely verbal. First, the indicators come apart in actual cases: there are regions that lose monumental inscription and elite settlement hierarchy while population, language, and subsistence practice persist or even improve, and regions that shed a central administration while craft production and regional exchange expand. So the question becomes whether "collapse" is one latent quantity measured noisily, in which case the disagreements are empirical and in principle closable, or a family of distinct processes under one word, in which case no amount of new excavation can settle the dispute. Second, the verdict is frame-sensitive in both time and space: some cases look like collapse over fifty years and like relocation over two hundred, and over a thousand years the same indicator vector may simply re-equilibrate; likewise, a region can be scored as collapsed only relative to a unit whose boundaries the archaeologist chose, and choosing the palace-centred polity rather than the watershed or the language community changes the answer. Third, and this is the part I most want pressed, the weighting of indicators is not neutral: an operationalisation that treats inscription density and monumental construction as load-bearing has already built in the identification of a society with its state apparatus, which is precisely what the critics deny, so the operationalisation cannot adjudicate the dispute it was meant to settle. There is also a smaller methodological wrinkle I would like folded in: the indicators differ in temporal resolution, so apparent synchrony between a climatic signal and a social one can be manufactured by comparing a finely resolved proxy against a coarse ceramic chronology, and any decision rule has to say what it does about that rather than quietly averaging across resolutions.

Please turn this into a proposal of roughly two pages. It needs a single research question precise enough to have a determinate answer rather than a mood; an account of why the three positions above leave that question open; the method, which should be something a philosopher can actually do — constructing and comparing candidate decision rules against a fixed shortlist of cases, and asking at each divergence whether some further evidence could in principle resolve it, or whether the divergence is stable under all possible evidence; a statement of what a successful result looks like (I suspect it is a usable rule plus an argument about the concept's structure, but tell me if you think otherwise); an explicit account of what would count as the project failing, including the possibility that every reasonable operationalisation turns out to agree and the whole issue is verbal; and an honest scoping section on what makes this too large for two years and where I should cut. Do not supply a bibliography or a literature review by citation — I will handle the references myself — but anything you assert about the empirical record that I would need to check before submitting should be flagged as such rather than presented as settled. Above all, do not hand me a menu. I want the proposal to take a definite stand on whether collapse is one thing or several, to commit to one weighting of indicators as the one the project will argue for and to say why that weighting is not question-begging, to say which of the three problems above it treats as the crux and which it subordinates, and to name the specific finding that would force it to abandon that stand. Where you are making a judgement call rather than reporting a consensus, say so and give your reason.
