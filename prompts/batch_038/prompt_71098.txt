I am preparing a forty-minute talk for a workshop whose audience divides evenly between people who build dialogue systems and philosophers of language. At rehearsal, a colleague from the computational side offered what he took to be the one picture both halves could share: conversation is a noisy channel, the sentence a speaker produces is the signal, an implicature is the payload the hearer's decoder recovers on top of that signal, Grice's Cooperative Principle is the shared codebook, and context is the prior that settles whatever remains indeterminate. He then drew a conclusion from it — if that is the right picture, speaker meaning is an estimation problem, the apparatus of intentions and cooperation and the hearer's recognition of the speaker's intention is decoration, and whatever a hearer cannot recover by decoding was never implicature at all but only suggestion. A philosopher in my department objects that the analogy is not neutral, since a decoder never has to represent what its encoder intended, but he has not been able to say where exactly it breaks.

Please audit the analogy: state the correspondence explicitly, identify where the mapping fails, separate the failures that genuinely threaten his conclusion from those that are harmless artifacts of comparing two unlike things, name what the communication-engineering picture imports into conversation that conversation lacks, and say whether the conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table. If there is a better analogy for this same mixed audience — one that keeps the computational half engaged without conceding the reductive conclusion — give it, and say what it costs.

Return exactly five numbered items of one or two sentences each, in this order: the mapping; the single failure that matters most; the failures that do not matter; what the analogy smuggles in; and your verdict on the conclusion together with the better analogy if you have one. No preamble and no closing remarks.
