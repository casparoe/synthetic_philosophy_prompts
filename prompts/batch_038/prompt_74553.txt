so I'm putting together a short position paper for a workshop at a staff college — mixed room, some philosophers, mostly officers — and before I hand it in I want to know if my package actually hangs together. Here's what I'm committed to:

(1) jus ad bellum and jus in bello are separate questions: you can be fighting a war you had no right to start and still fight it permissibly;
(2) combatants on both sides have an equal permission to kill enemy combatants, and I mean that as a real moral permission, not a legal fiction we keep around to make postwar trials manageable;
(3) the reason civilians may not be targeted is that they aren't doing the fighting — they don't bear arms;
(4) but I also think what makes killing someone permissible at all is that they're morally responsible for an unjust threat to you or yours;
(5) a 19-year-old conscript, drafted into an army of aggression three weeks ago and given no choice, may be shot by a defender, and the machinist who volunteers at the shell plant for the overtime and has never held a rifle may not be;
(6) the leaders who plan an aggressive war do something very wrong and it doesnt follow that their soldiers do;
(7) the permission in (2) doesn't extend to anyone hors de combat — prisoners, the wounded, someone who's dropped his weapon.

I also want to be able to tell a room of junior officers that a soldier who was lied into a war hasn't thereby become a murderer, which is part of why I'm holding onto (2).

Which of these are genuinely in conflict, and which only look like they are? Rank the real conflicts by severity, and for each one say what would have to go and what it costs me to give it up — I care about the cost to (5) and to the thing I want to say to the officers specifically. And is there a single revision that would clean up the most, or am I stuck holding a package that can't be made coherent at any price?
