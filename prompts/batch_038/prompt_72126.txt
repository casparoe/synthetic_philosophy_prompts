I'm drafting the following for our office, and I'd like you to take the role of a political philosopher who has actually had to make a theory of freedom usable by people who will never read it — someone equally at home with Berlin's two concepts, with Pettit's account of freedom as non-domination, and with the way a city attorney's office reads a checklist. Please think carefully before answering, and work in American English throughout.

The situation: I direct a small policy-analysis unit inside a mid-sized American city government. Two years ago the council passed an ordinance requiring a written impact statement for every proposed regulation, and last spring it added a line item asking us to state, for each proposal, the effect on "the liberty of residents." That phrase is doing no work at all right now. My staff is four analysts, none with philosophy training; we get one to three proposals a week; each statement goes out within two business days; and everything we publish is read aloud at council meetings by members who have already decided what they want the answer to be. I need a procedure they can run, not a seminar.

Here is the part that a two-hour argument with a friend left unresolved, and it is the crux I need you to settle in order to build anything. My friend's position is that non-domination is not a distinct value at all: on her view, to say that someone is dominated is just to say that they face a high enough probability of interference in some future situation, so that any checklist that tracks domination separately is double-counting and should be collapsed into a single probability-weighted assessment of interference. My position is that she is describing the wrong thing — that the republican complaint is about who holds the switch and whether that holder's decisions are constrained and answerable to the person affected, so that two arrangements with identical expected interference can differ in freedom, and that a procedure built on her reduction would systematically miss the case of the unexercised power. Relatedly, she thinks the whole tripartite apparatus is redundant, since the standard triadic analysis — some agent being free from some constraint to do or become something — already absorbs the positive and negative conceptions once you allow that internal incapacity and unavailability of means count as constraints. I do not need you to referee the argument for its own sake. I need you to pick a side, because the procedure has to be built one way or the other.

What I am asking you to produce:

1. A short opening section in which you work out loud: list the candidate tests you considered for whether a power over someone is arbitrary or answerable, note which you rejected and why, and state your verdict on the reduction question above together with what the procedure gives up by being built that way.

2. The procedure itself, as an ordered sequence of questions an analyst can ask of a proposal, with: what counts as a sufficient answer to each question; where the procedure stops and yields a determinate result; where it yields a genuine conflict between the concepts and must report the conflict rather than average it away; and where it hands the decision to a human because the remaining question is one of values or of justice rather than of freedom. It must include an explicit rule for what to do when we have no evidence about a relevant fact — and that rule must not quietly presume that anything unmeasured is free. It must also keep liberty separate from desirability: a proposal can be one our unit favors and still score badly, and the procedure has to be able to say so in public.

3. A one-page output template the analyst fills in, giving a direction and a confidence level under each concept and a one-line rationale, in language a council member can follow.

4. The procedure run on three specific proposals, in enough detail that I can see it actually functioning rather than being described:
   - Replacing our discretionary special-event permit system, in which the police chief may deny a permit for unstated reasons with no appeal, with a rule-bound system using published criteria and a hearing officer, but imposing a hard cap of one event per block per weekend.
   - A city apprenticeship and guaranteed-placement program for residents aged eighteen to twenty-four, which a matched applicant may not decline: refusing a match forfeits eligibility for three years.
   - A tenant-protection ordinance creating a rent board whose members serve at the council's pleasure and may grant case-by-case variances with no written standards and no route of appeal. I favor this ordinance on grounds of justice, and I want the run on it to be honest rather than flattering.

5. A closing section stating what the procedure cannot do: the classes of proposal it will misjudge, the steps that are judgment calls wearing the costume of procedure, and the failure mode that would make me want to redesign it.

On tone and commitment: I want a definite design, not a menu. Where the theory is contested — above all on the reduction question, and on whether positive liberty needs a threshold of usable options rather than formally available ones, the case of the road that leads nowhere — choose, build, and give your reasons, and say what your choice costs you. Flag explicitly the two or three places where a design decision is doing substantive philosophical work while looking like an administrative convenience. No bibliography and no reading list; I care about the reasoning, not the references. Assume we already know the definitions, and skip any preamble about what freedom might mean. Aim for something whose core an analyst could keep on a single laminated page, with the reasoning in an appendix.
