I run a small podcast on the philosophy of cognitive science; the listeners are mostly working software engineers who have skimmed some Dennett and a scattering of graduate students who have not skimmed. I first met the four-tier taxonomy — Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, Gregorian creatures — years ago in the rationalist blogosphere of the late 2000s, where it was usually deployed to make a point I still think is worth making: a mind is not one mechanism but a stack of selection processes running at different locations, and asking what kind of mind something has means asking at which of those locations the bad options get thrown away. Next month's guest is a philosopher of cognitive science who accepts that framing and pushes it considerably further than I have seen it pushed. Two of her claims matter for my purposes. First, she reads the tiers as a scale over where the cost of being wrong is absorbed: for a Darwinian creature the unfit design is culled from the population; for a Skinnerian creature a response tendency is extinguished; for a Popperian creature an internally represented option is vetoed before the body commits to it; for a Gregorian creature an external tool, notation, or technique is abandoned. What individuates the tiers, on her reading, is not what is inside the skull but what kind of thing gets eliminated when the system turns out to be wrong. Second, and more provocatively, she holds that this severs Gregorian creaturehood from Popperian creaturehood: since what matters is that the coupled system discards artifacts rather than organisms, a system can count as Gregorian even when it is unclear whether it models its options internally at all — which is how she wants to classify present-day LLM-based agents equipped with retrieval, tool use, and human-in-the-loop revision.

I need seven or eight questions for a fifty-minute recording. My co-host is a computational neuroscientist who regards the whole taxonomy as unfalsifiable dressing and has promised to complain if we come away without anything a lab could test; the guest, for her part, characteristically answers pressure by redescribing her framework at a higher level of abstraction rather than committing to a case, so every follow-up should be one that redescription cannot discharge — it has to force a claim about some particular system. Please include at least the following. A question on whether "eliminated" means the same thing at all four tiers or is a metaphor stretched four ways, and what she says to the charge that if the criterion is merely that selection happens somewhere, the taxonomy dissolves into the units-of-selection debate by another name. A question on the nesting, seeded by the case I keep returning to: a patient with dense anterograde amnesia who nonetheless retains intact procedural learning and manages a competent life by means of a phone calendar and a notebook system — Gregorian, it looks, without being Popperian — which I want her either to claim for her view or to explain away, together with whether the severing she proposes is local to that one joint or general, since if it is general she no longer has a hierarchy but a list. A question on what the creature is, exactly — the model, the model plus its toolchain, or the human-model dyad — and what becomes of a set of deployed weights sitting in a datacenter with no users attached. A question on whether the training process re-imports Darwinian and Skinnerian selection one level above the artifact, and whether a single system is therefore permitted to occupy different tiers of its own organization. And a closing question on whether a system that authors its own scaffolding is a fifth kind of creature or simply Gregorian creaturehood with the toolmaker relocated inside the loop.

For each question, give me the wording as I would actually say it aloud, the answer you expect her to give, and a follow-up keyed to that specific answer rather than to the topic. Keep them answerable live: no question may depend on her having read anything I have read, and none may require me to quote a page number at her. Order them so that the interview opens on the most sympathetic version of her position and ends on the one she cannot talk her way around. And please no questions about her intellectual formation or what drew her to the subject — she tells the same anecdote every time, and my audience has heard it from other guests.
