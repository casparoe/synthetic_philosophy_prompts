I've been reading a book about machine ethics -- not an academic one, more of a smart popular thing by someone who works on AI alignment -- and there's a section that leans hard on Bernard Williams's internal/external reasons paper to make a point about whether you can give an artificial agent moral reasons at all. The section runs roughly like this:

"Williams starts from a simple observation: a reason has to be someone's reason, so it has to hook onto what that person is actually moved by. He calls this your subjective motivational set, which is basically the list of things you want. On his view, 'A has a reason to φ' is true only if φ-ing would satisfy one of the items on that list. External reasons -- the ones supposed to apply to you whether or not you care about anything they hook onto -- therefore can't exist: if there's nothing in your set for the action to serve, the claim that you have the reason is simply false, and the person insisting on it isn't really asserting anything, they're just scolding you. It also follows that if someone says they ought to give to charity but feels no pull toward it, either they're mistaken about their own desires or they don't really think they ought to. Williams himself draws the consequence that the amoralist is beyond rational criticism -- there's no argument that can reach him. And this generalizes cleanly to machines: you can't give an artificial agent moral reasons by writing norms into it, because reasons aren't the kind of thing that can be installed. Alignment has to be desire engineering."

Two reactions and I can't tell which one is right. Part of me thinks this is just Williams -- I'd always mentally filed him under Humean-about-motivation anyway. Another part of me remembers someone saying Williams really didn't like that label, and that the amoralist business is a distortion, but I don't know enough to say what's actually being garbled.

So walk me through the passage line by line and tell me what a Williams scholar would nod at, what they'd call a fair simplification, and what they'd say is flat wrong. Specifically I want to know (a) whether the "list of things you want" gloss survives contact with what Williams says the set actually contains and how it's supposed to get consulted -- like does an agent's current wants really settle it once you allow for false beliefs and imagination; (b) whether the paper is even in the business of talking about people who judge they ought to do something, because that charity sentence seems to slide from one claim to a different one; and (c) whether "the amoralist is beyond rational criticism" is Williams's conclusion or the author's, and what Williams thinks is left over by way of criticizing such a person. Then tell me whether the AI inference survives the corrections -- does anything true in the passage actually get you to "you have to build in the desires," or is the author just borrowing Williams's authority for a claim about engineering that the paper doesn't touch? I'd rather have an honest audit than a defense of the book.
