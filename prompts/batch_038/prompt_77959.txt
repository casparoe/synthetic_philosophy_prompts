I'm a software engineer without any formal training in philosophy, so please bear with the imprecision below. I recently read an excerpt, posted online by someone else, of a long essay Bentham wrote arguing that sexual acts between consenting adults of the same sex should not be punished. I have lost the link and never learned the title; I gather it was not published until long after his death. What has stayed with me is not the conclusion but a piece of the argumentative machinery, and I have not been able to find a name for it.

As I read him, Bentham works through one by one the harms such acts might cause, on the principle that harm is the only thing that could justify punishment. When he reaches the harm consisting in other people's distress — the revulsion or alarm that bystanders feel at the mere thought of the act — he grants that this distress is real pain and that his own calculus therefore has to count it. But he also observes that the revulsion is not a fact of nature: it has been cultivated by generations of preaching and of punishment, so that a large part of the weight now sitting on the scale against the act was placed there by the laws whose continuation that weight is being used to justify. If my paraphrase distorts him, say so; the structural question stands either way.

I keep seeing the same shape elsewhere. A company forbids employees to discuss their salaries, on the ground that such talk breeds resentment and distrust; the resentment is documented in engagement surveys; but it seems to arise mostly from the secrecy itself and the suspicion of unfairness that the secrecy sustains. And at my own workplace, changes to the payments service require sign-off from a small group of senior engineers, justified by the observation that incidents in that service are worse than average — yet because the review queue takes days, people route around it, hiding payments logic in other services or duplicating it, so the incidents get worse and are read back as confirming that the gate is necessary.

Is there an established concept for this? I know people say such arguments are circular, and I have heard the phrase about a prophecy that fulfills itself, but neither seems to be quite it: the harm in these cases is a genuine, measurable fact rather than a premise being smuggled in, and the loop runs through the world rather than through the inference. So I want the carving, not just a label. What exactly is the phenomenon; which established concepts come closest to it, and how does it differ from each of them; and if nothing in the literature names it, what would you call it, defined so that it excludes a rule that simply fails to reduce a harm that exists independently of it, and excludes a rule that creates some harm other than the one it cites? I would also like to know whether the loop is fatal or absorbable — can a proponent of the prohibition concede the origin of the harm and still defend it?

I am not asking for a bibliography; I have no way to chase one down. But please tell me how confident you are in the identifications and distinctions you offer, and single out the one point you are least sure of. In particular, if you are not certain that a term is genuinely established rather than merely plausible, say that plainly instead of supplying one — I would rather be told the field has no word for this than be given a word I cannot check.
