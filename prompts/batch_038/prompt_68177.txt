I work as a research associate at a small policy institute. Our current project is a public brief arguing that famines should be treated as matters of political and legal accountability rather than as natural catastrophes, and the argumentative spine of the draft rests on two slogans that circulate constantly in this field. The first is that famines are easy to prevent. The second is that famines are about the distribution of food rather than its supply — closely associated with Amartya Sen's entitlement analysis — together with the stronger claim usually attached to it, that no famine has ever occurred in a functioning democracy with a relatively free press.

A senior colleague reviewed the draft and objected that we are doing a great deal of work with a great deal of vagueness. He says the first slogan is either trivial or plainly false depending on what "easy" and "prevent" are taken to mean, and that the second is undercut by the very cases we rely on: the Chinese famine of 1959–1961 involved a substantial fall in grain output alongside the procurement quotas and export decisions we emphasize, and Bengal in 1943 involved war-related disruption to harvests and transport, not merely a collapse in some people's ability to command food. I cannot tell whether he has landed a real blow or has simply fastened onto the weakest available reading of our claims.

What I need is a precise dissection rather than a defense. Take each slogan in turn and lay out the distinct claims it could be making — a menu of readings, running from those that are bare platitudes, through those that are substantive and defensible, to those that are false or at least seriously contested. For the prevention slogan, be explicit about what varies across readings: easy for whom; easy in which respect (technical, logistical, fiscal, informational, political); easy relative to what baseline of comparison; and preventing what exactly, since averting a formally declared famine and averting the excess mortality are different achievements. For the distribution slogan, be explicit about what it denies — that aggregate food availability matters at all, or only that it is sufficient on its own to explain who starves — and about whether the entitlement framework is offered as a causal theory or as a description of the mechanism by which particular groups lose access to food, since those are not the same commitment. For the democracy corollary, say whether it is best read as a causal mechanism, as a correlation with obvious confounders (wealth, colonial status, integration into trade networks), or as something closer to a claim about what a government facing electoral punishment and an uncensored press can get away with; and say how much of our brief survives if only the weakest of those readings is true.

Then tell me, and this is the part that matters most for my purposes, which readings — and only which — would actually license the conclusion the brief needs: that the deaths issued from decisions that could reasonably have been made otherwise, and so from culpable agency rather than from weather. Flag any place where one of these slogans, deployed as we have deployed it, tends to obscure something the brief would do better to say directly. I am not looking for references or a reading list; I want the analysis, and I would rather be given two or three points at which our framing is actively misleading than a charitable survey that leaves the draft standing as it is.
