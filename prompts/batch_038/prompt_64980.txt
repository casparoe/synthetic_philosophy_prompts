The passage that interests me runs as follows:

"The economist Thomas Schelling, who won the Nobel Prize for applying game theory to conflict, transformed our understanding of the nuclear age. His starting point was the logic of the second strike: if both sides can absorb a first strike and still destroy the other, neither has any incentive to strike first, and the balance holds by itself. What remains, then, is not war but bargaining, and Schelling's great contribution was to show that deterrence and compellence are two faces of a single mechanism — a communicated threat of harm meant to make an adversary behave differently than it otherwise would — so that one set of rules about credibility governs both alike. Those rules are demanding. A threat works only if the adversary is convinced you would actually carry it out, which in the nuclear case is impossible, since no rational leader would trade his own cities for a defeated ally or a humiliated client. Schelling's answer was what he called the rationality of irrationality: if you can persuade an opponent that your judgment is not entirely under your control, that you might respond out of pride, panic, or domestic fury regardless of the cost to yourself, your threats acquire a credibility they could never otherwise have, and you hold an advantage in every crisis you enter. This is what brinkmanship means, the deliberate surrender of one's own freedom of action. Like a driver who flings his steering wheel out of the window where the other driver can see it, the statesman demonstrates that he can no longer swerve, and the other swerves for him. The Cuban Missile Crisis is the paradigm case, in which Kennedy's blockade deterred Khrushchev from putting missiles into Cuba. The same framework explains why the superpowers fought small wars while avoiding a large one: once mutual vulnerability makes general war unwinnable, both sides are relatively safe to compete violently at the margins, a pattern known as the stability–instability paradox. The practical lesson is bracing but clear. A state whose retaliatory forces cannot be disarmed has nothing to fear and should therefore bargain hard, because in a contest of this kind the side that cares more about the object in dispute, and is more visibly prepared to accept catastrophe, is the side that prevails."
