I cover science and risk stuff for a general-interest magazine and a huge chunk of my job is people giving me ranges instead of numbers. "Somewhere between 20 and 60 percent." "Low single digits, maybe up to 10." "Depends on the model, call it 3 to 30 times baseline." My editor wants one figure in the lede basically every time, and I've been quietly averaging things I don't think should be averaged.

There's a whole formal literature on belief that's a set or an interval rather than a single number -- robust Bayes, imprecise probability, whatever the current name is -- and I want to actually use it, not cite it. So: write me a procedure I can run in like 20 minutes before filing, no equations passed what a smart non-specialist reader will follow, on a laptop in a newsroom with no statistician on call and a print deadline behind me.

In order, it should cover:

- what to actually extract from a source when they hand me a range. There's more than one thing hiding inside "20 to 60 percent" -- one model's output, several models I'm lumping together, or genuine ignorance about which world I'm in -- and I suspect the answer changes what I'm allowed to do with it.
- when it's legitimate to collapse a range to a point for the copy and when it isn't, and what to write in the sentence instead.
- two sources, two ranges: what combining them can even mean, and the conditions under which "experts say between X and Y" is simply false.
- the thing a statistician told me that I never resolved: conditioning on new evidence can make the interval wider rather than narrower, sometimes no matter which outcome you learn. Tell me whether that's a bug in her example or a real feature of the math, and whether my procedure needs a step for it.
- how to choose which end of a range to write down when a decision hangs on it (go pessimistic? some weighted compromise?) and exactly where that stops being math and starts being an editorial values judgment I should flag to readers rather than bury.

Then run it on three cases:

1. Two epidemiologists on how likely a new variant is to substantially evade existing immunity. One says 30-60 percent, the other 45-80. Desk wants "around 50 percent" in the first graph.
2. One expert puts a drug at 20-80 percent chance of working. A week later a small favorable trial lands and she revises to 10-95, saying she's now more sure of the direction but less sure of the size.
3. A source gives me a flat "60 percent" and then admits on the record that he made it up as a placeholder for "more likely than not."

One of these three should come out in a way that will annoy my editor -- I'd rather hear it from you now than from her. And be explicit about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure, which distinctions from the formal theory you deliberately dropped to keep this usable, and what the procedure flat out cannot settle.
