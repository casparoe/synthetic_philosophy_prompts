I recently joined a consciousness lab as a research assistant, and at last week's journal club the discussion of the color phi argument — a red dot and then a green dot flashed in quick succession at nearby locations, with subjects reporting that they saw the color change somewhere along an apparent path between them — turned into a standoff between two senior members. Marta held that the setup cannot decide between the two accounts of what happened: the Orwellian one, on which the subject really does experience the red dot and the record is edited afterward, and the Stalinesque one, on which the second dot arrives in time to settle the content of experience before there is anything to edit. Her reason is that both accounts entail the very same verbal report, so whatever we collect is evidence about reports rather than about the revising of experience.

Dev disagreed, and five moves followed. (1) He said the accounts differ in when content is settled, and that should leave a trace: under the Stalinesque story settling must wait for the second dot, so awareness ought to lag on change trials in a way a probe-flash detection task could pick up. (2) Marta answered that a probe response is itself just another report, so the design presumes a datable moment of experience to catch — precisely what is in dispute. (3) Dev proposed an unannounced second-chance report on the first dot's color: contamination there would show the first dot had been experienced and then overwritten. (4) Marta said contamination is equally expected under the Stalinesque account, since the red transient was processed even though never conscious, so no behavioral residue belongs to either story alone. (5) Dev's last move was that postdiction — later input reshaping the representation of an earlier one — is already accepted in other motion illusions, making the Stalinesque account the default rather than the exotic option; Marta called this a category error, since postdiction in perceptual processing says nothing about postdiction in experience.

Could you go through the five moves and say who is right at each, then say who is more right overall and what, if anything, both of them are missing?
