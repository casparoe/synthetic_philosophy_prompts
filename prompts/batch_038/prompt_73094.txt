In three weeks I present a futarchy pilot proposal at a governance-innovation workshop. The room is small and specific: two political theorists, one a deliberative democrat who thinks legitimacy comes from the exchange of reasons and one who works on delegation to expert bodies; a market microstructure economist who studies thin markets and informed-trader manipulation; a public choice scholar; and a county budget officer who has actually run performance-based budgeting and been burned by it. Nobody there is a prediction-market enthusiast, and nobody is hostile on principle. They are practical.

The proposal is deliberately narrow, and I want the objections aimed at it rather than at futarchy in general. A county board fixes one scalar target — say a two-year composite measure of a program's outcomes — drafted and then frozen by a standing panel before any contract opens, with no renegotiation afterward. For each pair of candidate policies, a market pays out on the frozen target. Three guardrails: a no-trade default, so that if volume and price separation never reach a set threshold the status quo policy simply stands; a manipulation bond, so large positions are collateralized and, if the target is later found to have been moved by the trading itself, the payout is voided and the collateral forfeited; and a priced veto, so elected officials may still overrule the market but the estimated cost of overruling is charged visibly against the general fund. My pitch is that these guardrails convert the familiar objections into engineering problems with answers.

Give me the Q&A prep sheet. Eight or so objections in descending order of how much damage each would do in this room, each in the idiom of the person most likely to raise it — the microstructure economist's version of the manipulation problem and the deliberative democrat's version of the legitimacy problem should come out as different objections, not the same one twice. For each, a reply I can deliver in under two minutes and an honest note on where that reply is thin. Then tell me what to concede in the opening five minutes so I don't have to concede it later, which single reply has to land for the pilot to survive the room, and the question I am hoping nobody asks. Finally, state how confident you are in your read of this audience and its priorities, and single out the point in your own prep sheet where you are least sure of yourself.
