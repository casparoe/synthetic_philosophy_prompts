I'm putting together a discussion text for a small comparative-political-theory reading group and I want a dialogue on the question of which of the two Scandinavian neighbours, Sweden or Norway, is actually the more liberal — and, more to the point, on what would settle that question if anyone asked it seriously.

Set it between two people who know each other well and are both sharp: let's say a Norwegian political philosopher and a Swedish legal theorist stuck on the train between Oslo and Stockholm, or over a long dinner. One has to land on Norway, the other on Sweden, and neither of them is allowed to be the slow half of the conversation. I want genuine back-and-forth — every line reacting to what was just said — not two mini-lectures politely taking turns.

Give them at least four concrete things to fight about, and don't let them drift off into generalties:

- Norway staying out of the EU after two referendums but sitting inside the EEA, so it implements a big pile of Brussels rules it had no vote on, versus Sweden as a full member that votes and then has to live with the outcome. That one should cut both ways and one of them should say so out loud.
- Taxing wealth and inheritance: Norway still levies a wealth tax, Sweden got rid of both its wealth tax and its inheritance tax. Does the Norwegian tax make Norway less liberal, or does the Swedish repeal make Sweden less liberal by locking in dynastic money? Let them disagree about which framing is even the right one.
- Access to land: Norway put the right to roam into statute back in the fifties, Sweden's allemansrätten is still mostly unwritten custom. Does writing a liberty down protect it, or does it fence in something that used to be broader?
- The route the populist right took to influence: a party of that family actually sat in cabinet in Oslo and held the immigration portfolio, whereas the comparable party in Stockholm props up a government from the outside without holding ministries. Which of those is worse for liberalism, and is that the right question to be asking?

Then the part I really care about: the fight about method. Have one of them propose an explicit test — a criterion that would decide the matter if we could apply it cleanly — and have the other show that the test either smuggles in the verdict it's supposed to deliver or can't actually be applied to two countries this similar. Then a counter-test from the other side, and the same treatment in return. Along the way they should notice that they keep sliding between at least three senses of "liberal": absence of state coercion, constitutional limits on what a majority can do, and the range of lives a person can realistically live. At least once, one of them should catch the other switching senses in order to win a point, and the switcher should either own it or defend the switch.

Two ground rules. No invented statistics or index scores — if they want to appeal to the freedom indices, have them observe that the indices disagree and that the gap between these two is inside the noise, rather than quoting a rank. And no verbatim quotes from constitutions, statutes or judgments; describe what a provision does instead.

Roughly 1400 words. Don't let them reach a verdict. One of them should concede something real in the middle and explain why it still doesn't settle anything — make the concession feel earned, not diplomatic. End with each saying what would genuinely change their mind, plus a short, slightly grudging diagnosis from one of them about what the whole argument has really been about.
