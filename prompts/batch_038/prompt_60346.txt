I am in the second term of a graduate sequence on decision and game theory, and our section leader has a stock line he reaches for whenever someone questions a solution procedure: "never play a dominated strategy." He used it three times in five minutes last week, and each use struck me as a different claim, but I could not say how they differed and he dismissed my hesitation as pedantry. A friend in the cohort agrees with him, so I would like to arrive at the next session with the distinctions already written down. The occasion was this fragment of a larger game, where the payoffs shown are the row player's and Column plays L or R:

```
        L    R
   U    3    0
   M    0    3
   D    1    1
```

He declared D eliminated, on the grounds that the fifty–fifty mixture of U and M yields 1.5 against either column and therefore strictly dominates D. I objected that neither U nor M dominates D on its own, and that a mixture is not something anyone literally plays — surely if Column could watch the coin land, the comparison would not go through — and he replied that the objection was confused: dominance is dominance, the order in which you delete is irrelevant, and the whole procedure presupposes nothing beyond the rationality of the players, no equilibrium concept and no beliefs about beliefs.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than as a defence of your favourite reading, the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making? For each candidate reading I would like the claim stated exactly, with the parameters that are usually left implicit fixed explicitly: whether dominance is strict or weak; whether the dominator may be a pure strategy only, a mixture, or something more general such as a correlated plan; whether a strategy counts as dominated in the game as originally specified or in the reduced game left after earlier deletions; and whether the entries are being read as an ordinal ranking or as von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities over lotteries, since I suspect the appeal to a mixture quietly requires the latter. Attach to each reading a verdict — platitude, substantive but defensible, false, or genuinely contested among theorists — and say which readings, if any, actually license the three moves he made: discarding D by appeal to a mixture, asserting order-independence as a general property of the deletion procedure, and claiming that the procedure rests on rationality alone.

I am most interested in the second and third moves, and in two questions I could not resolve on my own. First, is order-independence a property of iterated strict deletion only, and what specifically goes wrong when weakly dominated strategies are admitted — can you indicate the kind of example in which different deletion orders leave different survivors? Second, is there a sense in which eliminating strictly dominated strategies coincides with eliminating strategies that are never a best response to any belief about the opponents' play, and does that coincidence depend on whether the beliefs in question are permitted to be correlated across opponents rather than independent? I would also like to know whether my own objection about the observable coin has any force at all, and whether the answer changes if a mixed strategy is read not as a deliberate randomization but as an outside observer's description of her uncertainty about what the player will do. Please give me the reasoning and, where a reading fails, a concrete counterexample rather than an appeal to authority: I am not after a bibliography, and if a claim's status is disputed I would simply like you to say so plainly. Finally, if some of these readings turn out to be ones my section leader could not consistently have meant given everything else he asserted in the same breath, point that out as well.
