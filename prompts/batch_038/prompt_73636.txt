For thirty-seven years I was a statistical mechanician, and I retired three years ago; philosophy has since become my principal occupation, and I have been working through the philosophy of probability largely on my own. I want to understand a conceptual difficulty concerning the Principal Principle that I have not managed to resolve. I should note at the outset that I have no means of consulting sources, so please do not direct me to readings, and no bibliography is wanted — I need the reasoning laid out, not the state of play surveyed.

Here is the difficulty. I arrived at the Principal Principle with a physicist's habits of thought. Chance struck me as a physical magnitude, on a par with mass or viscosity: a quantity a system possesses, which measurement can estimate and which theory can predict. On that reading the Principle looks like a simple norm of tracking — an agent's degree of belief in an outcome should be set to the magnitude she takes the system to possess, just as her belief about a body's mass should track her best measurement of it. Two claims I have since encountered sit badly with that reading, and I cannot see how to fit them together.

First, the claim that the Principle is not a rule of updating at all, but a constraint on the prior credence function, so that an agent who begins in perfect deference can, by entirely ordinary conditionalization on subsequent evidence, end up out of deference. Please explain what sense this makes. If conditionalizing on new evidence can break the relation the Principle demands, then either the Principle is not a norm for a continuing agent, or it must be read as governing conditional chances rather than unconditional ones. I would like to see why the second move is the natural one, and precisely what it costs.

Second, the claim that the difficulty becomes acute if chance is held to supervene on the total pattern of particular events. On such a view a chance hypothesis is itself partly a claim about how the actual sequence of outcomes goes, and so evidence about outcomes is evidence about the hypothesis. Please make this concrete with a small worked example and explicit arithmetic: a sequence of trials under a chance hypothesis assigning, say, 0.4 to the outcome of interest; an actual run that comes out strongly the other way; and a further trial whose probability I am trying to assess. Walk through what the Principle demands at each stage, what conditionalization delivers, and exactly where the two come apart. Then tell me which premise is doing the damage — the deference norm, the supervenience thesis, the unrestricted application of conditionalization, or the assumption that all evidence about outcomes is admissible — and whether removing it genuinely repairs the situation or merely empties the norm of content.

Interwoven with this is a claim of my own that I suspect is mistaken and would like corrected. I reasoned as follows: if chances are nothing over and above summaries of actual frequencies, and if my credence concerning a future trial should equal my expectation of that frequency, then the Principal Principle adds nothing to ordinary conditionalization — it restates what a rational agent does in any case. I have been assured of the opposite: that the Principle is what makes chance the sort of thing it is, and that without a deference norm the Humean summary would be inert bookkeeping. Please adjudicate between these two pictures and say which is right, and why.

Finally, please set out the principal strategies of repair — restricting deference to conditional chances, denying the supervenience thesis, tightening the notion of admissible evidence, weakening the norm to something approximate or asymptotic — and assess each against a standard I care about: does the repaired norm still tell an agent what to do, or has it been rendered true at the price of being unusable? Close by telling me directly where my mass analogy misleads, or, if you think it does not, where my reasoning above first went astray. Please keep the notation light and the exposition self-contained.
