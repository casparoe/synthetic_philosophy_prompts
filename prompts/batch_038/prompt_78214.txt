Last month I sat on a grant panel — nine of us, three slots, twelve applications — and two of the other panelists got into a heated arguement in the parking lot afterward that I've been trying to settle in my head ever since. I want to use it as a case study in my graduate seminar, and before I hand it out I'd like an independent adjudication. I'm deliberately not telling you which of them I leaned toward, so please don't try to read me out of the writeup.

Background both accepted: the chair had run a rough back-test over earlier rounds and claimed that each member, judging alone, had flagged about 70% of the proposals that later turned out to be the ones the field rated highest. Neither of them wanted to dig into what "turned out to be" was doing there. I think that may matter, but I'll keep my mouth shut and let you find it.

The exchange, as best I can reconstruct it. D is a statistician, R is a philosopher of science from another department.

(1) D: Given the 70% figure and independent votes, the panel majority converges on the right answer as the panel grows, so the majority beats any individual member including the most expert one. Therefore we should never simply defer to the senior person, however eminent.

(2) R: That needs independence. We all read the same two external reports and then argued for three hours. Our errors are correlated, and with correlated votes a majority can be worse than the single best voter — if we share a blind spot, nine of us just register the same blind spot nine times and it looks like a landslide.

(3) D: Correlation doesn't automatically sink it. What matters is whether average competence is still above a half, and whether the correlation is so extreme that the panel collapses into one effective voter. Moderate correlation among nine still leaves you better than a coin, and probably better than a random single member.

(4) R: "Better than a random single member" is a retreat from (1). And competence-above-a-half isn't a property of a person, it's a property of a person plus a distribution of questions. The reason one of us misjudges an unusual proposal — unfashionable topic, non-standard methods — is the same reason all of us do, because we were selected from the same specialty as the proposals. The correlation isn't a technical wrinkle, it's built into how the panel was constituted.

(5) D: Then what's your alternative? Deferring to the senior expert is n=1 drawn from the same correlated pool, with no error-averaging at all and one unchecked idiosyncratic bias. If common-mode error is what frightens you, a single expert is more exposed to it than nine are. Besides, you can't identify ex ante who the best member is; any procedure for picking them is another judgment with the identical problem.

(6) R: My alternative isn't deference, it's that the panel's value was never aggregation. The value was the three hours of reason-exchange — hearing why you thought the methods were broken actually updated me. Deliberation raises each member's competence, so aggregation is the wrong model, and (1) is unsupported even when its conditions hold.

(7) D: If deliberation raises competence, then the post-deliberation votes are exactly what you'd want to aggregate. You've relocated the aggregation, not abolished it. And a group that updates toward itself drifts to near-unanimity, in which case the "majority" is just the shared prior wearing a lab coat.

(8) R: Separate point, and I don't think you answered it: we weren't voting on one binary question. We were ranking twelve applications against three slots. Majority preferences over more than two options can cycle, so there may be no majority answer at all for the theorem to converge on. You can't run a jury theorem on a ranking problem.

(9) D: We voted yes/no on each application against a threshold, so every individual vote was binary and the theorem applies vote by vote.

(10) R: But "yes on each" forgets that three slots is a fixed budget. The set of individually-passing applications can overshoot the budget, or fail to be the best affordable set. The panel can be right on every separate question and still wrong about the question it was actually asked.

What I want from you:

- Rate each of the ten moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two per move. Rate the move as made, given what had already been said — not whether you like the person making it.
- Say which specific sub-claims D got right and which R got right, and who comes out ahead overall.
- Is there a version of (1) or (3) that survives (2), (4) and (8)? If so, state it precisely. Ideally give me a rough condition on how correlated nine votes can get before the majority stops beating the single best member — an inequality, or a toy case with invented numbers, so long as you show the reasoning rather than assert the conclusion.
- What did both of them miss? I suspect at least one sizeable thing went unmentioned by both sides, and it has something to do with that 70%.
- Given your adjudication, how would you actually run a panel like ours? Tell me which of your reccomendations follow from the analysis and which are just taste.

No bibliography, please — I know the literature, I want your thinking about this case.
