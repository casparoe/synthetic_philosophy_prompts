Ok, strange thing has happened to me and I could use some help. I'm 71, a retired radiochemist — 35 years of my working life spent measuring how fast radionuclides crawl through clay and fractured granite, which is to say the numbers that go into the safety case for a deep waste repository. I never read a page of political philosophy until about two years ago and now I read far too much of it, badly, at night.

In the spring I recieved one of those letters, "you have been selected at random to serve," and after some grumbling I said yes. Our state is convening a citizens' assembly: 100 of us, six weekends, charged with recommending whether and on what conditions the state should site and license a repository for intermediate-level waste — the medical and industrial stuff, not spent fuel — and if so, what kind of say the host community gets and what happens if it says no.

The facilitation firm asked for a volunteer to give the opening remarks on the first Saturday morning, six to eight minutes, to the other 99 members. I stupidly put my hand up. Now I have to write it and I have stage fright.

The room: almost none of them have heard the word sortition. Several suspect they've been conscripted. Ages 19 to 84. Two nurses, a forklift driver, a woman who runs a bakery, a union rep, a retired sheriff, a few parents, and two farmers whose land is near one of the three candidate sites. In the gallery there'll be a political scientist observing, a reporter from the regional paper who will lift one sentence out of it, and two ministry staff who commissioned the whole thing. On the members' forum beforehand, one of us — a woman in her forties, quite angry, and honestly not wrong — wrote that the assembly is theatre: the government wrote the question, picked the three sites, and "randomly selected" a hundred people who happened to agree to show up, so we're a rubber stamp with catering.

Here is what I want the speech to do, and I mean these as hard constraints, not garnish:

1. It has to deal with my own expertise, and I want that to be the spine. I've decided to declare it in the first minute and then put it in a box: I am not going to be the room's authority on diffusion coefficients, and I want to say why — because the point of choosing people by lot is precisely that we weren't chosen for what we already know, and a room where the expert talks first is not the room we were promised. But I don't want fake modesty and I won't disown 35 years of work either. There's a real line between "my knowledge is irrelevant here" (false) and "my knowledge entitles me to steer" (also false, and corrosive). Find it and build the speech on it.

2. It has to answer the angry woman honestly, without naming her and without dunking. Facts I got from the secretariat: invitations went to roughly 12,000 addresses pulled from the voter file plus a supplementary list of licence holders; about one in twelve of the people actually reached said yes; and the final hundred were then balanced for region, age, sex, education, and whether they think climate change is real. So we are not a mirror of the state. We are a mirror of the part of the state that answers its mail. I want that said out loud — my view is that a body which can't state its own sampling error shouldn't be trusted with anybody else's — and then argued through to why our recommendation is still worth the paper it's printed on. It must not collapse into a shrug, and it must not pretend the problem is solved.

3. Use the one analogy I actually have. In modelling, when you can't integrate something analytically you sample it at random, and the reason that works isn't that the individual draws are clever; it's that they're unbiased, so each one carries information about the whole in a way a hand-picked draw doesn't, and the error shrinks at a known rate with the number of draws rather than with how smart the draws are. Get that across in plain words. Then mark exactly where the analogy stops, because this matters to me: a Monte Carlo estimate has a defined quantity it's converging toward, and our job is not to converge on the correct opinion that already exists somewhere in the state or in the ministry. We aren't measuring anybody. We're forming a judgment. I want the room to hear that difference. No equations, and one number at most.

4. Get at why being picked and being elected are different jobs. Nobody sent us, we have no mandate, there's no re-election, so there's nobody to perform for — and the harder, more honest flip side: we also can't claim authority from having won anything. We have exactly as much authority as our process earns, and we haven't earned any yet at 9 a.m. on Saturday.

5. One sentence in it should be liftable by that reporter without turning into something I'd disavow in tomorrow's paper. Tell me which sentence you intend that to be.

Tone: plain, warm, a bit dry. People say I'm funny in a dead way; two light moments maximum, and no half-life jokes, I've made them all already. No sentence that needs re-reading aloud to parse.

You're both a working political philosopher who has thought hard about sortition and lottocracy and an experienced speechwriter for people who aren't politicians — wear both hats and don't let the second one sand down what the first one insists on. Think carefully and don't start drafting straight away. First, in five or six bullets, tell me: (a) the two or three things this speech absolutely cannot say, (b) what it has to concede and where, and (c) exactly where the sampling analogy breaks and what words you'll use to mark it. If you think something in my setup is broken or unworkable, say so in one line before the bullets rather than quietly papering over it. Then write the speech itself as continuous prose, 800 to 950 words, no headings, no bullets, no stage directions other than maybe one [pause], no names of philosophers, no book titles, no citations. And don't append a reading list or sources afterwards — I can't chase them and I don't want them.
