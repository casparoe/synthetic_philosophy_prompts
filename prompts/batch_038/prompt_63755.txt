Background first: I'm a retired comparative physiologist. Thirty-five years of sensory electrophysiology in coldwater fish, mostly recording from cranial afferents, and since I stopped going into the lab I've fallen hard into philosophy of mind and animal ethics. I do a tiny podcast now — me, a borrowed mic, and the spare room.

My guest next week is a philosopher of biology who argues that teleost fish and decapod crustaceans are both sentient, and more specifically that the evidence has already cleared the bar for treating them that way in regulation, even while she freely admits the metaphysics of experience isn't settled. Her evidential basis is behavioral-criteria stuff: motivational trade-offs between avoiding a noxious stimulus and getting at a resource, prolonged site-directed rubbing or grooming after injury, learned avoidance that persists, responsiveness to analgesics, preference tests. She thinks the arguments that fish or shrimp can't feel anything because they're missing some particular piece of neural hardware beg the question against them.

Here's my problem, and why I need your help. I spent a chunk of my career generating exactly the kind of data she leans on and I never got anywhere with it philosophically. In my own trout recordings nearly everything was in the myelinated conduction-velocity range — I know people have reported slow units since, so I don't want to hang the whole interview on that — but I also can't shake the feeling that her criteria list was assembled out of vertebrate pain science and is now being exported, unchanged, onto animals whose nervous systems are organized completely differently. I want to press that hard without turning it into a gotcha show. I don't want a hit piece, and I don't want softballs either.

Write me ten to twelve interview questions, numbered, and under each one give me the answer she's most likely to give plus a follow-up keyed to that specific answer. Make sure the set covers:

- why responsiveness to an analgesic is supposed to show there's an experience in there rather than just a nociceptive reflex loop being modulated, and what would distinguish "the fish avoids it" from "the fish is bothered by it"
- whether the criteria are vertebrate-derived, and what she'd say if someone built the list out of decapod neurobiology instead and then asked whether mammals pass it
- her response to the hardware objection, including what would have to be true for that objection to actually succeed rather than beg the question
- whether "they're sentient" is a claim about the world or a claim about how to act, and if it's precautionary, what she thinks a false positive costs in a system producing that many animals
- which practices she'd kill first and in what order: slaughter without stunning, CO2 narcosis, ice-slurry chilling of shrimp, asphyxiation in air, eyestalk ablation in broodstock, live transport and crowding — and whether the ordering follows from sentience or from something else like headcount or duration
- whether cortisol, gill-beat rate, and the rest of the physiological stress metrics track welfare or just physiology, and if she uses them
- a case where acting on her view makes welfare worse, say a ban on the least-bad available slaughter method pushing producers to something cheaper and nastier
- and one closing question that can't be answered with "more evidence of the same kind" — something that would move her confidence down, not up

Also, flag two spots in there where a hostile interviewer would spring a trap, and rewrite those two as straight questions that keep the pressure but drop the trap.
