I've been going down a rabbit hole on how Singapore sets ministers' pay — a friend of mine who works in gov tech there brought it up at dinner last week and I genuinely can't stop thinking about the design of it. As far as I can tell it works like this. An entry-level minister's salary is benchmarked to the median income of the top 1,000 Singapore-citizen earners, with a flat 40 percent haircut applied on top of that, and the stated reason for the haircut is that political service involves a kind of sacrifice and the discount is supposed to reflect that ethos. About 65 percent of the package is fixed; the other 35 percent is variable, split between a personal performance bonus that the PM decides after consulting his senior ministers, and a national bonus computed from four equally weighted macro indicators: real median income growth, real income growth of the bottom 20 percent, the unemployment rate, and real GDP growth. Two details I find wierd. The PM himself gets no personal performance bonus, on the explicit reasoning that nobody is positioned to assess his performance, so he gets double the national bonus instead. And the President draws the same monthly salary as the PM plus the standard variable component, but no performance bonus and no national bonus at all, because the President doesn't set policy. There's also a clean-wage principle — everything in cash, published, no hidden components or perks — and you draw the same pay whether you hold one portfolio or three. The benchmark number itself hasn't been moved since 2011, even though the thing it's pegged to has gone up a lot since then and there are supposed to be reviews every five years.

So what theory of political legitimacy does this whole apparatus presuppose? The country's own story, as I understand it, is that the government gets to rule because it delivers — housing, growth, order, incorruptibility — and this pay formula looks like that story converted into arithmetic, but I can't tell whether it actually expresses that view or something else. Benchmarking to the top 1,000 earners seems to assume governing is skilled labour priced by what the private market would pay you instead, and that talent is scarce and portable across sectors. But then the 40 percent sacrifice discount seems to assume something closer to the opposite: that office has a non-market character you're meant to feel. Which of those is doing the real work, and does the package quietly rule out other views — that office-holders should resemble the people they govern, that authority has to be justified by consent or procedure rather than by results, that public service has internal goods which market-rate pay would crowd out rather than attract?

I'd especially like you to dig into the national bonus, because it looks like a very strong commitment about responsibility rather than just a compensation choice: paying a cabinet for GDP growth and unemployment takes a stand on whether those outcomes are attributable to them at all, and they did take pay cuts in the 2009 crisis, which wasn't exactly their doing. Does that presuppose an account of responsibility where outcome luck counts, or is it better read as a pure incentive device making no desert claim whatsoever? Same for the PM exception — if the justification is that no one can assess him, what does that say about who the system thinks the principal is, and why not apply that logic to everyone? And what distinction is the President's exclusion drawing, between holding office and being the author of outcomes? Please be careful to separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits nicely with, and flag which parts are underdetermined, where several rival accounts would license the exact same formula. A classmate also pointed out that China runs on a delivery story too but pays its cadres comparatively nothing and uses promotion against numeric targets as the incentive currency instead of salary — worth a couple of lines on whether those two arrangements presuppose the same thing about legitimacy or something quite different. Finish by telling me what would have to change in the practice if you swapped in a different account, e.g. one where legitimacy comes from procedure and consent, or one where the currency of political success isn't economic at all.
