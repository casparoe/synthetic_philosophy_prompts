Quals reading on well-being, and I sketched an argument against hedonism that my advisor says proves way too much. I can't see where it slips. Here it is:

(1) Hedonism says an experience is good for you in virtue of a single property — being pleasant — and the property is supposed to do the explaining, not just restate the verdict.
(2) So if one property F explains why all the Fs are good, the Fs must share something you can specify without just repeating that they're good.
(3) But the pleasure of a cold beer on a hot day, of a proof finally clicking, of an itch getting scratched, and of being right about someone you love share nothing in the way of feel. There's no way to say what they have in common except that each is enjoyable.
(4) And being enjoyable is just another way of saying being good to have. So what (3) shows is that the only thing these experiences share is the goodness.
(5) So there's no property left over for the hedonist to point at, and hedonism is either empty or collapses into the trivial claim that good experiences are good for you.
(6) Except the same move works on any category whose members don't look alike: knowledge, justice, games, pain. None share a specifiable common feature, so none name properties — which is obviously too strong.

I want to keep (1) and (3). I'm a hedonist and I don't think I'm lying about the phenomenology, so biting the bullet on heterogeneity isn't available to me either. Which step do I reject, why did it look innocent going in, and what does rejecting it cost — including what I'd owe if I tried to keep (3) and drop (2)?
