I've been cataloguing Machiavelli's verdicts on his own examples for a paper on fortune and virtù, and I want a prediction about a case he never takes up.

Track record as I've got it: Cesare Borgia did everything right and was wrecked anyway by what Machiavelli treats as freak bad luck — verdict, hold him up as a model for anyone who takes a new state. Agathocles of Syracuse seized power by massacring the senate and then held it against enemies and conspiracies — verdict, cruelty well used, but empire without glory, and cruelty isn't virtue. Fabius Maximus, slow and patient, wore Hannibal down — verdict, his caution fit the times, and he'd have been destroyed if the times had demanded an attack. Julius II, charging into Bologna while everyone else was still negotiating — verdict, his recklessness fit the times; had he needed to go carefully he'd have come to grief. Piero Soderini, mild and patient — verdict, prospered while the times allowed it and was ruined the moment they didn't, because a man can't deviate from what his nature inclines him to. Pertinax, trying to make disciplined soldiers out of men who loved license — killed for it.

New case: a prince of naturally cautious, bookish temperment who has spent twelve quiet years drilling a citizen militia, filling granaries and rebuilding the walls, and who over those same years has delibrately thrown himself into risky public gambles so that boldness becomes a trained habit rather than a taste. Now the times turn violent and need sudden action.

Where does he land? I want the rules his verdicts above could all be instances of — at least three of them, including any the list only hints at — what each predicts about my case, your best guess and the reasons for preferring it, and one further example, historical or invented, that would tell the rules apart.
