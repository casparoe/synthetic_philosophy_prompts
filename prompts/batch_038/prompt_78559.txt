I'm on the city council of a mid-size post-industrial town, and next month we vote on a pilot local currency that a downtown merchants' association has been pushing for two years. The proposal as it stands: the city issues up to $250,000 in digital tokens, distributed as a match on payroll at six anchor employers and through the food bank; roughly sixty local businesses have signed on to accept them; the city will accept them for parking meters, permit fees and the municipal pool; and each token expires ninety days after issue. There's also a clause letting residents exchange tokens back for dollars at a 10% haircut, which the merchants hate and the food bank loves.

The reason I'm writing is that I've been reading Berkeley's economic pamphlets rather than his philosophy lately — the one written as a long list of numbered questions, and the anonymous tract he put out when he was bishop of Cloyne lobbying for a public national bank in Ireland. My takeaway, and correct me if I've got it wrong: for Berkeley money isn't itself wealth, it's a token or counter whose whole worth lies in what it sets in motion; a nation is rich in proportion to the industry of its people, not to the gold in its vaults; and money that sits idle in a chest is money failing at the one job it has. That seems to me like an argument for the pilot almost by design — a token that can't be hoarded because it expires, that only works if it circulates, and whose success you'd measure in shifts worked and shelves restocked rather than in reserves. But our most sceptical member, a retired commercial banker, says this is sentimental nonsense: a token with no backing and no convertibility outside town isn't money at all, it's a discount coupon with extra steps. His objections are (a) leakage — most of our merchants buy their inputs from distributors two counties away, so the tokens just get spent once locally and the value walks out; (b) stigma — the food-bank distribution means the token becomes the currency of poor people, and merchants will quietly refuse it; (c) the haircut clause makes it a liability on our books with no asset behind it.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate to the finance commitee before the vote. I want a real recommendation — approve, approve with conditions, or reject — with reasoning the banker could actually reply to, not a summary of both sides. Tell me concretely where the Berkeleyan picture does work in this decision, and let it bite on design details: whether to keep the ninety-day expiry, whether the city should accept tokens for property-tax-adjacent fees or only for trivial ones like parking, whether the initial issuance should go to employers or to households, and whether the 10% haircut clause survives his objection (c). And then tell me where Berkeley actively misleads us — an 18th-century bishop arguing for a national bank in a closed, coin-starved economy is not the same problem as a small open town with a dollar economy already running through it, and I want you to say what changes because of that. Give his objection (a) its full due, since I think it's the strongest thing on the table and I don't want my own reading of Berkeley to steamroll it. Keep it to roughly 700 words, plain prose with a few subheads, no citations or reading lists (I can't chase those down anyway), and don't hedge the bottom line. The very last line must be exactly in this form: Verdict: <APPROVE or APPROVE WITH CONDITIONS or REJECT> — <the single most important condition or reason, ten words or fewer>.
