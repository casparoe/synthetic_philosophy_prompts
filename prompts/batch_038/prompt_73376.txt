I sit on a three-person committee at a regional housing-assistance nonprofit — about forty staff, an intake line, an after-hours distress line, and a state funder that wants paperwork on time. We have been asked to produce a one-page rule for when a software system may act in the organization's name rather than merely assist a person who then acts. Everyone on the committee keeps gesturing at the difference between a tool and an agent, but nobody can say what that difference amounts to once the tool is fast, cheap, and usually right; the former public defender on the committee insists the answer is obvious and will not say what it is, and our board wants the policy in March. So I did the thing I always do when a distinction feels slippery: I wrote down cases and marked them by gut, and now I want to know what principle, if any, my marks add up to.

Here they are, with my verdicts. (1) The system drafts a reply to a routine enquiry and a caseworker reads it and presses send: pass, trivially. (2) It sends those same replies with nobody reading them, but each one sits in an outbox for ten minutes and any staff member can recall it inside that window, and recall has worked every time we tested it: pass. (3) It prepares and submits our annual return to the state funder under the executive director's digital signature with no human looking at it first; if a figure is wrong we may file an amended return within ninety days and we have never once missed that window: fail — and I cannot explain why, since the correction path here is far more reliable than in (2). (4) It places orders with our approved supply vendor up to a fixed cap each, logging every order in a ledger the office manager reads each Friday: pass. (5) It negotiates a lease renewal with our landlord's own negotiating system, whatever the two of them settle on binds us, and no one sees the exchange until after both sides have committed: fail. (6) Exactly (5), except that the full transcript is retained and a named human may void the deal within forty-eight hours, for any reason or none: pass, though uneasily. (7) It runs after-hours scheduling — reassigning shifts, approving swaps, calling in substitutes — inside rules the supervisors set, and any supervisor can undo an assignment up to the moment the shift begins: pass. (8) It answers the distress line at night and speaks warmly in the first person, staying on the line with a caller in crisis, without ever saying that it is not a person, though callers never ask: fail. (9) Identical to (8) except that the call opens with a plain statement that the caller is speaking with software: pass. (10) It deletes files it has classified as duplicates, keeping a thirty-day recovery bin that has never failed a request: pass. (11) It deletes messages it has classified as spam, permanently, at an error rate under one in ten thousand: fail, even though the harm to any single person is smaller than the worst case in (10). And the one I keep flipping on: it sends a condolence note in the director's name from a template a human approved, with no human looking at the particular instance — the name, the dates, the cause of death. Some mornings that passes for me and some mornings it doesn't.

My tentative principle is that a system may act for us without a person in the loop if and only if (i) every act it performs can be undone by a named human within a stated window, and (ii) any outsider who might take the act for a person's doing is told, at the time, that it is not. Case (3) appears to satisfy both — the funder's portal records that the filing came from software, and the ninety-day amendment window is real — and I still fail it. Case (6) satisfies (i) only barely, since voiding a deal is not the same as never having been bound by it, and for two days we are bound; I pass it anyway. So either the principle is wrong or my marks are.

What I want back: a principle, or the smallest set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that a stranger could apply it to a case I have never described, with each clause tied to the case that motivates it. Then tell me plainly which of my marks it cannot accommodate — and if it faces the choice between saving all of them with additional machinery and overturning one or two with a clean rule, say which it recommends and what turns on that choice. Apply the result to four situations I did not list: a triage system that decides which intake callers reach a human today and which are offered an appointment next week, where the ranking is shown to no one and callers are not told they were ranked; a system that refuses to carry out a supervisor's instruction because it judges the instruction unlawful; two of our own systems bargaining over an internal reallocation of budget between programs, with no outside party anywhere in the loop; and an emergency in which no human is reachable and the system acts, irreversibly, to stop a client's eviction being executed the next morning. Finally, two questions I cannot settle on my own: do (2) and (3) differ in kind or only in degree — is there something about attesting to a fact in someone's name that is not simply a species of hard-to-reverse commitment, or am I mystifying a signature? — and does the distinction I am circling live in the system, in the act, or in the relationship with whoever is on the receiving end? I suspect the third, but I have no argument for it. I am after the reasoning, not the sources, so please do not spend words on references or further reading.
