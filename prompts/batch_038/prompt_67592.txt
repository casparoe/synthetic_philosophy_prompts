I am preparing a paper for an interdisciplinary seminar on the history of money, and I want to read Shakespeare's The Merchant of Venice through Aristotle's account of money and exchange: the Politics' distinction between household management and wealth-getting, with its verdict that taking interest is the most unnatural form of the latter because money was meant to be used in exchange rather than to generate more of itself; and the Nicomachean Ethics' treatment of justice in exchange, where need is what holds a community together and money is a conventional stand-in for need, a measure that renders unlike things commensurable and that can itself be changed and rendered useless. The audience knows the plot loosely — the loan, the bond, the caskets, the courtroom, the rings — but nothing of Aristotle, so I would like the philosophical content stated clearly enough to stand on its own.

Two claims I want tested, both of them my own coinages rather than anything in Aristotle or in the criticism I have read. First, I have been distinguishing what I call measure-by-convention from measure-in-kind: the first fixes a quantity in a unit everyone agrees to use, the second demands that the quantity be taken in the very substance whose worth is in question. On this reading the pound-of-flesh bond is not an image of greed but a demand for measure-in-kind, and Portia's verdict is not a lesson in mercy but a demonstration that the demand cannot be met: the bond can specify an amount and even a location, but it cannot specify the flesh apart from the blood, so commensuration fails at the point of specification. Is that a point Aristotle could make, or am I bending his account of the measure to fit the scene? Second, and more embarrassingly for my reading, Shylock waives interest on this loan and takes the bond in its place. If Aristotle's objection is to money breeding money, then the play's most famous moneylender is not a usurer in the transaction that destroys him, while Antonio — the gratis lender, whose practice drives down the going rate and who looks from Aristotle's standpoint like the virtuous alternative — is presented as corrosive, contemptuous, and the very cause of the bond. I want you to say whether the play knows what it is doing here, or whether I have found a real dislocation between the drama and the theory.

I also want the honest critic's version of where the play resists the lens. Does the casket episode function as a parody of commensuration, with three metals offered as measures of worth and two of them punishing those who trust the shining one? Does the ring plot in the final act turn on objects whose value is precisely that they refuse to be exchangeable, which Aristotle would treat as a confusion about measure rather than a discovery? And is Venice — capital at sea, a loan underwritten against expected returns, a lender's compensation for risk — a world for which Aristotle's origin story of money, arising because grain and wine were awkward to carry to barter, has no category at all?

Three constraints. Paraphrase scenes rather than quoting them, and do not supply line or act references I cannot check. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations; if a scholar's position is genuinely needed, describe it without naming a title. Above all, keep faith in both directions: do not reduce the play to an illustration of Aristotle, and do not stretch Aristotle to cover the play. Where they simply diverge, say so plainly, and close with a short statement of what The Merchant of Venice contributes to the debate about money as measure that argument alone would not deliver.
