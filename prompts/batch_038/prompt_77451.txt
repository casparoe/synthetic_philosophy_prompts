I am a fourth-year doctoral student in philosophy of medicine, writing a dissertation on how determinations of death are justified in the transplant context. As part of my fieldwork I sit in on the weekly case conference of a university hospital's organ donation committee. Last Thursday the debrief after one case turned into a sustained argument between two senior members: an intensive care consultant with two decades of experience signing neurological determinations (call him H.) and a visiting philosopher of biology who works on organismality and biological individuality (call her V.). The case was a woman in her late twenties, sixteen weeks pregnant, who had met whole-brain criteria after a catastrophic hemorrhage; the family and the team had elected to continue somatic support in the hope of reaching viability, and the committee was trying to settle how her status should be recorded in the chart — H. wanted a dual entry, a determination of death alongside a separate notation that somatic support was continuing for a viable pregnancy, and the argument began there. I took detailed notes and reconstructed the exchange below. Neither of them is a straw man; I have kept both positions at their strongest and deliberately suppressed my own view, so that you will be judging the moves rather than reacting to me.

(1) H. opened by conceding more than the committee expected. He said the neurological criterion is not a discovery about organisms but a legal and pragmatic settlement: the bedside tests establish the irreversible loss of two capacities, consciousness and spontaneous breathing, and we call that death because doing so serves families, patients, and the supply of organs. He then catalogued what such bodies demonstrably go on doing under support — they heal surgical wounds, mount febrile and inflammatory responses to infection, hold blood pressure and temperature with pharmacological help, regulate water balance under hormone replacement, retain spinal reflexes, and, as this very case showed, they have carried pregnancies for months and delivered live infants. His conclusion: if death is the irreversible loss of the integrated functioning of the organism as a whole, then these bodies are not dead, and intellectual honesty requires saying that we have chosen a definition rather than found a fact. (2) V. replied that this misdescribes what is happening at the bedside. The residual functions are real, but they are locally integrated rather than organismally integrated: a healing wound is a tissue-level process that any perfused tissue performs, and whatever coordination exists among the systems is being supplied from outside — by the ventilator, the vasopressors, the hormone infusions. What makes an organism one thing, she said, is that its parts are organized by the whole rather than merely coordinated with one another by external means, and brainstem destruction removes precisely that source of organization; a heart beating in a chest whose gas exchange is being done by a machine is no more evidence of a living organism than a heart beating on a perfusion rig. (3) H. answered that she then has no principled place to stop. Dialysis substitutes for the kidneys, a pacemaker for the sinoatrial node, insulin for the islet cells, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation for heart and lungs, pressors for vascular tone; if substituted function disqualifies integration, a great many patients in his unit are candidates for not being alive, and if it does not, the ventilator is not special. His deeper point was that irreversibility is not a property of the body at all but of the body indexed to an epoch and to whether anyone attempts anything: this woman would not have been maintained at all twenty years ago, and some technique we cannot presently describe might in thirty years restore what we are now calling irreversibly lost. So death becomes a fact that can change truth value over time for one and the same body, which is not how a biological kind behaves. (4) V. said indexing is not a concession but the content of the concept — irreversibility has always meant irreversible given available means and reasonable attempts, and the unindexed reading would make whether someone is dead depend on whether a bystander happens to know CPR. She took the future-technology objection to prove too much, since on that reading nobody is ever dead and any body could be reconstituted by sufficiently advanced means, so it cannot be an objection to her view in particular. On the slope: the pacemaker and the dialysis machine substitute for subsystems, and subsystems can fail without the whole failing, whereas the ventilator substitutes for the capacity on which the coordination of all the other subsystems depends — losing the brainstem is not losing one organ, it is losing the organ that holds the rest in relation to one another. She added that H.'s proposed dual chart entry was itself an admission, since nobody writes two vital statuses for one organism unless one of the words has stopped doing biological work. (5) H. said that last sentence assumes the issue. The brain holds the rest in relation to one another only if one has already decided that an organism is a hierarchical system with a master, and the patients we are calling dead are the counterexamples: this woman's body is digesting, excreting, thermoregulating, mounting immune responses, and gestating — arguably the most integrative thing a mammalian body ever does. Meanwhile, he noted, the criterion says that a patient in a permanent vegetative state, who has no consciousness and no prospect of any and who bears none of the interests the concept exists to protect, is unambiguously alive; and it says that an anencephalic infant who breathes spontaneously and grows is alive while a hormonally managed brain-dead body carrying a pregnancy is dead. So the criterion tracks neither consciousness nor integration, and V. has not yet said what it tracks. (6) V.'s final move was to make the stakes explicit and deny that the dispute is merely verbal, because it settles whether procurement kills. If H. is right that these bodies are living organisms, then either the dead-donor rule collapses or neurological determination must be abandoned in favor of circulatory determination plus a change in the law on consent. She pressed him with a dilemma: either he admits that we are, with consent, causing the deaths of living human beings for their organs — which no legislature would authorize and which he would not sign — or he concedes the criterion tracks something real. H. said the dilemma is false and that there is a third position: keep the neurological criterion, keep the dead-donor rule, and stop claiming the criterion reports a fact about organisms. Let death be a legal status, defined by the law for the law's own purposes, justified by the observation that the interests the law protects — bodily integrity, the interest in not being killed, the interest in the continuation of a life — have no bearer here, because nothing capable of being harmed remains. That is where the meeting broke up.

Please adjudicate the exchange move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each of the six numbered moves, say whether it succeeds, fails, or partially succeeds, assign a rating from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and justify the rating. Where a move is logically sound in form but rests on a premise the other side has not granted, name the premise and assess whether it is independently defensible or merely assumed. If a term is being used in more than one sense across the exchange — I believe at least one is, though I will not say which — identify the shift and state which sense each party actually needs for their argument to go through. Then tell me what both of them are missing: whether either is entitled to assume that death must be a single binary event for a whole organism when tissues and organs plainly cease at different rates; whether H.'s appeal to the absence of an interest-bearer quietly substitutes a different question for the one V. was asking; whether V.'s organization-versus-coordination distinction can be cashed out at all without appealing to the brain specifically; whether the technology-indexing argument in move (3) is a genuine problem or a restatement of what the criterion always meant; and whether the legal-status view H. retreats to in (6) is stable, or whether it collapses into either a personhood-based criterion or an open concession that procurement kills. Finish with an overall verdict on who is more right and on which single issue the disagreement turns, and pose one follow-up question to each of them — the one you would ask to expose the weakest point in their position. Argue from the concepts and the cases; I want to know who is right rather than who has said what, so please do not spend space surveying the literature.
