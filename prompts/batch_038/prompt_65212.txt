I coordinate trainings for a volunteer network that supports people arrested at protests, and I keep running into a distinction I can no longer take for granted. The line I hear from journalists, from sympathetic commentators, and even from some of our own trainees — roughly, not verbatim — is that an act only counts as genuine civil disobedience if the people doing it act openly and stay to be arrested, and that being willing to take the punishment is part of what separates a conscientious protester from an ordinary lawbreaker. On that view, the masked activists who disable equipment at night and take care never to be identified are excluded from the category, however grave the wrong they are acting against.

Two things about this bother me. First, in the cases we track, the defendant who pleads guilty, expresses remorse, and asks only to be allowed to explain themselves in court tends to fare visibly better than the one who contests the charge; so the criterion seems to be rewarded by the same machinery whose authority it is supposed to be questioning. Second, the people our culture treats as paradigmatic heroes of conscience — those who ran the Underground Railroad, the households that hid neighbors from deportation — broke the law deliberately, concealed what they were doing, and did everything in their power to avoid arrest. Applying the criterion consistently seems to demote them to mere fugitives.

Please reconstruct what this admission rule for the category actually commits us to. Specifically: does it presuppose that the state's demand for compliance retains some force even against someone convinced the law is unjust, and that the act is addressed to a public rather than aimed at stopping the harm directly? Which rival accounts of political obligation does it quietly rule out, and which does it merely sit comfortably with without needing? Can the criterion even be applied, given that it seems to require an inner attitude of allegiance that the most effective lawbreakers often lack — and if we drop the attitude and look only at the behaviour of staying put, does anything normative survive? How should the rule handle our celebrated covert lawbreakers: is there a principled distinction between regimes, or does the criterion collapse there? And is it possible for someone who regards the legal order as wholly illegitimate to satisfy the criterion at all, or does satisfying it make the punishment part of the message rather than its price?

Please organize your response under the following headings, in this order, and keep to roughly the lengths given:

- Bottom line (3–4 sentences)
- The practice, restated neutrally (~120 words): separate what the criterion describes from what it evaluates.
- What it strictly presupposes (~250 words): the commitments that no version of the criterion can avoid.
- What it merely fits with (~200 words): fuller theories of obligation and of punishment that are compatible but not required, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined.
- The heroes problem (~250 words): the covert-lawbreaker cases, whether any distinction rescues the criterion, and what the sentencing asymmetry shows.
- Verdict (~150 words): whether the criterion does legitimate work or mainly lets the powerful set the terms of dissent.
- What would have to change (~150 words): if we replaced submission to punishment with a criterion built on non-violence and openness of purpose, for courts, for movement strategy, and for how we classify historical cases.
