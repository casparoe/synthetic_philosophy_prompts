Help me get clear on what exactly goes wrong with "bootstrapping" a source's reliability. Setup: I've got a cheap bathroom scale I've never vetted, and no other way to check my weight. Every morning I step on, note what it says, and conclude I weigh that much. After a few months I string together all those little pairs — it said 170, I weighed 170; it said 170.2, I weighed 170.2 — and infer inductively that the scale is reliable. Intuitively that inference is bogus: I've manufactured a reason to trust the scale out of the scale's own outputs.

Now suppose I swap in a twist: once a month I get weighed on a calibrated scale at the doctors office, so now I do have independent confirmation of some of the readings, and the very same inductive pattern looks totally fine. So is the whole objection just "you need independent checks"? Two things I want thought through. First, that doctor's scale is a source I never vetted either, so this seems to move the bootstrapping back a step rather than kill it — does it regress all the way down, or does it have to bottom out in some source I'm allowed to trust circularly? Second, if reliabilism is true and I genuinely know each individual premise in the bad case, and deduction and induction preserve knowledge, then it looks like I should know the conclusion too. So where's the actual failure point, and does the reliabilist have to just declare the reasoning bad by fiat? Give me the conceptual lay of the land, not a list of who argued what.
