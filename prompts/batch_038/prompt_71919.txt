Good's theorem on the value of information — the result that, for an expected-utility maximiser who will conditionalise on what she learns, costless information can never have negative expected value, and has zero value just when it cannot change her decision — is ordinarily read as a vindication of looking before one leaps. I work in a clinical genetics service, where predictive testing for Huntington's disease is inexpensive, close to definitive, and yet taken up far less often than was predicted once it became available: most adults at fifty per cent risk decline it, and no treatment exists that a positive result would unlock. Please set out the most important implications of the theorem for advising such a person. In particular, I want to know which of its assumptions actually bear on this case — that the news would alter what she knows but not what she wants; that her future self will update as the theorem supposes; that the result is hers alone, when it also bears on her siblings' and children's risks; and that she can always ignore it, when the information concerns her own future and cannot simply be set aside — and, given all that, what the theorem does and does not imply about whether a refusal to be tested can be rational rather than merely excusable. I am less interested in the proof than in where the result survives intact and where it quietly stops applying.
