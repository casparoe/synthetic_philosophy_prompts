So I'm doing my monthly column for the ideas desk, and this month it's on how much the sunk cost fallacy gets overused as life advice — basically every columnist out there thinks "walk away, the money's already gone" is a complete argument. I want to build the counterexample properly instead of hand-waving at it.

Can you put together a scenario for me where an ordinary person comes out ahead by honouring a sunk cost — where quitting is actually the mistake — under constraints that kill off all the easy routes? Specifically:

- one person deciding alone. No committees, no repeated games, no audience who'll punish them for bailing, so no reputation or signalling effects.
- the past spending has not taught them anything, and hasn't left them with a skill, an option, or an asset that makes continuing more valuable. So the "but you've already learned so much" move is banned, as is "you've built up equity in it."
- realistic setting: a house reno, a small business, a degree, a marriage, whatever. No sci-fi, no trolley problems.
- stakes a normal person could face, and the person isn't confused or weak-willed — they're reasoning well by their own lights.

Two or three paragraphs for the scenario, then do the check for me:

1. Spell out the exact preference structure they need for continuing to be right. Not "they're loss averse" — tell me what their utility is a function of, and why that isn't just a cognitive bug wearing a nice coat.
2. Show that the case collapses if you assume they care only about future states of the world, and point at precisely where the assumption that value is separable across time is doing the work. If you lean on reference points or regret at any stage, say whether it's doing normative work or merely describing an error, because the piece is about what's rational, not what people happen to do.
3. Tell me which single feature of the scenario is load bearing, what happens if I delete it, and how a straight-laced economist would attack the case. I'm expecting some version of "fine, they just have weird preferences" — give me the strongest form of that objection and whether it actually lands.

And please flag it plainly if you think the spec is impossible as written, i.e. if there's no non-cheating way to make continuing rational without smuggling in one of the banned routes. I'd much rather hear that up front than get handed a case that only works by sneaking something back in. No citations or reading lists — I'll do my own fact-checking, I just want the case and the reasoning behind it.
