I am trying to establish what my own betting intuitions commit me to about coherence, and I want the principle — or the smallest set of principles — that sits behind them. My verdicts, in the order I wrote them down:

**A.** A fair coin. I will buy a bet paying £1 on heads for 45p, and one paying £1 on tails for 45p, and I will buy both together. No misgiving about any part of this; the package is a sure gain for me.

**B.** I am certain that a bill either passes or does not, and that these exhaust the possibilities, yet I rate each at 0.6, and my prices follow: I buy £1 on its passing at 60p and £1 on its failing at 60p, and lose 20p come what may. That loss is a genuine indictment. It traces to my degrees of belief and to nothing else, and I deserve the reproach.

**C.** My brother is having an operation. I would pay 70p for a bet paying £1 if he recovers, and I would also pay 70p for a bet paying £1 if he does not; together, £1.40 for a guaranteed £1. Structurally this is B over again, but here I deny that the sure loss says anything about my probabilities. Money is worth far more to me in the world where he does not recover, and the loss measures my values rather than my beliefs.

**D.** I am offered a bet now on the bill, and I expect that next week, after a fortnight of lobbying and several dinners with people I wish to impress, I will be far more confident of its passing than any evidence warrants. I decline the bet now, even though my present credences are in order, and I think declining is right: I can see the coming distortion for what it is.

**E.** Now suppose the change comes instead from a drug that leaves my evidence exactly as it was. I again decline to bet now at prices my altered future self would find attractive — but I do not think either of us is irrational, and a book made against the pair of us would not show that we were.

**F.** A bet on an arithmetic proposition I have not had time to check. I decline every price in both directions, short of certainty, and I take that to be a rational stance rather than an evasion.

For what it is worth, I also think that whether I accept given odds depends on the size of the stake, and I do not regard that as a criticism of my beliefs either.

The hard pairs are B/C, which share a shape and split on the verdict, and D/E, in both of which my future self updates badly and I decline now, yet only one of those refusals seems to rest on a criticism.

So: state a principle, precise enough that I could apply it to a case you invent, that separates packages and belief changes which indict degrees of belief from those which indict — or merely reflect — something else. Say which case forces each clause. Say which of my verdicts your principle cannot keep, and which you would overturn outright. Finally, construct one further case on which the two most promising candidate principles come apart, and tell me which I should find more plausible and why. Please do not bother with references; I will supply them.
