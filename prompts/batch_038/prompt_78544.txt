I am a novelist, six months into a third book, and I need help with the intellectual spine of a single chapter. One of my two protagonists is a translator of poetry, a woman in her late fifties who has spent thirty years turning other people's work into English and who, over the course of the novel, gradually loses the ability to justify what she does. The chapter in question is built around one translation that she makes, withdraws, and remakes four times, and I want the argument inside her head to be genuinely contestable rather than decorative — which means I need the two positions she oscillates between stated at full strength. The first is Hofstadter's program in Le Ton beau de Marot: the book takes Clément Marot's short Renaissance poem to a sick young lady, a poem in three-syllable lines, and surrounds it with dozens of competing English versions, ranking them by how well each rebuilds the original's formal puzzle; the poem on this account just is its pattern, and the translator's task is to find the target-language analogue of the constraint rather than the analogue of the words. The second is the literalist pole, of which Nabokov's Onegin is the most uncompromising instance: the sense rendered with an exactness maintained regardless of elegance, the music frankly conceded as untransportable, and the loss made good in commentary. The reconciliation I keep encountering says the choice between these two is a matter of temperament, or genre, or how much loss a given readership will bear. That strikes me as evasive, because it treats the quarrel as a disagreement about quantities of loss when I suspect it is a disagreement about something prior — what the object under translation actually is.

Let me give you the idea I want the chapter to turn on, which I have not found stated anywhere and which I would ask you to treat as a serious hypothesis rather than as a novelist's ornament. Call it the displacement thesis: when a formal constraint cannot be carried across a language boundary, it is not lost so much as relocated — pushed down into another level of the text, where it surfaces as strain, as diction slightly off, as syntax wrong in a way readers register as intensity. On this view, much of what we praise as poetic in a translation is the visible residue of a rule that could not travel; and that residue reads as art rather than as error only when the reader somehow knows the rule that produced it, which in a translation almost never happens. To test it, here is the poem my character is working on. I invented it, so there is nothing to look up: it is by a minor Portuguese modernist, twenty-two lines, and every line ends with a verb in the first-person plural of the imperfect subjunctive drawn from a single conjugation, so the rhyme is not selected from the lexicon at all but falls out of grammar automatically — and because that form means something like "we might have been doing," the constraint also locks the whole poem into a mood of collective, unrealized past. The cost is that at line fourteen the poet, having exhausted the plausible verbs, uses one whose sense is close to nonsense: form has damaged meaning inside the original. English possesses no inflection that could yield a grammar-generated rhyme of that kind, so the constraint can only be displaced — into a repeated auxiliary, an anaphora, a rhyme landing on the wrong element of the line, a syntactic tic.

What I want is a real comparison rather than a digest of each camp. Please work through the following, in this order. First, state each pole at its most demanding, and say what each is claiming a poem to be — whether the difference between them is a difference in ontology, in the ethics of obligation to an author, or in some third thing I have not identified. Second, run my invented case, and specifically line fourteen, through both programs: the literalist must decide whether fidelity obliges her to import the damage, and whether damage survives at all when the reader cannot distinguish a forced word from a merely bad one; the Hofstadterian must say what counts as the analogue of a grammatical constraint in a language lacking that grammar, and must confront the possibility that an imported badness will be read as incompetence rather than as constraint. Third, tell me whether the displacement thesis is a genuine third position, an entailment of one of the two poles, or a confusion — and if it is a confusion, show me where it breaks. Fourth, assess the two poles against each other directly: is there any translation problem on which they issue different and checkable instructions, or do they merely differ in what they choose to describe? And fifth, describe what a decisive counterexample to the displacement thesis would look like — a case where the constraint transports perfectly and the poem nevertheless dies, or where nothing at all transports and something nevertheless arrives. Aim for roughly fifteen hundred words, in American English. I will supply the references myself, so please include no bibliography, no works-cited apparatus, and no invented titles; referring to positions and arguments by name is more than enough.
