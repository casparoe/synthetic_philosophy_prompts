I am moderating a ninety-minute public interview, before a departmental audience that has already received a primer on general relativity, with a philosopher of physics whose recent book argues that Gödel's 1949 rotating-universe solution does genuine metaphysical work. Her claim, roughly: because the very field equations we accept admit a stationary, homogeneous solution in which pressureless dust and a negative cosmological constant are balanced against one another and closed timelike curves pass through every event, an objective lapse of time cannot be a consequence of those laws. She is scrupulous about the historical record and will not be drawn into exegesis, so I want questions that engage her argument rather than Gödel's biography or his texts.

Three of her commitments are worth pinning down. First, she insists the argument is modal rather than empirical: what matters is that a Gödel-type geometry is nomologically possible, not that our universe happens to be Gödelian, and she dismisses the objection that the real universe expands and appears to have a positive cosmological constant as what she calls the actualist prejudice. Second, she maintains that the rotation is inessential: the closed timelike curves carry the argument, and any solution containing them would serve equally well. Third, when pressed on Gödel's own concession that the round trip would demand prohibitive velocities and energies, she replies that the argument turns on what the geometry permits, not on what engineering could deliver.

Write eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up aimed at the answer she is most likely to give. At least one question must address each of the following: what precisely licenses the step from the existence of a solution containing closed timelike curves to a conclusion about time in the actual world; whether her conclusion is any stronger than the four-dimensionalist reading already extracted from the relativity of simultaneity, and if not, why the rotating universe was needed at all; whether an exact solution whose cosmological constant must be fine-tuned to the dust density earns metaphysical weight, and how she meets the charge that such solutions are mathematical curiosities; what survives of her argument if a chronology-protecting constraint — Hawking's conjecture, or the semi-classical divergence of vacuum fluctuations at a chronology horizon — can rule closed timelike curves out, since a principled global distinction between geometries that admit objective lapse and geometries that do not looks like the opposite of her thesis; whether her real target is objective lapse or something narrower, such as the possibility of acting on one's own past, and if the latter, whether the familiar compossibility reply — on which changing the past conflates what is possible with what is possible holding the past fixed — simply removes the premise her reductio requires; whether the loss of global hyperbolicity, rather than the loss of local temporal structure, is the right locus of the difficulty, given that the Gödel spacetime retains a perfectly sharp local distinction between timelike and spacelike directions; and finally, what observation, theorem, or distinction would count against her reading.

Keep each question short and pointed. No softballs, nothing about her intellectual formation, and no request that she summarize the model for non-specialists.
