I spent thirty-six years in a government laboratory, the last fifteen of them running a small group that produced and verified probabilistic forecasts of pollution episodes, so when I took up philosophy properly after retiring I was drawn first of all to the programme that grades credences by their accuracy: its machinery — strictly proper scoring rules, expected inaccuracy, dominance arguments — is machinery I used at work for decades without knowing what the philosophers called it. As I now understand the orthodox position, three claims belong together: an incoherent credence function is accuracy-dominated by some coherent one; conditionalising on one's total evidence maximises expected accuracy from the agent's own prior point of view, so long as the scoring rule is strictly proper; and from the second it follows that free, unambiguous, relevant evidence can never be expected to make one less accurate — the value-of-evidence result. It is the third claim I want to press against, and I should like you to build me the instrument for doing so.

Please construct a thought experiment, to specification, in which an accuracy-first theorist is obliged to advise an agent to decline a piece of evidence that is free, unambiguous and relevant, because taking it would leave her worse off by her own prior lights. My specifications are these. First, the setting must be a real place of work — a laboratory bench, a monitoring station, a clinic — where a measurement is made and a report arrives; no trolleys, no urns of coloured balls, no coins tossed in seminars, and none of the usual stage machinery: no demons, no prediction engines, no drugs, no amnesia, no anaesthetics, no self-locating or indexical evidence. Second, the agent's prior must be perfectly coherent and her knowledge of the apparatus's likelihoods exact; nothing in the case may be purchased with incoherence, vagueness, ambiguity of content, or an improper scoring rule, and the Brier score must be the measure throughout. Third, the one defect permitted is the agent's own known and quantified doxastic bias: she has established, from her own logged calibration record, that when she inspects a fine-grained reading she over-reacts to it, and this over-reaction must be a specifiable function of the reading rather than a random perturbation. Fourth, the policy she adopts in place of naive inspection must be a recognisable epistemic policy — deliberately attending only to a coarser and more robust feature of the result and forgoing the fine detail — and not an ad hoc instruction of the form "then knock fifteen hundredths off". Fifth, keep it to at most four states of the world and three possible reports, give every number explicitly, and confine the scenario itself to a single paragraph. Sixth, the verdict must be generated by the arithmetic rather than asserted: I want the expected Brier inaccuracy computed for each of the three policies in play — declining the fine evidence, accepting it and conditionalising on it correctly, and accepting it and over-reacting as the agent knows she will — with the resulting ordering stated plainly.

For what it is worth, I tried this myself and failed. I chose a highly diagnostic assay, one that shifted the odds by roughly nine to one, and found, exactly as the theory predicted, that even a grotesquely over-confident posterior still beat the policy of declining to look; my numbers would not come out the way I wanted them to. The design must therefore be more careful than mine, and I rather suspect the parameters cannot be chosen at random. Alongside the case itself, please tell me: which single feature of the scenario is doing the work; a demonstration, with every other number held fixed, that switching that feature off restores the orthodox verdicts; the strongest reply a defender of the orthodoxy could make — I expect it to concern whether the theory is really evaluating posterior states or plans, and whether its norms were ever intended for agents such as mine — together with what I ought to say back; and your own considered view of whether what you have constructed is a genuine counterexample to the value-of-evidence principle or merely a curiosity about non-ideal agents. Please do not append references or a reading list, as I no longer have library access and would much rather the answer stood on its own reasoning. Kindly write the whole of it in British English.
