I am the registrar at a small university museum whose holdings are mixed — Mediterranean archaeology, European decorative arts, mineralogy, and a modest contemporary collection — and I supervise two student cataloguers. Between acquisitions, returns from loan, and the backlog we accession several hundred items a year, and nearly every one of them forces a question the metaphysics literature knows as the special composition question, in a form nobody trained us for: is this one object, or several objects that should be recorded separately? A kylix that arrives as sixty sherds. A coin hoard lifted as a single block. A cabinet whose panels have been replaced across three centuries. An installation of three hundred identical bulbs whose loan contract insists it is one work. A quartz cluster, and next to it in the tray, a crystal that has broken off it.

Our collections system permits either outcome: one object record with part-level sub-records, or several object records bound to a shared group. The choice is not cosmetic. It drives insurance valuation, loan eligibility, what a conservator is authorized to treat as a unit, and how the item is counted and described on a label. At present the decision is made by whoever happens to be cataloguing, and my two students disagree with each other and with me.

Please write a decision procedure my cataloguers can run in sequence on a single incoming item, within the time we have — under an hour, by someone with no philosophy background, with the curator signing off at the end. For each step give me: the question as the student should actually put it, what counts as a sufficient answer, what the step returns when the evidence is missing or unclear, and where the procedure must stop, default to recording the pieces separately, and hand the remainder to a curator because what is left is a matter of institutional convention or value rather than fact.

Ground the instrument in the special composition question as it is genuinely debated, not in common sense about wholes and parts. Build the sequence out of the candidate restrictions on composition — that composition is unrestricted; that it never obtains; that contact or fastening suffices; that only what is alive composes; that what composes is fixed by function or by the interests of whoever is describing; that there is a fact of the matter but no informative general account of it — and for each step state which candidate it operationalizes and what becomes of that step if that candidate is false. Where two candidates would generate identical cataloguing instructions, say so rather than implying the procedure separates them; and tell me plainly whether the procedure is invariant under the extremes, so that a nihilist and a universalist would run it the same way and get the same records.

Then apply it to four items: the sixty-sherd kylix with documented losses; the hoard of some four hundred coins lifted together, where I expect the procedure to give me an answer I will not like, since our counting, publishing, and valuation all assume the coins are separable; a restored cabinet in which most of the original material has been replaced, which looks like a diachronic problem rather than a synchronic one — say explicitly whether the same tests can carry it or whether it needs a separate module, and if so, sketch the module; and, briefly, the quartz cluster with its detached crystal, as a check on whether the procedure's verdicts depend on the item being an artifact at all.

Close with a short statement of the procedure's limits: which of our cataloguing decisions it leaves indeterminate, which it settles by fiat, and how much metaphysical work it is really doing, given that accessioning is a documentary practice with financial and legal consequences rather than an inquiry into what exists. Where a candidate view has a settled name in the literature, use it; where it does not, describe it. Do not pad the answer with a survey of the debate — I want the instrument and nothing else.
