So I just started as the technical officer for a private seafood certification scheme — think a standards body that retailers sign up to, not a government agency — and my first real job is drafting the options paper for a harvest/killing-method case the technical panel votes on next month. The panels nine people, mostly vets plus two industry reps and an academic, and I've been told to frame the decision "in light of our own precedents." Trouble is I can't work out what the precedents add up to. Their track record over the last eight years or so, roughly in order:

1. Approved electrical water-bath stunning for farmed salmon, conditional on logging current and exposure per batch and keeping a working backup kill on the line in case a fish recovers.
2. Rejected CO2 saturation for salmon even though it's cheap and scales fine — the fish thrash and try to jump out for a good while before they go under.
3. Approved percussive stunning at a small trout farm that handles fish individually with trained staff.
4. Turned down a proposal to require percussive or electrical stunning at every certified trout farm regardless of size, and instead capped air exposure at harvest; the recorded reasoning was that they'd regulate what can actually be done competently at scale rather than what's ideal on paper.
5. Rejected ice-slurry chill-killing for tilapia. The tilapia made escape attempts when first dropped in, and separately the panel wasn't satisfied that "stops moving, loses its righting reflex" amounts to unconsciousness — cold slows an animal down long before it switches it off.
6. Approved a gradual chilling step at a cold-water char operation, where the target temperature sat inside the species' normal range and the fish showed no escape response at any point.
7. Refused to certify live boiling of crabs and lobsters by member operations, required a stun or mechanical destruction-of-the-ganglia step first, and later approved a specific device that does it.
8. Banned eyestalk ablation in shrimp broodstock outright. Hatcheries lost alot of business, a couple of members left the scheme, and the panel didn't budge — recorded view being that you can't certify an amputation performed on an unanesthetized animal for a production convenience.
9. Rejected an electrical stunning protocol for shrimp that had worked in trials, for three reasons: shrimp returned to warm water recovered; the lab saw prolonged tail-flicking and attempts to get out of the bath before the animals went still; and nobody had shown that the loss of responsiveness was unconsciousness rather than paralysis.
10. Declined to set any slaughter requirements for mussels and oysters at all, saying the evidence base for sentience in that group doesn't support regulating and they wouldn't act on precaution alone there.

The new case: a member farming whiteleg shrimp in a tropical country has built a plant where shrimp are pumped straight from the pond into two-stage chilling — a few minutes in a tank at about 10°C, then ice slurry at roughly 1°C — and from the slurry they go directly into a freezer tunnel. Shrimp stop moving within about 20 seconds of hitting the slurry, and there is no point on the line where they could be pulled out, rewarmed, and recover. The producer says this replaces what they do now, which is draining the pond and leaving shrimp on ice in air for up to an hour. Our own commissioned lab work found that shrimp pulled out after 20 seconds mostly recovered and swam normally once rewarmed, shrimp pulled out after three minutes did not recover, and the lab couldn't tell whether that non-recovery reflects unconsciousness or an animal that's dying and paralyzed. As for alternatives: one supplier has a pilot-scale electrical unit, nothing at this throughput (~25 tonnes/day), it would cut throughput by about a third, and it's the same family of protocol the panel already turned down in item 9.

So — what do you think they'll actually vote: approve, approve-with-conditions, or reject, and if conditions, which? More than the outcome I want the rules. Which general principles are jointly consistent with all ten decisions? At least two of them have to come apart on the shrimp case, so say which ones, what each predicts here, and which single precedent is the closest analog and why. I also want to know whether any of the ten is hard to fit at all — I keep feeling like item 8 and item 4 are pulling in opposite directions and I can't articulate how. Then: is there some other hypothetical case I could put to the panel that would discriminate between the top two candidate rules better than the shrimp case itself does? And whichever way they go, what principle are they thereby committing to — what future case becomes hard for them to refuse afterwards? I have to stay neutral in the paper, but I'd like to know where the real fork is, and if you think ten decisions genuinely underdetermine this one, please say that plainly instead of picking a winner for the sake of it.
