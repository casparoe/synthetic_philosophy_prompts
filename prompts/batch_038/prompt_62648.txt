I'm putting together a session for a philosophy-of-religion reading group I run out of my living room — mostly non-Jewish, mostly professional philosophers, one extremely persistent undergrad — and the week is on reasons for the commandments, and I keep coming back to a historical moment I don't think anyone in the group has thought about properly. The moment is the decades after 70 CE, when the Romans destroyed the Second Temple and the sacrificial cult just stopped, cold, after roughly a thousand years of continuity. The rabbinic response is what I care about. The tradition has Rabban Yohanan ben Zakkai smuggled out of besieged Jerusalem in a coffin by his own students so he could get an audience with Vespasian, and the thing he asks for is not the Temple's survival — it's Yavneh and its sages, i.e. permission to keep a school running. Over the following generations the rabbis build a whole replacement architecture: the fixed daily prayers are arranged to mirror the daily offerings (the Talmud records a sage saying the prayers were instituted to correspond to the sacrifices, matching them in timing and structure), a midrash has ben Zakkai comforting Rabbi Yehoshua — who is grieving that the place where Israel's atonement happened is now a ruin — by pointing at a prophetic verse about God wanting kindness rather than sacrifice, and atonement gets relocated onto prayer, Torah study, and deeds of lovingkindness. Meanwhile the liturgy never lets go of the old thing: the morning service includes a recitation of the order of the daily offering, one of the blessings in the central prayer asks God to accept Israel's offerings and restore the Temple service, the additional service on sabbaths and festivals petitions for the cult to come back, and on Yom Kippur the congregation listens to a blow-by-blow narration of what the high priest used to do. So you get substitution and restoration-hunger sitting in the same texts, produced by the same people, at the same time.

The theory I want to run all that through is Maimonides' account of the sacrifices in the Guide, which is a famously deflationary one. His picture: a people fresh out of Egypt, living in a world where sacrifice was the universal grammar of worship, could not have been expected to abandon it overnight, so instead of demanding an impossible leap, God transferred the existing practice to his own name and confined it to a single place — a way of destroying idolatry while keeping the people aboard. He likens it to how a sensible parent handles a child's filthy habit, by redirecting it rather than confronting it head-on, and to the forty years in the wilderness, which he reads as God deliberately hardening the people by degrees because human nature cannot absorb abrupt change. On that reading the sacrifices are a concession and a piece of pedagogy aimed at a historical audience rather than something with value in themselves, and the asymmetry is telling: prayer gets made obligatory everywhere and at fixed times, sacrifice stays tied to one spot. Then his law code says the messianic king will rebuild the Temple and restore the offerings exactly as they were, and it devotes enormous space to Temple procedure. Readers have been taking sides ever since — some say the Guide is the real Maimonides and the code is him in his rabbi's hat, some say the concession passage is an esoteric or noble-lie move aimed at philosophically tempted readers, some try to harmonize by arguing both texts agree the sacrifices have no magical efficacy so restoring them would be about conforming to an eternal will rather than needing them, and at least one eighteenth-century rabbi was disturbed enough to suspect the Guide passage was a forgery.

What I want from you is the analysis, running in both directions, and I want it disciplined about the mapping rather than vibes. First: if Yavneh is a case of the concession theory being enacted, who are the players? Is God the accommodator with the rabbis as the ones finally taking the hint, or are the rabbis now doing to their own community what Maimonides says God did at Sinai — accommodating a population that could not simply be told the cult was finished? Second: what options did the rabbis actually have in 70 CE, and how does the theory rank them — total abandonment, substitution-and-mourning, substitution-plus-restoration-prayer, something else? Third, and this is the part I find most interesting: the liturgical evidence seems to cut against the theory in a specific way. If sacrifice was a weaning device whose whole point was to be outgrown, why does the substitution literature hard-code the desire to bring it back, and why does Maimonides' own code insist it will return? Is that an anomaly the theory can absorb — say by distinguishing why a law was originally given from why it remains binding — or does it show the concession account was never a theory about what should happen when a Temple falls, meaning Yavneh is a case it never anticipated because its author was explaining origins and not predicting institutional change? And then tell me where the theory is just silent: what does the post-70 material raise that Guide-style reasoning has nothing to say about — atonement without blood, the fact that the substitute was legislated by people rather than revealed, whether a concession can be revoked and by whom. If it's useful there's one later data point you can bring in or leave out: an attempt under the emperor Julian in the 360s to rebuild the Jerusalem Temple, which came to nothing. I'll let you decide whether that's evidence of anything.
