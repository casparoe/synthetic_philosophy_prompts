I don't have an academic background in economics or philosophy — I run a small land trust in a river valley, which means I spend a fair amount of time thinking about whether we should be spending our endowment now to buy floodplain parcels or preserving it for board members who aren't born yet — and I recently read a long essay in a general-interest magazine about the economics of climate policy. It was well written and I mostly agreed with it, which is exactly why I want a second opinion, because I suspect I agreed with it for the wrong reasons. The passage that stayed with me runs like this:

'The entire argument about how much climate action is worth comes down to a single number nobody can measure: the discount rate. Economists shrink future costs and benefits the further away they are, so that a catastrophe in 2200 barely registers against a small gain today, and that is not a technicality but a moral decision wearing arithmetic's clothes. The practice was condemned at its origin. Frank Ramsey, in the 1928 paper that founded this whole tradition, wrote that discounting later enjoyments in comparison with earlier ones is "ethically indefensible" and arises "merely from the weakness of the imagination" — so the founding text already gave us the answer, and the profession spent the next century ignoring it. What economists actually do is assemble the rate out of two pieces: how much less we care about the future for its own sake, and how much richer the future is expected to be, which matters because an extra dollar does less good for a rich person than a poor one. The big British review of 2006 set the first piece at essentially zero and landed near 1.4 percent; the Yale economist who answered it set the first piece at 1.5 percent and landed near 5 percent, and the distance between those two numbers is roughly the distance between acting now and waiting a few decades. Notice the asymmetry: the second piece is empirical, a matter of growth forecasts you can check, while the first piece is a bare preference about when things happen, supported by no evidence at all, only by a refusal to treat the future as real. That is why the honest thing to do is set it to zero — and once you also stop assuming that future generations will be richer than us, the whole rate collapses to nothing. A discount rate of zero is not a radical position. It is simply what remains when you stop pretending that people who have not been born yet count for less.'

Could you go through this claim by claim and tell me how much of it I should trust? I'd like to know which parts a careful welfare economist or a philosopher working on intergenerational ethics would accept without complaint, which are fair simplifications for a general audience, and which are wrong or quietly slide one thing into another. Two spots in particular bother me and I can't tell whether my discomfort is justified: the way the essay uses Ramsey's remark, and the way its last paragraph moves from the first piece being indefensible to the conclusion that the whole rate should be zero, given that the essay itself said the rate is made of two pieces. Please explain in plain words what those two pieces really are, whether the essay has described them accurately, and whether the difference between the 2006 review and its critic is really the difference the essay says it is. If there's something important the essay leaves out — a reason for a nonzero first piece that isn't just prejudice, or a technical wrinkle that changes what zero would even mean — fold that in too. What I don't want is a reading list or citations; I want your own judgment about where the summary holds up. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you'd write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
