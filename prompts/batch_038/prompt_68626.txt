I write about science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a long piece about what happens when a philosopher's thought experiment gets enacted into statute and then litigated. My centerpiece is Germany's Aviation Security Act, and what I need from you is a disciplined case analysis that runs in both directions: the theory applied to the episode, and then the episode turned back on the theory.

The facts as I have them. Use these rather than rebuilding the case; flag anything you think I have wrong. In the aftermath of the attacks of September 11, 2001, and after an incident in January 2003 in which a mentally disturbed man circled a light aircraft over Frankfurt's banking district and threatened to crash into a tower, the German legislature passed the Aviation Security Act of January 11, 2005. Its section 14(3) authorized the armed forces to shoot down an aircraft by direct use of armed force where the circumstances indicated that the aircraft was to be used against the lives of human beings and the shootdown was the only means of averting that present danger. The federal president signed the bill while recording considerable doubts about its compatibility with the right to life and urging judicial review. On February 15, 2006, the First Senate of the Federal Constitutional Court declared the provision void. Paraphrasing the dignity ground: insofar as uninvolved people on board would be affected, the authorization was incompatible with the right to life taken together with the guarantee of human dignity, because the state may not sacrifice innocent lives for the benefit of others, and killing those people in this way would treat them as objects of state action rather than as persons whose lives are not the state's to weigh against anyone else's. The court separately held that the federation lacked the competence it claimed: the Basic Law's disaster-assistance provisions do not permit deploying the armed forces with specifically military weapons.

Now the theory. Take the doctrine of double effect in its standard shape — an act not otherwise impermissible, a bad effect foreseen but not intended, the bad effect not among the means by which the good effect is produced, and a proportionality condition linking the two — and map the shootdown onto it carefully, marking where the map runs out rather than stretching it. Three specific things I want resolved. First, who is the agent? The doctrine is agent-relative, so the answer changes the verdict: run it for at least two candidate agents — the legislature that drafted the authorization and the pilot who would execute it — and say which one the theory privileges and on what grounds. Second, are the passengers' deaths intended, merely foreseen, or a means? The good effect is that the aircraft never reaches its target; is that effect produced by their deaths or by the destruction of the aircraft? Engage the closeness objection head-on and tell me whether this is a good instance of it or a bad one. Third, the proportionality condition: the doctrine contains one, and the court refused to weigh lives at all. Is that a rejection of the fourth condition, a claim that proportionality can never be satisfied against persons who are not themselves aggressors, or a different structure entirely — a status-based constraint rather than an intention-based one?

For the piece I have been calling the difficulty I keep running into the standing-order problem, and I would like you to develop it and then criticize it. Double effect is written for a single agent, at a moment of choice, with a determinate intention describable in a sentence. Here the intention would have to be located in a text adopted years in advance by hundreds of legislators, addressed to unknown future agents, in circumstances nobody can specify. Can a general authorization have an intention? Note that the foreseeing is generic — whoever happens to be aboard — which seems to make the closeness objection simultaneously worse, because nothing specific was intended, and easier to answer, because nothing specific had to be. Consider whether the pilot's intention is fixed by the statute, by his orders, or by his own psychology; whether an agent can execute a standing order without adopting its intention; and whether a doctrine that evaluates rules rather than acts is still double effect or has quietly become something else. Then run the counterfactual: had the court upheld section 14(3), how would the doctrine have been recruited in its defense, and would that defense have been available to the legislature that wrote the provision or only to the pilot who acted under it?

Next, the reverse direction. What does the episode show about the doctrine? The candidates I can see are that double effect is vindicated here, reaching the court's verdict by the means-versus-side-effect route; that it is bypassed, because the court's constraint is not about intention at all and the doctrine has nothing to say; or that it misdescribes the case, because it cannot register the difference between the state killing the passengers and the hijackers killing them, or the fact that the passengers were already doomed. Pick one and defend it. Also tell me where the doctrine is simply silent: the threat to the passengers originates with the hijackers, not the state; the crew may have professional obligations of their own; and there is the mixed case, which I want you to treat as a hypothetical rather than as anything the court decided — would the doctrine endorse a statute limited to aircraft carrying only the perpetrators, and would its verdict there differ in kind or only in degree? Finally, does the doctrine have an evidential dimension, or does one have to be bolted on, given that a real shootdown decision is made in seconds under uncertainty about whether the aircraft is in fact renegade?

One more thing, because my editor will ask. Philosophers reached immediately for the trolley framing when this decision came out. Say whether that framing is faithful: the deaths here look like the means, which puts the case nearer the footbridge than the switch, yet nobody is pushed and the danger to the passengers is not of the state's making. If the episode is structurally unlike both variants, say so and explain what the trolley frame hides.

Deliverable: 1,800 to 2,200 words of prose under a few subheads. No bibliography, no citations, no invented references — describe positions and lines of objection, and name well-known figures only where they are matters of common knowledge. Assume I know the standard history of the trolley problem; do not recap it. Where you are relying on recall rather than on my summary and are not confident, mark it. Close with two or three sentences I could actually print: what this episode, on your analysis, tells a general reader about the difference between a thought experiment and a law.
