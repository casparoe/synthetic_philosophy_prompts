My partner's brother Kieran is a materials engineer and their housemate Ruth did a philosophy degree, and the two of them went at it for close to two hours last week while I mostly just sat there eating chips. I've tried to write it up as a numbered sequence, because they kept sliding past each other and I genuinely can't tell which steps land.

Background they both accept: Ruth bought a phone with a shear-thickening gel layer bonded over the back panel — the kind that goes rigid on impact. The panel underneath is ordinary soda-lime glass. In a normal drop, the gel hardens, spreads the load, and the glass survives; a bare panel of the same glass from the same supplier shatters.

1. Kieran: the phone is fragile, end of story. What a drop test tells you is about the assembly, not about the glass. Fragility is a fact about structure, and the structure of that panel hasn't changed one bit.
2. Ruth: on your own terms its not fragile. "Fragile" just means it would break if struck or dropped. This one wouldn't. So it isn't.
3. Kieran: that's the simple conditional analysis, and this is a textbook mask — the glass keeps the intrinsic causal basis of fragility while being prevented from manifesting it. The Lewis-style repair (the basis has to persist through the stimulus) is built for finks, and masking is the well-known case it doesn't fix. So you can't just read the disposition off the bare counterfactual.
4. Ruth: fine, I'll add a normal-conditions clause. But you've got a symmetrical problem: if fragility were nothing but structure, you could never get from the structure to what the thing would do without already smuggling in a counterfactual, and then your "structure does all the work" claim is empty. Dispositions are irreducible — neither the counterfactual nor the categorical base.
5. Kieran: an irreducible power that no possible test could tell apart from the structure isn't doing any work; the structure is what causes everything, so the disposition gets screened off and "fragile" is bookkeeping. And here's the case for you: the manufacturer prototyped a second back panel where the layer instead releases a crosslinker on impact and permanently cures the surface. Identical drop-test results to the gel version. But you'd write a seperate shipping spec for each. Explain that difference without appealing to behaviour.
6. Ruth: easily — they have different modal profiles. Abrade the gel off and the first phone is fragile again; the cured one never will be. That's a difference in what would happen, not in what does happen, and specs are written for what would happen.
7. Kieran: but that difference only ever shows up when the protection is removed, which means the disposition is only detectable through the structure it was supposed to explain — so it's not adding anything. Plus: the gel is bonded on. Is "the back panel" the glass, or the glass-plus-gel? If it's the glass alone, you're ascribing fragility to something nobody owns, ships, or drops. If it's the composite, your argument from preserved structure is dead, because the composite's structure includes the mask.
8. Ruth: object boundaries follow function, and functional boundaries aren't arbitrary. But she did concede the last bit has a cost — the composite is what carries the phone's dispositions, so she has to say the composite is fragile-and-masked rather than the glass underneath being fragile.

Then tell me who's more right overall, and what both of them are missing, if there is something.
