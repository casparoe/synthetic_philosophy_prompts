I am drafting the hinge scene of my third novel and I want the argument in it written properly, not approximated. I am a novelist; I will do the polish. What I need from you is the argumentative spine carried in dialogue, and I want it under constraints tight enough that neither side can cheat.

**The dispute.** I have been reading Rousseau's 1758 letter to d'Alembert on the theatre — his reply to the proposal that Geneva open a playhouse — and what strikes me is how little of it is about the eighteenth-century stage and how much of it is a structural argument about any art that must please the people who pay for it. I want that argument tested against a working novelist in a contemporary setting, in a scene that assumes the reader has never heard of the letter.

**The scene.** Eleven at night, a square outside a repertory cinema in a mid-sized industrial city. A sold-out benefit screening has just ended: a film adapted from the novel of one of the two characters, a book about the closure of the town's last foundry. The auditorium has emptied but the crowd has not gone home; somebody has brought out an accordion and a crate of plastic cups, and a petition demanding a public inquiry into the closure is circulating and getting signed. The two characters stand at the edge of it, smoking, and begin.

- **Ilse Brandt, sixty-two.** Ran a regional theatre for nineteen years and resigned from it eleven years ago; she now convenes citizens' assemblies for the municipality. She is not a puritan and must not be written as one — she loves the thing she left. She holds, and must argue rather than assert, five theses drawn from Rousseau but in her own idiom: (i) a performance's governing law is that it must please its paying audience, so it can confirm what that audience already feels but cannot correct it, and reform would cost it its house; (ii) pity felt from a seat is pity discharged — the spectator weeps, is pleased to have wept, and considers the debt to virtue settled; (iii) comedy makes the man who will not flatter into the joke and the accommodating man into the admirable one, and the same law now governs the vogue for the articulate wrongdoer: the more persuasively a book renders a bad man's inner life, the more it recommends him; (iv) the performer's trade is a trade in duplication and in the esteem of strangers, and the author on a festival stage is selling the same commodity; (v) the alternative to the spectacle is not abstention but the festival — a gathering with no performers and no audience, in which the crowd is its own spectacle. She must be able to describe a festival she actually ran, concretely, and say why her proposal is not nostalgia.

- **Nora Feld, forty-six.** The novelist. Her book was the source of tonight's film. She must not defend art on the grounds that art needs no defence, or that it is intrinsically valuable, or that it raises awareness. She holds a mechanism and must stick to it: individuation. A book supplies the inside of a person the reader will never meet and has no incentive to consider, over a duration — hours, days, a fortnight — that no speech, poster or petition can match, and supplies it as a particular rather than a case. She should concede thesis (i) outright, then distinguish: the pressure to please is economic rather than formal, and a book paid for once and read alone is under weaker pressure than a nightly box office. She should also say honestly, when pressed, what her own advance and the adaptation deal did to her ending. And she should turn the tables: Rousseau wrote the letter against the stage and then wrote one of the most tear-producing novels of his century, prefaced it with a fictional dialogue in which the usefulness of such a book is argued over, and let his readers weep — so either his practice contradicts his theory, or his theory permits a form he refused the stage, in which case his objection is not about representation as such.

**What each side is allowed.** Both must land at least two blows the other cannot parry, and each must concede at least one substantive point that costs something. Ilse's best material should come from the film itself: the most vivid and most sympathetically written character in it is the plant manager, the retired fitters have been reduced to a chorus, and the ending was changed so that the manager resigns on principle rather than being made redundant alongside the men. Nora's best material should come from the square: three former fitters who recognised their own speech in the book, the two hundred signatures the petition is collecting while they talk, and the fact that the film gave a closure its shape so that people could grieve it publicly instead of privately as personal failure. Neither may claim the evening as evidence outright; the counterfactual — would the square have assembled without the film, would anyone have read the book without the closure — is not testable, and at least one of them should say so.

They should also discover an agreement roughly a third of the way in, and it should narrow the dispute rather than soften it: both think the economy of celebrity that has grown up around art is corrupting, and both think most of what is streamed is flattery.

**The ending.** No conversion and no draw declared. The axis must shift: by the last exchanges they are no longer arguing about whether representation moves people but about whether a form that must please can ever turn on its own audience. The final beat should be Nora noticing, in real time, that the square she is standing in is already becoming material for her next book — Ilse's fourth thesis landing on her while she is arguing against it. She refuses to draw the abstention conclusion from it and says why, but she concedes the diagnosis. The shift must be legible from what they say, not from narration telling us about it.

**Craft constraints.** Scene runs 1,400 to 1,900 words. Dialogue carries it: no more than 250 words of narration in total across the scene, for setting, gesture and business. No single turn longer than 140 words, and nobody speaks three times running. Each character interrupts and is interrupted at least once, and one interruption must change the subject for the better part of a page before the argument resumes in a different register. Somewhere in the middle a non-discursive event must break in — the accordion striking up, somebody from the film crew asking Nora for a line for the local paper, a stranger handing her a folded letter — and it must either give them a concrete object to argue about or force the argument into a new key. Speech tags are *said* and *asked* or nothing at all; no adverbs on tags; no *she said, exasperated*. The register is intelligent lay speech: neither of them uses the words *narrative*, *discourse*, *problematic*, *trauma* or *authentic*, and neither ends more than two turns in a row on a rhetorical question. Between them, a character may summarise the other's position back to them at most once, and never as a way of stalling.

Do not quote Rousseau. Not one line, not one clause in quotation marks, from the letter or from the novel or from the prefatory dialogue. The characters may each refer once to what Rousseau argued, in their own words, and I want them shown remembering imperfectly: if Ilse attributes to the letter a claim that is really her own extension of it, let that stand uncorrected.

**What would count as cheating.** Ilse conceding for sentimental reasons; Nora scoring by wit rather than by argument; the crowd in the square being used as evidence that could only support one side; the dispute being dissolved by an external interruption, a change of subject, or an embrace.

After the scene, give me a note of about 300 words: which of Ilse's five theses held up best in a contemporary register and which had to be softened or abandoned, and why; the strongest reply available to each side that neither character used; whether the disagreement as you have staged it is substantive or partly verbal — specifically, whether Ilse and Nora mean the same thing by an audience being moved; and what you would have to change to make Ilse win outright.
