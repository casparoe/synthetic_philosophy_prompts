So I chair the committee for the pass festival in our valley — let me explain, because it's the whole reason I'm writing. Our town and Ostermund, eight miles up the road, have for as long as anybody living can remember done this thing on the first Saturday of Lent: each town carries a stone from its own riverbank up to the cairn on the pass between us, the two bells get rung at the same moment, we swap a loaf and a stone, some kids read poems they wrote, brass band, then everyone drinks in the field. Supposedly it marks a pact from 1525 that ended a border feud over grazing. This year we're billing it as the 500th anniversary and I've been asked to give the address, about six minutes, right before the handover.

The problem is my niece. She's a historian, works on nationalism and that sort of thing, and she found the paperwork in the county archive. The custom was cooked up in 1898 by a schoolmaster, a newspaper editor and — she's pretty sure — the railway company that had just opened the branch line, right in the years when the valley dialect was being flattened out by the new national school system, and partly to cool down a lawsuit over grazing rights that was very real and very recent. The 1525 pact doesnt appear in a single document from before 1898. The first walk was 1899, so it's been 126 years and not 500. She'll be in the front row, along with the mayor of Ostermund, four generations of families who have walked the procession, a brass band, and about ninety children who mean their poems.

I can't lie. I won't stand up there and let people think the cairn is five centuries old, and I won't do the weasel version either, "for hundreds of years," when I know exactly what year it started. But I also refuse to give the speech where I tell these people their beloved custom is a Victorian marketing stunt — that isn't true either, and it would poison the thing here for a decade.

Write the address for me. It has to leave the two towns feeling genuinely bound to each other and leave those kids wanting to walk it next year, it has to use nothing but what's true, and it has to absorb the 1898 fact without turning my niece's research into a correction of everyone in the field. Then tell me separately what you decided to do about the anniversary number, since that's the bit I've been going back and forth on all week.
