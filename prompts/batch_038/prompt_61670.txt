Write a joke concerning costly signalling and the handicap principle, of the kind that might be told at a behavioural ecology conference dinner rather than in a general-audience comedy set.

The set-up should place two researchers — or one researcher and a sceptical layperson — in front of a conspicuous biological display, such as a peacock's train, a red deer's roar, or the elongated tail feathers of a widowbird. The punchline must turn on the substance of signalling theory rather than on a generic gag about animals, scientists, or research funding. In particular, the humour should draw on at least two of the following three points, and should not depend on any other piece of technical content:

1. The distinction, in the tradition of Maynard Smith and Harper, between an *index*, which is reliable because its magnitude is physically constrained by the underlying quality and therefore cannot be faked, and a *handicap*, which can in principle be faked but is not worth faking.
2. The fact that, in Grafen-style models, honesty is secured by the *differential* cost of signalling across types rather than by the absolute size of the cost — since the marginal cost of a given display level rises more steeply for a low-quality signaller. Consequently a signal can be perfectly honest while being, for the individual who can genuinely afford it, quite cheap; and a signaller's honesty is a matter of his budget rather than his character.
3. The embarrassment for Zahavi's original formulation that a display is informative precisely *because* it is wasteful, so that the peacock who offers a lucid explanation of why his tail is an honest signal is thereby drawing attention to the one thing his audience cannot verify.

Further constraints. The joke should run to no more than roughly 150 words. It must be intelligible to a reader with no formal training, requiring no mathematical notation, no equations, and no knowledge of any specific empirical study — yet it should land appreciably harder for someone who has actually solved a costly-signalling model than for someone who has merely heard the phrase "survival of the fittest peacock". Please avoid the stock punchlines in this territory: vanity, sexual selection as matchmaking, mating success as the reward for extravagance, and any joke whose final line is a pun on "handicap" in the sporting or the pejorative sense.

Present the joke on its own, and follow it with two or three sentences — clearly marked as a note rather than as part of the joke — identifying which of the three conceptual points above the punchline actually turns on, and saying what would be lost if the set-up were swapped for a different display trait. Write your answer throughout in British English.
