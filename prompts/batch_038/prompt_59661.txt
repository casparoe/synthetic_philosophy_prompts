Next term I'm co-teaching an upper-level seminar that pairs the contemporary analytic literature on blame with a long nineteenth-century novel, and our unit on the standing to blame will be built around Dostoevsky's *Crime and Punishment*. The philosophy half of the unit covers roughly the current map: the idea that blame is a form of second-personal address that presupposes the blamer's own commitment to the norm invoked, and that the hypocrite wrongs the person blamed by refusing to treat them as a moral equal (Wallace); the dispositional account on which hypocrisy consists in making an unwarranted exception of oneself, thereby offending against the equality of persons that is said to ground standing (Fritz and Miller); Todd's project of unifying the several grounds on which standing is allegedly lost — hypocrisy, meddling, complicity, ignorance of the wrong, and being oneself subject to manipulation — under a single rationale about what it takes to be in a position to make a moral demand at all; and the dissenting line holding that hypocritical blame is objectionable because of what it reveals about the blamer's character or because of its epistemic unreliability, rather than because of any forfeiture of an entitlement. I want the novel put to work against that map, not merely illustrated by it.

Here is the reading I'd like you to write, as a handout of perhaps fifteen or twenty minutes' length for students who know the book well but have not internalized the philosophy: take up the question of who, in the novel, is entitled to blame Raskolnikov, and on what grounds the novel answers it. I'm particularly interested in four figures. Luzhin at Katerina Ivanovna's funeral dinner, who slips a hundred-rouble note into Sonia's pocket and then publicly accuses her of theft: is that a case of forfeited standing, or of blame that never had standing because it was manufactured, and does the current literature have the resources to say what is distinctively wrong with it as opposed to a merely insincere accusation? Sonia herself, who tells Raskolnikov to go to the crossroads, bow down to the earth he has defiled, confess publicly, and accept suffering — while being a person whom Petersburg society has stripped of every claim to address anyone. The standing literature locates standing mainly in the blamer's own commitments, but Sonia suggests it may be at least as much a social fact, distributed by others' willingness to hear you; I'd like you to press that thought and say whether it is a genuine challenge or a category confusion. Porfiry Petrovich, the investigator who is convinced of Raskolnikov's guilt and uses intimacy, insinuation, and cat-and-mouse manipulation to produce a confession: he has legal authority and no personal grudge, yet he seems to enjoy the trap, so does his manipulation cost him standing in Todd's sense, and if it does, why does the novel nonetheless treat his demand that Raskolnikov confess and take his suffering as authoritative? And Raskolnikov himself, whose extraordinary-man theory amounts to a claim of exemption from blame, and whose self-accusation — at the police station, and again in the much-disputed Epilogue, where he reports a guilt he does not yet feel — raises the question of whether one can lose standing to blame oneself, and whether self-blame even has standing conditions.

What I don't want is a survey of the theories followed by examples pulled from the book, and I don't want a bibliography, a reading list, or verbatim quotations from the novel or from the philosophical articles; paraphrase will serve, since my students have the translations in front of them. What I do want is an argument about where the two sides genuinely conflict. Specifically: does Dostoevsky dramatize something the standing literature misses, and is the miss structural — for instance, that a theory built on the blamer's own commitment to the norm cannot accommodate a world in which the person best placed to address the wrongdoer is the one whose social position has been destroyed, or a redemption that arrives as a change in the wrongdoer rather than as the restoration of anyone's entitlement? Or does the novel's theological machinery — suffering, expiation, grace, the Epilogue — simply sit outside the secular debate, such that the honest critic should say the book is not really about standing at all and stop forcing it? Take a position, defend it against at least one obvious objection, and be explicit wherever the reading is being imposed on the text rather than drawn from it. Please close with two or three sentences on how you would pitch the Luzhin scene in class so that students see it as a philosophical problem rather than a plot device.
