I run a philosophy-of-economics reading group — about 22 undergrads, one 75-minute session, everyones had a stats course but nobody's had decision theory beyond expected value. I want an in-class game that makes them feel the gap between maximizing expected value and maximizing the growth rate of your wealth across repeated plays. The thing where a bet with a hugely positive expected value still wrecks you if you put too much of your bankroll on it each round, and where the group's total wealth keeps climbing even while almost every individual player is drifting toward zero. I've seen this described as non-ergodicity, or geometric vs arithmetic reasoning, but I dont want the session to be a lecture about that vocabulary — I want them to run into it and only get the words afterward.

Here's the gamble I want fixed, and I want you to do the arithmetic before you design anything: each round a fair coin is flipped; a player stakes some fraction f of their current chips; heads they get the stake back plus three times the stake, tails they lose the whole stake. So all-in is literally what expected value tells you to do, and it's also ruin half the time. Work out for me: what fraction maximizes long-run growth, what happens to someone who bets half their chips every round, where the overbetting threshold is past which a positive-expected-value bet makes you poorer over time, and roughly what the spread of final wealth looks like after ~20 rounds across 22 players. Build the design around those numbers rather than around a vibe, and show me the numbers.

Then give me:

- rules and round structure — how many rounds, how stakes get declared and recorded, and how you keep 22 people playing simultaneously without each round eating ten minutes;
- a token scheme that needs no real money and no calculators. I've got index cards, a whiteboard, poker chips, and one laptop that can run a spreadsheet if that actually helps;
- a way to make the divergence between the room's total wealth and the typical individual's wealth visible on the board as it accumulates, so they can see it happen before anyone has explained anything;
- a second stage where the same bet is offered just once, for something they actually care about, and where the reasoning genuinely changes. I want them to notice that it changes and start arguing about why, not be told;
- a debrief that maps particular moments from the run onto the distinction, and that has an answer ready for the kid who bet everything every round, got lucky, and says so it obviously worked;
- the honest part: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this (I'm already worried about the people who bust in round four and then check out for the remaining twenty minutes), and which piece of the idea a chip game with fake stakes simply cannot reproduce, so I know what I have to supply by talking.
