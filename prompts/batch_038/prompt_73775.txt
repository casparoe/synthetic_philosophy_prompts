So — I've spent way too many years on the same argument with a friend who does forecasting for a living, and I've decided the only way to settle it is to stop trying to settle it in prose. The argument: he thinks a model only earns credit when it's surprised, and that the old-evidence problem is basically a bookkeeping artifact you fix by refusing to let any probability hit exactly 1 — smear everything a little, keep the mass movable, and then even a fact you've known since you were nine can in principle move you. I think that's cheating, and that what you actually learn when your theory finally explains the sediment record from 1987 isn't the sediment record, it's the sentence *my theory entails the sediment record*, which was itself uncertain for you right up until you did the algebra. He says fine, but then you need a probability distribution over logical implications, becuase you'd have to hold credences in entailment claims you've never bothered to state, and that's not a thing a brain or a spreadsheet can hold. I think he's right that it isn't holdable. I think that's the actual story and not a footnote.

Can you write me that story? Roughly 1500–2000 words, close third person. Setting, adjust freely: a mid-sized port city in some not-quite-our decade where a municipal tribunal adjudicates "novelty claims" — you get paid when a model of yours retroactively explains a measurement that's already sitting in the public archive, and the payout scales with how much your own credence in the model moved as a result. The case on the docket: an elderly hydrologist (or tide-table clerk, or archivist — your pick) who has just proved that her forty-year-old theory of sediment transport entails a gauge reading the whole town has known since before she was born. Opposing counsel is a hardliner who insists the payment schedule only covers surprises, and that a proof is not an observation. The POV character is the junior clerk who has to compute the number and sign it.

The bit I care most about: make her ledger a physical object with limits. It only has columns for propositions someone has actually thought of, so the moment she tries to carry a probability for the entailment claim she discovers how many such sentences there are — including ones pairing theories she's never read with data she'll never see — and the ledger does not grow to fit. I want a scene where she tries to just adopt my friend's fix, keeps nothing at exactly 1 adn feels briefly enlightened, and then notices that this also renders her confidence in the subtraction she's doing in her head technically revisable, and that this is not a liberating thought. Also: no exposition dumps about Bayes' theorem, and if a character explains the mechanics it should be to an apprentice and should be wrong in a way that turns out to be load-bearing. There's a broken tide gauge subplot that should matter at the end. And please don't let the story resolve the dispute with a clever line — the payout gets made or withheld for reasons that have nothing to do with who's philosophically right, which is exactly the part my friend and I keep talking past.
