I sit on a three-judge panel of a state intermediate appellate court, and I have been assigned to draft in a statutory-interpretation appeal on which my two colleagues have signaled that they may split. Before I write, I want to know what our panel's own record commits us to. The jurisdiction is a composite and every statute below is invented for this exercise; treat the summary as the entire record and do not reach for outside authority, real cases, or citations of any kind.

**Our panel's last six statutory cases, in order:**

1. *Refund deadline.* The revenue code required a claim for refund to be filed "within thirty days of the date the notice of assessment was mailed." The thirtieth day fell on a day the department's offices were closed by executive order, and the taxpayer filed on the next business day. We held the claim timely: a period whose last day cannot be used is not a thirty-day period.

2. *Fireworks.* The fireworks act made it a misdemeanor to "sell fireworks at retail without a permit." A volunteer fire company gave fireworks away at its annual fair and admitted visitors for a suggested donation at the gate. We reversed the conviction. The opinion conceded that every safety concern behind the permit requirement — storage quantities, untrained handlers — applied in full, but held that the operative word was "sell," and that a donation at the gate was not consideration for the fireworks.

3. *Enhanced penalty.* The criminal code enhanced the penalty for assault committed "in the presence of a minor." The defendant assaulted his brother in a bedroom while his nine-year-old daughter, put to bed down the hall, watched on a baby monitor; the trial court found that she saw it. We affirmed the enhancement, holding that "presence" describes what the child perceives rather than where her body is.

4. *Hearing request.* A licensing statute enacted in 1987 required that a licensee's request for a hearing be "in writing and delivered to the board." In 2011 the legislature amended the neighboring section of the same chapter to permit filings "in writing or by electronic means" and left the hearing-request provision alone. The licensee had emailed. We held the email insufficient, resting on the meaning of "written" at enactment and on the 2011 amendment.

5. *County residence.* A property-tax exemption for disabled veterans required that the applicant "have resided in the county for the one year preceding the application." The applicant had been domiciled in the county for eleven years but spent the fourteen months before applying in a state hospital sixty miles away under a civil commitment order. We held him eligible, reasoning that "resided" carries its legal sense of domicile, which involuntary absence does not defeat.

6. *Kitchen equipment.* The health code required every commercial kitchen to have "a portable extinguisher rated for grease fires." A restaurant had instead a fixed Class K suppression system over every cooking surface — more effective, and what the fire marshal's current guidance recommends — but no portable unit. We affirmed the citation: a superior device is not the device the provision names, and the operator's route was the variance process. The opinion noted that the restaurant association had twice asked the legislature to modernize the provision and twice failed.

**The new case.** Section 12 of the watercraft safety act, enacted in 1994, provides: "No person shall operate a motorized watercraft within two hundred feet of a marked swimming area." It was adopted after a jet skier struck a swimmer at a reservoir beach; the sponsor's floor statement refers to "thundering ski boats." The defendant was operating a stand-up paddleboard fitted with a factory electric-assist motor that adds propulsion up to four miles per hour. He was inside the marked boundary of a reservoir swimming area in mid-afternoon in July and does not dispute that he knew the area was marked; under power, the board covered the two hundred feet from the boundary line to the swimmers in roughly a minute. He was convicted. Two further facts matter. In 2009 the legislature amended the act's registration section to define "vessel" to include "electric-assist craft," without touching section 12. And last session — after this prosecution began — a bill rewriting section 12 to cover "any craft propelled by machinery, however powered" passed one chamber and died in the other.

The defendant argues that "motorized watercraft" meant, to the enacting legislature, craft propelled by an engine; that reading the phrase to reach a paddleboard whose motor assists a person traveling at walking speed stretches the words past anything that body had in view; and that the 2009 amendment shows the legislature's own understanding that electric-assist craft had to be named. The state argues that the board has a motor and is watercraft, that the text contains no speed or power threshold, and that the hazard section 12 addresses — a powered craft that can close on a swimmer faster than the swimmer can get clear — is squarely present.

**Please draft the bench memo I will circulate to my colleagues before conference, addressing the following:**

(a) Lay out the candidate interpretive commitments that could account for all six precedents — at least three, stated precisely enough to be applied rather than labeled. For each, explain how it handles the precedents that seem to pull the other way. In particular, show how a commitment to resolving ambiguity in penal provisions against the state accommodates case 3, and say frankly whether any candidate rule has to treat one of our decisions as an outlier or as poorly reasoned.

(b) Apply each candidate to the new case and report whether it yields conviction, reversal, or no determinate answer. Where a rule runs out, identify what further input it needs — above all, whether our record supplies any method for fixing the level of generality at which a statute's purpose is stated, since the outcome may turn on whether section 12's purpose is "protecting swimmers from powered craft" or "stopping fast boats near beaches."

(c) Recommend a disposition, and identify which of the six precedents is the closest analog and why it is closer than those pointing the other way. State the strongest consideration on the losing side in terms I could carry into the opinion without caricaturing it, and name the precedent that will be hardest for us to distinguish if we rule that way.

(d) State the general principle the panel adopts either way and what it commits us to in the run of future cases. Address whether the 2009 amendment and last session's failed bill do any work at all here, and whether the negative-implication inference from the 2009 amendment is even available if the phrase already covered the device.

(e) Name one further case, describable in a paragraph, that our record does not decide and on which the candidate rules split most cleanly — a variant on these facts is fine, such as whether a craft whose only motor drives a bilge pump and a GPS is "motorized," or whether section 12 reaches the same board at an unmarked, unbuoyed shoreline. Tell me what each candidate rule predicts there and which of them that case would eliminate.

Write in American English, in the register of a memo to colleagues rather than a law review article, with the recommended disposition stated up front. Aim for 1,100 to 1,500 words.
