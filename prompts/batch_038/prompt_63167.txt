I've got 18 months of bought-out time as a postdoc and I want to spend it on the "why here and not there" family of questions in comparative economic history — the Needham-style stuff, why industrialisation took off in northwestern Europe rather than in the Yangzi delta, that whole literature. But the more of it I read the more I think there's a prior problem nobody wants to touch: before you can answer a contrastive question you have to know what it's actually asking, and I'm not convinced most of these questions are well posed at all.

Here's the half-baked thing I've been working on, I call it the foil discipline. Roughly: a contrastive historical question is only well posed once you've pinned down three seperate things — (1) the slice, i.e. what date or stretch the two cases are really being compared at, since the answer flips depending on whether you're looking at 1400, 1750, or 1850; (2) the similarity space, i.e. in what respect the two cases are supposed to be comparable enough to be contrasted in the first place; and (3) the contrast target, whether you're contrasting outcomes (who industrialised first) or trajectories/rates (who was pulling ahead faster). My suspicion is that these three come apart constantly and people slide between them mid-argument, so a lot of the famous "disagreements" in the divergence literature are really just different authors answering different questions and talking past each other.

So what should I actually do with my 18 months? Candidate lines, though tell me if I've missed the real one:

- sharpen the semantics — build the foil discipline into something precise enough that you could apply it to actual papers and show exactly where they equivocate
- the selection problem — we only ever contrast the cases that survived and left records, so the foil is drawn from a badly biased set
- whether macro-counterfactuals spanning centuries are evaluable at all, or whether the whole contrastive project is just unfalsifiable decoration
- skip the meta-work, pick one live case (the divergence question itself) and try to settle it

Rank these for me and defend the ranking. I want the reasoning more than the list: which ones are upstream of the others, which are tractable for a single person with no data-science team and no money for archival work, and which would leave the field exactly where it started even if I nailed them. Also say what would have to happen — some result, some new evidence, someone else publishing first — for you to re-order things. And be blunt about whether the foil discipline is a real contribution or just a repackaging of the contrastive-explanation material philosophers of science have had for decades, because if its the latter I'd rather find that out now than in month sixteen.
