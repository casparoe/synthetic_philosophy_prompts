**DRAFT: "Recruitment and the Two Senses of Normal Conditions"**

**§1.** Teleosemantics holds that a state's content is fixed by its proper function, and proper function is fixed by a selection history: an item has the function F because ancestors of it did F and were reproduced in virtue of doing it, and it is deployed under the conditions in which ancestors succeeded. The machinery is meant to solve the disjunction problem — to explain why a state caused by both flies and by bee-bees means *fly* rather than *fly-or-bee-bee* — by making the historical pedigree, and not the current causal covariance, the determinant of content. The role of "normal conditions" is essential here, because it is what separates the conditions the mechanism was selected to work in from the conditions in which it merely happens to fire.

**§2.** My claim is that "normal conditions" does two distinct jobs that are systematically run together. Call the first **operational normality**: the background conditions under which the item, once triggered, actually succeeds at what it is now deployed to do — adequate illumination, no malfunction, the right downstream coupling. Call the second **etiological normality**: the conditions under which the item's ancestors did the thing that got them reproduced, i.e. the historical environment that fixed the function in the first place. In the textbook cases these coincide, which is why the ambiguity has been invisible: the frog's fly-detector operates under the same conditions that selected it, so one phrase can serve both roles without strain.

**§3.** Recruitment cases break the coincidence. A mechanism with a well-established function F is co-opted into a new circuit where it now contributes to G, and it is G-related success that is now being rewarded. Candidate examples: an approximate-quantity system recruited into symbolic arithmetic; object- and edge-recognition machinery recruited into letter and word identification; threat-detection circuitry recruited into social evaluation. In every such case operational normality now points to the G-enabling conditions while etiological normality still points to the F-enabling conditions, and the two come apart precisely where content assignment matters.

**§4.** So what does the recruited state represent? Index content to etiological normality and the states subserving arithmetic get the content *roughly this many*, not *seven*; the states subserving reading get *there is a dark vertical edge of this length*, not *there is a B*. Those are the wrong contents, and they are wrong in a way that cannot be repaired by adding a disjunction, because the states are reliably and non-accidentally right about sevenhood and B-hood. Index content to operational normality instead and you get the intuitively correct contents — but operational normality is fixed by the item's current causal role in the whole system and by the environment it currently sits in, which is exactly what selected-effects accounts were built to make irrelevant. Consumer teleosemantics therefore faces a dilemma: in recruitment cases it either assigns the wrong contents or becomes a causal-role semantics wearing a historical vocabulary.

**§5.** The natural repair is to weight. Let proper function be a weighted sum over the item's selection history, with more recent selection weighted more heavily, so that content drifts from F toward G as the recruitment consolidates. Two problems. First, the weights are unmotivated inside the theory: nothing in the notion of a character reproduced because of the successes of its ancestors delivers a discount rate, so the weighting is an external parameter chosen to match our intuitions about the cases. Second, whatever weighting is chosen, in the limit content will track the fact that G is what the mechanism is currently used for and currently rewarded for — and being currently used for and rewarded for is a fact about the practices of the system's community, not about its phylogeny. The repair thus converts the theory into interpretivism, which is what the naturalistic project set out to avoid.

**§6.** That leaves three options, none comfortable. Bracket recruitment cases as rare — but they are not rare; they look like the normal condition of adult human cognition, and they cover the very capacities whose contents we take to be most determinate. Accept a period of content indeterminacy for every recruited mechanism — which is to concede that a great deal of ordinary thought lacks determinate content. Or admit that "normal conditions" was doing unexplained work all along, work that only an account appealing to an interpreter's perspective can do. My conclusion is deliberately narrow: consumer teleosemantics is not refuted, but its claim to be a genuinely naturalistic theory, rather than a redescription of our interpretive practice in biological language, fails in exactly the region where it was most confident.

**MY FRIEND'S REPLY, which I could not answer.** He makes two moves. First, he says §4 misdescribes the machinery: on the standard account, proper function requires that ancestors were reproduced *because* they did F, and the ancestors of my recruited mechanism were not reproduced because they contributed to G. So the recruited mechanism simply has no proper function with respect to G, and therefore no G-content — not an indeterminate content, but none. He thinks this is a cost I have inflated into a refutation, since the content of a neural state is not the same thing as the content of a person's belief, and it is only the latter that our talk of determinate content is about.

Second, and more seriously, he says a person's belief can inherit content from public symbols, and that the copying-with-selection that sustains a symbolic practice is a perfectly good species of reproduction. If so, the practice itself has a reproductively established character, etiological normality applies to it directly, and the contents of reading and arithmetic are fixed by that history without any appeal to what an interpreter takes anyone to be doing. On his picture my §5 collapse never gets started: I have conflated *an interpretive practice assigning content* with *a practice being selected*, and only the first is interpretivist.

He then accused me of a more general vice: that whenever a selected-effects theory delivers a content I dislike, I call the deliverance "indeterminate" or "collapsing into interpretivism," and that these are placeholders for an argument I have not given. I do not think that is fair, but I am not sure I can show it.
