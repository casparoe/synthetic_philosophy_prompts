**Superrationality is a symmetry premise, not a decision rule**

Hofstadter's move, roughly: two superrational agents facing the same symmetric game will do the same thing, because there's only one correct answer to a well-posed problem and they're both good enough to find it. So the off-diagonal cells of the matrix aren't live options, and in a prisoner's dilemma you compare (C,C) against (D,D) only, and cooperate. The standard complaints are that this is magical thinking about logical causation, or that it's just evidential decision theory wearing a funny hat. I think both complaints miss the load-bearing part.

The load-bearing part isn't the rule (restrict to symmetric profiles, then maximize — that part is trivial). It's the premise that your opponent's reasoning is transperant to yours: that you can read their move off your own without any empirical evidence about them. On its own, EDT recommends cooperating only when there's some correlation between your choice and theirs, and correlation has to come from somewhere — common cause, news, evidence. Superrationality manufactures the correlation out of a uniqueness claim about correct reasoning. So the honest formula is: EDT + "there is exactly one thing a correct reasoner does in this game, and we're both such a reasoner." That's why it doesn't feel like EDT. EDT needs news; this needs an identity.

Here's where it gets shaky. Give each agent the same prisoner's dilemma, except your temptation payoff is 5.01 and mine is 5.00. The games aren't identical, so the uniqueness premise doesn't apply — nothing guarantees that the correct output in the 5.00 game matches the correct output in the 5.01 game. But intuitively this is exactly the superrational situation and we should cooperate anyway. So what you actually need is a premise about *near*-identity of decision problems, or about similarity of reasoning processes, which requires a metric: how close must two problems be before their solutions are forced to agree? Nobody has one, and without one the theory's scope is fixed by an arbitrary line-drawing.

The second problem I think is worse. Suppose the agents break ties, or play mixed strategies, using private random sources. Then two reasoners running literally the same algorithm diverge, the diagonal stops being a live possibility, and the whole restriction is void. To rescue it you have to assume shared randomness — the same seed, as it were — which is a substantive and frankly strange assumption: what is it for two separate people to be drawing on one random source? I suspect Hofstadter's examples quietly assume the reasoners' outputs are functions of the problem statement alone.

So superrationality isn't a rival decision rule. It's a research program about when two decision procedures are close enough that you can predict one from the other. That's a real question. The label just makes it sound like something you could act on today.
