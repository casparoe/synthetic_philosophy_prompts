I'm giving a paper at a law school workshop in about three weeks and I could really use a Q&A prep sheet. The room is mostly criminal law and contracts people, plus two clinicians who sit on a hospital ethics committee, plus one guy who does experimental work on the folk concept of consent. Nobody there works on consent theory full time, which is exactly the problem: my view is fairly technical and I'm worried it will either bounce off them completely or get torn apart on workability grounds. I was arguing about it last week with a friend who's a public defender and she basically told me I'd built a theory that no jury instruction could ever express, and I haven't been able to shake that.

The view: consent is valid just in case the description under which the agent endorsed the act matches the description under which the act is actually performed, where what counts as "the description" is fixed by what the agent herself was treating as decision-relevant, not by what a reasonable person would have cared about. So deception doesn't void consent because deception is bad, or because it causes harm, or because it violates a disclosure duty — it voids consent only when it produces a mismatch at the level the agent was actually operating at. Two consequences I'm going to say out loud, both of which I know sound perverse. First, enormous lies can leave consent completely intact: if I agree to a procedure described to me only as "the standard thing your team does for this," and you lie about your own training while doing the standard thing, I endorsed exactly what happened. Second, a trivial deception can destroy consent: if the single thing I framed my yes around was "nobody else will be in the room," then a lie about precisely that voids it, even though nothing about the act changed and I ended up better off. I argue for this in two steps — that consent is a performative and performatives fail on description mismatch rather than on harm, and that this story explains why the law already carves out fraud in the factum from fraud in the inducement better than any materiality test does, and explains why the informed consent standard keeps sliding toward what this particular patient would want to know. On competence I run something parallel: competence isn't general rationality or the capacity to understand and weigh information, it's the capacity to hold a description fixed across a decision, so a brilliant person who keeps re-describing the act to herself mid-process can fail it while a person with a cognitive disability who holds a thin but stable description can pass. Coercion I also treat as a mismatch-maker rather than as option-removal — the agent endorses "I'm taking this job" and what gets performed is "I'm working off a debt under threat," and that gap is what does the invalidating.
