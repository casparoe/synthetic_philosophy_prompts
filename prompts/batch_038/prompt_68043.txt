so I'm a junior and I'm on my school's student council, and there's this thing blowing up in my town: in 1968 our district shut down the Black high school a couple years after integration and turned the building into a bus garage, and it's still a bus garage. Some people on the board want to do a formal apology this spring and there's a public comment night where students can speak, and I signed up because I want to say what I actually believe instead of just vibes. My friend who's way better at this stuff read my list and said "like three of these can't all be true at once" and then wouldn't tell me which. So can you do an actual audit on it instead of a lecture?

Here's what I hold:

1. Nobody alive is to blame for what that board did. Guilt doesn't get passed down, and I hate it when people online make whole groups of living people apologize for being alive.
2. The district is the same organization that did it. Same name, same charter, and it's still the district that owns the old school and gets the use of it. So it's the one that can apologize.
3. But also, when you get down to it, "the district" is just shorthand for people. Only people do things. Institutions don't literally act, that's just how we talk.
4. The apology has to say the closing was wrong, flat out. No "it was a different time," no "everyone was doing it," no context sandwhich.
5. An apology that costs nothing isn't an apology. But the cost can't be money, because the second money moves it's a settlement and not an apology, and then people are just buying quiet. So the district should give up something it actually cares about that isn't cash.
6. Whether it counts isn't up to the district. The people it's aimed at — the alumni who's still alive and their families — get to say whether it landed. If they say it didn't, it didn't.
7. It's okay that this also makes the district look good. If doing the right thing never made anybody look good, nobody would ever do one.
8. Current students shouldn't lose anything over this. Not our budget, not our programs, not our class time. I want to help with it, but a 16 year old shouldn't get handed a bill.

What I want back: which of these are genuinely pulling against each other, ranked by how bad it is, worst first — not all flagged as equally suspicious. Which ones only look like they conflict and stop looking that way once you say what's really going on. And for each real one, what's the cheapest thing to give up and what does giving it up cost me, like what sentence I'd have to stop saying at the mic. Then tell me the single revision that cleans up the most, because I'm not rewriting the whole list from scratch.
