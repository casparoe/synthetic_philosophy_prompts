I am preparing a section of a paper on levels of description for a philosophy-of-science reading group, and I have constructed an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept. I want a diagnosis, not encouragement, and not a survey of the positions in the literature. The target of the argument is the middle position I myself hold: macro-level properties that are nothing over and above the microphysical base, yet still autonomous in explanation. I state my stipulations first because the argument depends on them; please work inside them rather than objecting to them — though if a stipulation is doing covert work, that is exactly the sort of finding I am after.

**Stipulations.** For a system S, let M(S) be a complete and true description of S at the fundamental physical level: the exact microphysical state, the initial and boundary conditions, and the laws.

- (D1) A property P is *entailed* for S just in case M(S) logically entails that S is P. This is deliberately an ontic notion: it makes no reference to whether anyone can produce, verify, or compress the entailment.
- (D2) P *adds no causal power* beyond M(S) just in case there is no pair of possible situations that agree throughout in M yet differ in P, and hence none in which P could make a difference that M does not already make.
- (D3) P is *explanatorily autonomous* just in case some phenomenon E is best explained by citing P, and no explanation of E that cites only the predicates of fundamental physics is as good — where "as good" means at least as general, at least as informative about what E counterfactually depends on, and no less unified.
- (D4) Two theories are *notational variants* just in case they have the same models up to relabeling, so that every claim expressible in one is expressible in the other.

**The argument.**

(1) Physicalism: for every property P instantiated by S, P is entailed for S in the sense of D1; otherwise something over and above the physical would be real.
(2) If P is entailed for S, then P adds no causal power beyond M(S) in the sense of D2: the situations in which P would have to earn its causal keep do not exist.
(3) If P adds no causal power beyond M(S), then any true claim couched in P-predicates can be replaced by claims about M(S) *salva veritate* and without loss.
(4) If (3) holds for every predicate of a theory T, then T is a notational variant of the fundamental physical theory in the sense of D4.
(5) Notational variants have identical explanatory content, since whatever is best about the best explanation of E is preserved under relabeling.
(6) Therefore no property satisfies D3, and there is only one legitimate level of description: every special science is dispensable shorthand for a description no one could in fact write down.
(7) Two observations are what make me uneasy about (6). First, the critical point of a simple fluid and the ordering transition of a uniaxial magnet share critical exponents while sharing essentially nothing at the level of M; what does the explanatory work there seems to sit above any particular microphysical description, which looks like a direct counterexample to (6) rather than a relabeling of it. Second, if I try to protect that observation by denying (1) in such cases, then the macro property is not entailed by M at all — which is strong emergence, and given the generality of (1) it would apply equally to being a helium atom and to being a table. So the real conclusion appears to be a dilemma: either there are no autonomous levels, or there are so many that the notion goes trivial, and in either case my middle position is unavailable.

**What I would like from you.**

My own suspicion is that (2) is the weak step — that the transfer from entailment to causal impotence is illegitimate. Tell me whether that suspicion is right or whether (2) is a decoy and the damage is done elsewhere.

Then, in order: (a) identify the step you would reject, and rank the candidate rejections by what they cost me, since several are individually deniable and I want to know which leaves the most of the middle position standing for the least expenditure; (b) explain why the step you pick looked innocent, and which true principle it is a shadow of; (c) say whether you read the argument as invalid — in which case name the slide precisely, and say whether it is an equivocation on "loss" in (3), on "the same" in (5), or on the ontic framing of D1 — or as valid with a merely attractive false premise; (d) tell me what a physicalist who retains (1) through (5) is still entitled to say about the critical-exponents case, if anything, and whether that story is consistent or simply a restatement of the conclusion she was trying to avoid.

I would rather give up the autonomy of the special sciences than give up physicalism, but I want to know whether the argument actually forces that trade, or whether I have mispriced it.
