Help me figure out what principle is actually behind my views on moral trades — I keep giving answers that don't sound consistent with each other. My rough rule going in was: if a trade makes both parties better off, doesn't hurt anyone else, and the price is one I'd genuinely accept, it's fine — and the price I'd accept is the honest measure of how much I really object. Now the cases.

- A friend and I each donate $500 to the other's favorite cause instead of arguing about which is better: good, better than the argument.
- Someone who cares way more about a school board seat pays me $5k to drop out of the race: fine.
- A museum offers me $200k for letters my grandmother wrote me. Nobody else cares about them, I'd never reread them, nobody's hurt. I wouldn't sell, and I think selling would be wrong, but I can't say why.
- A poor guy sells a kidney to a rich patient, both understand the risks, nobody's coerced: fine, and it should be legal — though I wouldn't do it myself.
- $50k for my vote in an election that comes down to a few hundred ballots: not fine.
- A developer pays the loudest objector on my street triple what everyone else got to drop his objection: fine, his objection was his to withdraw — but I feel bad about it, and the bad feeling seems to be about the rest of us not being paid.
- My sister pays me to stop telling our mom about her drinking: fine, a relief.
- A company pays me to keep quiet about a legal but genuinely awful thing I watched happen: not fine.
- I pay a lobbyist to stop lobbying against a bill: fine, arguably praiseworthy.

Letter vs. kidney is the pair I can't handle. No third-party victim in either, both are mine to give, the buyer wants it more, the price is life-changing in both. The sort of account that says personhood-bound things shouldn't be for sale seems to make the kidney the harder thing to sell and the letters the easier, which is backwards from my verdicts.

Is there a principle that gets all nine, or close? State it precisely enough that I can check it clause by clause, say which case motivates each clause, and tell me which of my verdicts you'd drop if a simpler principle forces the choice. Then give me its verdict on two things I havent worked out: whether the queasiness about the paid objector tracks something real or is just envy, and a company that buys my patent for a much better mousetrap purely to shelve it.
