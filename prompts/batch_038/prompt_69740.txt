I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine and I'm putting together a long feature on reward hacking in AI labs, with a sidebar that's meant to be a self-sorting quiz for readers: which position on wireheading do you actually hold. I've got room for maybe 900 words plus the graphic, so eight to ten questions, tops.

The audience is smart general readers who have seen the rat-with-the-lever photo and know the experience machine exists but couldn't tell you who came up with it. So please don't build any question around the experience machine itself — they've already sorted themselves on that one. Use variants and fresher cases instead: the bliss that comes with a recall clause the person refuses to honor, the two identical states where one was earned over thirty years and one was injected Tuesday, the dial that trades one memory per notch for one percent more reward, the person who knows perfectly well it's fake and wants it anyway. And I want at least two of the questions to be about a machine rather than a human — an agent that rewrites its own scoring function so it always reports a win, that kind of thing — because that's what my piece is about and I want readers to catch themselves on whether they're applying the same standards to software as to people.

Here's the thing my interviews keep running into, and it's the reason I want this quiz to exist rather than just a box of definitions. People are arguing past each other on three different axes and nobody in the debate says so out loud: whether the blissed-out state is itself valuable, whether the person's own endorsement or choice changes anything, and whether the causal history of the state does moral work over and above what the state is like. Build the instrument so those three come apart in the scoring instead of collapsing into one liberal/conservative-on-pleasure number. No question should ask about a label — nothing like "do you think pleasure is the only good," which is just asking people to self-report and lets them pick whatever sounds respectable.

Every question a concrete case, two or three answer options each, all of them things a reader could pick without knowing which theory they're implying. Then the scoring key mapping answer patterns onto named positions, and I want the leaves I know are actually out there: straight hedonism, the desires-are-about-the-world view, the achievement or objective-goods view, the autonomy-and-consent view, a reality-contact view if that survives as something distinct from achievement rather than collapsing into it, and the deflationary one where nothing wrong has happened at all and it's just a broken instrument. Name the positions in plain English, not in jargon, and don't send me a reading list or citations — I'll be getting my quotes from interviewees.

Two more things. Tell me which combinations of answers nobody seems to actually hold, and for each of those give me a sentence on why: incoherent, unattractive, or just unexplored. I want to run that as the endnote. And be honest about which pairs of positions the quiz can't really seperate — if two leaves always end up with the same answer pattern, say so flat out instead of pretending the tree splits them.

Last one: don't rig it. I don't want a quiz where every path leads to "you're shallow," because then it's worthless as a diagnostic. Give the pro-wireheading options the strongest version of their case, phrased so their genuinely appealing to a tired person on a bad week, and let the sorting happen on the combinations.
