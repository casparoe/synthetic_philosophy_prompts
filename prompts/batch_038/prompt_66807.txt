I teach a philosophy of law seminar for advanced undergraduates — eighteen students, one 110-minute session — and I want to run a self-amending rule game in the spirit of Nomic: the initial rulebook is handed out at the start, and everything in it, including the amendment procedure itself, is open to change by the players.

My aim is narrower than "students see that rules can change." I want them to run, unassisted, into the bind that arises when the rule conferring the power to amend is applied to itself, and into the standoff that arises when a rule declares itself unamendable and the players first amend the declaration away and then repeal the rule it protected. Ideally they leave on opposite sides of a live dispute about whether something that happened during the session was a valid move, with the rulebook genuinely not settling it — not with a consensus principle that dissolves the problem.

Please design the game. I need the following:

1. The full text of the initial rulebook — short enough to hand out on one page and to be tracked by eighteen people. It must contain at least one self-referential provision and at least one entrenchment clause, and it must impose a delay before an amendment takes effect, so that at some point the question arises whether a move is valid under the rulebook in force when it was proposed or the one in force when it was adjudicated. Write the provisions so that the bind emerges from ordinary play rather than from a move I have to engineer from the front of the room.

2. An in-game procedure for settling disputes about what the rules permit. I do not want to act as referee: my standing authority as instructor is precisely the thing I want them to notice is doing work the rules cannot do. But the dispute-settling procedure will itself be amendable, which seems to reintroduce the problem one level up. Tell me how your design handles that, or why it does not matter.

3. A scoring or winning condition that requires no money and gives players a reason to keep amending rather than settling into ordinary play. I am open to the winning condition being deliberately unappealing, if that is what generates the amendments, but say which route you took and why.

4. A minute-by-minute timeline for the 110 minutes, including how the rulebook is distributed and how turns are ordered among eighteen people without most of them idling.

5. A debrief that maps specific moments from the play onto the distinctions that matter: a rule of change as opposed to whatever it is that ultimately validates the rule of change; a rule that has not yet been amended as opposed to a rule that cannot be amended; validity as a matter of pedigree as opposed to validity constrained by content; and a gap in the rulebook as opposed to a contradiction inside it.

6. An honest account of what will go wrong the first time I run this — including the real possibility that no one thinks to make the self-referential move at all, and how I can nudge the group toward it without naming it — together with the features of the underlying puzzle the game cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by hand. I suspect it cannot reproduce the fact that entrenched rules in a real system bind people who were absent when they were made and who cannot simply outvote them, and that the brevity of a single session accounts for the rest; tell me whether that is the right diagnosis or whether the more serious shortfall lies elsewhere.
