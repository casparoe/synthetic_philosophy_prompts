I am in the closing weeks of a seminar on the philosophy of life's meaning, and this week's set text was Schlick's short essay "On the Meaning of Life." My instructor has taken to compressing the entire argument into one formula — "the meaning of life is play," sometimes with the addition, as though it amounted to the same claim, that it is youth — and he deploys the formula to close discussion rather than to open it. In a single session it did that work twice. When I asked how the formula is supposed to separate the absorbed, self-justifying engagement the essay praises from ordinary time-killing that appears to fit the same description (I had in mind a classmate who spends most of her waking hours inside a game and reports precisely the loss of self-consciousness and the sense of activity worth pursuing for its own sake that the essay treats as the mark of meaning), I was told that this was a question the slogan had already settled. When I asked whether the view is not simply hedonism under another name, and therefore exposed to the familiar objection that pleasure taken in worthless or cruel objects cannot make a life meaningful, I was told that play is not pleasure. I cannot see how both answers are consistent with one another, and I suspect the difficulty is that the formula is being made to do several different jobs at once.

There is a further wrinkle I did not get to raise. As I recall it, the essay insists that what distinguishes play from work is not the kind of activity involved but the activity's relation to purposes: the same doing, undertaken for the sake of its consequences, is work, and undertaken for its own sake, is play. If that is right, then "the meaning of life is play" is either a claim about an attitude rather than an activity, in which case almost anything qualifies and the thesis threatens to be unfalsifiable, or a claim about activities after all, in which case it sits badly with the essay's own distinction.

What I want is a careful unpacking of the formula. Set out the distinct precise claims it could be making, running from the platitudinous to the radical, and be explicit about the differences among them: is "play" functioning as a phenomenological category, an axiological one, a structural claim about chains of purposes, a developmental claim about youth, a stipulation about what "meaning" is to mean, or an empirical generalization about what people in fact find fulfilling? For each reading, tell me whether it is trivially true, substantive and defensible, or false, and then tell me which readings would genuinely license my instructor's two dismissals and which would leave him with nothing. I would also like to know whether the argument depends on sliding between two senses of "meaning" — sense and purpose — or whether that objection misfires, and whether any reading of the formula bears on a choice I am actually facing, between a secure position I would take for its consequences and an uncertain one I would take for the activity itself, or whether it can only console.

Please do not hand me the menu and leave me holding it. Take a position: say which reading you think is the best candidate for a thesis worth defending, what would have to be added to it or surrendered for it to survive the two objections, and what I ought to have said in the room, with your reasons. Write it as continuous prose, the way one would write a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. No references or reading lists; I have no way to check them and I want your reasoning rather than your bibliography. Paraphrase the essay as you understand it, and mark clearly any point where you are unsure whether you are reporting Schlick or reconstructing him.
