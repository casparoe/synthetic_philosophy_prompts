I sit on the board of trustees of the Kettleton Mining Heritage Centre, a small museum in a former colliery town, and I have been asked to settle a dispute that has now run four months and threatens to hold up the redevelopment of our main gallery. The gallery, "The Last Seam", covers Beckside Colliery from the 1979 productivity agreement to its closure in 1992. The point of contention is the 150-word orientation panel that visitors read first, which has to answer the question "Why did Beckside close?". Two drafts are before the board. The first, from our academic adviser — a recently retired economic historian who spent two years in the regional records of the nationalised industry — lists five factors and assigns each a share of the explanation in percentage terms: the exhausted and geologically difficult seams (30%), the reorientation of national energy policy towards gas and nuclear (25%), the availability of cheaper imported coal (20%), the pit's own productivity record (15%), and the 1988 corporate decision to concentrate investment in a handful of much larger "super pits" (10%). The second, drafted by the Beckside Former Miners' Association and backed by two trustees, tells the story as a decision: the pit was viable enough to keep, and it was closed because those with the power to decide wanted it closed. We vote on Thursday, the fit-out contractor needs final text by the end of the month, and the gallery opens in eleven weeks. I should declare an interest of a sort — my grandfather hewed coal at Beckside, and I find the second draft more congenial than I probably ought.

What has made this so hard to resolve, and I suspect the reason the two sides keep talking past each other, is a problem neither draft faces up to. Our adviser's own archival work turned up something awkward for him: the regional board drew up a closure programme in 1986 that included Beckside on grounds unrelated to the pit's accounts, and separately a 1988 internal study projected that the remaining workable reserves would run out within four years whatever subsidy was provided. Each of those routes, he concedes, would have produced closure by 1992 on its own. If that is right, neither was a but-for cause — the closure would have happened without either, and yet it is absurd to conclude that nothing caused it. He answers that his percentages are not about but-for dependence at all but about what he calls "explanatory weight", though he has not told the board what that means, how the numbers were arrived at, or why they should sum to a hundred. The Association, for its part, argues that the two routes were never independent — the concentration decision was taken partly *because* the reserve position was known — and that a panel telling visitors the closure was over-determined will be heard as saying nobody is answerable for it, which, after three decades of being told their town failed itself, is the one message they will not tolerate.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the trustees on Tuesday, in British English, no longer than two sides. It should recommend a concrete decision — adopt one of the two drafts, or adopt a third approach that you specify — and give trustees reasons they could act on rather than a survey of what philosophers and historians have said about causation. Specifically, I need it to do four things. First, explain in plain language but without fudging what the adviser's concession does to the question "what caused the closure", and whether a panel can honestly name one thing as *the* reason when two independent routes would each have got there on their own; part of that, I think, is whether our question is even well posed, since "Why did Beckside close?" may not be the same question as "Why did it close in 1992 rather than in 1988?" or "Why did it close rather than run at half capacity?". Second, give a verdict on whether a percentage weighting of causes can be defended at all, and if it cannot, say what should replace it on a 150-word label without losing the fact that some of the five factors mattered more than others. Third, take the Association's objection at its strongest — including the possibility that they are causally right and not merely wounded, if the concentration decision pre-empted the running-down of the reserves rather than simply accompanying it — and say what follows from that for the wording. Fourth, list the claims the final panel must not make, given what our evidence can and cannot actually support. I would rather the memo told the board that the dispute as framed is the wrong dispute than that it split the difference between the two drafts.
