I am putting together a seminar case study on the authority of advance directives, and I want a disciplined analysis of the Nancy Cruzan litigation — the Missouri case that produced Cruzan v. Director, Missouri Department of Health — read against Ronald Dworkin's account of precedent autonomy and the objections to it. I know the broad outlines of the case; what I need is the careful mapping and an honest verdict on how well the theory fits.

Here are the facts I want you to work from. In January 1983, Nancy Cruzan, then twenty-five, lost control of her car on a Missouri road, was thrown into a water-filled ditch, and was deprived of oxygen for long enough to cause irreversible cortical damage; she emerged from coma into a persistent vegetative state and was maintained on a surgically implanted gastrostomy tube for nearly seven years. She had left no living will. When her parents asked the hospital to withdraw the tube and the hospital refused without a court order, the Missouri trial court authorized withdrawal, relying in part on a finding that Nancy had said, in a serious conversation with a housemate, that if she were sick or injured "she would not wish to continue her life unless she could live at least halfway normally." The Supreme Court of Missouri reversed, holding those conversational statements unreliable for determining intent and refusing to let anyone assume the choice for an incompetent absent the statutory formalities or clear and convincing, inherently reliable evidence. The United States Supreme Court affirmed 5–4 in June 1990: a competent person has a constitutionally protected liberty interest in refusing unwanted medical treatment, but a state may demand clear and convincing evidence of an incompetent's prior wishes before that treatment is withdrawn, given the irreversibility of the error, the risk that family members are not disinterested witnesses to what the patient wanted, and the state's interest in the protection of human life. On remand that winter, additional friends testified to further statements of Nancy's, the trial court found the standard satisfied and authorized removal, and she died shortly thereafter.

Please give me the analysis in both directions. First, sort out what question the case was actually about. My own reading is that the litigation was overwhelmingly interpretive rather than authorizing: almost no one argued that Nancy's wishes, once established, should be overridden, and the entire dispute concerned whether they had been established at all. If that is right, say what work Dworkin's machinery is being asked to do in a case where the later self — irreversibly vegetative, with no experiential life left — arguably has no interests of the kind his distinction between critical and experiential interests is designed to weigh. Dresser's objection to Dworkin gets its grip precisely where the contented demented patient still has experiences worth having from the inside; tell me whether that objection has any purchase at all in persistent vegetative state, and if not, what is left to justify honoring the directive — and for whose sake. Second, run the other candidates: a Parfitian reductionist view on which psychological continuity has come apart entirely, the Buchanan-and-Brock style competence and authenticity conditions on directives, and Missouri's Living Will formalism against a merely conversational expression of wishes. Which of these actually has something to say about Cruzan, and which is being invoked only because it sounds apt?

Then turn the case back on the theory. I want three things in particular. What does the clear-and-convincing standard do to the neutrality of the framework — a rule that resolves every evidential indeterminacy in favor of continued biological existence seems to presuppose a substantive claim about the value of that existence for a patient with no experience of it, and I would like that presupposition named and tested rather than assumed. What should we make of the fact that the standard was satisfied on remand once the litigation had publicized what the family needed, and that the earlier self's wishes were thereby reconstructed under evidentiary pressure by the people with the strongest motive to reconstruct them — does that undermine the very notion of the directive as the patient's own authorship, or is it simply what authorship looks like when the author cannot speak? And finally: is Cruzan a confirmation of Dworkin's position, an anomaly for it, or a kind of case it was never built for — one in which the right-to-die and the anti-euthanasia camps were fighting about evidence while the interesting philosophical disagreement lay elsewhere entirely?

Where a framework is simply silent on the case, say so rather than stretching it to speak. Do not give me a bibliography or a list of further sources; I want the argument. Close by telling me how confident you are in the claims you have made, and single out the point you are least sure of — including any place where you suspect you are misremembering the case or misdescribing one of the positions.
