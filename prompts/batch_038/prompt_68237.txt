I am a third-year philosophy student in a seminar on nineteenth-century critiques of mass society, and our first weeks have been spent on Kierkegaard's 1846 literary review of Two Ages, with its portrait of a reflective, passionless present age and its claim that "the public" is an abstraction the daily press conjures into being rather than a body it merely reports on. Since then a single formula has been circulating in our discussions as a way of closing them down: "the crowd is untruth."

The occasion that prompts my question is a rather odd one. Our department recently ran a pilot in place of the usual student assembly on a proposed policy governing generative-AI use in coursework. Instead of debate, a dashboard ingested the free-text comments of roughly four thousand undergraduates, clustered them with a language model, and published an hourly, engagement-weighted summary of what students are said to think. A classmate objected to the entire design in one sentence — the crowd is untruth, therefore a dashboard of four thousand opinions can tell you nothing — and the conversation moved on. I suspect the objection is right or wrong depending entirely on what it asserts, and I would like to be precise before I write my paper on whether such a dashboard counts as a new species of the press Kierkegaard had in view.

Could you lay out, as a numbered menu, the distinct theses that one sentence could be expressing? Please vary the parameters explicitly: what sort of thing the crowd is on each reading (an assembled physical multitude, a statistical aggregate that never meets, or a category manufactured by media institutions); what sort of failing "untruth" names (holding false beliefs, being an unreliable belief-forming process, or a normative incapacity whereby no single individual can be held responsible for anything, so that nothing is truly held at all); the scope of the claim (all collectives, or only mediated ones); and its force (a contingent empirical generalization versus a conceptual impossibility). For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude nobody disputes, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false — including where it runs into familiar considerations about aggregation, such as the fact that forecasting pools and juror-style aggregation often outperform individuals, and that the dashboard's clusters are after all computed from individual responses.

Most importantly, say which readings would actually license my classmate's dismissal of the dashboard's epistemic value, and which would leave the objection standing only against something narrower: for instance against engagement weighting, against treating the summary as a mandate for action, or against the substitution of measurement for deliberation, rather than against the aggregate as a source of information. I would also like to know which reading you think someone repeating the phrase in a seminar most likely intends, and which reading does the most philosophical work.

Finally, please distinguish carefully between what is settled and what remains genuinely contested among people who work on this material. I gather there are live disputes about whether the target here is democratic politics as such or specifically the institution of the daily press and the category of "the public" it produces, about whether the formula is chiefly a religious claim concerning the single individual before God rather than a claim in social epistemology, and about how much continuity there is between the 1846 review and the later polemic against the state church. Where the scholarly situation is unsettled, present the range of positions and say plainly that it is unsettled rather than offering one interpretation as the consensus. I am not asking for references or a reading list; I can chase those down in the seminar bibliography myself.
