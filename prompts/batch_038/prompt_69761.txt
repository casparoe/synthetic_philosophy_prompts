**Question (25 marks):** State the paradox of deontology. Then explain and critically assess two responses to it: the appeal to agent-relative value, and threshold deontology.

**Departmental descriptors:** 18–25 = precise statement of the paradox, plus evaluation of each response that identifies what it concedes and what it blocks; 12–17 = accurate statement with some genuine critical engagement; 8–11 = broadly relevant but containing substantive philosophical errors; below 8 = major misunderstanding of the material.

**Student's answer:**

Deontology is characterised by agent-centred constraints: prohibitions on performing acts of certain kinds even when performing them would minimise the number of violations of the very same prohibition. If a surgeon can save five dying patients by killing one healthy visitor for his organs, the constraint forbids the killing, even though the outcome in which she acts contains one killing rather than five.

The paradox arises because deontology appears committed to both of the following. First, killings are bad, so a state of affairs containing five killings is worse than one containing a single killing. Second, it is impermissible to bring about the one-killing state when one could instead let the five-killing state come about. Morality therefore directs the agent to realise the worse of two outcomes, which seems incompatible with the whole point of having a morality: morality is supposed to make the world better, and here it forbids exactly the act that would do so. Because the two claims cannot both be true, deontology is straightforwardly self-contradictory: it entails both that the surgeon ought to kill the visitor and that she ought not.

One response is to deny the first claim by appealing to agent-relative value. On this view, the badness of a killing is not a neutral feature of states of affairs that anyone has reason to minimise; it is indexed to agents. That I would be the killer is what gives me reason not to kill, and that is the whole of the killing's deontic significance. The response therefore concedes that the five-killing state is worse in the agent-neutral sense, but denies that agents are always required to prevent what is worse. Since the requirement to promote the good is a separate thesis from the claim that the good is agent-neutral, the deontologist can accept the axiology and reject the maximizing principle, and the paradox loses its bite.

Threshold deontology holds that constraints bind only up to a point: when the consequences of honouring a constraint are sufficiently dire, the constraint lapses and the agent may minimise violations. This escapes the paradox entirely, because the deontologist is never directed to bring about the worse outcome. In ordinary cases the worse outcome is only marginally worse than the better one, so nothing of significance is lost by respecting the constraint; in catastrophic cases the constraint gives way and the agent does what minimises violations. What is left over is the question of where exactly the threshold lies, which is a problem about vagueness rather than a problem about the coherence of the theory.
