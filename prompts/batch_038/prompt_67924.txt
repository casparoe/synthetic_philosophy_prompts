I'm a second year in a metaethics seminar and I built an argument for my term paper that my supervisor called "either trivial or unsound, work out which," and I can't work out which. It's supposed to show that the is-ought gap plus the open question argument together rule out justified normative claims altogether, which is a conclusion I definately don't want to be committed to. Here it is.

(1) Deduction can't get out what you didn't put in: in a valid argument, nothing shows up in the conclusion that wasn't already in the premises. (2) Purely descriptive premises — anything you'd find in a physics or psychology paper — contain no normative terms. (3) So no valid argument from purely descriptive premises can have a normative conclusion. (4) And you can't sneak one in by definition either: if 'good' just meant some natural property N, then "is this N?" and "is this good?" would be the same question, but for any N you propose it still makes perfect sense to ask, of something everyone already agrees is N, whether it's good — so 'good' doesn't mean any natural property. (5) So a normative claim can only be supported by premises that are already normative. (6) And by (3), a normative premise can only be supported by further normative premises. (7) So any chain of justification for a normative claim either goes in a circle, goes back forever, or stops dead at a premise with no support at all. (8) Therefore no normative claim is justified.

Seminar went sideways at (8). Another student said I could run the exact same argument with 'quark' in place of 'ought': no valid argument from premises without 'quark' in them can conclude that there are quarks, so quark claims can only be supported by premises already about quarks, so it's circles or infinite regresses or brute unsupported premises again, so no quark claim is justified either. I said that was obviously a bad parallel and she said "then say how its bad," and I couldn't. A couple of people also insisted that (3) isn't even Hume's point, that he was complaining about moralists who slide from 'is' to 'ought' without warning rather than announcing a theorem, but I don't see how that helps me, since (3) looks true on its own terms even if he never said it. So: which step do you reject, and why did it look so innocent going in? What does giving it up cost me — I'm attached to (1) because it seems to just be logic, and (4) is the one I'd sacrifice first, but I want to know whether (4) is bad in itself or only bad the way I've used it here, and whether there's any way to seperate the two problems. And is the quark version a genuine reductio of my argument, or is there a disanalogy I can actually point to in front of her?
