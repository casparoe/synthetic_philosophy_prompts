I have been working through the question the Letter Spirit project puts at its center — what makes every token of the letter 'a' a token of 'a' — and rather than argue from theory I want to lay out the verdicts I actually have about particular cases and ask what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here are the cases and my verdicts.

1. A geometric sans-serif 'a' built from a circle and a straight stem (the single-story form) and a text serif's double-story 'a' share essentially no stroke structure, yet both are 'a' without hesitation. Shared shape is therefore not what I am tracking.

2. A child's mirror-reversed 'a' — the bowl opening the wrong way — I still call an 'a', just a badly made one. The same child's mirror-reversed 'b' I will not call a 'b': in isolation I say the child has written a 'd', or that I cannot tell what was intended. Same transformation, two different verdicts.

3. An 'a' in a dense Gothic textura that I cannot decode without being told the alphabet is nonetheless an 'a', because someone who knows the script reads it as one. My own failure to read it does not demote it.

4. A mark in an abstract painting that is, so far as I can tell, shape-identical to a handwritten 'a' is not a letter at all. It is a form, not a character.

5. A coffee stain, or a scribble by someone who has never seen writing, that happens to match a printed 'a' exactly, is not an 'a'.

6. The lowercase Cyrillic 'а', which in most modern typefaces is drawn identically to the Latin 'a', is not a Latin 'a'. A Russian text does not thereby contain our letter; it contains a look-alike that is a different letter.

7. The ampersand and the at-sign are both descended, as I understand it, from scribes' abbreviations built out of letters, and the at-sign still visibly encloses an 'a'. Neither is a letter, and I would deny that the at-sign is a token of 'a'.

8. A designer strips the crossbar from a double-story 'a' so that the result reads to everyone as an 'o', then explains in the launch material that it is a stylized 'a', the client's name beginning with one. I say it is not an 'a', and the declaration does nothing for it.

9. An artist invents an alphabet and stipulates that a certain ornamental form — unreadable to me as any letter — is her 'a'; she then uses it consistently wherever an 'a' belongs in the texts she sets. I say this is an 'a'. The stipulation plus the consistent deployment seems to make it so.

10. A typeface in which the bowl of the 'a' is left open, so that the counter is unclosed, is read by everyone as an 'a'. It is an 'a'.

11. The 'æ' shape: in English typography I treat it as a decorative ligature of two letters, while in Danish it is a letter in its own right, alphabetized after 'z'. Same mark, different status, and the difference seems to come entirely from the system it sits in.

The principle I keep circling is roughly this: a mark is an 'a' just in case a competent reader of the writing system to which the mark belongs would take it for an 'a'. It handles 3, 8, 10 and 11 comfortably, but it strains elsewhere. Cases 4 and 5 seem to need a prior answer about whether the mark belongs to a system at all, which is close to the question I started with. Case 6 requires me to know which system is relevant before I can identify the competent reader, and I am not sure how I know that except by already knowing the verdict. Case 9 appears to have no competent readers to appeal to. Case 2 makes the verdict depend on context in a way I find both indispensable and embarrassing. And 8 and 9 are the pair I cannot reconcile: in both, an author declares what a mark is, and I accept the declaration in one and refuse it in the other. My only explanation is that 9 involves a whole system and consistent use while 8 involves a single glyph and a contradicted reading, and I suspect that is a rationalization rather than a principle.

What I would like from you:

- One candidate principle, or a small number of them, stated precisely enough that I can check each against all eleven cases, with an indication of which case forces each clause.
- A candid statement of whether any single candidate delivers every verdict, and — if the only way to save them all is a disjunctive or system-indexed formula — whether that formula is a principle or a transcription of my prejudices.
- If a simpler account requires giving one of my verdicts up, which one is cheapest to abandon, and what it costs me to abandon it.
- The candidate's verdicts on cases I have not raised: a machine classifier that reads a smudged impression as 'a'; a font in which the forms of 'a' and 'e' have been swapped throughout, so that the internal contrasts of the system are preserved but every reader misreads every text set in it; and, as a control, whether the account makes a knight in a chess set a knight for the same kind of reason a glyph is a letter, or for a different one.
- Finally, what the answer implies for the project that started this. If letterhood is a matter of role rather than shape, in what sense does a typeface designer produce the same letter again and again across an alphabet — and is the style that carries across the alphabet, the spirit, part of what makes each glyph an 'a', or orthogonal to it?
