I'm three quarters through a regulatory-economics sequence in an MPP program and something in it has been bugging me more and more, to the point where I asked about it in office hours and got an answer that basically amounted to "that's just how the analysis is done." So here's the practice I mean. When an agency has to decide whether a rule is worth issuing — a fuel-economy standard, a cleanup of contaminated groundwater, a restriction on some food additive — it converts everything into dollars and compares the totals. The benefit of a statistical life saved is a fixed number, and that number was itself estimated by looking at how much extra pay workers demand for doing riskier jobs, or how much more people pay for houses in cleaner or safer neighborhoods. The benefit of a lake you can swim in, or a species that survives, comes from surveys asking people what they would pay. Then the rule counts as an improvement if the winners' total willingness to pay beats the losers', even though no compensation actually happens, and even though a dollar of willingness to pay from a rich person and a dollar from a poor person are counted exactly the same. My roommate, who did an econ degree, says this is all just the standard efficiency test and that I'm confusing a tool with a metaphysics, which may be fair, but I don't think so.

Here's what's nagging at me. The story I was told in intro micro is that utility got deliberately emptied out at some point in the middle of the last century — that "utility" came to mean nothing over and above what people actually choose, precisely so that economics wouldn't have to claim it knows anything about anybody's inner states or their happiness. But the apparatus above looks like it needs utility to be a measurable quantity, denominated in money, additive across different people, and readable off what someone *says* on a survey rather than off what they do in a store. Those two commitments look like near-opposites to me. So: reconstruct what this practice really has to assume in order to run at all, and be strict about the distinction — which assumptions it literally cannot function without, versus which ones it merely happens to sit comfortably with, because I suspect several quite different theories of welfare would license the exact same arithmetic. Then tell me which rival views it quietly forecloses: the idea that some preferences are deformed by the conditions that produced them, the idea that what people choose and what makes their lives go well come apart in systematic ways, the idea that welfare isn't preference-satisfaction at all, the idea that interpersonal comparison in dollars is a category error rather than a rough approximation. And what would actually have to change in the day-to-day work of a regulatory agency — not in the philosophy seminar, in the guidance documents and the spreadsheets — if willingness-to-pay got replaced by something else, or if we went back to a strictly ordinal framework where only rankings and unanimous improvements count?

One more thing about how I want you to handle it. Don't sand down the edges to make it look tidier than it is. I want a clear line between the parts where people who work on this genuinely agree and the parts that are still a live fight — my suspicion is that a lot of what gets handed to students as technical consensus is actually a contested position wearing a lab coat. If you end up thinking I've mischaracterized the practice or that the two commitments I think are in tension actually aren't, say that directly instead of building me a strawman to knock down.
