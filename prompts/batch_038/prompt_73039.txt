I am attending a reading group on the foundations of quantum mechanics, and three different analogies for the Everettian probability problem have come up in as many weeks. I would like a careful audit of each, since the group keeps treating them as though they settled matters they do not in fact settle.

The first comes from a physicist friend who is hostile to the many-worlds interpretation. He says that if every outcome of a quantum measurement really occurs, then the situation is just like a lottery in which every ticket wins: there are a certain number of distinct outcomes, each of them equally real, so the only non-arbitrary assignment of chances is to count them and divide evenly. On that counting, a measurement prepared so that one outcome has squared amplitude 0.9 and the other 0.1 should be assigned 50-50 chances, which contradicts the Born rule. He concludes that Everettian quantum mechanics either has to add the Born rule as a separate postulate, in which case the interpretation has explained nothing, or it is empirically inadequate.

The second analogy is mine, and I am less sure of it than I was when I proposed it. I said that my friend's error is like insisting that a federal assembly must give each member state one vote. No one thinks that is forced on you by the existence of the states; you can weight representation by population instead, and once you do, the question "but how many states are there?" simply stops being the relevant one. Likewise, I suggested, worlds come with weights built into the formalism, and branch counting is a category mistake rather than a rival hypothesis.

The third comes from a philosopher in the group who is friendly to Everett. She says the probability problem dissolves once you see that a pre-measurement observer is like someone who has been told that a copying machine will produce many continuants of them and has not yet been told which room she will wake up in: the uncertainty is genuine, it is ordinary self-locating ignorance, and once the branching has occurred there is a fact about how many continuants she has and what they are like, so her degrees of belief have something to latch onto.

Please address the following.

1. For each analogy, give the correspondence explicitly: what in the source plays the role of the branching structure, the observer, the measure over outcomes, and the passage of time. A table is welcome.
2. Identify the disanalogies, and separate those that actually undermine the conclusion the analogy was used to support from those that are merely loose fit.
3. Say what each analogy quietly imports that the Everettian picture may lack: for instance, a determinate count of outcomes, discrete and instantaneous branching rather than continuous decoherence, persistent identities for worlds, or an independent grounding for the weights.
4. Consider the standard move of rewriting an unequal-amplitude superposition as a larger number of equally weighted branches, with the ratio of branch numbers reproducing the squared amplitudes, and irrational ratios reached by a limiting argument. Does this rescue my friend's counting from within, or does it show that the counting was never a free-standing alternative, since the decomposition into equal branches is stipulated rather than discovered? Take a position.
5. Tell me whether each of the three conclusions survives: empirical inadequacy or explanatory emptiness on my friend's side, category mistake on mine, and dissolution of the probability problem on the philosopher's side. Where a conclusion fails, say what weaker claim the analogy could still support.
6. If none of the three is adequate, propose a better comparison, and say what work it can and cannot do.
