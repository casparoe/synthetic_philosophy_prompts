You are a philosopher of social epistemology who has also sat on enough institutional committees to know how these documents get used in practice. I need a decision memo, not an essay.

I serve on the Public Programs Committee of the board of trustees for a county library system: twelve branches, roughly 340,000 residents, comprising a small university town, two commuter suburbs, and several unincorporated rural communities. On Thursday the full board votes on a one-year, $85,000 pilot proposal titled *Reading Across the Divide*. It has three components.

1. A standing "Across the Aisle" display in every branch, placing news and opinion outlets from across the political spectrum side by side, with a printed card explaining why each was selected.
2. A six-session "Check the Source" workshop series teaching one specific technique: before evaluating a story, open a new tab and look up what other outlets say about the outlet that published it.
3. Eight facilitated small-group conversations, recruited through branch sign-ups and deliberately balanced across stated political commitments, on locally contentious questions.

The proposal is written entirely in the vocabulary of epistemic bubbles. It assumes patrons are missing information they would act on if only it were in front of them, and that the institution's job is to supply it. The branch manager in our least affluent rural community objects that this misdiagnoses her patrons. In her town, an organized network has spent three years circulating the claim that the county health department falsified well-water testing results to shield a landfill operator, that the regional newspaper is paid off, and that library programming is an arm of the county. Her patrons are not missing contrary evidence; they have been given reasons, source by source and by name, to discount it in advance. On the standard distinction between a bubble and an echo chamber — a structure of omission that better information can pop, versus a structure of cultivated distrust in which outside testimony has been actively discredited — she is describing the second, and the proposal is built for the first.

Her sharper worry is one I have started calling *credential capture*, though I am not sure the term earns its keep: when an institution attempts to hand out neutral credentials — a blessed source list, a stamped "we verified this" card — the institution becomes a named party in the dispute, and inside a chamber its endorsement is absorbed as fresh evidence about which side it is on. On this picture the display case does not merely fail to reach the network; it hands the network a citable exhibit. A related asymmetry I would like you to test rather than accept: the workshop component may cut the wrong way as well, since a technique for assessing the trustworthiness of sources, given to someone who already holds a ranked list of which sources are compromised, may be a more efficient instrument for confirming that list than for revising it.

For what it is worth, there is a well-known randomized field experiment in which partisans were paid to follow an automated account surfacing content from the opposing side, and the treated group moved further from that side rather than closer. I do not know how far it generalizes, and I would rather you weigh it than treat it as a verdict.

Please think carefully before drafting, and do not simply reflect my framing back at me with better prose. Begin with no more than five bullet points under the heading **Scratch work (not for circulation)** stating where you think the real cruxes lie, which of my two coinages above is doing genuine explanatory work and which is decoration, and where my description of the case is self-serving. Then give me the memo itself — 1,100 to 1,400 words — in exactly these sections and in this order:

- **Recommendation** (~120 words): approve, approve with conditions, or reject, stated plainly enough that a trustee could vote on it Thursday.
- **What the proposal is actually for** (~250 words): diagnose each of the three components separately, saying what epistemic structure it would plausibly affect and which it would leave untouched.
- **The case for approving as written** (~200 words): the strongest version, argued rather than strawmanned, including whatever is right about the bubble diagnosis.
- **The case against** (~250 words): including an explicit verdict on whether the asymmetric-risk claim about the workshop survives scrutiny.
- **Conditions** (~350 words): concrete modifications that library staff could implement inside the existing pilot budget, each with an owner and a date, plus what should be cut outright if the conditions cannot be met.
- **Reversal triggers** (~150 words): what observations during the first six months would show that this memo's reasoning, not merely its prediction, was wrong.

Three constraints. The audience is trustees with no philosophy training, so any technical term must be glossed in a clause on first use. Do not cite studies, name books or authors, or append a reading list; if your argument depends on an empirical claim I have not supplied, flag it in brackets as something staff should verify before relying on it rather than asserting it as fact. And commit: a memo that splits the difference between the proposal and the objection is worse than useless to a board that has to vote.
