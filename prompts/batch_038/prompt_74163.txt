I sit on the Northsound Inshore Groundfish Council, an eleven-seat co-management body that administers a single stock under an agreement with the state: two seats each are nominated by the two harvest cooperatives, three are held by at-large interests (a shore-side processor, a recreational anglers' association, and a tribal government with a reserved right), and the remaining four are appointed, including mine. The annual total allowable catch is 1,400 tonnes, split 55/45 between Kettle Cove Cooperative (41 small hook-and-line boats) and Bight Fisheries Association (nine mid-water trawlers). Our in-season rule provides that any overage by either cooperative is deducted from the other cooperative's allocation the following year at a multiplier of 1.5, on the theory that neither can be caught individually within a mixed fishery but each can be made to police the other. The rule has worked, after a fashion: both cooperatives now withhold roughly 12 percent of their entitlement every season as a buffer against being charged for the other's overage, so some 170 tonnes go unharvested annually, and Kettle Cove absorbs most of the cost because its members carry fixed debt against a nine-week season.

In February a visiting economist from the state university, retained jointly by both cooperatives, filed a proposal on their behalf. Each cooperative would lodge with the council a binding instruction to its own vessels: fish up to the full entitlement, but only so long as the other cooperative's identical instruction is also on file; the instruction reverts automatically to the 12 percent withhold if our in-season monitoring reports the other cooperative exceeding its entitlement. He calls this a 'safe Pareto improvement' — a deal in which each party's committed strategy guarantees it, in expectation and by its own lights, at least what it would receive under the status quo whatever the other party does, and at least one party strictly better — and he argues that a council which refused it would be keeping both cooperatives poorer in the name of a precaution they have already priced in. Our staff biologist disagrees. The assessment carries a confidence band of roughly ±35 percent and has been revised downward twice in four years; her model says that if both cooperatives fish to the full entitlement and the stock turns out to lie in the lower third of the range, the probability of recruitment failure within two seasons rises to about 0.3, and in that state the fishery closes and both cooperatives lose far more than the withheld 12 percent ever cost them. She also notes that the automatic-revert clause depends on landings data reaching us with a 30- to 45-day lag while the trawl season runs nine weeks, so the clause would fire after most of the harvest is already landed. Meanwhile one of the appointed members has tabled a motion to reject any such filing out of hand, on the ground that a private agreement between two entitlement holders should never be permitted to alter what the council's rules require of them.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the other ten members before we meet on April 14: roughly 1,100 words, with the recommendation stated in the first paragraph (approve, reject, or approve under specified conditions) and the reasoning after it. My own training is municipal budgeting, and although I have spent the last two weekends with the mechanism-design material the economist recommended, three things I cannot resolve on my own should be taken up head-on. First, whose expectations define the safety guarantee — the cooperatives' own privately held stock models, which the biologist considers outdated and which we have never been shown, or the council's assessment, which the cooperatives consider conservative? Second, the choice of baseline: the deal is an improvement only measured against a status quo our own cross-penalty rule manufactured, and we could simply repeal that rule, so I want the memo to say whether it is coherent for us to sanction a private fix for a harm we are causing ourselves, or whether the honest recommendation runs through the rule rather than the trade. Third, over what population the deal is supposed to be improving things — the two cooperatives, or everyone the council represents, including the processor, the anglers, the tribal government, and the stock. Please also give me a short set of criteria the council could apply to the two further filings already queued (a gear-swap arrangement and a joint seasonal-closure trade), so that we are deciding a category and not merely a case; if you recommend conditions, they need dates and an in-season trigger we could realistically administer. Argue from the facts I have given you rather than citing statutes, cases, or literature — I need no references and could not check them — and do not spend words restating the background, which the other members know. Finally, I want the strongest version of the case for the losing side set out in its own paragraph, including the point that the members least able to absorb the current withhold are the ones asking us to approve it.
