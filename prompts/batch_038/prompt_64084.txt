I'm 68 and three years into retirement after 34 years of fish systematics, spent almost entirely on one genus of small cold-water river fish in a folded mountain range — in the lab we never called them anything but the gray bellies. I described something like a dozen of their populations as species and then spent the back half of my career quietly lumping several of them, which is a humbling thing to have to do in print. Since I stopped working I've been reading philosophy for pleasure and have recently landed in philosophy of biology, where the species problem has me properly stuck. Every treatment I've found starts at the same door — do these populations interbreed? — and then walks you down a corridor of named concepts. That's no use to me, because in my own fish the questions never arrived in that order, and the one that settled things was usually about something else entirely.

Here's the situation I actually lived with, and I'd like the instrument you build to be able to chew on it. There's an upstream population above a waterfall, cut off a long time: diagnosable at a glance (darker, smaller, different fin-ray counts), but on the mitochondrial marker we had in those days it sits neatly inside the downstream population rather than as a sister to it. There's a population in the next river over, equally diagnosable, spawning six weeks earlier on different substrate — but where the two ranges touch it hybridizes with the downstream fish freely, with fertile second-generation hybrids and a wide messy swarm in between, so nobody can call that a reproductive barrier and nobody can call the two things one tidy lineage either. And there's an all-female form in a single tributary that reproduces by taking sperm from the downstream fish to trigger egg development without incorporating the male's genome, so its an obligate dependent of the very population it's supposed to be distinct from. Whatever I ended up calling each of those, I first had to decide what my word was even doing, and that deciding had nothing to do with a slogan.

So here's what I want. I'm putting together a diagnostic quiz for two audiences at once: my old grad students, who are out in agencies and survey work now, and a philosophy reading group I've joined at the library, most of whom have never held a specimen jar. Write me a decision tree of ten to fourteen questions that takes a person from wherever they start to a named position on what species are. My rules. Every node has to be a concrete case or a question about one — a waterfall, a hybrid swarm, a clone, a fossil series with no gap you could point your finger at, a bacterium trading genes sideways, a ring of populations where each links to the next — and never anything that leaks the label, so no "do you think species are real?" and no "does reproduction matter to you?" The root node must not be about interbreeding: start somewhere more basic, like whether the term is meant to name a joint in nature or just a filing system, or whether it has to work for all of life at once, and work your way down from there. Put the genuine positions at the leaves — the biological one, the evolutionary one, the phylogenetic/diagnosability one, the ecological one, the cohesion- and recognition-flavored ones, genotypic clusters, pluralism, outright nominalism — plus any others you think belong. For each leaf, tell me the field situation in which someone holding it would most regret the choice. I also want the tree built out of the logical space first rather than out of a list of names: show me which combinations of answers are available but empty, and for each empty cell say whether nobody lives there because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or because nobody has bothered to explore it. And be honest about the pairs of leaves your questions can't actually separate, and say why they blur — I'd rather see that admitted than painted over. Don't give me a bibliography or a reading list and don't quote anyone at me; I'm not chasing anything down, I just want it argued out in plain language. Last thing, and it's the one I care about most: make sure at least one node forces the reader to say whether being nested inside another population defeats diagnosability, or whether diagnosability survives nestedness anyway.
