I write interpretability tooling for an applied ML team. My background is software engineering and I have no formal philosophy training, so please pitch the philosophy at someone who knows the machine-learning side cold and knows these debates only from summaries. Two people in a reading group I attend have argued for the better part of a year about the language of thought hypothesis, and I would like the document they should have produced: the joint statement of a good-faith adversarial collaboration.

The parties. The first holds something close to the Fodorian line: cognitive systems traffic in internal representations with constituent structure and a combinatorial syntax, and productivity together with systematicity is the evidence that this is so. The second holds that this picture is either false or empty when applied to trained neural networks — that what looks like constituent structure is a graded, superposed binding of the tensor-product or gradient-symbolic-computation sort, or else is not in the model at all but in the corpus and the task, recovered by probes that impose the structure they claim to detect.

Pin the whole thing to one shared case rather than to intuitions about humans: a transformer trained on a synthetic language with productive role–filler syntax, where activation patching recovers directions that appear to encode which filler occupies which role, and where the model generalizes to combinations absent from its training data.

Then produce:

1. A numbered list of claims both parties can sign. This should include what both accept about the case study — what patching and probing can and cannot establish, and why behavioral systematicity does not by itself settle anything about internal structure — together with which of the classic productivity and systematicity arguments the first party should now regard as retired and which still bite.
2. A disagreements table. Each row states one contested claim twice: once in wording the first party would sign, once in wording the second party would sign. Add a column marking the row substantive or terminological, and a column giving each side's reason for thinking its own wording is not merely cosmetic.
3. Four cruxes — sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one party. For each, name the experiment, architecture comparison, or analysis both agree would bear on it, record each party's prediction in advance in a form specific enough to be scored, and state the result that would force that party to concede.
4. A joint glossary that keeps at least four things apart: structure in the content, structure in the vehicle, structure in the causal dynamics of the network, and structure in the training distribution and task. Use it to identify which stretches of the year-long argument were only about words, and which disagreements survive the disambiguation.
5. A closing paragraph on whether compositionality should be treated as all-or-nothing or as admitting of degrees, and whether the two parties' dispute depends on settling that question one way.

Begin with the joint statement itself. No preface, no framing remarks, no restatement of this request, and no references or reading list — I want the argument, not the literature.
