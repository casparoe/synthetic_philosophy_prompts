You're going to play a philosopher of aesthetics who also knows the Harlem Renaissance arguments about art and politics cold — the Du Bois side of it and the pushback he got from his younger contemporaries. Think carefully. Before you commit to an answer, brainstorm out loud: list the candidate rules that could be generating our track record, test each against every case, and only then tell us what to do. No reading lists, I just need the reasoning.

Here's the situation. I sit on the programming committee of a neighborhood Black studies center. Once a year we pick six works for our public series — books, films, plays, music, whatever — and for four years running our charter has been explicitly Du Boisian: we assume art is propaganda and always will be, and we treat the only real question as what counts as propaganda done right. Half of what we pick is local and never got reviewed anywhere, so let me describe the items instead of naming them.

Our record so far:

1. A verse cycle about an eviction wave, written by a member of the tenants' union that was fighting it. Crude — uneven meter, some stanzas are basically lists of names and dates — but scrupulously accurate about the facts, printed by the union and handed out at its own meetings. Took it, no argument.

2. A glossy historical novel about a Reconstruction-era town. Beautiful prose, enormous with our own community's readers, and it turns a real massacre into a triumphant victory, inventing the happy part wholesale. Turned it down; two members fought for it hard.

3. A broadcast documentary, filmed over three years in a Black rural county by an outside crew. Nothing staged, subjects paid and consulted, no lies in it. The network sold it as showing America its shame. Turned it down.

4. A jazz oratorio setting our churches' music for concert hall, by a composer from here. No message, no argument, nothing anybody could call agitational. It's the piece our own audience kept begging us to bring back. Took it.

5. A satirical novel, self-published by a barber two blocks from the center, aimed entirely at the neighborhood — most of the jokes don't land unless you know the streets. It flopped locally and then got big with suburban book clubs who read it as a charming picture of how people live down there. We took it anyway, in a long fight, knowing exactly how it was being read outside.

6. A stage adaptation of a well-known memoir by a writer from here about her own family's addiction and violence — unflinching, nothing invented for effect, sold out downtown, praised by our readers and by outsiders alike. The local organizers' coalition wrote and asked us not to feature it, because the reviews kept quoting the worst passages back at the neighborhood. Turned it down, narrowly.

Now this year's problem. A novel by a woman who grew up three blocks from the center. It's about a police corruption scandal in the city and about the violence inside one neighborhood family that the scandal was covering up. No stereotypes, no invented atrocity — she documented everything — and our own readers say it's the best-written thing submitted this cycle. The trouble: it's out with a big house whose jacket copy and ad campaign are aimed squarely at a general readership, pitched as the truth about a city nobody wants to look at. The author says she wrote it for her neighbors and she's doing free readings at the branch library and the union hall. And the organizers' coalition has again written to ask us not to program it — the police union and two columnists have already been excerpting it — while our series catalog goes into the public schools.

So: what should we decide if we want to be consistent with ourselves? I want (a) which of the six precedents is nearest to this one and which detail of it is doing the work, (b) the rules that actually fit all six verdicts — I suspect at least three do, and I suspect some of them only fit by getting stretched, so say which ones are straining and how, (c) which of the surviving rules diverge on this novel and what each one tells us to do, (d) your best guess at our verdict with the reasons you'd give a room full of people who disagree, and (e) if the record genuinely doesn't settle it, what rule we'd be committing ourselves to with each possible choice, stated plainly enough that we could write it into the charter and own it next year.

Then name one further submission — describe it, invent it — that would tell us apart for good, and say what each rule predicts about it. And if your honest view is that the Du Boisian standard itself is what's making this case undecidable, because telling the truth and doing the community's work can come apart and the standard doesn't say which wins, say that and tell us what a tie-break would have to look like.
