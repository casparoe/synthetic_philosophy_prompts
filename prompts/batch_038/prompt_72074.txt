ok so I got into a genuinely long argument last night with a friend of mine — we do this maybe twice a year and it's never productive — about whether a city can bind itself at all. He's a lawyer, I'm not, and he kept throwing around phrases like "Ulysses contract" and "entrenchment" and "the dead hand problem," and I could follow maybe 70% of it. His position was that any rule a council passes today that a future council can't undo is just the past tyrannizing the present. Mine was that that's obviously wrong, because otherwise a city could never do anything hard: you'd never get a debt brake or a protected transit fund through, since everyone knows the same people who vote for it will vote to raid it in year three. He said that's exactly my problem, I'm admitting the voters can't be trusted with their own money. We went in circles for an hour and neither of us moved.

Here's why I actually need this and not just a tiebreaker. I work in the city clerk's office in a midsized city, about 180k people, and I'm the one who ends up writing the staff analysis on charter amendment proposals. Three to five times a year we get one that's self-binding in some way — a tax limit requiring a supermajority, a dedicated fund that's "lockboxed," an independent commission with staggered terms that a future council is barred from abolishing or reconstituting. Right now I have no framework. I just describe what the thing does, note that reversing it would require another charter amendment, and let the councilmembers draw their own conclusions, which is cowardly and gets me complaints from both sides. Our office is six people, one of whom is a half-time assistant city attorney, so whatever you write can't assume anybody has read anything, and I usually get about two weeks between a petition being certified and my analysis going out for public comment.

So: write me a screening procedure I can actually run on a proposal. I want a sequence of questions to ask in order, what counts as a satisfactory answer to each, and clear exits — where the procedure comes out as "this isn't a precommitment device at all, it's an ordinary policy with a fancy label on it," where it comes out as "this binds in a way that will probably backfire, and here's the mechanism," and where it has to stop and hand the thing to the council because what's left is a question of values and not of facts. The real distinctions from the precommitment stuff need to survive the translation — who exactly is binding whom, whether the device constrains a choice set or tries to guarantee an outcome, whether there's an outside enforcer with their own incentives or it's just the same body promising itself to behave later, and how expensive exit is once you want it — but expressed as questions a normal person can ask of a piece of drafting, not as theory. Throw in a couple of cheap tests I can run directly on the text of a proposal. Then run it on three things: (1) a charter amendment requiring a two-thirds council vote to increase the property tax levy, no sunset; (2) a transit lockbox dedicating a fixed share of a new local sales tax to capital transit projects, worded so it survives any future council trying to reallocate it; (3) an independent redistricting commission with staggered eight-year terms that the charter says no future council may abolish. Be straight with me about where the procedure lands on an answer I wouldn't like — I suspect at least one of those three is something I'd personally vote for. And end with a short list of what the procedure genuinely can't settle, including the thing my friend would say it can't settle: whether the people who wrote the rule had any standing to write it for people who werent in the room yet.
