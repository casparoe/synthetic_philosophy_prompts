You are a philosopher who has spent twenty years teaching nineteenth-century German philosophy and who has also, reluctantly, become the person friends ask to speak at funerals. Think carefully before drafting, and treat the structural requirement below as binding rather than decorative.

The occasion. My sister Elena was a cellist. She died in February at fifty-eight, and her memorial is next Sunday in the recital hall of the conservatory where she taught. I speak for about four minutes at the front of the programme; then her colleague Marta plays the whole of the Bach suite in C minor, unaccompanied, and that is the entire service — no clergy, no other readings, no reception speeches. Roughly two hundred people will be there: her students and the faculty, her husband and two grown sons, and a great many neighbours who have never read a page of philosophy and who are coming because they liked her. I am her older brother, and everyone in the room knows that I teach philosophy.

The difficulty I want you to solve. For twenty-five years Elena played the sarabande from that suite every morning before anything else, always first, always alone, and she never once performed it in public or allowed anyone to record it; she said it wasn't for an audience, and that ended the discussion. When she was diagnosed she asked me, half-joking, to speak at her memorial about Schopenhauer's claim that music is not a picture of anything, on the grounds that it would annoy the faculty. So the speech has to be built on that doctrine from his aesthetics in The World as Will and Representation: the distinction between the arts that give us copies of the Ideas and therefore give us the world only as it appears, and music, which he takes to be a copy of the will itself and so to speak the inner essence of striving, satisfaction and frustration with none of the particular objects that ordinarily occasion them. I also want his observation about registers used structurally — the fundamental bass as the crudest and slowest grades of the will's objectification, the free and swiftly moving melody as the highest, the individual who wills.

Build the speech the way that doctrine says music is built. There should be a bass line: one short, plain sentence stating nothing but the fact, that she died and is not coming back, returning at least three times at intervals, unchanged or very nearly so. Everything else is melody and may move freely above it — her mornings, her refusal to be recorded, her sons, her temper, something funny. The returns of the bass line are what should keep the wandering from becoming a ramble.

Hard constraints. Do not put a single word in quotation marks as his, and do not supply page numbers, section numbers, or an improvised rendering of a German term: I know a few of these lines by heart and I will not have a misquote printed in the programme. Name him at most once and name the book not at all; the idea must do the work, not the citation. Do not offer the family any consolation his philosophy cannot pay for — no afterlife, no reunion, no sense in which she is at peace somewhere. What it can offer is that grief in music is grief in the abstract, stripped of its occasion, and that listening is one of the few states he allows in which we stop pressing against the world and simply contemplate it; making that land for a room of non-philosophers without sounding like a seminar is the whole task. It must also be warm and occasionally funny, because she was.

Format. Lead with the bottom line: a single sentence, before anything else, stating the claim the speech is going to make good on. Then no more than six short planning notes, 150 words in total between them, marking where each return of the bass line falls and what the melody is doing at that point. Then the speech itself, in full and ready to be read aloud, at most 650 words. Do not exceed that limit and do not append any commentary after the final line.
