I'm a software engineer, no philosophy training, so bear with me. A guy on my team (ex-econ PhD) argued me into a corner last week about the recsys we build and I still can't find the exit.

(1) All we ever observe of a user is what they picked, out of what was on offer at the time.
(2) So a user's "preferences" just are the pattern of their picks — there's nothing else for the word to grab onto.
(3) And given any pattern of picks, you can always write down a scoring function that generates it: score whatever got picked above whatever didn't.
(4) So "users maximize utility" can't come out false. Any behavior at all is compatible with it.
(5) A claim that's compatible with every possible observation isn't telling you anything about the world.
(6) Therefore all the preference/utility talk in our stack is just a fancy redescription of clicks, and there's no fact of the matter about whether our users "really" prefer anything.

He says it generalizes way past my job, which is why he's smug about it. But something's off, and I think it's connected to (3). Here's the thing I can't settle: we ran an A/B test where the identical list of items went to the identical users, only the display order was shuffled, and the picks changed. Now either ordering counts as part of "what was on offer," in which case (1) and (2) just get fatter, (3) still works, and the shuffle isn't a counterexample to his argument, it's fuel for it. Or ordering doesn't count, in which case the same situation produced two different picks and something in (2)/(3) breaks. I can't tell which horn is the bad one, or whether I'm even describing a real fork.

Which step do I actually reject, and what do I pay for rejecting it? I'd really like to keep (2), since our entire pipeline is built on it — treat that preference as a fixed thing you can learn and rank against. Also tell me straight: is the shuffle experiment a counterexample to his argument, or the thing that makes it go through?
