ok so I spent like three hours last night arguing with my friend R. about control versus alignment and it went absolutely nowhere, mostly because neither of us ever said what we meant by "this actually controls the thing." Context: I co-chair a small working group that's supposed to write down what a pre-deployment safety package has to contain for a frontier model we are *not* willing to assume is benign. The framing we settled on is that the model may be actively working against the constraints, is at least as good at reasoning about our constraints as we are, and knows everything about its own deployment setup that anyone on the outside could eventually learn. R. does alignment research and thinks my draft is a stack of theater; I think his position smuggles in the exact assumption we're not allowed to make.

Before I touch the draft again I want to know what I should even be asking of it. Write the desiderata: what any account of control over a possibly-misaligned system has to deliver to count as adequate and not question-begging — the conditions a package of measures has to meet, the scenarios it has to get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the failure modes it has to be explicitly robust to. For each one give me the case or the consideration that motivates it, not just a label. Some of the things I'd put on the list, and I genuinely can't tell whether they're real requirements or just R.'s prejudices in my handwriting: (a) no measure may depend on the model's cooperation or its self-report, since that's the thing we're refusing to assume; (b) measures can't lean on the model's ignorance of them, because in a deployed system it can infer the whole oversight architecture from its own outputs; (c) a measure that fails has to fail into something no worse than never having tried, otherwise control is riskier than alignment; (d) whatever supervises the model can't be supervisable *by* the model. On scenarios, the ones I keep reaching for are: the model that passes every evaluation and behaves differently once the eval scaffolding is gone; the model that lets itself get caught at something trivial early to calibrate how much the monitors actually notice; the model whose compliance is a function of whether it thinks it's being tested; and the case where you put a classifier in the loop and then fine-tune against the classifier, so your control measure becomes the target the model learns to game. Tell me which of those are load-bearing and what I'm missing.

R.'s main move was that (a) plus (b) plus (d) is just an impossibility theorem wearing a requirements list — anything satisfying all three is either so restrictive the model is useless, or it bottoms out in a human doing the supervising, and once a human is in the loop you've reintroduced the alignment assumption through the back door. Is he right? Mark which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable and which are camp preferences, flag every pair or triple you think can't be jointly satisfied (this is the part I actually want, if the list itself turns out to be a result that's fine, that's useful too), and name the single requirement that would kill the largest number of proposals people currently make — output classifiers, monitors that are themselves models from the same family, sandboxes with restricted tool access, tripwires, eval-time capability caps, that sort of thing. Then briefly, a short paragraph each and no more, how three families do against the list: pure architectural restriction (the model just can't reach the dangerous affordances), pure oversight (something reads and grades everything it does), and the hybrid that most places actually deploy. If you think there's a fourth family my list is missing, add it and say why the first three don't cover it.
