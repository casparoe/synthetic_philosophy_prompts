I work as a research assistant on a comparative politics project coding institutional change in Hungary since 2010, Poland during the years of Law and Justice government, Turkey after 2013, and — more recently and far more awkwardly, since the comparison cases are much more disputed — the United States. In the course of reading government-side justifications, I keep hitting the same argumentative pattern, and I have not been able to find a name for it. It goes like this. A government takes a step that critics describe as damaging to democracy: restructuring a constitutional court and enlarging it, replacing the members of an electoral management body, rewriting the licensing conditions for broadcast outlets, subjecting a foreign-accredited university to a new permit requirement, obliging NGOs above a funding threshold to register as foreign-supported. When challenged, the defenders almost never argue that the step is undemocratic but necessary. They argue that it is unremarkable — that other established democracies have considerably more political judicial appointment systems, that several EU member states impose stricter media ownership limits, that France and Austria have comparable NGO transparency laws — and the comparison they invoke is, in each individual instance, usually accurate. What I noticed is that this defense is always delivered measure by measure, and that it is sufficient to defeat any demand that critics identify the moment the democracy ended, while nobody on either side is ever required to defend the sequence as a whole; and the sequence, taken together, produces a configuration that no single member of it resembles or predicts.

So the thing I want named is not the gradualism itself — I am aware that people speak of democracies being hollowed out rather than overthrown, and of erosion versus rupture. It is the narrower move by which an aggregate is placed beyond criticism because every increment can be matched to a legitimate precedent elsewhere, and the burden of pointing to a line-crossing act is shifted onto the critic even though, by the construction of the case, no single act crosses one. Is there an established concept or term for this, in the backsliding literature or anywhere adjacent? Please be systematic about the near neighbors rather than settling for the first one that comes to mind: the salami-slicing and boiling-frog family of images; threshold vagueness of the heap-and-bald-man sort, and whether it applies here or merely rhymes; the charge of composition — reasoning from each part being acceptable to the whole being acceptable — and whether that charge actually lands, since the defenders are not obviously inferring anything about the whole; and a doctrine I have a hazy memory of from another branch of law, possibly in investment arbitration or takeover regulation, that deals with exactly this shape of problem, a series of measures each falling short of a legal threshold that together reach it. For each of these, tell me what it captures about my pattern and precisely where it stops short, and which one fits best.

If the honest answer is that nothing names it cleanly, then coin something: a definition, criteria for application, and at least one boundary case that must be excluded — for instance a genuinely single, unmistakable line-crossing act, or a case in which the aggregate objection is simply motivated reasoning by the other side dressed up as a cumulative argument. I would also like your judgment on whether the move is a fallacy at all, or sometimes a legitimate methodological objection; I can see that the defender has a real point when they say that benchmarking individual measures against other democracies is precisely the discipline their critics are trying to evade, and I do not want an answer that assumes otherwise. Finally, please keep separate throughout what is settled among people who work on this from what is genuinely contested — including the prior question of whether the family of concepts I am working within is a coherent object of study or largely a partisan label — and do not present a contested position, whichever side of it you find more persuasive, as though it were the consensus.
