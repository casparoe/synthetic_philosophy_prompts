I am preparing the in-class essay paper for a graduate seminar in the philosophy of cognitive science that spends its middle block on Dennett's taxonomy of creatures — the Darwinian, Skinnerian, Popperian, and Gregorian stages set out in *Kinds of Minds* (1996), with the last named for the Bristol neuropsychologist Richard Gregory and characterized by the use of cognitive artifacts designed outside the creature's own head. My problem is that the taxonomy is easy to recite. A competent student can reproduce the ladder, note that each rung supposedly adds a further way of testing candidate behavior against the world without the organism paying the price of failure itself, observe that the rungs are meant to be cumulative, and sit down. What I want to test instead is whether the ladder really is cumulative — whether the stages are ordered by genuine design-dependence relations, or are a list of separable capacities that merely happen to co-occur in us, with the ordering smuggled in from our own case.

To that end, a colleague and I have built three thought-experimental systems over the last two years. Please treat them as stipulated in the question itself; they are ours, not drawn from any literature, and the candidates will get the descriptions in the stem. (a) The *kesh*: a corvid-sized bird whose entire behavioral repertoire is developmentally canalized. Laboratory work shows no measurable operant conditioning, no individual learning of any kind, and no sign that a kesh forms and evaluates candidate actions before performing them — it acts on whatever sequence is elicited. Yet kesh populations in different valleys perform different multi-step tool sequences, each locally well-adapted, and the differences persist across generations because the founding individuals of each valley differ genetically and the regional variants have drifted apart. The repertoire is inherited without ever being learned. (b) The *simulacrum*: an engineered artifact carrying an explicit internal model of its environment that generates candidate action sequences, runs them forward in the model, and executes only those that meet a built-in criterion. It has no reinforcement signal whatsoever: nothing about the consequences of an action ever raises or lowers the probability of any future behavior, and its world-model cannot be revised. It plans but cannot learn from doing. (c) The *relay*: a distributed system — an automated ecology of recommendation and trading agents — that generates, copies, and modifies external symbolic artifacts at very high speed, where each component manipulates symbols it was nowhere designed to understand. No part of the whole has a model of what the artifacts are about, yet the artifacts reliably accumulate structure that later components exploit. A fourth item belongs to the paper too: my colleague has proposed adding a fifth rung to the ladder, provisionally called *Lovelacean creatures*, defined as creatures that manufacture cognitive artifacts whose subsequent selection no longer falls under the creature's own control, so that the artifacts become an environment the creature must adapt to rather than an instrument it deploys. The candidates are told explicitly that this is our coinage and not part of any published taxonomy.

Please write one essay question built on this material, together with everything needed to mark it. The question should be self-contained, so that the three case descriptions and the Lovelacean proposal appear in compressed form in the stem, and a candidate answering in two hours, without notes, could produce roughly 1,200 words against it; it should demand a definite position rather than a survey — my preference is that it ask whether the kesh, the simulacrum, and the relay are counterexamples to the cumulativeness of the ladder, which of them the ladder can absorb and at what cost, and whether the Lovelacean rung names a genuinely new stage or a mis-described Gregorian one — and it should be answerable from the case material and the taxonomy alone, with no outside reading and no citations expected of the candidates. Then give a model answer of about 900 words that actually takes a side on each of those points, followed by a marking scheme worth 25 marks total: five must-hit points, the marks each carries, and for each, one specific error that should cost the candidate those marks. The errors I most want penalized are the ones my students actually make — reading "Darwinian" as a synonym for "innate" and "Skinnerian" as a synonym for "learned," treating the Popperian stage as nothing more than the possession of beliefs, assuming the Gregorian stage just means having language or having culture, reading cumulativeness as a claim about phylogenetic order of appearance rather than as a claim about what capacities presuppose which others, and answering with a list of classifications where an argument about the taxonomy's status as a hierarchy of kinds was required. The model answer should also state plainly what would have to be true of the relay for the Lovelacean rung to earn its place. Begin your response directly with the question text as it would appear on the exam paper, with no preface, no note on difficulty, and no closing commentary; use clear headings to separate the question, the model answer, and the marking scheme, and nothing else.
