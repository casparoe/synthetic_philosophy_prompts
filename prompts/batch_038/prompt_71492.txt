I am assembling a demonstration case for a graduate seminar on social choice, and I want it built to order rather than borrowed. Its purpose is to make vivid a distinction that students reliably collapse: that "utilities are interpersonally comparable" is not a single property but two — comparability of *units* and comparability of *levels* — and that a utilitarian verdict can be overturned by fixing one of these and leaving the other free. This fixes what counts as cheating. A case that gets its verdict by declaring one claimant's numbers the true ones is worthless to me; the reversal has to be generated by the calibration choice itself, out of representations that are equally defensible on the evidence a real committee could have.

Construct the case to the following specifications.

**Setting.** One paragraph, at most 200 words. Realistic and institutional rather than science-fictional: a hospital ethics committee, a public housing authority, a municipal relief board — some body holding a fixed, indivisible benefit to allocate between exactly two claimants. No trolleys, no utility monsters, and no welfare-measuring devices of any kind: no hedonometers, no neural scans, no perfectly empathetic observer. All interpersonal information available in the scenario must be of a kind that actually exists — observed choices, stated indifferences over ordinary gambles, self-reports, clinical assessments.

**The claimants.** One has lived for many years with a serious but stable impairment and reports, sincerely and consistently, that her life goes well; the other has recently lost an equivalent level of function and is still in the acute phase of adjusting to it. Their preference *orderings* over the relevant outcomes must be identical in rank — each prefers the treated outcome to the status quo to the worst outcome — so that nothing in the case can turn on ordinal disagreement. Everything must turn on cardinal structure.

**Numbers.** Give each claimant a von Neumann–Morgenstern utility function over three outcomes, written out explicitly, with arithmetic simple enough for a reader to check by hand. One constraint on the construction, stated in a representation-invariant way: pick any common unit you like, and the ratio of one claimant's utility range (best outcome minus worst) to the other's must fall between 1/2 and 2. Nothing may ride on one person simply having much larger swings. All probabilities invoked must be ones the committee could plausibly elicit; no infinities, no outcomes whose value is itself the disputed question.

**Two calibrations.** Present two ways of placing the two functions on a common scale, each a respectable method rather than a strawman, each grounded in evidence the committee has, and each built from the *same* three outcomes and the same underlying preference data:

- *(i) Unit fixed, level free.* Calibrate both functions so that a specified equal-odds standard gamble carries the same utility increment for each claimant — fixing the unit by risk attitude — while each person's zero point is left at her own baseline. Compute the utilitarian sum for each possible allocation and record the ranking.
- *(ii) Level fixed, unit free.* Give both functions a common zero — some baseline the committee can point to for each claimant alike — while leaving each person's unit to her own risk attitudes, which differ. Compute the sums again.

The two rankings must genuinely conflict. If your first pass at the numbers makes them agree, revise the numbers and show the revised arithmetic; do not quietly abandon the specification.

**Then do the demonstration.** After the scenario and the numbers:

1. Verify each specification in turn — paragraph length, setting, the no-device rule, identical ordinal ranking, the bounded ratio of ranges — with a one-line compliance note apiece.
2. Show the utilitarian arithmetic under both calibrations.
3. Say precisely which parameter does the work: how much of the disagreement is attributable to the choice of zero, how much to the choice of unit, and whether in your numbers one of them is doing all of it. Note whether the adapted claimant's adaptation is essential or merely convenient.
4. State what further evidence would settle which calibration is right, and whether any of it is obtainable by this committee. If your conclusion is that nothing settles it, say what follows for the decision procedure — calibration by convention, refusal to aggregate, or retreat to a non-utilitarian rule — and give a reason rather than a shrug.
5. Stress-test the construction. Remove the long-run adaptation (suppose both claimants are in the acute phase) and report whether the flip survives. Then impose a common unit *and* a common zero simultaneously, and report whether the case collapses into a trivial tie or leaves a genuine residual disagreement.
6. Give the strongest available attack from the losing side: someone who holds that only the level-fixed calibration is honest, or who holds that both are dishonest because welfare is not a magnitude at all. State the argument in its best form and say whether it succeeds in showing that your case begs the question.

**Format.** Scenario as one paragraph; then the numbers; then the demonstration in clearly labeled parts matching 1–6 above. No bibliography, no citations, no named economists or theorems by title — define any term of art in a clause where it first appears. If you find that some specification cannot be met alongside the others, say which one you relaxed and why, rather than relaxing it silently.
