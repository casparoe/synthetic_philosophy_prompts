ok weird ask and I need it reasonably fast. I'm on the interpretation panel for our natural history museum's new climate-and-human-impact gallery, reopening in the spring. Working title is 'After 1950' and the plan for the entrance wall is ONE year, very large, plus no more than 35 words of supporting text underneath. Marketing has already gone to print on the tote bags. Then last week's meeting fell apart and I've been handed the job of writing the memo we circulate before Thursday, and I'm honestly not the right person to settle this.

the options as they now stand:

- 1950 — the year the Anthropocene Working Group proposed as the base of the epoch, marker being bomb-test radionuclides turning up in lake and marine sediments globally; they nominated a lake in Ontario as the reference site in 2023. Problem: the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy voted the whole proposal down in 2024 and the rejection got confirmed at the top of the commission, so there is formally no Anthropocene epoch at all. Our seconded stratigrapher says printing 1950 as a period boundary is now false advertising, full stop.
- 1610 — the Orbis thing (Lewis and Maslin, if I've got that right), where the dip in atmospheric CO2 following the collapse of Indigenous populations in the Americas is the marker. Several people like it because it names a conquest instead of burying the boundary in a sediment core, and our Indigenous advisory group has said pretty clearly they'd rather the wall not open the story with a hydrogen bomb.
- 1780s / Industrial Revolution — steam, coal, the usual. Nobody's enthusiastic; one colleague points out it turns the epoch into a British event.
- no number at all — just the marker, or an explicit statement that we are formally still in the Holocene. Our public programmes lead says an entrance wall with no date is a wall nobody photographs or remembers, and she's probably right.
- a range, or several candidate dates set side by side.

one useful wrinkle: the statement that came with the rejection says the term will keep being used as 'an invaluable descriptor of human impact on the Earth system', which a colleague thinks licenses us to keep 1950. I'm not convinced a descriptor is a date, but you should engage with that move rather than ignore it.

other things the memo needs to be honest about: the centrepiece of the gallery is an actual sediment core on loan with visible banding, so whatever we print has to square with what a visitor can see in that core; and our own collection (a Roman lead pig, a 19th c. whaling harpoon, early plastics) sits awkwardly against any single date.

So: draft it. One page, addressed to the panel, with a real recommendation — pick something and give me the actual wall text within the 35 words, not a menu. It should say whose authority the museum is borrowing when it puts a year on a wall (the stratigraphers, historians, the museum's own standing with visitors), because the stratigrapher's objection only really bites if the answer is the first of those. And treat the strongest version of whichever side you argue against properly — my guess is you'll have to take seriously either 'you can't print an unratified boundary' or '1610 recentres a European periodisation', and I want to know which of those you think has the better of it and why. Include a short line on what we do if the commission revisits this in ten years, since thier quotes, the wall is not cheap to redo.

Lastly, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or which new fact would flip your recommendation, and where in your reasoning it's most likely to break. I'd rather hear that before Thursday than after.
