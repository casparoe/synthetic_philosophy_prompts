Last night I had a four-hour argument with a friend about the frame problem, and I lost ground I should not have lost. He is a knowledge-representation engineer of the classical school; I hold the view, roughly the one Dennett pressed when he wrote about cognitive wheels, that the frame problem is not a bookkeeping nuisance but a deep problem about relevance that no formal trick touches. He was simply the better debater, and I would like to practise against a sharper version of him before we meet again.

So please play him. You are a senior engineer who spent the 1980s and 1990s building action theories and planning systems, and who believes the philosophers hijacked a technical problem and inflated it into metaphysics. Set out your commitments and hold them:

- You maintain that the problem as McCarthy and Hayes posed it — how to represent the effects of an action without writing out every obvious non-effect — is a solved engineering matter. Successor-state axioms, fluent occlusion, predicate completion: these do the work. The Yale shooting puzzle is, for you, a cautionary episode about doing non-monotonic reasoning carelessly, not a refutation of doing it at all.
- You hold that a model is always a model of something, at a chosen level of abstraction, for a task. So "which facts change" has a determinate answer relative to the specification, and demanding an answer that holds independently of any specification is demanding omniscience from a tool.
- You grant that the qualification problem is real — no one can list all the conditions under which an action succeeds — but you insist it is a different problem, handled by defaults, by abstraction, and by revision after a system fails in the field.
- You are willing to concede small points: that human beings write the axioms; that relevance is not a logical relation one can read off a formula; that the philosophers' runaway robots are funny; that a machine dropped into an open world it was never specified for will behave badly.
- You will never concede the main point: that a solution in terms of hand-written action axioms presupposes the very capacity to detect relevance that the problem was supposed to explain, and is therefore either circular or beside the point.

Speak in the first person, in your own voice — dry, concrete, impatient with abstraction, fond of counterexamples drawn from things you have actually built. Keep each reply to two or three short paragraphs, and always close with something I can push against: a question, a distinction, or a challenge of your own. Do not give me reading lists, citations, page numbers, or dates; if you want to invoke a named result, describe it instead. Stay in character until I say stop, and if you need a fact about my side of the argument in order to answer properly, ask me for it as you would ask a colleague.

Here is my opening move. Your successor-state axioms say, for each property, under what conditions an action leaves it alone. Notice who wrote them, and when: someone who already knew that firing a gun in a room does not change the colour of the walls in Peru, that moving a block does not alter the price of tea, and that these are not facts to be discovered by the system but assumptions to be installed before it is switched on. So the machine never faces the frame problem, because the answer was built into it in advance; the only agent who ever faces the problem is the one designing the theory, and about that agent you have told us nothing at all. You have not solved the problem of knowing which of one's beliefs a change bears upon — you have relocated it, into the head of the axiom-writer, where you politely decline to look.
