Six months ago I bought into Fern Court, a five-unit housing co-op, and last month the rest of the board talked me into chairing the rules committee, which I have regretted roughly weekly. Our charter has a clause everybody's very proud of, §7 "personal domain": it lists a handful of choices each member makes for themselves and that the membership cannot vote to override. Ruth decides whether her Tuesday support group gets the community room; Devin decides whether he gets it for his drum-and-film nights; that kind of thing. Then there's §12, which says no arrangement shall stand if an alternative is available that all members agree would be better. Two weeks ago those two clauses walked into each other and I still cant tell what the wreckage means.

The situation: the room is free Saturday nights, and there are exactly three states. Nobody uses it (call that o), Ruth's group uses it (a), or Devin uses it (b). Ruth would rather the room sit dark than have her group in it — the group is folding anyway and she'd have to set up and clean — but if somebody is going to use it she'd far rather it be her than Devin, whose drumming she hears through the wall. So Ruth: o, then a, then b. Devin genuinely thinks a dark common room betrays the reason the co-op was founded, and he likes Ruth and wants her group to survive, so he ranks a, then b, then o. Tomás, the fourth member, doesn't care at all: a and b are exactly equal to him and both beat o. Now apply §7 literally. Ruth is decisive over o versus a and prefers o, so o ranks above a. Devin is decisive over o versus b and prefers b, so b ranks above o. Transitivity forces b above a — Devin's drum night over Ruth's group. But §12 seems to say a ranks above b, since Ruth and Devin both prefer it and Tomás doesn't object. No consistent ranking. We punted the vote, and afterwards Marguerite and Theo got into it at the potluck and I ended up between them taking notes.

Marguerite was an econ major and never lets anyone forget it. Theo runs the free-repair table in the basement and has read more philosophy than he admits to. Here's how it went, more or less in order:

1. Theo: nothing actually went wrong on Saturday. A right isn't a row in a ranking, it's a choice someone gets to make. Ruth declined, Devin took the room, we ended at b. Nobody's §7 right was violated. Sure, a was better for Ruth and Devin — but "you may decline" means precisely that you're allowed to end up somewhere worse, and §12 was written to catch situations where we're all stuck together in a bad arrangement, not to overrule a member's own call. Marguerite: that's not an escape hatch, it's a confession. If §7 can land us on b when a is preferred-or-equal for everyone, §12 is a dead letter, and you should say so and amend it instead of insisting the charter is coherent. Also, "no right was violated" only sounds clean because you're grading the outcome by the rights and grading the rights by nothing at all.

2. Marguerite: the engine of the whole thing is that Devin's ranking is about what Ruth does inside her own domain. Rule that out — require that nobody have preferences over what others do within their §7 sphere — and the cycle can't be assembled; I'm pretty sure Sen himself floated something like that. Theo: two problems. You can't honestly classify those preferences as meddlesome, because Devin's preference is about a shared room going unused and Ruth's is about noise through her wall, not about each other's conduct as such. And even if you could, that restriction doesn't solve the paradox, it deletes exactly the cases the paradox exists for — the whole reason you write a §7 is that your neighbors disapprove of what you do.

3. Theo: also, Tomás. §12 says "all members agree would be better." Tomás doesn't agree a is better than b, he's indifferent. So §12 never fires and there's no contradiction this week. Marguerite: on that wording you've made §12 permanently inert, since one indifferent or silent member blocks it forever, and any efficiency clause worth the ink requires only that somebody prefer the alternative and nobody prefer against it. And anyway next month Tomás might care, so you've won the week and lost the charter.

4. Marguerite: let them trade. If §7 domains are waivable and transferable, Devin can hand his Saturday claim to Ruth in exchange for the storage closet, we end at a, cycle gone. Theo: waiving is what already happened in spirit and we still ended at b, because §7 gives Devin authority over the pair (o, b), not a preference to be netted out. And if the domains are freely transferable, someone can buy up everyone's §7 sphere and decide everything, which isn't liberalism, it's a dictatorship with paperwork.

5. Theo: the real defect is drafting. §7 assigns authority over pairs of social states that overlap — Ruth's (o, a) and Devin's (o, b) share o — and the leftover pair (a, b) belongs to nobody, so it gets settled by transitivity and §12 wrestling. Write the domains as separate dimensions each member controls and there's no cycle. Marguerite: you can't partition it. One room, one Saturday. The overlap isn't a drafting slip, it's scarcity: whenever two personal domains both bear on the same scarce thing, the pairs overlap, and that will be most interesting cases.

6. Marguerite: notice what you just did — you proposed fixing this by re-drawing the domains, not by picking a clause to kill. So you agree the trouble is in the formalization, and my point was never that Sen's formalization is sacred. Theo: then we agree. Marguerite: we don't, because your redrawing still leaves somebody deciding a versus b.

7. Theo, parting shot: your framing assumes a standing ranking of social states has to exist. Maybe the co-op shouldn't have one — just a procedure: ask Ruth, ask Devin, done. Marguerite: a procedure is a ranking you declined to write down, and your procedure ranks b above a, which is what §12 forbids.

Please go through this move by move rather than giving me a general essay about Sen. For each of the seven exchanges say who's right, or who's right about which part of it, and flag the ones where you think it's genuinely unresolved. Then answer me four things specifically: does Tomás's indifference actually save us, and does the wording of §12 matter as much as Theo thinks; is Theo's choice-versus-ranking distinction an escape or just a restatement of the problem; is Marguerite's no-meddling restriction a fix or a change of subject; and can two personal domains over one scarce room ever be written non-overlapping, or is overlap unavoidable in principle? Finally, which of them came out better overall, and what did both of them miss — because I have to bring a recommendation to the vote Tuesday and right now I'd be inventing one. If there's a version of §12 that could actually coexist with §7 as written, I'd like to see it spelled out in charter language.
