I'm in a comparative philosophy seminar and next week each of us has to hand in a "position card" — our own stated view on one topic — so the rest of the group can spend the whole session attacking it. Mine is Zhuangzi on death. I've been sitting with this for a couple of months and I thought my set was defensible, but a friend who reads a lot more Chinese philosophy than me said, sort of half-joking, "you can't have three and four on the same card," and then wouldn't explain, which is maddening. Here's the card, roughly:

1. The drumming-on-the-basin episode isn't Zhuangzi showing off about being unmoved. In the passage he asks his friend whether he thinks he didn't feel it like anyone else when she first died, and what he faults afterwards is the continued wailing. So the target is a belief about death, not the first pang of loss.

2. The belief he's attacking is that death takes something away from a me that persists. His alternative is the transformation picture: he traces her back to before she was born, before she had a body, before she had any spirit at all, into some murky undifferentiated jumble out of which one change produced spirit, another produced a body, another produced birth — and now another change and she's dead, which he compares to the four seasons running their course. Nothing is lost in the sense the wailing assumes, so the wailing is a kind of category error about what happened. He says as much: to keep sobbing would show he doesn't understand ming.

3. But I don't think he's merely neutral about death either. In the story where he comes across a dry old skull on the road to Chu, pokes it with his whip, speculates about how it got there, then uses it for a pillow and dreams of it, the skull tells him that among the dead there are no rulers above and no subjects below and no chores of the four seasons, that their springs and autumns are as endless as heaven and earth, and that even a king facing south on his throne has no more joy than that. When Zhuangzi offers to get someone in charge of fate to give it back its flesh and bones and send it home to its parents and wife and old friends, the skull knits its brows and refuses — why would it trade a joy greater than a king's for the toil of being alive again? I read that as Zhuangzi's considered view: death is better than life, not just not-worse. That's what keeps his calm from being clenched-jaw stoicism.

4. At the same time I want to insist he's a skeptic in a strong sense. The bit about how do I know that loving life isn't a mistake, and whether hating death isn't like a child who got lost young and forgot the way home, plus the butterfly at the end of that chapter, are there to show we simply can't know what it's like on the other side. He isn't on the hook for positive claims about death.

5. I take the whole enterprise to be therapeutic rather than doctrinal. The question to ask of any of his remarks about death is whether it loosens the grip of the fear, not whether it's true, and he'd be perfectly happy using a claim he wouldn't defend in an argument.

6. And I don't think he's indifferent to surviving. Half the book is about keeping your life intact: the tree that gets spared because its wood is good for nothing, the butcher whose blade never dulls because he goes in through the gaps, all that stuff about nourishing life and not letting yourself get consumed by other people's projects. So the calm about death comes with a real preference for staying alive.

One thing I want checked separately: people keep telling me 1 and 2 can't both be true, on the grounds that if you really accepted the transformation story you wouldn't feel anything at all, so the first pang proves he doesn't accept it either. My answer is that a pang is a reflex and not a judgment, and 2 is about judgments. Does that kill the objection or is there something left in it?

So: go through the card and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and how badly — rank the real ones by severity rather than dumping a list where everything sounds equally urgent — and for each one, what the cheapest revision would be and what it costs me. Then say which single change to the card clears the most trouble at once, and whether my friend is right. I'd also like you to flag clearly where you're telling me something that's actually settled among people who work on this text versus where you're taking a side in a live disagreement, because I've been burned twice already by someone presenting one reading as "what Zhuangzi obviously means." Please end with a line in exactly this form: Verdict: worst pair is (X)/(Y) — cheapest fix: <short phrase>.
