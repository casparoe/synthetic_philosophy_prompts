Fourteen months ago I had a nephrectomy in order to give a kidney to a stranger — a non-directed donation, arranged through my regional transplant center — and I have spent a good part of the recovery year writing about it. I am not an academic philosopher; I work in insurance regulation. But I have read as much of the transplant-ethics literature as a layperson reasonably can, and I have drafted an essay of about 1,300 words that I intend to submit to a general-interest practical-ethics venue. Two friends have read it. One thought it was the most interesting thing she had read all year; the other said it was clever and wrong, and could not say which parts were which. I am hoping you can do better than either of them.

The essay follows. Note that I have deliberately paraphrased every position I invoke and quoted nothing, because I was working from memory and did not want to fabricate a line of somebody's prose. Where I have described a view, treat the description as mine and check it, not the wording.

**The Debt and the Price**

**1. The asymmetry.** Nearly every wealthy country prohibits the purchase and sale of kidneys for transplantation while permitting, praising, and in some cases quietly subsidizing their gift. In the United States it is a federal crime to buy or sell a human organ for transplant; it is a civic honor to give one away. We already pay people for other parts of the bodily economy — plasma donors are compensated by the visit in several countries, and gamete donors are paid substantial sums, with egg donors commanding far more than sperm donors. Nobody argues that the plasma or the sperm is degraded by the payment. So the question is not whether the body can enter the market. It is why one particular organ, moving in one particular direction, is thought to be spoiled by money.

**2. Two objections, and the one that survives.** The standard objections to kidney sales come in a pair. The first is about consent: a person selling a kidney in a slum, or under a debt, or with a child's surgery to pay for, is not choosing freely in any sense that matters. The second is about corruption: pricing an organ expresses a valuation that is inappropriate to it, converting a person's body into a thing of use, and in doing so degrades both the organ and the person.

The consent objection is, on inspection, not an objection to kidney sales at all. It is an objection to poverty. If the reason the transaction is wrong is that the seller's alternatives are so bad that this is the least terrible of them, then the wrong lies entirely in the alternatives, and the prohibition does nothing about them. It leaves the would-be seller exactly as poor as before, minus an option, and lets the rest of us feel that we have taken a stand against desperation by removing one of the few things a desperate person can do. This has the structure of a hostage demand. What the consent objection really wants is redistribution, and it settles for a ban because a ban is cheap.

The corruption objection is the one that is supposed to do the real work, because it is the one that can explain why the gift is fine and the sale is not. And here I think it fails. If the wrong consists in treating a kidney as a transferable object at all — as something that can be separated from a person and handed over — then the gift is exactly as corrupted as the sale, since the kidney moves either way, and moves for the same reason: somebody else needs it. If instead the wrong consists only in the presence of a price, then the corruption objection collapses back into the consent objection, since the only thing a price adds is a payment whose adequacy we can assess by looking at the seller's circumstances. Neither branch generates the asymmetry the objection was summoned to generate. The corruption objection is either too strong, indicting the transplant system as a whole, or too weak, restating an argument about poverty.

**3. Repugnance.** A third response is that markets in organs are repugnant, and that repugnance is itself a constraint on what can be traded. I take the point seriously but think it is misdescribed by its friends. Repugnance is, in the end, an aggregate of individual distaste, and distaste is a preference like any other: it can be satisfied, traded off, and, crucially, revised by information. Usury was once repugnant and is now invisible; horse meat and dwarf-tossing bans tell us how arbitrary the feelings can be. On this view, repugnance toward organ sales is a prediction about our own ignorance — that once people understand that a paid donor in Chennai and an unpaid donor in Ohio are exercising the same capacity under different budgets, the feeling will thin out the way it has thinned out before. It is not a reason. It is a symptom of a reason nobody has yet articulated, and the prohibition should be treated as provisional.

There is also a structural problem. Repugnance is characteristically felt by people who are not party to the transaction, about transactions they will never be offered. We do not generally permit third parties to veto exchanges that cost them nothing. If I find your dinner repugnant, my disapproval is not a reason for you to skip it. The organ case is treated as different only because the third parties here are numerous and the dinner is a person.

**4. The unrepayable debt.** The gift is not costless to the recipient, and this is where I think the argument against sales has been looking in the wrong place.

Gift exchange creates obligations. Anthropologists have long noted that a gift binds: there is an obligation to give, an obligation to receive, and an obligation to reciprocate, and the third is what makes gift-giving a social institution rather than a transfer. Most gifts can be reciprocated, eventually, in kind or in something like it. A kidney cannot. The recipient of a kidney receives something that can never be returned, to a person they will in most cases never meet, and therefore occupies a permanent and asymmetric position of debt.

I have seen this from the other side, and it is not comfortable. I did not want anybody's gratitude and I have no use for it, but I now hold a standing claim on a stranger's moral life that I cannot cancel, and I have become, in a small and unwanted way, a creditor. When people learn what I did, they treat me differently for a while. The system calls this beautiful. It is at minimum a burden distributed without anybody's consent.

A sale, by contrast, extinguishes the obligation. Once the money has changed hands nothing is outstanding; neither party owes the other anything, and both can walk away whole. If we care about the moral position of the recipient and not only about their creatinine levels, we should notice that the gift system reliably manufactures unrepayable debts and then counts the manufacturing as a virtue. The obvious reply is that gratitude is a good rather than a burden — that a life saved by a stranger's generosity is a richer thing than a life purchased. But gratitude is a good where it can be expressed, answered, and eventually squared. Where it can only accumulate, it is a lien.

**5. Forbearance.** A final point, which I am least sure of. The gift system is not sustained by a norm that most people follow. It is sustained by a norm that almost nobody follows, plus the fact that almost nobody sells. The donors are a tiny fraction of the population, and the system works only because they are surrounded by a much larger group of people who refrain from converting the same asset into cash. I want to call this the forbearance dividend: part of what pays for the gift system is the payment that prohibited sellers never receive, and which is therefore never recorded as a cost anywhere. The moral credit goes to the donors. The cost is also borne by people whose contribution consists in not doing something.

This has an awkward implication. The gift system is admirable only at low volume. Its beauty depends on scarcity — of donors, and of alternatives.

**6. What I would actually propose.** Not a market. A single public buyer: the state as monopsonist, at a fixed price, with a mandatory cooling-off period, independent advocacy counsel for the seller, lifetime follow-up care at public expense, and the option of taking the price as an annuity or directing it elsewhere. Note what that last clause does. If a seller directs the whole payment to a charity, or to their own child's education, or simply declines to spend it, we have arrived at the gift by a longer route — with the same organ, the same recipient, and the same absence of enrichment. That we would call this praiseworthy and the identical transaction with the money kept a moral disaster suggests, to me, that the objection was never to the transfer. It was to the visibility of the price.

Then give me a verdict on the central claim in section 2 — that the corruption objection cannot ground the gift/sale asymmetry, because it is either too strong or too weak. **Second, an interpretive audit.** Tell me where I have misdescribed a position I am invoking, or collapsed a distinction that was doing necessary work. I am especially unsure about three places: my account of the repugnance argument in section 3, my claim in section 2 that paid gamete and plasma markets make the organ case a question of degree rather than kind, and the move in section 4 from the anthropology of gift exchange to a claim about what recipients of kidneys actually owe. Do not send me a reading list or citations — I cannot use them and I am not asking for scholarship. If you think I have got a position wrong, state what the position actually says, in your own words, and explain what changes for my argument once it is stated correctly. Similarly, mark any empirical claim I have made that needs support I have not provided, but do not supply figures you cannot stand behind; I would much rather you flag the gap than fill it with something plausible.

**Third, an editorial judgment.** Is section 4 the best thing here, and should the unrepayable-debt idea be promoted from the middle of the essay to its center — with the corruption dilemma demoted to supporting material? Or is it a striking observation that does not survive the reply I gesture at in the same section? And separately: is this essay interesting, or merely contrarian? Concretely — would a reader who already believes kidney sales should be legalized get anything from it that they did not have before, or have I written an argument whose only appeal is to people who disagree with its conclusion?
