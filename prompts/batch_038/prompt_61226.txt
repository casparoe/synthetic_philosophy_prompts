I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy writing the middle chapter of my dissertation on determinism, prediction, and computational limits. I need a thought experiment built to order, and I want to be explicit about what it is for, since that fixes what will count as cheating.

My target is a familiar claim: that an open future — one not already settled in a way accessible from inside the world — requires physical indeterminism. Determinists, on this view, can offer only unpredictability in an agent-relative, epistemic sense, which is supposed to be philosophically inert. I want a case that makes it hard to dismiss computational intractability that way, by exhibiting it alongside the two other standard candidates and showing that it does work they cannot.

Please design four worlds, W1 through W4, sharing one concrete substrate so that the matching is checkable: the same deterministic lattice-style system with the same local update rule, the same embedded observer who must learn in advance whether she will perform one specified action at a later time (say, whether she will close a valve), the same classical, non-chaotic physics. No quantum randomness, no exotic spacetimes, no oracle machines. The worlds may differ only in two controlled slots: (a) the computational profile of the prediction task — feasible, intractable, or not Turing-computable at all, with any non-computability entering through the initial condition rather than the law (for instance a state fixed by a non-computable real); and (b) whether the prediction, once produced, is causally available to the observer before the predicted event or sealed in a record she can read only afterwards.

The verdicts I need: in W1 the dynamics is feasible but the prediction is available to the observer, and prediction fails for diagonal reasons of the kind behind Popper's argument against forecasting our own future knowledge and its modern formalizations in terms of embedded inference devices. In W2 the prediction task is intractable — PSPACE-complete or EXP-complete in the number of steps to be predicted — so the observer cannot finish before the event and the self-referential route never opens. In W3 the output is sealed and the initial condition is non-computable, so the task is undecidable for any device at Turing-machine level. W4 is the control: feasible dynamics, sealed output, prediction succeeds. You may stipulate complexity profiles, but you must argue for them: give a reduction sketch or a structural reason why the prediction task really sits in the class claimed, and why the observer's resource bound is credible for a physically realistic embedded device rather than an arbitrary assumption.

One further constraint on W1: the coupling between prediction and action must not be a stipulated "do the opposite" mechanism bolted onto a machine built to refute prediction. It has to fall out of the observer's own reasons or functional organization — an ordinary disposition that a non-perverse agent might have.

Then demonstrate that the design works, in this order:

1. For each world, state precisely which obstacle blocks the prediction and rule out the other two — showing, not asserting, that the diagonal route is unavailable in W2 and W3 and that the intractability is genuinely absent from W1.
2. For each blocked world, name the single feature whose removal collapses the obstacle, say what the world then becomes, and say whether the philosophical point survives that collapse.
3. Answer the matching objection head-on: the sealed-versus-available slot is a causal difference, so the worlds are not identical. Argue that the variation is innocent, or concede exactly what it costs.
4. Give the best attack a defender of the indeterminism-only thesis can mount against W2 specifically — I expect her to say that intractability is a fact about the observer's limitations rather than about the world, and so leaves the metaphysics untouched — and the strongest reply available to me without importing new machinery.
5. Close with your honest assessment in no more than five sentences: which of the three obstacles, if any, does real work for the claim that a fully deterministic world can leave the future open in a sense that is not merely epistemic. If your answer is none, say so and say what the case shows instead.

Format: at most 200 words per world's description, roughly 1,400 words overall. Formalism is welcome, but every formal step needs a gloss in prose, and nothing may depend on a result you cannot explain from scratch. Do not cite or recommend literature — I have the bibliography and want the reasoning. The case fails if the verdicts are stipulated rather than generated by the structure of the worlds, and I will be reading specifically for that.
