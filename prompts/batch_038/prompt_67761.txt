Bit of a weird one. I write novels — family sagmas, mostly — and I keep a folder of half-finished essays for the ugly middle of a book. This one started because a character kept outrunning me: a woman who drinks, badly, and then forbids her son from drinking, and the scene only works if the reader feels she's somehow *not allowed* to say it while also feeling she's right. So I tried to write down what that "not allowed" actually is. Below is the skeleton. I'd like comments, especially on the parts I flag.

The skeleton:

1. Two things get run together under "standing." Call them authority (are you a legitimate participant in the practice of holding people to this norm?) and clarity (can the blamed person actually hear you, or does your own conduct drown out the words?). The interesting cases are the ones where you have one and not the other.

2. Proposal: standing is indexical to a wrong, not to a person. My character hasn't lost the right to say anything about anything; she's lost it about *this*, in *this* register, right now. That predicts that a flat "I did the same thing and it ruined me" lands differently from "you mustn't" — the first isn't blame at all, it's testimony, so it doesn't need standing. I want a name for that second mode and "confessional blame" is obviously bad, since it isn't blame.

3. Mechanism claim, the part I'm least sure of: blame asks someone to accept a cost. When the blamer has dodged the same cost and hasn't acknowledged it, the request is defective not because it's insincere but because it's *unpriced* — she's assigning a cost she's never agreed to pay. So standing comes back not by becoming blameless but by showing up on the ledger: apologize, admit, or visibly carry the same cost. That makes standing recoverable by acknowledgment alone, which feels too cheap, and I cant tell whether it's too cheap or whether that's just the shape of the thing.

4. Test cases I threw at it, with what the view seems to say:
- the reformed drinker who never once mentions her past: view says full standing, which strikes me as wrong, or at least sad;
- the brother who's still drinking and says "don't end up like me": view says no standing, but the novel insists this is the strongest scene in the book;
- the neighbour with no history either way who says the same words to the same kid: view says full standing, and everyone in the room agrees she's got no business saying it.
That third one might kill the view, because "no business" doesn't look like a standing failure at all on my account — it looks like something else entirely.

What I want:
- Does the authority/clarity split in (1) do real work, or does it just redescribe the mess in two voices?
- Is (3) a mechanism or a metaphor I've fallen in love with? If it's a metaphor, what's it hiding?
- Pick one of the three test cases and tell me whether the view can absorb it. If it can't, say whether the fix is local or whether the whole ledger picture goes down with it.
- Tell me bluntly if part of this is something people have already said better — just say which part is stale. No reading list, please, I can't chase it down right now and half the fun was thinking I'd found it myself.

Also flag anywhere I sound like I'm reporting a settled debate when it's actually wide open. I have no training in this, so I've probably walked into a room where everyone's already arguing.
