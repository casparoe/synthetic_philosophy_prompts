I teach a 90-minute advanced undergraduate seminar in comparative politics with 18 students. Most of them have already read the standard treatments of democratic backsliding and have arrived with settled verdicts about particular governments. I want an in-class simulation that makes them feel two things the literature asserts but that reading never produces: that a sequence of individually survivable steps can amount to something no single step would have, and that "has this country stopped being a democracy?" is partly a question about who is asking and what hangs on the answer.

Please design the activity in full for an invented country — not Hungary, Poland, Turkey, or the United States — so that students cannot substitute a prior political verdict for the work of applying criteria. I want the following mechanics built in, and I want you to make them work as a single game rather than as four separate exercises:

1. **Sealed bright lines.** Before the simulation begins, each student privately writes down the one event that would convince them the country has ceased to be a democracy. The cards are collected unread and returned only at the end. The payoff should turn on whether the sequence actually triggered each student's own threshold, and on whether they noticed when it did.

2. **A non-monotone dossier.** Write out, in full, ten to twelve measures enacted over roughly eight fictional years, each with a plausible public justification in the register its sponsors would actually use. At least three must be genuine democratic improvements or neutral housekeeping, so that voting "anti-democratic" is not a free default and the students cannot smell a trap. After each measure, students vote twice on hand signals: whether the measure itself is anti-democratic, and whether the country is still a democracy as of that moment. Both tallies go on the board and are never erased.

3. **Secret conceptions.** Under each seat is a card assigning one of three conceptions of democracy — competitive elections only; elections plus constraints on the executive and protected rights; equal effective political influence. Students vote from within their assigned conception without disclosing it. Separately, each student also receives a public role with a stake in the classification: government counsel, an opposition legislator, a regional organization's rapporteur whose body controls access to funds, a judge whose jurisdiction was just narrowed, an editor at an outlet receiving state advertising, a mayor whose town depends on central transfers, and so on. Write the role cards so that no role may argue in the register of naked self-interest; every justification must be sincere public reasoning, and every role-holder must state one point in the other side's favor before voting. Some students will hold a role that contradicts their assigned conception — I want that tension deliberately, and I want it surfaced in the debrief.

4. **The ratchet and the election shock.** Before the end, students classify each measure as reversible by an ordinary future majority, reversible only by supermajority or constitutional amendment, or practically irreversible. Then announce that the opposition wins 44 percent of the vote and a bare majority of seats, and ask what it can actually undo. Immediately follow with the incoming government's first bill, which reopens the judicial appointments, and ask whether that is itself backsliding.

Halfway through, appoint three students as a formal classification committee whose finding triggers a funding-suspension clause in a regional treaty. The rest of the class knows this. Give the committee members a wager: they gain standing tokens if a majority of the class would endorse their final report at the end, and lose them if not. Everyone else earns tokens back by being able to state, at the end, a single decision rule that reproduces their own voting record, checked against the tally by a peer jury. Specify the token arithmetic and what tokens buy.

Then give me: a minute-by-minute timeline; the exact materials and board layout; a debrief with ordered questions that maps moments in the game onto the diagnostic threshold problem, the reversibility problem, and the who-decides problem, and that finally asks whether the disagreement the class just had is about facts or about the concept; a closing phase that maps the twelve steps onto two real cases by structural match only, without asking anyone to render a verdict on a real country, with a facilitator script for when the American case hijacks the room; a frank list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including students who game the vote by declaring a dictatorship early and students who play their roles as villains; and an honest statement of which features of backsliding the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply myself. I already suspect the compression of eight years into forty minutes destroys the baseline-shifting effect the whole thing is supposed to demonstrate — tell me whether that is right and what can be salvaged.

No bibliography, reading list, or citations, and no quoted passages. Write the answer in American English.
