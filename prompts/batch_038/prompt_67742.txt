I run a monthly civic evening in a public library — about 24 adults, mixed ages, no philosophy background, 90 minutes. Design a structured deliberation, using an invented town and an invented honoree, that makes participants feel the collapse of a move people constantly make in monument debates: the claim that we can honor someone for one specific thing while withholding approval of the rest of what they did and believed. I want the rules arranged so that this move succeeds early in the session and then fails later, once the committee's own earlier decisions have committed it to something — and fails without my ever saying so. They should hit the wall before anyone names it.

Give me the case, with two or three facts deliberately left underdetermined (say which, and what each omission is there to provoke), the roles or blocs, the sequence of rounds and votes with a minute-by-minute timeline, and whatever short handouts are needed. Then a debrief that maps what just happened onto two distinctions: between honoring a person and honoring a deed, and between what a monument said at its dedication and what it says for as long as the community keeps maintaining it. Close with a candid account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, how to steer it back, and which feature of the problem no exercise of this kind can reproduce.
