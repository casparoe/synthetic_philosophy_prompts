I'm teaching a philosophy-of-economics seminar this term, 16 students, 75 minutes, and one of the weeks is on interpersonal utility comparisons — whether you can actually weigh one person's welfare against another's, or whether that's a category error dressed up as arithmetic. I want an in-class game that makes them run into the wall themselves. Nobody should hear the phrase until the debrief, and I don't want to lecture beforehand.

Here's my seed; make it work or tell me to throw it out. Every student gets a sealed card with a private conversion rate — how much felt-good one token turns into for them — and only I hold the master table. They can talk, bargain, form coalitions, whatever, but any number they say out loud is on thier own unanchored scale, so "that's worth 10 to me" means something different in every mouth. Before the pot gets divided I want a calibration phase where the group has to try to agree what one unit even is, and I want them to find out they cant get there from ordinal information alone (only "you'd rather have A than B" is admissible at that stage). Then a fixed pot is split by whatever rule they land on. I also want one hidden extreme card in the deck — someone whose conversion rate is wildly out of line with everyone else's — and I want the class to notice what their own allocation did to that person before I say a word. On top of that, pay a few of them on reported numbers rather than true ones, so when the reports don't line up at the end they can't tell whether they're looking at lying or at incommensurability. That collision is the whole point, so please don't design it away.

What I need back: rules and materials, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a payoff scheme using a real but tiny stake — I can offer 20 participation points that have to be split, no actual money. Then a debrief that pins specific moments from the game onto specific moves in the debate: the value-judgment objection that got these comparisons kicked out of welfare economics in the 1930s, the social-welfare-function way of smuggling them back in, and the impartial-observer/extended-sympathy trick. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it live, and give me an honest account of the cheat baked into the design — I'm holding a ground-truth table of everyone's conversion rates, which is exactly what the real problem says nobody can ever have. What does that do to what they learn, and what could stand in for it? Last thing: flag anything in there that's likely to make a student genuinely uncomfortable (being handed a card saying you feel things at a tenth of the normal rate isn't nothing) and how you'd defuse it.
