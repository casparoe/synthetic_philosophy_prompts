I'm a third-year PhD student in philosophy of science, and below is a draft section of a paper I'm circulating ahead of a workshop on determinism and prediction. I want comments rather than a rewrite.

Draft:

The standard moral drawn from chaos is that determinism no longer delivers predictability. Given a positive Lyapunov exponent, an uncertainty δ in the initial condition grows roughly like δe^{λt}, so the span over which a forecast stays inside a tolerance Δ is about T ≈ (1/λ)·ln(Δ/δ). Because the dependence on δ is only logarithmic, tenfold better measurement buys one extra e-folding time: a fixed increment, not a proportional one. On this telling, Laplace's demon dies of arithmetic rather than of metaphysics, and determinism turns out to be perfectly compatible with wholesale epistemic failure.

I think that is right for the wrong reason, and that stating it this way hides what is actually interesting.

First, the argument is pitched at the level of individual trajectories, and there it is at least partly answered by shadowing. In a hyperbolic system, every sufficiently fine pseudo-orbit — every sequence of states that almost satisfies the dynamics, which is what numerical integration and iterated noisy observation produce — stays uniformly close to some genuine orbit. So the demon's computational surrogate is not emitting nonsense: it is emitting an accurate description of a real trajectory of the system. What it cannot do is say which trajectory. Chaos, in the hyperbolic case, is not a failure to compute orbits but a failure to identify them, and the shadowing orbit is generally not the orbit through the state we in fact started from. That relocates the difficulty from the dynamics to something indexical: the deficit is not that no true trajectory matches the computation, but that nothing internal to the computation selects ours.

Second, the trajectory framing misdescribes what a forecast is for. What is predicted and what is measured are never bare states but cells of a coarse-graining Π. Call a system Π-predictable when evolving-then-coarse-graining and coarse-graining-then-evolving agree — when the image of the cell containing the datum under the dynamics stays inside the cell reached by evolving a representative of it. Chaos is the systematic breakdown of that commutation: the dynamics stretches a cell across many others, so the two routes diverge even though each is perfectly well defined and deterministic. Sensitive dependence then looks less like the explication of unpredictability than like a symptom of it. That reading has some independent support, since sensitivity is known to follow from weaker hypotheses (transitivity together with dense periodic orbits), which is a reason to treat it as a consequence rather than as the core notion.

Third — and this is where I am least confident — the first two points pull against each other. Shadowing says the fine structure of the dynamics remains computationally accessible; the coarse-graining account says predictability is fixed by a descriptive scale we choose. Predictability thus comes apart into a robust dynamical fact and a contextual fact about description, and I want to argue that the deterministic-yet-unpredictable case gets all of its bite from the second. If that is right, the familiar claim that chaos restores contingency inside a deterministic world is not merely unsupported but a category error, since nothing in the coarse-graining story supplies a genuine alternative possibility. What chaos supplies is underdetermination of the cell by the datum, which is ignorance wearing a dynamical costume.

End of draft.

The comments I want:

1. Is the shadowing paragraph technically defensible as written? In particular, does shadowing really license the stronger gloss that the computation identifies the wrong orbit, or only the weaker claim that some orbit is nearby, and how should the draft hedge if the latter?
2. Is the Π-commutation proposal substantive, or is it sensitive dependence re-described in the language of partitions? Tell me which, and point to the sentence where the draft should make a concession it currently avoids.
3. Flag anywhere the argument would run differently if the system in view were stochastic rather than deterministic — I suspect the second paragraph quietly assumes determinism and the third quietly does not.
4. In the third paragraph, is "category error" too strong? The obvious reply is that coarse-graining plus the unrecoverability of the cell is all any defensible notion of openness could amount to, and I do not yet have an answer. Be blunt about overclaim wherever you find it.
