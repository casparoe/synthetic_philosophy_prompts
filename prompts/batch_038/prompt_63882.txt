I convene a small reading group on cause prioritization. We score candidate causes on importance, tractability, and neglectedness, and for the purposes of the argument below we stipulate the following operational reading: neglectedness is not a head-count but a marginal-value term, N(C) being the expected value added by one further full-time researcher working on C, given the number and skill of the people already working on C, with a cause's overall score approximately the product of its importance, its tractability, and its N. One of our members has produced the following argument, which I cannot refute and whose conclusion I do not accept.

1. The framework tells an agent to work on the cause with the highest score, where score ≈ importance × tractability × neglectedness.
2. By our stipulation, N(C) is fixed by the actual allocation of effort: N(C) is high only insofar as too few people are working on C relative to its importance and tractability.
3. Publishing a high N(C) moves people into C, and moving people into C lowers N(C).
4. So any correct public use of the framework lowers the score of the very cause it recommends: the recommendation destroys the property that made it a recommendation.
5. The framework is offered as a decision procedure for a community of agents who share their reasoning openly, not as a private rule.
6. A decision procedure that produces worse outcomes the more widely it is adopted cannot be the correct procedure for such a community.
7. Hence our framework is incorrect; and since step 3 used nothing specific to it, every publicly stated criterion that scores options by how few people are already pursuing them is incorrect.

I would like to keep (1) and (2), which seem to be mere bookkeeping, and I am reluctant to abandon (5), since a criterion that works only while it is concealed strikes me as no criterion at all. I am aware that these preferences may be exactly what the argument is exploiting.

Please diagnose it. Reply as a numbered list of exactly five items and nothing else — no preamble, no closing summary — each item one or two sentences:

1. The step you reject, with the precise defect that licenses rejecting it; name the equivocation, suppressed assumption, or invalid inference rather than merely describing the unease.
2. The next-most-plausible candidate for rejection, and what giving it up costs.
3. The candidate after that, and its cost.
4. The third candidate, and its cost.
5. What survives the diagnosis: either the restricted domain in which neglectedness-weighted scoring remains usable or the minimal repair to the framework, together with the single further question that would settle whether the repair works.
