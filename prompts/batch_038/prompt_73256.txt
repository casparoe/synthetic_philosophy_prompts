At our department's informal reading group two people got into a fight about van Fraassen's Reflection principle and I said I'd get an outside opinion. Ravi opened by claiming Reflection is a real requirement of rationality: if you're sure that tomorrow you'll have credence 0.8 in some claim, then your credence in it now, conditional on your having that opinion tomorrow, should already be 0.8 — and anyone who violates that is exposed to a diachronic Dutch book. Elena said that's obviously false becuase a lot of the changes you can foresee in your own opinions are ones you know will be caused by junk: exhaustion, a new romance, a grant you're hoping for, a lecture you know will move you rhetorically rather than logically. If I know that at 3am, running on no sleep, I'll be certain my paper is brilliant, I shouldn't be certain of that now; I should just plan not to email it to the journal at 3am.

Ravi said the standard qualification handles that — Reflection is only meant to govern changes you expect to come from experience or from reasoning, not from a distortion you've already diagnosed — and that the Dutch book result is what does the real work anyway. Elena said the Dutch book result begs the question, since the pump only bites if your later self actually goes along with the later bets; if I know the 3am self is compromised, the rational move is to bind him, Ulysses-style, not to defer to him. Precommitment and deference can tell you to do opposite things, so this isn't just bookkeeping.

Then Ravi asked what Elena's view does with the boring cases: by tomorrow you'll have actually read the referee report, so if you're sure that afterwards you'll think the proof is broken, isn't that already a reason to think it's broken? Elena said no — that's evidence about your psychology, not about the proof, exactly like knowing that once you're tenured you'll think your early work was overrated. Ravi said she cant have it both ways: in the referee case the change is triggered by evidence you can foresee getting but can't access now, while in the tenure case it isn't. Elena's closing move was that this makes Reflection useless rather than wrong — you can only sort the two cases by already knowing what evidence your future self will get, so Reflection either follows from that knowledge or contradicts it, and never does any independent work.

Neither budged and I honestly couldn't referee it. Can you go through the exchange move by move and say who has the better of each one, rather than just giving an overall winner? In particular: does the distortion clause Ravi retreat to actually save the principle, does Elena's point about binding your future self rather than deferring to it hold up, and is her closing charge fair — is Reflection vacuous when true and false when load-bearing, or is there a version that survives both? If there's something important neither of them raised, tell me what it is.
