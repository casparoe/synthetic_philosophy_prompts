**No Determinate Onset: Postdiction and the Two Axes of the Cartesian Theater**

**§1. The debate as it is usually staged.** The case against the Cartesian theater has two components that are almost always run together. The first is against a place: there is no locus at which the brain's parallel streams of processing converge in order to be presented to a subject. The second is against a time: there is no moment at which the content of an experience becomes fixed, no instant of editorial judgment after which what is experienced is settled. In place of both, the multiple drafts model holds that content is elaborated in parallel by distributed processes, that various probes — including verbal report — sample that activity at different points, and that there is no completed draft against which the samples could be checked.

Critics respond that the second component is either trivial or false, and either way idle. If it says only that the assembly of content has duration rather than a sharp boundary, it is uncontroversial and compatible with everything the critic wants: a theory on which content becomes globally available already allows that the availability has a rise time. If it says there is no fact about when an experience occurs, it is false, both because subjects are often good at temporal order judgments and because any architecture in which content becomes available for report must have a first time at which it is so available.

**§2. My proposal: split the denial along two axes.** I want to distinguish two claims that the existing dialectic treats as one.

(A) *Content determinacy*: for a given interval of phenomenal time, there is a fact about what the experience is like across that interval.

(B) *Onset determinacy*: there is a fact about the time at which the content of that interval was fixed — that is, about when the process that settles what the experience is like was completed.

The theater affirms both. The multiple drafts model, as its critics read it, denies both. My central claim is that the position actually licensed by the evidence affirms (A) and denies (B), and that this is a stable view rather than a halfway house: the phenomena that motivate the denial of (B) do not, on inspection, impugn (A) at all.

**§3. The empirical warrant.** Four families of case share a common shape.

First, apparent motion between two differently colored lights: whether the first light is experienced as changing color partway along the path depends on the color of the later light, and reversing the colors moves the reported point of change.

Second, the mislocalization of a moving object relative to a spatially aligned flash: the size of the displacement depends on what the moving object does after the flash, and terminating the motion at the flash markedly reduces the effect.

Third, the illusory redistribution of brief taps along the arm: the felt locations of the early taps depend on the timing and number of the later ones.

Fourth, the referral of cortically initiated experience backward in time by a substantial interval, which is what forces the felt onset of such an experience to be dated earlier than the process that determines it.

In each case the content of an interval at time t is fixed only by processes completed after t. That is a direct failure of (B): there is no time at which the content of the interval was settled, because the settling is done later. But note what is *not* shown. The reports in these cases are crisp, confident, and stable across repetition; subjects do not experience vagueness and then resolution. Nothing here suggests that the content of the interval is indeterminate while it is being experienced. The phenomena bear on the time of fixing, not on the determinacy of what is fixed.

**§4. What the reframing buys.** Three things.

(i) *Against the charge of emptiness.* The conjunction of (A) and the denial of (B) is empirically committal in a way the critics' caricature is not. It predicts a systematic asymmetry: content judgments and temporal order judgments come apart in one direction only, with later events revising the content of earlier intervals rather than merely earlier events constraining later ones. It further predicts that the window over which revision is possible is bounded and of roughly comparable magnitude across sensory modalities — a prediction I have not yet checked and on which I would welcome scrutiny.

(ii) *Against the charge of falsity.* The real target of the denial of (B) is any theory that assigns a timestamp to the moment content becomes globally available. Such a theory sits badly with postdictive revision unless it locates the timestamp at the later, revising process — and if it does that, then the experience is dated after the fact, which is the concession my view asks for.

(iii) *Dissolution of a stalemate.* The objection that subjects are good at temporal order, so there must be a determinate time of experience, is now visible as an argument for (A). It does not touch (B). Much of the literature has taken the strength of the former to settle the latter.

**§5. The cost: a tense problem.** If the content of the interval at t is fixed at t + Δ, in what sense is it the content of the experience *at t*? Three options.

(1) *Retrospective determinacy.* The fixing relation is not tensed: the state at t + Δ determines what the experience at t was. The cost is that the phenomenal past is partly constituted by the present, which strains the intuition that experience is lived as it happens.

(2) *Indeterminate, then settled.* The content at t is genuinely indeterminate until t + Δ. The cost is that this denies (A), collapsing into the view I set out to resist, and it predicts that subjects should sometimes register the indeterminacy, which they do not.

(3) *Two clocks.* Phenomenal time is itself a constructed ordering, distinct from processing time. Onset determinacy fails in phenomenal time and is recovered in processing time. The cost is that processing time appears to need a privileged reader, which looks like the theater reinstated at a new address.

I lean toward (3). My plan for the remainder of the paper is to argue that the reader requirement can be discharged, on the grounds that dating does not require reading: which trace is causally upstream of which is a fact about the system whether or not anything consults it. I am not yet sure this is more than a verbal maneuver.

**§6.** The reframing does not vindicate the multiple drafts model in its full advertised generality. It vindicates a narrower and, I think, more defensible claim: that experience has determinate content without determinate onset, and that the second fact, not the first, is what the theater picture cannot accommodate.

I would be grateful for comments organized under the following headings.

1. **Empirical accuracy.** Take the four families of case in §3 one at a time and tell me where my gloss is wrong, oversimplified, or where the phenomenon has a standard alternative explanation that I have ignored. I am especially worried about the second case: if the effect I describe can be explained without any revision of earlier content, the load-bearing member of §3 goes.

2. **The inference from §3 to the denial of (B).** Does postdictive revision really show that there is no fact about when content was fixed, or only that there is no fact *discoverable from the subject's own perspective* about when it was fixed? If my denial of (B) is covertly epistemic, say so plainly and tell me what the metaphysical version would have to add.

3. **Redundancy.** State the most dangerous charge that the (A)/(B) distinction is a redescription of something already on the table — for instance, that it is the distinction between phenomenal and access consciousness worn in temporal dress, or merely the difference between asking when an experience became conscious and asking when it became reportable. Then tell me which charge is hardest to answer and what the answer should be.

4. **§4(ii).** Is my treatment of global availability theories a strawman? Give me the strongest reply such a theory can make, and say whether it survives the reply or whether I should soften the claim.

5. **§5, option (3).** Does the causal-upstream move rescue the two-clock view, or is it word play? Please formulate the strongest version of the objection that any two-clock account needs a reader somewhere.

6. **Structure and cuts.** Where does the draft lose the thread, what should be removed entirely, and is §4(i)'s claim about a cross-modal window load-bearing — if that claim turns out to be false, does the paper survive without it?

7. **Blunt verdict.** Tell me whether (A)/(B) is a distinction without a difference. If it is, I would rather know now, along with the minimal repair that would let it earn its place.

Please do not supply references, a bibliography, or suggested reading; I will handle citations myself. Judge the argument and the empirical glosses exactly as they are stated above, and where a gloss is too vague to evaluate, say what additional detail you would need rather than supplying a favorable interpretation on my behalf.
