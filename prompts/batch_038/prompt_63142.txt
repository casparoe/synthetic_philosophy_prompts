On Thursday the board of the foundation where I serve as one of nine trustees votes on a proposal drafted by our head of programs, which he describes in the cover memo as a "safe Pareto improvement." The foundation funds product development for diseases that fall almost entirely on low-income countries; our annual research budget is about $60 million, and the partnership we fund through is running roughly $40 million a year short of its target. There are four peer funders of comparable size to us.

The proposal is this: we would publish a standing commitment that for every dollar a peer funder pledges to the partnership, we will add fifty cents — but only to peers that have themselves published a reciprocal conditional commitment of the same kind. Peers that decline to commit would receive our baseline grantmaking and nothing more. As drafted the matching is uncapped. The argument offered for it is that if any peer reciprocates, total disbursement to the partnership rises, and if none does, we will have spent exactly what we would have spent anyway; so the policy either makes things better or changes nothing, and there is no branch of the tree in which we come out worse off.

Three directors oppose it, for three reasons I regard as genuinely distinct, and I would like the opinion to engage them separately rather than folding them together as institutional timidity.

First, verification. The claim that we cannot lose assumes we can distinguish a real reciprocal commitment from an announcement that mimics one. Peer funders are opaque to us. At least one of them may already be funding the partnership unconditionally, in which case our conditionality buys nothing and spends goodwill we currently hold with them.

Second, reference. Our general counsel observes that if a peer adopts a mirror-image policy — disbursing its extra fifty cents only if we have committed to matching — then each side's obligation is defined by reference to the other's policy, and it is not obvious that such a pair of policies has a determinate outcome at all, any more than two contracts that each incorporate the other by reference do. He thinks the proposal may be indeterminate rather than merely risky, and no one in the room has had an answer for him.

Third, standing. Whatever gains the arrangement produces accrue to us and to the peer funders. The losses, if there are losses — disbursement delayed while commitments are negotiated, a funding cycle consumed by bargaining — fall on patients who are not players in the game and have no seat at the table. The directors pressing this objection say the proposal is a Pareto improvement only once the people it exists to serve have been excluded from the accounting.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate to the trustees in advance of the meeting. It should recommend adopting, rejecting, or adopting with specified conditions, and the conditions should be concrete enough to vote on: eligibility criteria for a qualifying reciprocal commitment, a verification mechanism if you think one can work, a cap, and a review or sunset date. It should state plainly what has to be true for the safety claim to hold, which of those conditions are within our power to secure unilaterally and which are not, and where you think the proposal's author is right that his opponents are merely describing ordinary risk under a different name. Give the second objection a section of its own; my own suspicion is that it is the deepest of the three, and if the policy can be repaired against it I want to know how, and if it cannot, I want that said rather than worked around. Finally, treat the strongest consideration on whichever side loses the recommendation as something the losing directors could answer, not as something to be noted and dismissed.
