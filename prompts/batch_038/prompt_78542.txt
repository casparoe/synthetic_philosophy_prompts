**Question (25 points).** A policy unit proposes to raise participation in organ donation and in workplace retirement saving by switching both to opt-out defaults, arguing that because this is a nudge it raises no worries about either coercion or manipulation. In 400–500 words: (a) explain what it is about default effects that makes them the paradigm case of a nudge, and identify the psychological mechanisms that give defaults their force (8 points); (b) say whether the proposal is coercive, and justify your answer (7 points); (c) state and evaluate what you take to be the strongest philosophical objection that the proposal is nonetheless objectionably manipulative (10 points).

The seminar's reading pack covered the standard statement of the nudge framework, a selection of philosophical responses to it, and Feinberg's discussion of soft and hard paternalism. No prior philosophy coursework is assumed. The scale I use is: A, 23–25; A−, 21–22; B+, 19–20; B, 17–18; B−, 15–16; C+, 13–14; C, 11–12; D or F, below 11.

Here is the answer, submitted within the word limit.

> Defaults are the paradigm nudge because a default changes nothing about what a person may choose and yet reliably changes what people do choose. Under a presumed-consent rule for organ donation, or automatic enrolment in a workplace pension, every option stays on the table: one can opt out by returning a form or unticking a box, and no penalty attaches to doing so. That fits the standard definition of a nudge as a feature of the choice architecture that predictably alters behaviour without forbidding options or significantly changing economic incentives. What gives defaults their force is loss aversion, which is what status quo bias consists in: once an allocation is presented as the starting point, people experience moving away from it as a loss, and losses weigh more heavily than equivalent gains. Because the mechanism at work is a bias, and not a change in the options or in the incentives, the proposal is not coercive. Coercion operates by threatening to render some alternative unacceptable, and here nothing is threatened; the opt-out remains as available in principle as it was before the change.
>
> It follows, I think, that the proposal is not manipulative either. Manipulation differs from coercion precisely in depending on concealment: an influence exercised openly cannot deceive anyone, and the authors of the nudge framework insist that nudges should be announced rather than hidden. That insistence does real work in this case, since a presumed-consent rule has to be publicised in order to operate, so no citizen can fail to know that consent is being presumed on their behalf. Publicity also costs the policy nothing, because announcing a default cannot reduce its effectiveness — people remain subject to the same bias whether or not they know they are being steered. Feinberg's soft paternalism supplies the philosophical warrant for the whole arrangement: soft paternalists hold that the state may steer citizens away from self-regarding harm provided the means are gentle rather than compelling, which is exactly what a default is.
>
> The proposal should therefore be welcomed, with one qualification, which I take to be the strongest objection available to a critic. It is not that anyone is forced, but that the choice architect substitutes her values for the citizen's. There is no neutral default: whoever sets the contribution rate, or decides whether the opt-out is a checkbox or a registered declaration, has already taken a substantive position on what counts as a good outcome. That is a genuine worry. But it applies to every default, including the opt-in regime it would replace, which is also a choice made by someone with values. It is thus an argument for selecting defaults carefully, justifying them in public, and revising them when they misfire — not an argument for declining to choose at all.
