I am in the final year of a joint mathematics and philosophy degree, and this term I am the paid peer tutor attached to our introductory axiomatic set theory course — ZFC presented formally, with the antinomies of naive comprehension handled in weeks two and three. Each week roughly a dozen students come to my drop-in session carrying a proposed set-builder specification that an exercise tacitly treats as a set, and my job is to tell them within a few minutes whether the specification is legitimate; if it is not, what exactly has gone wrong; and how to repair it. At present my method is recognition rather than procedure. I have memorized the Russell, Burali-Forti, and Cantor antinomies and I pattern-match against them, which works on textbook cases and fails on novel ones — and the novel ones are the only situation in which a student genuinely needs me.

Please write me a triage procedure I can run on an arbitrary specification of the form {x : φ(x)} or {f(x) : x ∈ A}, where φ is a formula of the language of set theory possibly with parameters. It should satisfy the following.

First, give me a fixed sequence of questions to ask in order, each with a stated pass condition and a stated verdict if the condition fails. It has to be runnable in five to ten minutes with pencil and paper by someone at my level, so it should not presuppose model theory, forcing, or reflection principles.

Second, use a closed vocabulary of verdicts, each defined sharply: something along the lines of ADMISSIBLE BY SEPARATION; ADMISSIBLE ONLY VIA A SPECIFIC CONSTRUCTION PRINCIPLE (identify which, and in what order it must be applied); PROPER-CLASS SIZED; NOT EXPRESSIBLE IN THE FIRST-ORDER LANGUAGE; REQUIRES AN ANTECEDENT SET THAT HAS NOT BEEN SUPPLIED. What I need most here is a discrimination test separating the last three. In practice a student's defective construction could be any of them, and my current instinct is to announce "Russell's paradox" in all three cases, which is wrong at least twice.

Third, for every failing verdict, supply the standard repair — restrict to a set-sized parameter already in hand, promote the collection to a class, reformulate as a schema indexed by the metatheory, or change theory — and state plainly when no repair is available without leaving ZFC.

Fourth, for each verdict give a one-sentence student-facing explanation pitched at someone who has seen the axioms but none of the metatheory.

One correction I want built into the procedure rather than argued about afterwards. I have been telling students never to write a defining condition that quantifies over all sets, on the grounds that this is what generates Russell's paradox. I now believe that advice is false as a general rule, because separation licenses defining formulas that quantify over the whole universe provided a set to separate from has already been given. Tell me whether I am right, and if there is a real kernel in the intuition I was acting on — something about the difference between quantifying over a totality and treating it as a completed object, or a predicativity constraint that ZFC does not actually enforce — locate it precisely and say where it belongs in the procedure, or tell me to drop it entirely.

Then run the procedure end to end on five cases: (i) {x : x ∉ x}; (ii) the collection of all groups of order p, for a fixed prime p; (iii) the collection of all well-orderings of ω and the ordinal obtained by taking their order types, where I expect the student to be certain this is Burali-Forti, so I want to be able to exhibit the antecedent set that makes it legitimate and to say whether any choice is needed; (iv) the real number that diagonalizes against all real numbers definable in fewer than twenty-five English words, where I suspect the failure is not about size at all; and (v) {X ⊆ A : X is finite} for A an infinite set, which I believe is legitimate but which students fear because the defining condition seems to range over an unbounded totality.

Finally, mark explicitly which steps of the procedure cannot terminate in a verdict and must be escalated to the instructor rather than answered by me; tell me which of the five cases the procedure is most likely to misdiagnose and why; and state what the procedure cannot do, in particular where its verdicts would shift if the ambient theory changed — a theory with classes as first-class objects, or one admitting a universal set — and whether I should be raising those alternatives at all with students who ask.

Two practical constraints. I cannot look anything up while tutoring, so please give me no references, further reading, or historical attributions; the reasons must be stated inline so the procedure is self-contained. And format the procedure itself so that it fits on one page I can keep in front of me: numbered steps, verdicts in capitals, and a compact table for the five worked cases. Roughly 1,200 to 1,600 words in total.
