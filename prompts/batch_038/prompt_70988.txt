I am in my second year of a philosophy PhD, working on decision theory, and this term I have been sitting in on the probability group's seminar. Last Thursday's talk concerned prospects whose expected value fails to exist, and it was built around the game usually called the Pasadena game: a fair coin is tossed until the first head appears, and if that first head lands on toss n, the player receives (−1)^(n+1)·2^n/n dollars — so two dollars, then minus two, then eight-thirds, then minus four, then thirty-two fifths, and so on, with the stakes growing without bound and flipping sign each time. Multiplying each payoff by its probability of 2^(−n) yields the series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ⋯, the alternating harmonic series, which converges to ln 2, or roughly sixty-nine cents. But it converges only conditionally: the corresponding series of absolute values is the harmonic series, which diverges.

Afterward, in the corridor, our speaker — call her Marta, a philosopher working on the foundations of decision theory — got into a long argument with a postdoc from the probability group, whom I will call Yusuf. I was standing close enough to follow all of it. I could not tell who was winning, and when I said so out loud each of them insisted I had just conceded their point. You have no stake in the matter, so I would like you to referee.

The exchange ran roughly as follows.

(1) Yusuf opened by saying there is no paradox here at all, only a failure to check integrability. The payoff variable has infinite expected absolute value, so it has no expectation, and that is simply the end of the story. The ln 2 and the rearrangement tricks are ordinary facts about conditionally convergent series, which are facts about series rather than facts about decisions; being shocked by them is like being scandalized that 1/x has no finite integral near zero. A prospect of this kind falls outside the domain of expected utility theory, and the theory is entitled to say so.

(2) Marta replied that outside its domain is doing a great deal of quiet work. Expected utility theory is supposed to rank prospects an agent might actually be offered, and this one involves nothing infinite in its description: a coin, a payoff schedule, a finite stake on every toss. A theory that goes silent is not modest but incomplete, and incompleteness is a defect when the theory's job is to guide action. She added that the measure-theoretic verdict is not the neutral default it appears to be, since Lebesgue integration builds in absolute convergence, whereas the game arrives with a canonical ordering given by the experiment itself — toss one, toss two, toss three. Sum in that order, or equivalently truncate at toss N and let N grow, and you get ln 2. Truncate instead by payoff magnitude and let the cap grow, and you get the same answer, because the stakes increase with the toss number. Two independent natural limiting procedures agree, which is more determinacy than Yusuf's verdict permits.

(3) Yusuf said that two natural procedures agreeing is not the same thing as a value being well defined. He offered the regrouping: take each positive term and follow it with two negatives, giving 1 − 1/2 − 1/4 + 1/3 − 1/6 − 1/8 + 1/5 − 1/10 − 1/12 + ⋯, which is the very same collection of probability–payoff pairs and now converges to half of ln 2. Nothing about that way of listing the outcomes is less legitimate than Marta's, because outcomes as such come with no canonical order. A framework that returns different numbers for the same prospect depending on how the outcomes are enumerated is not incomplete but unsound, and no expectation is the only coherent verdict.

(4) Marta's answer was that the regrouping does not describe the coin game at all. To realize one positive followed by two negatives you would have to change what the coin pays on each toss, which is to specify a different payoff schedule and therefore a different game. So Yusuf has not shown that one prospect has two values; he has shown that two prospects have different values. The actual game has exactly one enumeration, fixed by the temporal structure of the experiment, and under it the value is ln 2. Order-dependence would be a problem only if the ordering were ours to choose, and here it is not.

(5) Yusuf said that this concedes the point that matters. If temporal order is what pins down the value, then expected utility theory is not a theory about distributions over outcomes but a theory about temporally indexed processes, and its verdicts shift when the labels are permuted. Distributions are unordered collections of probability–payoff pairs. If order is to carry normative weight, a reason is needed, and the fact that the coin flips happen one after another is not one, since nothing in rationality cares which second an outcome arrives in provided the payoffs and probabilities are unchanged. He added that the truncation argument proves his case rather than hers, because truncation is a summation procedure and Marta has simply smuggled in a preferred one.

(6) Marta then said that if that is the worry, bound the utility function. Any real agent's utility saturates, and once utility is bounded by some M, the sum of probability times absolute utility is at most M, so every such series converges absolutely and the rearrangement problem evaporates entirely. The paradox requires an agent with unbounded utility, which is the same agent who will pay any price for a St. Petersburg ticket; what should have been given up long ago is not expected utility theory but that idealization.

(7) Yusuf's last move was that this is a genuine response but an expensive one. Bounded utility is a substantive claim about every agent's values, and it is being adopted here precisely because it makes hard cases disappear. It is also unavailable to a good many people who care about such problems, since anyone who treats the welfare of arbitrarily many future people as additive and unaggregated cannot accept it. And he noted the direction of the concession: to save the theory, Marta has restricted the class of agents it applies to, which is just Yusuf's original claim that the theory is silent, or at best conditional, exactly where the interesting cases live.

Then tell me which of them is more right on the whole, and what both of them are missing — including whether there is a third position neither of them articulated, such as changing the aggregation rule rather than defending or restricting the expectation.
