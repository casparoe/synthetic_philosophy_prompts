I convene a final-year undergraduate (MPhil-exportable) paper in philosophy of mind, and I am setting the last of three essay questions on the final. The question I need concerns the causal exclusion argument and mental causation. It is worth 25 marks and students have 45 minutes for it. My problem is that every question I have drafted so far can be answered competently by someone who has merely memorised the architecture of Kim's argument — closure of the physical, the exclusion principle, the denial of systematic overdetermination, the epiphenomenalist conclusion — and my markers cannot tell those students apart from the ones who actually understand the dialectic. I want a question whose difficulty comes from a case, not from recall.

To that end, build the question around the following stipulated case, which I have constructed and which you should present to students in your own clean prose (label the stipulations S1–S4 so they can be referred to):

Ayesha presses a button. Let M be the type of mental event that is her deciding to press it, and let B be the button-press. Her decision-token on this occasion is realised by a neural event-token R1.

- **S1.** R1 is, in the circumstances, sufficient for B.
- **S2.** M is multiply realisable: R2 is a distinct neural event-type, disjoint from R1, which also suffices for B in these circumstances and also realises M. It is possible to intervene so as to substitute an R2-token for the R1-token while leaving M unchanged. Call this the swap intervention.
- **S3.** It is not possible to intervene so as to remove the R1-token while leaving M unchanged. Every intervention that switches off R1 in these circumstances is also an intervention that switches off M, and B fails to occur.
- **S4.** R1-tokens also occur, on other occasions, as part of an automatic motor sequence — Ayesha drumming along to a rhythm, say — in which R1 occurs, B occurs, and M does not.

The question must then require students to do three things: work out what an interventionist or difference-making account of causation says about each of the two interventions S2 and S3 make available, and hence whether R1 or M is the difference-maker for B; say whether that result defeats the exclusion principle or merely deprives it of an applicable test case, since S3 appears to rule out any intervention that could show R1 screening M off from B; and, finally, confront the fact that S4 cuts the other way, because M is not a difference-maker for B when R1 can produce B without it — so students must say whether the natural repair, restricting the model to cases where M is present, is a legitimate move about background conditions or a question-begging one about which variables belong in the causal model at all. A verdict is required; hedging between the two readings is not acceptable.

The question must be fully answerable from the case and the module's core material. Do not ask students to cite, name, date or quote anything, and do not include a bibliography or reading list anywhere in your response; at most the model answer may mention Kim and Woodward by name, and nothing else. Do not put any text in quotation marks. Write in British English.

Please structure your response in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **The examination question** (about 200 words). Student-facing prose, self-contained, presenting S1–S4 and the three required tasks, and ending with a single directive sentence.
2. **The model answer** (900–1,000 words). It must reach and defend a definite verdict, must explicitly dispose of both S3 and S4 rather than noting them, and must identify the one hinge on which the interventionist strategy either succeeds or begs the question.
3. **Marking scheme** (about 350 words). Break the 25 marks into named points. It must state explicitly that an accurate recitation of the exclusion argument's premises earns no more than 5 marks, that an answer which stops at recitation is capped at a lower second, and which specific moves a first-class answer makes that a strong 2:1 omits.
4. **Answers that impress but fail** (about 300 words). Four such answers, each with a diagnosis of the precise error and the highest mark it could realistically reach.
5. **Setting rationale** (about 150 words). For the examiners' meeting: what this question discriminates that a conventional exclusion-argument essay does not, together with a frank note on which of S1–S4 is the most contentious stipulation, whether a sharp student could legitimately challenge it rather than use it, and whether you regard that as a flaw or a feature of the question.
