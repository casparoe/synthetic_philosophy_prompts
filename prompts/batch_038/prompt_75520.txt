I am preparing a graduate seminar session on Nyāya epistemology, and before assigning the reading I want to pressure-test a diagnostic criterion I have been developing against the classical dispute over upamāna. I would like you to run that test for me, and to tell me honestly whether the criterion survives it.

The criterion, which for the moment I will call the distinctive-failure test, is this: a candidate means of knowledge earns a place on the list of pramāṇas only if there is some way in which it can go wrong that cannot be redescribed, without loss, as a way in which an already-recognized pramāṇa goes wrong. The motivating thought is that success may be shared — many different processes can issue in a true cognition — but only failure individuates. What marks off one kind of knowing from another, on this view, is the proprietary character of its malfunction.

Against that test I want two positions compared. The first is the Nyāya position that upamāna is a pramāṇa in its own right, alongside perception, inference, and testimony: roughly, the means by which one comes to know what a word denotes on the basis of its similarity to something already known, as in the stock illustration of the person told that the gavaya is like a cow who later meets such an animal in the forest and knows it to be the thing the term picks out. The second is the reductionist family of positions that denies this and absorbs upamāna into some other pramāṇa — into perception together with memory, into inference, into testimony, or into recognition. Present the two so that they genuinely contradict each other rather than merely differing in emphasis, and then apply the test.

Three specific things I need from you. First, take at least two of the reductive strategies and determine, for each, whether the errors it inherits are truly redescribable in the resources of the pramāṇa it reduces upamāna to, or whether the redescription quietly imports something the test was supposed to be neutral about — a notion of content, of memory, or of the speaker's reliability that is doing unacknowledged work. Second, judge whether the test is fair to Nyāya or is structurally stacked in favor of reduction; if it is stacked, say whether that is a defect in the test or a discovery about what Nyāya is actually doing when it multiplies pramāṇas. Third, report whether any candidate in the wider classical lists — including the postulation and non-apprehension admitted by schools that Nyāya thinks overreach — passes the test, and if none does, state plainly what remains of the pramāṇa framework once the test is applied consistently, rather than trying to rescue it.

Please do not write me a survey of the schools or a map of the doxography; I want to know whether the criterion earns its keep, and I would rather you argue it into the ground than preserve it because it is elegant. No bibliography and no verbatim quotation of primary texts: paraphrase the arguments, and I will supply the textual apparatus myself.
