I am working through a recent trade-press chapter on group minds and corporate responsibility — the sort of book that wants to explain to a general reader why a company or a committee can be an agent at all. Its central exhibit is the doctrinal paradox, and the passage I want audited reads as follows:

> Here is the trouble with group belief. Suppose a three-judge panel must decide a contract case in which liability follows if and only if two things hold: that the defendant performed a certain act, and that the contract forbade it. All three judges accept that equivalence; they disagree about the facts. Judge 1 accepts both premises, Judge 2 accepts only the first, Judge 3 only the second. Put the questions one at a time. On each premise a majority says yes, so the group ought by majority rule to say that both premises hold and hence that the defendant is liable. Put instead the single question, is the defendant liable, and a majority — the same two judges who divided their support between the premises — says no. Nothing has changed except the order in which the questions were asked. This is the doctrinal paradox, or discursive dilemma, and it is the belief-shaped cousin of Condorcet's cycling majorities: majority voting over logically connected propositions can hand a group a set of judgments that no member of that group holds, and that is not even internally consistent.
>
> Two general lessons follow. First, the remedy is obvious once stated: vote on the reasons rather than on the conclusion. Aggregating premise by premise always produces a consistent collective set, because the premises are logically independent of one another and the conclusion merely packages them; it is the conclusion-based vote that generates the contradiction, and a court that wants to be rational should therefore always decide element by element. Second, this is no quirk of small panels or of the legal setting. List and Pettit proved a general impossibility: no aggregation rule can at once admit every possible combination of individual judgments as input, treat all voters alike, treat all propositions alike, and always deliver a complete and consistent collective judgment set. Since each of those conditions is one any democrat should want, the only way out is to designate a dictator — to let one member's judgments stand for the group's. So the phrase "the committee believes" turns out to be a figure of speech. Groups can be held responsible and they can be dangerous, but they cannot be believers: wherever a group appears to have a mind, either one person is doing the thinking or the thinking is incoherent.
