I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy. For the past two years I've been building agent-based models of organizations and markets, and I've been asked to contribute a one-page position statement to an interdisciplinary workshop on computational social science, laying out the methodological assumptions my modeling work relies on. The statement will be circulated in advance and I'll be expected to defend it. Before I write it up, I'd like an audit of whether the assumptions hang together. Please treat my wording as approximate rather than technical, and tell me if I've mangled a distinction I'm trying to draw.

Here is the package:

1. Only individual people have beliefs, intentions, and make choices. When we say the department decided to freeze hiring, or that the market punished the delay, I read that as shorthand: there is no further entity over and above the people doing the deciding. Someone told me this position is called methodological individualism; I don't know whether my version is a standard one, so part of what I want is a check on whether the label even applies to me.

2. At the same time, I think the best explanations of the phenomena I model are pitched at a higher level, and that they can't be rewritten as statements about individuals without loss. When a traffic jam forms and dissolves with no accident and no individual driving badly, "the jam" is doing explanatory work that no list of driver decisions captures; when a mild individual preference for similar neighbors produces near-total segregation in a Schelling-style checkerboard simulation, the segregation is the thing to be explained. I would not accept a translation into individual-level sentences as a substitute for these explanations.

3. I hold a deliberately deflationary view of emergence: a macro pattern is emergent just in case it is reproducible from simple individual-level rules but not feasibly predictable by any bounded reasoner working from those rules. No downward causation, nothing over and above. I assume this is fully compatible with what I said in 1.

4. I think all the facts about a social system are fixed by the psychological states of the individual people in it — their beliefs, desires, and dispositions. Fix those and you fix everything social. But there's a case I'm confident about and don't want to give up: two organizations could have members with identical beliefs, desires, and dispositions and still differ in who has authority to commit money, purely because of a signed document in one and a permissions row in a database in the other. I think that's a genuine difference in the social facts, and I think my models track it.

5. On causation: if the micro-level facts fully account for the macro-level facts, then whatever causal work gets done is done at the micro level, and a macro description doesn't add any causation on top. So when I say the network structure caused the cascade, I mean it as a compact description of many individual interactions, not as a claim about an additional cause. Note though that one of the headline findings I report to stakeholders is that in my simulations the cascade is produced by the topology rather than by any agent's dispositions: hold the individual rules fixed, change the wiring, and the pattern vanishes. I present that as a substantive claim about what produced the outcome, and I defend it against the objection that it's just a redescription.

6. In incident reviews I insist that we name the individual decisions and the people who made them, and I push back hard on formulations like the team's culture caused the outage. I think holding a group accountable is confused, and I think this follows from 1.

What I want from you:

- Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I expect some of my claims to be flagged and I expect at least one of those flags to be wrong, so please discriminate rather than list everything as equally problematic.
- Order the genuine tensions by severity. I care about which one would sink the position statement if a specialist pressed on it, not about getting a complete inventory.
- For each genuine tension, say what has to give and what the cheapest revision is — and then say what that revision costs me. In particular I want to know whether the fix would force me to weaken claims in my results section, or only claims in my framing.
- If there is a single revision that would clean up most of the package at once, tell me what it is and what I'd be conceding by making it.

I don't have a prior about which item is the weak one; I've argued for all six at various points, in writing, to colleagues.
