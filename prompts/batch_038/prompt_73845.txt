**§1. The shape of argument I want to embarrass.** When two agents hold different and partially incompatible value systems but must decide jointly, one common way of justifying compromise runs through gains from trade. Because each party cares more about some of the matters under joint decision than the other does, an arrangement that lets each prevail where their concern is greater and yield where it is lesser can leave both better off on their own terms than either would be absent agreement. Compromise is then presented not as a surrender of principle but as the realization of value that neither party could realize alone; and its terms are evaluated by asking how the resulting surplus is divided. I want to argue that this style of argument is incomplete in a specific and consequential way.

**§2. Thesis.** The surplus is not a quantity generated by the meeting of two value systems. It is a function of the non-agreement baseline, and the baseline is not given: it is produced by institutional rules and by the parties' own conduct, both of which are proper objects of moral assessment. Any argument that derives the permissibility, the attractiveness, or the fairness of a compromise from the size or the division of the gains from trade therefore takes as a fixed parameter something that is itself up for moral decision, and it conceals this by describing the parameter as a circumstance rather than as a choice.

**§3. Illustration.** A legacy of £12m must be disposed of by two co-trustees, Ara and Bram, whose joint signature the deed requires. Two programmes are available. Clinics yield health benefits at a rate of 10 units per £1m; a river-valley restoration with community stewardship yields land-and-community benefits at 5 units per £1m. Ara's value system registers only the first currency, Bram's only the second. Writing x for the millions spent on clinics, the feasible frontier is u_A = 10x and u_B = 5(12 − x), with endpoints (120, 0) and (0, 60).

If the trustees fail to agree, the deed's default clause sends the money to a court-appointed receiver, who spends two-thirds on clinics and one-third on the restoration. The receivership process consumes a quarter of the estate in costs, so the receiver disposes of £9m: £6m to clinics and £3m to the river. The baseline is therefore (60, 15), a point strictly inside the frontier.

Three familiar division rules give three answers. Equal division of the surplus in utility terms gives (70, 25): each party gains 10 over the baseline. The Kalai–Smorodinsky solution, which replaces Nash's independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives axiom with a monotonicity requirement and selects the frontier point equalizing each party's gain as a proportion of the distance from the baseline to that party's best feasible outcome, gives (72, 24): gains of 12 and 9. The Nash solution, maximizing the product of gains over the baseline, gives (75, 22.5): gains of 15 and 7.5.

Now two variations. First, suppose the receivership were costless. The baseline would be (80, 20), a point on the frontier. There would then be no mutual gains available at all: any agreement Ara prefers to the baseline Bram must prefer less, and conversely. The same two value systems, in the same institution, would generate either a surplus of 22.5 units or no surplus whatsoever, depending only on whether the breakdown process wastes value.

Second, suppose Bram applies for an injunction delaying the clinic contracts. The delay destroys a fifth of the health benefit the receiver's spending would have produced and costs Bram nothing he cares about. Ara's baseline falls to 48; Bram's remains 15. Recomputing the Nash solution on the new baseline gives (69, 25.5): gains of 21 and 10.5. Bram is 3 units better off than under the original baseline, Ara is 6 units worse off, and the measured surplus has risen from 22.5 to 31.5.

One technical observation I will rely on: because the frontier is linear, the ratio of the parties' gains under the Nash solution is fixed by the frontier's slope alone — here 2:1 — and is independent of the baseline. All of the effect of Bram's manoeuvre therefore appears in the levels, not in the proportion.

**§4. What the illustration is meant to show.** Three things.

(1) *Whether there are gains from trade at all* — that is, whether the circumstances of compromise obtain — is a property of the efficiency of the breakdown process, not of the parties' values. The first variation changes nothing about Ara and Bram and everything about whether they face a cooperative problem.

(2) *The measured surplus can be enlarged by conduct that creates nothing.* Bram's injunction produces no additional health benefit and no additional land benefit; it destroys twelve units of the former. Yet on the standard accounting it increases the gains from trade by nine units and Bram's own share by three. If the surplus is supposed to measure what cooperation makes available that non-cooperation does not, it fails at that task: it measures what cooperation makes available plus what one party has taken from the other's fallback.

(3) *The division of the surplus is driven by facts with no moral standing.* The 2:1 gain ratio under the Nash solution is a consequence of clinics producing twice as much value per pound as the restoration. Nothing about Ara's or Bram's moral position, or about the seriousness of what each stands to lose, explains it.

I should forestall one reply. Bargaining theory already recognizes that baselines are produced by conduct: where threats are treated as actions rather than data, one can define a prior game in which each party selects a threat and receives the payoff of the bargaining solution computed from the resulting baseline. My claim is that this relocates the problem rather than answering it, since that game must be given a set of available threats, and specifying which threats are available is exactly the normative question the gains-from-trade argument was supposed to bypass.

**§5. A criterion, and two problems with it.** My proposed repair is a constraint on admissible baselines. Call it the Own-Terms Constraint: a baseline is admissible for computing gains from trade only if each party's contribution to bringing it about is permissible by that party's own value system. Bram's injunction is admissible only if Bram's own values permit destroying health benefit for bargaining position; Ara's counters are admissible only if her own values permit them.

Two problems. (a) *Collapse.* If neither party may contribute to a fallback that their own values forbid, then where the parties' values are not themselves wasteful, the only admissible baseline will already lie on the frontier. There will be no surplus to divide, and the framework of gains from trade loses its subject matter. We are returned to first-order disagreement about what the right joint action is — which is where the argument began.

(b) *The scrupulousness penalty.* The constraint is indexed to each party's own values. Ara, who holds that allowing preventable deaths is impermissible even as a bargaining posture, is barred from threats that Bram, who holds that no one is entitled to be rescued from a baseline he did not create, may freely make. The constraint thus converts Ara's scrupulousness into a worse baseline for Ara and hands the surplus to Bram. A criterion introduced to stop predation on the baseline ends up subsidizing the less scrupulous party.

**§6. My partial reply, and where I am stuck.** I want to add a reciprocity clause: a threat is admissible for a party only if that party would accept its admissibility were they to hold the values of the party against whom it is made and to occupy that party's position. In the illustration this yields the intuitively right result — Bram, asked whether he would accept the injunction if he held Ara's values and stood where Ara stands, must answer no — while leaving Ara free to make threats Bram's own values would license. But the clause has an unattractive consequence: the set of threats admissible for one party is fixed by the other party's values, so a party holding sufficiently demanding values can unilaterally restrict the other's options, and a party holding sufficiently permissive values can liberalize them, in neither case for reasons bearing on the legitimacy of the threats themselves. My suspicion, which I cannot yet resolve, is that whenever one value system is uniformly more demanding than the other — the normal case in the examples I can construct — the reciprocity clause is extensionally equivalent to the much simpler rule admitting only threats permissible on both value systems. If so, the clause is decorative.

**§7. Conclusion.** Gains from trade can justify a compromise, and can help determine its terms, only relative to an account of which baselines are admissible. Any such account will itself be a substantive moral theory. The bargaining-theoretic framing therefore does not perform the work asked of it; it changes the subject, from the fair division of a surplus to the ethics of what parties may permissibly do to one another's fallbacks.
