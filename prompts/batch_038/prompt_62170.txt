ok so — I'm TAing a third-year epistemology seminar whose whole back half is on testimony, and the prof has asked me to set one of the questions for the two-hour final. I'd like you to help me design it. For this, act as a professional epistemologist who sets and marks a lot of exam papers, i.e. someone who knows how to write a question that can't be answered by reciting the standard story about Hume and Reid and then picking a side.

The topic is reductionism vs anti-reductionism about testimonial justification. My problem is that every version I draft turns into "explain the two views and say which you find more plausible," and about half the cohort will do exactly that from memory, write it up fluently, and coast to a B. I want the difficulty to come from a crux, not from breadth.

Here's the seed I want you to build the question around. Sharpen the wording of the cases if you like, but keep the structure and keep the confound in place — the confound is the point.

Case A. Nadia is a hospital pharmacist. Tomas has worked as her assistant for eight months and has repeatedly checked her calculations against lab results that he can see for himself; she has been right essentially every time. One evening he passes the staff room and hears her, alone, dictating into a phone app that the dose in a batch destined for ward six is wrong, together with a figure. She isn't addressing him or anyone else, doesn't know he's there, and would have no idea he'd acted on it. He forms the belief on the basis of what he overheard. It's correct.

Case B. Priya reads statements aloud at government briefings. She means what she reads, would be mortified to have said something false, and would correct the record if she found out. Dev, a journalist, has attended dozens of her briefings. He was able to independently verify three of her claims and all three held up; the rest he has no way of checking at all. His reason for thinking the office she reads for has a rigorous checking routine is entirely that people who work in the office say it does. He forms a belief on the basis of what she says at today's briefing.

The question should force students to (i) work out what each view predicts about each case, (ii) notice that the two cases differ along two dimensions at once — whether there's an addressee who could be held to account, and how much independent reliability evidence the hearer has — so a naive side-by-side comparison doesn't isolate anything, (iii) redo the comparison with one dimension held fixed, and (iv) reach a verdict on whether a genuinely diagnostic case between the two views is available at all, or whether the debate is at least partly verbal because the two sides individuate the belief-forming process differently.

Deliverables, in this order:

1. About ten lines of brainstorming before you write anything. Think carefully here about whether these two views actually make different predictions in these cases or whether they mostly talk past each other. If you conclude they don't really differ, don't hide that — build the question so that recognising it is what the top marks reward.
2. The exam question: 250–320 words, self-contained, cases stated compactly, task broken into lettered parts, and an explicit note that this is one of three compulsory questions so students should spend roughly 40 minutes and write about 700 words.
3. A model answer of about 700 words.
4. A marking scheme out of 25 built around five must-hit moves, with the marks for each, plus a one-line note per move on how students typically botch it.
5. A short paragraph on what seperates an A from a B answer here, in terms of specific moves a candidate does or doesn't make, not vibes.

Constraints: no bibliography, no reading list, no quotations, and no name-dropping in either the question or the model answer — nobody has sources in the room, and I want this answerable from understanding rather than recall. "Reductionism" and "anti-reductionism" are fine since we've defined them in class, but don't award marks for attaching the right label to the right contemporary author. Also, please don't let the model answer fizzle out into "both views have something to offer" — it needs to commit on point (iv), even if the commitment is that no diagnostic case exists.
