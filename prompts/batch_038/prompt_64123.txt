You are an expert in the philosophy of pain and in the neuroscience of nociception; please think the case through carefully before answering, working out the considerations internally rather than on the page.

Three years ago a left insular stroke left me with pain asymbolia. I still detect and localise noxious stimulation accurately — I will tell you that the kettle handle is hot, and I can rate the intensity — but nothing about it bothers me. I have to reason my way to the conclusion that I should pull my hand away, and if I don't, I simply let it stay there. This has made a long-held philosophical commitment personally urgent for me, and I want to know how much damage it can take.

The commitment: the unpleasantness of a pain is an intrinsic phenomenal property of the experience itself. Whenever someone genuinely feels a pain, they feel something bad, and its badness is a matter of how the experience is, not of what it disposes them to do — not a command, not an evaluation they endorse, not a desire to make it stop. On this view, my reports are misleading me: if I am really feeling these pains, they must be felt as bad, and the indifference must be a failure somewhere downstream in motivation, attention, or self-ascription rather than a counterexample to the account.

Tell me what would have to be true for this view to be false. I want the defeaters, not a survey: which findings from asymbolia and related lesion syndromes, which arguments from the rival accounts of what makes pains unpleasant, which phenomenological claims about my own introspective access, and which dissociations produced pharmacologically or surgically would each count against it, and how much each would count. Say what my view predicts that its rivals do not, and whether those predictions could in principle be tested rather than merely asserted. Identify where the view has been made safe by qualification — in particular whether the move of insisting that I am not really feeling the pains is available to me or is an ad hoc retreat — and say plainly whether my own first-person testimony should carry weight in adjudicating between these accounts at all. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each one say whether a defender of the intrinsic-unpleasantness view would themselves concede it as counting against them or dismiss it.

Finally, keep the settled separate from the contested: mark clearly which parts of this picture are agreed by people who work on it and which are live disputes, and do not present any contested position as though it were the consensus. Begin your answer with your bottom line in the first sentence — your verdict on how exposed the view is and which single consideration is most dangerous to it — and only then give the reasoning. No preamble, no restatement of the question.
