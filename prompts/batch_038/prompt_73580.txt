I never went to college and have no background in philosophy or economics beyond what I have read on my own, so please excuse any clumsiness in how I frame this. For about eleven years I have worked in a county permitting office, which means I spend my days reading municipal compliance handbooks, fee schedules, and public-notice ordinances, and I have developed a genuine affection for that genre of prose. Separately, over the past two years a friend lent me a game theory textbook and I have become somewhat preoccupied with the literature on common knowledge: Aumann's result that two rational people with a common prior cannot agree to disagree, the assumption of common knowledge of rationality that epistemic game theory leans on throughout, and Rubinstein's electronic mail game, which I found quietly disturbing, since it seems to show that a confirmation chain interrupted with even a vanishingly small probability behaves nothing like an uninterrupted one. What struck me is that all of these results rest on assumptions no real institution could ever verify, and that the natural bureaucratic response to an unverifiable requirement is to invent paperwork that pretends to satisfy it. That is the joke I want to make, and I would like your help making it properly.

Please write a satirical compliance FAQ of about 750 words in American English, issued by an invented municipal agency — the Office of Doxastic Concord, or something in that register, and feel free to assign it an ordinance number and form numbers — in a city where the agreement theorem has been enacted into law. The premise is that disagreement is now a code violation, and the document explains how a resident stays compliant. I want the standard apparatus of the genre: eight to ten numbered questions and answers in the flat, patient, faintly menacing voice of a real permit desk; one boxed sidebar; and one short table of fees or penalties. The questions should cover, at minimum: what a resident must post and where; the point that holding a belief privately is insufficient, since the ordinance requires the posteriors themselves to be commonly known, which the office administers through an "attestation ladder" of legally fixed finite depth — please have the office explain, with total straight-faced confidence, exactly why that depth was chosen; what to do if you and your neighbor still disagree once the ladder is complete; how to apply for a prior exemption, which should make clear that the exemption is available to anyone who can demonstrate that they never shared the common prior in the first place, so that deliberate childhood indoctrination, converting late in life, and refusing to learn the local language are all recognized routes to legal immunity; and the office's discounted Grade B notice tier, in which public announcements are carried by an unreliable courier that occasionally loses a message, and where — as the FAQ should state without the slightest unease — disagreement is entirely lawful and may be of any magnitude whatsoever.

Two things matter most to me. First, the humor has to come from getting the content right rather than from generic bureaucratic absurdity: every loophole, form, and exemption should correspond precisely to one of the assumptions the theorem needs — the common prior, common knowledge of rationality, and common knowledge of the posteriors — so that a reader who knows the literature can trace the mapping. The piece should never explain the theorem, never quote a real text, never name a real economist or cite a real paper, and never step outside the office's voice to wink at the reader. Please also build in one moment where the machinery fails on its own terms: I had in mind the office conceding that it cannot actually establish that anyone is rational and therefore substituting common knowledge of presumed rationality as a matter of administrative convenience, together with some consequence of the fact that the office must attest to its own attestations. Second, it still has to work for my co-editor, a retired middle school math teacher who has never heard of any of this: read purely as a document, it should be funny as a portrait of an agency drowning in self-referential paperwork. The target of the satire is the idealization itself, and the way formal discourse treats an assumption nobody could ever satisfy as a harmless convenience — not any actual person.
