ok so I spent maybe three hours in a pub last night with my friend Ravi arguing about finks and masks and I think he talked me into eliminativism about dispositions, and I dont want to be. He laid out this argument and I couldnt break it at the time, but on the walk home it started to feel like it proves way more than he thinks, and I can't tell whether that means it's a bad argument or just that I'm squeamish.

His argument, in the order he gave it:

1. Calling something fragile just is claiming a conditional: if it were struck sharply, it would break. Same for soluble, elastic, irritable, conductive — dispositional talk is conditional talk.
2. For any such conditional you can cook up a case where it comes out false even though the thing is exactly as fragile as it was a second ago. Two recipes. Fink: build the interferer into the thing itself, so the instant it's struck something inside it removes the basis of the disposition (Ravi's favourite was a circuit breaker: this server is disposed to fall over under load, but a watchdog daemon kills the offending worker the microsecond load crosses the threshold, so it never falls over — and the daemon isn't some separate object bolted on next to it, it's part of the server, part of its configuration). Mask: leave the thing alone but block the manifestation from outside — foam-wrap the glass, or schedule the server onto bigger hardware before the load ever lands.
3. So fragility is never equivalent to any conditional about what the thing would do.
4. And the standard patch — "would break, in normal conditions / absent interferers" — is either vacuous, because "absent interferers" just means "in conditions where fragile things break", or it names a further property of the thing, which is itself dispositional and needs the same patch applied to it.
5. A property that can't be cashed out in conditionals does no explanatory work. When the glass does break, what explains the breaking is the microstructure plus the strike; when it doesn't break, the conditional was false, so fragility explained nothing. Either way fragility never gets into the story.
6. Properties that never get into the story are just labels for how things happen to go.
7. So there are no dispositions. "Fragile" is shorthand for "this thing, with this microstructure, breaks when struck like this", and every disposition word reduces the same way: categorical base plus actual behaviour.

Ravi is extremely pleased with himself. He says 4 is the load-bearing premise and that anyone who wants to keep dispositions owes an answer to it.

Here's why I'm uneasy. Run 5 and 6 on something he'd happily keep. "This drug blocks the receptor" — the blocking can be masked (the patient is also on something that changes blood flow) or finked (a liver enzyme that eats the drug), and by 5 the drug's being a blocker is never in the explanation, only the actual molecular events are. Run it on laws of nature: any law's instances can be interfered with, so by 5 laws do no explanatory work either. Then run it on the microstructure, which is the only thing 7 leaves standing: what makes the microstructure explanatory is precisely what it would do if you perturbed it, and that's conditionals again, so 5 strips the base of the exact credit 7 handed it. If the argument eats all of that I think its too strong, but I can't say which line to pull.

So: where does it go wrong? Tell me the single step you'd reject and the reading of it that made it look innocent, then price the alternatives — if I keep 2 and 4 and give up something else, what do I lose? Is there any version of the "absent interferers" patch that's neither vacuous nor circular, or should I just accept the circularity and eat the regress? And how much of this turns on masks being a different kind of problem from finks, since the fink messes with the basis and the mask leaves it intact — is that difference doing real work here or is it a distraction?

I'd rather keep 2 than 7: I want finks and masks to be real phenomena, I just don't see why they're corrosive.
