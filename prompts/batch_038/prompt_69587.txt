I am a novelist, and my fourth book goes to its publisher in the spring. I have reached the stage in revision where I can no longer tell whether the structural conceit at the center of it is load-bearing or merely ornamental, and I have come to the conclusion that no amount of further reading of my own manuscript will settle the question. What I want is a philosopher's reading rather than a reader's report.

The novel is called *Low Water*. It is set in a fictional river town on the Ganga in northern Bihar over the course of a single monsoon, and it concerns the death of a sand-mining contractor, found beneath the ferry ghat as the water goes down. It is told in four parts, each named for one of the four pramāṇas of the Nyāya school: perception, inference, comparison, and testimony. Each part is a complete and internally coherent account of the same night, delivered by a different consciousness, and — this is the discipline I imposed on myself — no part contradicts any other at the level of observed fact. They differ only in what they permit a reader to know.

The first part, Perception, is narrated by a nearsighted girl who was on the steps at dusk and saw the thing happen, or saw a thing happen. I have tried to write the gap between what struck her eyes and what she has since been persuaded she saw: she cannot now say whether the second figure wore the blue shirt, and I want the reader to feel, in the act of her remembering, the precise moment at which she began to see a man rather than a shape. My intention was to write her error as a real relation between two real things — the rope that is mistaken for the snake, where both terms have their being — and not as a projection out of nothing. I understand the Naiyāyikas hold this against both the Buddhist and the Vedāntin accounts of error, under the name *anyathākhyāti*. If I have that wrong, tell me plainly, because the whole of the first part rests on it, and tell me too whether the prose as I have described it actually sustains that view of error or keeps sliding into the suggestion that she invented the man.

The second part, Inference, is narrated by a retired schoolmaster who for forty years drilled his pupils in the five-step argument — proposition, reason, example, application, conclusion — and who now, as the town's self-appointed inquisitor, builds his case against the contractor's business partner entirely out of marks: wet footprints on the wrong side of the boat, a brass lamp missing from the shrine, a dog that did not bark. I want this section to be a triumph of form and a failure of content, and specifically to be that: he has all the members of the argument and none of the pervasion, and the reader should be able to see, without being told, that he never establishes that his mark occurs only where the thing to be proved occurs. Say whether a novel can make a reader feel the absence of *vyāpti* without ever naming it, or whether that is asking narrative to do something only exposition can do.

The third part, Comparison, is the one that worries me most. It is narrated by a young man who has never met the contractor's brother and knows him only from his mother's description of him as tall, stooped, and moving like a heron, and who recognizes him in a market crowd by that likeness alone. My understanding is that schools admitting fewer pramāṇas have always objected that *upamāna* is not independent at all — that what my young man has is testimony plus perception, or a suppressed inference, and that the fourth category survives partly out of loyalty to the list. If that objection is sound, my third part is decoration and I should either cut it or write it as decoration knowingly. If it is unsound — if the likeness does something that neither the description nor the perception can do on its own — I need to know what that something is, because then at last I know what the section is for. This is the single question I most want answered.

The fourth part, Testimony, is the statement of a man the whole town knows to be a petty informer and a liar, whose account is nonetheless the only one that fits everything the other three have already conceded. The section turns on what the school requires of a speaker: that he know what he says and wish to say it as he knows it. My difficulty is that the reader can establish this only by weighing his statement against the other three, and the other three are precisely what his statement is supposed to adjudicate. I take it that Nyāya's critics treated testimony as a species of inference from a speaker's past reliability; my informer is the test case, since there is no past reliability to infer from. Is that circle a flaw in my construction or a genuine pressure point in the doctrine? And does the tradition have resources — in its account of doubt, or of defect in the cause of a cognition — for describing the reader's position at the end of the book, when all four accounts are standing and none has been dislodged? Is that state *saṃśaya* in the technical sense, or is it something the taxonomy has no name for?

The book ends without saying who killed the contractor, and without quite confirming that he was killed at all. Reviewers of my previous novel reached for *Rashomon*, and I am trying to write the opposite of what that word implies. I do not believe there are only perspectives, and neither, as I read them, do the Naiyāyikas, who are realists and think a cognition either conforms to its object or does not. What I want from you above all is a verdict on whether the four-part form can deliver that: whether a novel organized around four ways of knowing can also insist that there is a determinate fact of the matter which all four are, in different ways, failing to reach — or whether the form collapses inevitably into the relativism I am trying to refuse.

Two conditions. First, address the book I have described, not the history of Nyāya epistemology in general, and not my prose style; where a turn in your argument depends on a technical distinction I have blurred, name the distinction rather than assuming I have it. I read widely in translation while drafting, but I am not a philosopher and I have no Sanskrit. Second, please send me no references, no bibliography, and no reading list. I cannot chase them, and I would much rather have your own account of the doctrine, with an explicit flag wherever you are unsure of a point, than a set of places to look it up. Say too where the novel resists the framework you bring to it: a reading that treats my four parts as illustrations of four doctrines will be of no use to me at all. Two thousand words would be ample.
