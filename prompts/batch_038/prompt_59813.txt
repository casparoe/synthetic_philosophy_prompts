Each spring I teach an advanced undergraduate seminar in political philosophy whose middle weeks are devoted to responsibility-sensitive egalitarianism, and each year the same pedagogical difficulty recurs: my students can recite the labels but cannot tell you which position they themselves occupy. They announce that they are luck egalitarians and then go on to describe a view on which responsibility-sensitivity is only one consideration among several, or they invoke the expensive-tastes objection without noticing that their own reaction to the case commits them to something far more restrictive. I would like a handout, to be distributed before the discussion section and worked through individually in about twenty minutes, that takes the form of a decision tree: it begins from a single starting commitment that nearly everyone in the room shares — that it is unfair, and therefore a matter for collective correction, when some people end up worse off than others through no fault or choice of their own — and leads, by way of successive branch nodes, to a named position in the logical space.

Two design constraints govern the nodes. First, every node must be phrased as a concrete case or as a claim a student can accept or reject on its face, without already knowing which region of the space their answer delivers them into; no node may mention a theorist, a school, or a label, and no node may turn on a term of art that the handout has not defined in a subordinate clause. Second, the tree must be built from the space of commitments rather than from a roster of views: the branch questions and the answer combinations they permit come first, and the positions are then located at the leaves. Please draw your branch questions from at least the following fault lines, which are the ones on which my students reliably conflate distinct views: whether the unfairness in question attaches to a state of affairs as such or to the conduct of particular agents; what sort of thing makes a disadvantage "chosen" — an explicit decision, an unforced omission, a risk knowingly run, or merely a configuration of the agent's own values and tastes; whether any choice can be credited to the agent at all if the circumstances shaping that choice were themselves unchosen; what the currency is in which equality or its absence is measured; whether the claims of the imprudent lapse entirely or merely weaken relative to competing claims; and whether responsibility-sensitivity is required by fairness itself or is instead a concession to incentive, autonomy, and the refusal to police how people live.

For cases, I would like you to use, and to place deliberately within the tree rather than merely append: an inexperienced mountaineer who ignored posted warnings and whose rescue will be extremely costly; a resident of an expensive coastal city who declined better-paid work elsewhere because he values the surf and the light, and who now cannot cover his rent; two drivers who were equally inattentive at the same intersection on the same afternoon, only one of whom killed a child who happened to dart into the road; a violinist whose cultivated taste for a particular rare instrument makes her far more expensive to satisfy than her colleagues at equal talent and effort; a surgeon with marketable talents who, on the terms of the initial agreement, keeps more than her less talented neighbour; and a firefighter who chose a dangerous occupation, was injured in it, and also grew up in circumstances that made the choice itself a poor one. You may add or prune cases, but not so as to leave any of these six fault lines undiagnosed.

The handout should terminate in at least nine leaves, each carrying a name where the position genuinely has one in the literature and a one-sentence statement of its most awkward commitment. At least two of the leaves must be unoccupied regions — combinations the questions permit but that nobody, so far as I can tell, defends — and for each you should say which of three diagnoses applies: that the combination is incoherent, that it is coherent but unattractive, or that it is merely unexplored. Finally, be explicit about the limits of your own instrument: identify any pair of leaves the tree cannot in fact separate, and say what a student who ends up at that fork should do next.

I am not looking for a menu of alternatives or for the tree to remain agnostic about its own architecture. Commit to one ordering of the branch nodes, defend that ordering in a short paragraph as the most diagnostic sequence available, and tell me which of the cases is doing the greatest amount of separating work and which you judge the least load-bearing and why. Where you take a position on an unoccupied region, take a definite one: I would rather be given a reasoned claim that a region is unexplored than a shrug registering that the literature happens to be silent.
