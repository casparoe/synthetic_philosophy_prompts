So — second year in a legal theory program here, and my "dissertation-adjacent side project" is a Nomic group I run with a handful of people from the department. We play a heavily modified version of Hofstadter's rules game on Thursdays and one guy maintains an actual versioned git repo of the ruleset, which is more effort then the game deserves but which turned out to matter, because last month we got into a mess that I now think is the cleanest possible instance of the conceptual problem I want to write up for a graduate workshop. Our ruleset contained, among other things: Clause A, which says that Clause A may not be amended or repealed; and Clause B, which says that any clause other than Clause A may be amended or repealed by a two-thirds vote of the players. Everyone wanted A gone. What we did was amend B first (which B permits, since B is not A) so that the new B reads "any clause may be amended or repealed by a two-thirds vote," and then, under the new B, amend A out of existence. Half the group thought this was obviously legitimate — we never touched A, we only changed what B said about A. The other half thought it was a cheap trick that violated A in substance while respecting it in form, and one person, a logic student, said it was literally incoherent and that our repo now contained a ruleset that couldn't exist.

The framing I'm stuck on is this. One way to look at it — call it the inference model — treats a rule change as a kind of deduction: the new rule is derived from the standing amendment clause plus the facts of the vote. On that picture the maneuver is a disaster, because amended-A is simultaneously authorized by and inconsistent with old-A, and if a clause can't be turned on itself you get a regress: you need a higher clause to amend A, another to amend that, and so on, which is where Ross's dilemma about self-reference in constitutional law lives. The other way to look at it — the acceptance model — says validity was never a matter of derivation at all; a change is valid if the right people treat it as valid, acceptance can be retroactive, and the whole puzzle is an artifact of importing a proof-theoretic picture of legal change into a social practice. I can state both of these but I can't say what the disagreement between them actually amounts to. Is it substantive — are these two pictures making different claims about the same world, such that one of them could simply be false — or is it a choice of vocabulary for describing behavior both sides already agree on? And where does the temporal point cut in: someone at the workshop is going to say that the contradiction requires old-A and new-A to be valid at the same time, and since A stops existing the instant A-prime takes effect there's no contradiction, just succession. What's the best reply to that, and does it work against the two-step maneuver specifically or only against direct self-amendment?

Second thing, related: after that we voted unanimously, using the full amending power, to repeal the clause granting amending power plus everything else — Nomic suicide, no rules left. I'd like you to explain why that case is different in kind from the two-step rather than just a bigger version of it: once it goes through, is there still a system, and does the answer change depending on which of the two models above you adopt? Also please say whether any of this is really just the omnipotence paradox or the liar wearing a wig, or whether the legal/game setting adds something the purely logical versions are missing — my instinct is that it does but I can't articulate what. Don't give me a bibliography or a reading list, I've got those covered; I want the argument itself laid out. And be straight with me about the soft spots: tell me which of these questions you're most likely to get wrong, what consideration would move you from the acceptance model to the inference model (or the reverse), and where your reasoning here is most likely to break if someone pushes hard on it in the room.
