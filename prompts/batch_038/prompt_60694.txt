ok so I've been going in circles on what I actually mean when I say a physical state is "real" versus "just a description of what I know." Going case by case, here's where I land.

1. My probability distribution over the positions and velocities of the ~10^23 molecules in a box of gas: not real. That's my ignorance, full stop.
2. The temperature of that same box: real. Which is embarrassing, because temperature is also a summary of micro-details I don't know, and its definable out of the same probabilities in (1). So "it's an average over stuff underneath" can't be what makes something unreal.
3. A coin I haven't looked at yet: my 50/50 is not real.
4. Spin-up-along-z for a single electron: I lean real, though I'd struggle to say why.
5. But now take up-z and up-x for that electron. No measurement on a single copy can tell me which preparation I got. That makes me want to say the underlying physical situation must sometimes be compatible with both descriptions — overlapping states of knowledge about one common reality.
6. Then someone walked me through this and it wrecked (5). Prepare two electrons independently, each one either up-z or up-x, and do a joint measurement on the pair with four outcomes arranged so that each outcome is ruled out by quantum mechanics for at least one of the four possible preparation pairs. If up-z and up-x could share an underlying physical situation, then with nonzero probability both electrons land in a situation compatible with all four pairs at once, and one of those impossible outcomes should fire. It never does. So the overlap has to go — unless you're willing to deny that two independently performed preparations are genuinely independent, which sounds like a huge price.
7. The reduced state of one half of an entangled pair gets written as a 50/50 mixture that looks exactly like ignorance, but nothing is hidden from me there; the mixedness comes from the entanglement. Verdict: not epistemic. Except that if my lab partner measures her half and doesn't tell me the result, there suddenly IS something I don't know, and the very same 50/50 matrix now looks like plain ignorance. So my verdict seems to depend on the situation rather than the object, which I find unsatisfying.
8. The wavefunction of the whole universe: has to be real, because there's nobody outside it whose information it could be encoding.
9. Wigner's friend: she's in the lab with a definite result, I'm outside with a superposition, and I think we're both right and neither state describes the cat itself. That feels like assignment being relative to the assigner, i.e. epistemic. Which obviously sits badly with (8).
10. Interference. Amplitudes cancel each other out. Degrees of belief don't cancel. So whatever the wavefunction is, it isn't a belief.

My tentative rule is: a state assignment is epistemic just in case two people with different information could both be right to make it, and ontic when there's a single fact about which assignment is correct. But I think (7) breaks it, and maybe (2) does too.

So — is there a criterion that gets most or all of these? State it precisely, clause by clause, with the case that motivates each clause, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to give up. I'd rather drop one verdict than build a baroque machine, but say so honestly if the machine is what's required. Then apply it to two cases I haven't listed. First, Everett: the universal state evolves deterministically and is about as real as anything could be, yet I still assign probabilities to which outcome I'll find myself seeing — does one and the same state get to be ontic and also the source of genuine epistemic uncertainty, or does my criterion have to say one of those two things is a mistake? Second, if the criterion says the wavefunction is real, does it also say real *where*? A field in 3N-dimensional configuration space doesn't look like anything I'd normally call physical, and I don't want a criterion that makes reality hostage to how many dimensions I'm prepared to swallow.
