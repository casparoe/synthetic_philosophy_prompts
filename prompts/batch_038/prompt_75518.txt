So here's a thing that's been bugging me about how embryo research is regulated practically everywhere. There's a bright line: fourteen days after fertilization, no more culturing, no experiments, destroy it. Before that you can do essentially anything in the lab. But the same legal systems will let a fertility clinic keep frozen embryos on ice for twenty years without ever asking whether they're being harmed, and nobody treats the enormous number of embryos that just fail to implant on their own as a tragedy requiring intervention. So the line clearly isn't "the embryo is a patient, don't hurt it." It's tracking something else.

As I understand it, the usual rationale for fourteen days is that it's roughly when the primitive streak appears, and that's also about the point after which the embryo can no longer split into identical twins or fuse with another one. Which would mean the thing that matters isn't sentience (nowhere near it) or even being biologically alive — its being one individual. Becoming a someone rather than a mere clump that could still turn into two someones, or one someone welded out of two.

So: what metaphysics of organism and identity does that rule actually commit us to? Is "not yet a single individual" doing the real work, or is it a dressed-up version of "no nervous system yet"? Those two come apart — imagine an early embryo that for some reason can't twin but has no nerves at all, versus a later-stage thing you could still somehow split. Walk me through the hard cases too. Identical twins who were one entity until day ten or so — did one person become two, and does the pre-split period count as one life or two? Chimeras made from fused embryos? And what about the embryo-like structures people now assemble out of stem cells in a dish, which never involved fertilization but can organize themselves into something streak-like — should the fourteen-day rule even reach them, and does your answer show the rule is tracking a historical origin or a developmental capacity?

The other thing I can't shake is that the rule seems internally inconsistent. If being a single individual organism is what earns protection, why does the protection take the form of a ban on research rather than a ban on destruction? Why can you freeze it indefinitely, ship it across the country, or discard it with the couple's signature, but not study it? Reconstruct the theory of life the whole arrangement would need in order to be coherent, and then tell me plainly where it's underdetermined — where several completely different theories would license the exact same bright line, and where the practice is just frozen in place for reasons that have nothing to do with any theory at all.
