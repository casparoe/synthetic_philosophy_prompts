Write a poem of fifteen lines — three quatrains followed by a closing tercet — in which the form itself carries the asymmetry at the heart of David Benatar's case against procreation (*Better Never to Have Been*, Oxford University Press, 2006), rather than merely taking it as a subject.

The argument is built on four propositions, which you should treat as a grid and let the poem walk through in order: that the presence of pain is bad; that the presence of pleasure is good; that the absence of pain counts as a good even when there is nobody around to enjoy it; and that the absence of pleasure counts as a bad only where there is somebody whom it deprives. Give one proposition to each of the first three quatrains and the fourth to the closing tercet, and make the allocation legible — a reader who knows the debate should be able to say which stanza carries which claim, and why the last one is short.

Formal requirements:

- Iambic pentameter throughout, with the ordinary licence for substitutions. The quatrains rhyme ABAB, CDCD and EFEF in full rhymes rather than near-rhymes; the final stanza rhymes GHG, so that its second rhyme is left permanently unanswered.
- The missing fourth line must not be mourned. No ellipses, no suspending dashes, no blank space on the page, no apostrophe to the line that never arrived, no wry meta-poetic shrug. The tercet has to read as finished rather than amputated, because that is exactly what the fourth proposition asserts: an absent good is not a bad unless someone is deprived of it. A poem that flinches at its own shortfall has argued against itself.
- Do not name Benatar or his book, and do not use the debate's own vocabulary — no 'antinatalism', no 'the asymmetry', no talk of person-affecting reasons or non-identity. The propositions must be carried by image, syntax and stanza shape alone.

On tone and content: this should not read as a tract. The first stanza need not overstate its case, since the argument's force does not depend on life being intolerable — the harms it registers can be entirely ordinary, the daily abrasions, the illnesses, the bereavements that arrive on schedule. The second stanza should concede pleasure generously and without irony, for the position being rendered grants that a happy life is genuinely good while still refusing to call it better than never having begun. The third is where the strangeness lives: a credit entered to an account no one will ever draw on, a good that nobody is glad of, and the poem should let the reader feel how much weight that claim is being asked to bear. In the closing tercet the standard objection should surface as pressure rather than as argument — that the moment the speaker begins to love the child who was never conceived, there is somebody after all, and the absence may become a deprivation. Let that worry sit in the final line without being resolved.

The speaker is someone who has made the decision and is explaining it to no one in particular, which is part of the difficulty: an address with no addressee keeps very nearly becoming a person. To a reader who has never encountered the argument at all, the result should still work as a good poem about choosing not to have a child. Give it a title.
