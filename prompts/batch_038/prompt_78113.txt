I teach an undergraduate seminar in political philosophy: 24 students, and a single 75-minute session to devote to electoral systems, of which I want roughly 50 minutes of activity and 25 of debrief. The room has movable chairs, one whiteboard, and a printing budget of about 60 sheets. There is no money for props and no computer the students can use. I am the only facilitator. Please design the activity in full.

I do not want the usual demonstration, in which one set of ballots is counted under two rules and the class observes that one rule converts vote shares into seat shares more faithfully than the other. I can assert that from the front of the room and they will accept it without ever feeling it. What I want them to run into is that a large part of what an electoral system does happens before any ballot is counted, and that changing the system changes not only how many parties survive but who is competing with whom.

Two discoveries, in this order, neither of them named to the students until afterwards:

1. Holding the electorate and its underlying preferences fixed, the distribution of votes changes once voters begin anticipating one another. The disproportionality they then see in the seat totals is substantially the residue of that anticipation rather than a property of the counting rule alone. I want at least one student to volunteer something along the lines of not having voted for the candidate they actually preferred, without my prompting it.
2. When the same electorate votes under a multi-member rule in which votes attach to individual candidates rather than to party labels alone, the sharpest competition for a given voter's support comes from someone wearing the same label as the rival they were watching in the previous round. Their strategic reasoning changes character, not merely its outcome.

Design constraints:

- Three rounds, the same 24 voters throughout, with the rules changed between rounds. Tell me what each round is for, what the students are supposed to notice inside it, and how many minutes it takes.
- Write the voter cards out in full. Choose the preference profile deliberately rather than at random, and keep the numbers small enough that I can tally a round by hand on the whiteboard in under two minutes. In round 1, under single-winner plurality, the victor must be a candidate whom a majority of the class would rank below both of the others. The shift in round 2 must be generated by the students' own decisions, not by any instruction from me to vote strategically.
- Give each voter an intensity of preference and not merely an ordering, and make the point scheme asymmetric across voter types, so that for some of them a ballot that cannot affect the result is genuinely costly while for others it is a shrug. That asymmetry is what I expect to drive round 2, so put it explicitly on the cards.
- A payoff scheme in points rather than money, awarded individually and accumulating across all three rounds, ending in something the class cares about. Propose a conversion that touches nobody's grade and does not create an incentive to collude in a way that breaks the activity.
- Round 1 must permit no communication of intentions at all. In round 2, open exactly one bounded channel — a short campaign period in which the candidates address the electorate — and specify precisely what candidates may and may not say, since how much information they release is the variable I want the class able to point at afterwards.
- Round 3 needs a stated district magnitude, a stated counting rule, and one worked count, because I cannot run arithmetic live while also watching the room. Choose a rule you can defend as whiteboard-checkable.
- Precomputed answer keys for all three rounds: the intended outcome, plus the outcome under two or three plausible deviations, so that I can recognize on the spot whether the room has gone off script and what to say if it has.
- Tell me how many students play candidates, how many vote, and whether anyone is idle at any point. I do not want spectators.
- Withhold the vocabulary. The handouts must not contain the words proportional, plurality, Duverger, mechanical, or psychological; rules are to be described operationally. The debrief supplies the names — including the term for the two-party tendency under plurality, and for the distinction between what a rule does to votes already cast and what it does to votes not yet cast — and it maps specific moments of the activity onto specific parts of that distinction. It should also state what round 3 revealed that rounds 1 and 2 could not.
- A frank list of what will go wrong the first time I run this, in the order it will go wrong, with the cheapest fix for each item.
- A closing note on which feature of these systems the activity cannot reproduce at all — I expect at least one such feature to concern time and party organization rather than ballots — and what I will have to supply verbally to cover it.
