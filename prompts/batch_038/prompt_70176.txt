So, I'm two years into sitting on the amnesty panel of a truth commission. I'm deliberately not naming the country — partly confidentiality, partly because I don't want you pattern-matching onto the South African or Argentine or Rwandan material and handing me their jurisprudence instead of ours. Please also don't cite me court cases, treaty articles, or commission reports; I can't check anything you give me and I'd rather have your reasoning than invented authority. The setup is the usual conditional-amnesty genre: individual applications, amnesty only where the act was committed with a political objective and in the course of the conflict, a requirement of full disclosure of all relevant facts, and a clause saying the means used have to bear some proportion to the objective pursued. There are five of us — two ex-prosecutors, a human rights lawyer, a retired judge, and me, the philosopher, which was a funny thing to put on a CV about four months in. We sit Thursdays and the hearing I'm losing sleep over is on the 14th.

Our record so far, in the order we decided them:

1. GRANTED — four state security branch members, before the ceasefire, who shot four detained opposition fighters and staged it as an escape attempt. Written order from a district commander who was still alive and had never previously been connected to anything. Full disclosure; they gave us the commander and the location of the graves.
2. GRANTED — three opposition militia members, before the ceasefire, who shot two men at a roadblock after being told the men were informing. No written order, but the regional command treated it afterwards as unit business. Full disclosure; named two living participants nobody had identified.
3. DENIED — the spotter for a government mortar team, before the ceasefire, whose shell landed in a market. Nine dead, three of them children. Full disclosure, and he named the battery commander — who was already under indictment for a different shelling, so the account told us nothing we didn't have. Our written reason invoked the means being out of all proportion to any objective.
4. GRANTED — a railway clerk, before the ceasefire, who arranged a wagon on his stationmaster's instruction for the transfer of a unit that then abducted and killed two opposition organizers. He never saw anything. Full disclosure; gave us the mine shaft where they were. The two shooters who applied over the same incident were DENIED at the same sitting for evasive, self-contradicting accounts.
5. DENIED — a police sergeant, roughly five months after the ceasefire, who shot a man he said had informed on his brother. No orders, no unit involvement, nobody above him knew. He disclosed eventually but named no one we hadn't already spoken to and got the date wrong twice. Written reason: the act fell outside the conflict.
6. GRANTED — opposition militia, before the ceasefire, who killed two captured state soldiers. Full disclosure, and it produced the officer who'd ordered the capture, previously unknown to us. This is the mirror of case 1 and it's the only time we've said out loud in the reasons that we're keeping the two sides level.
7. DENIED — a man with no organizational ties at all who killed a neighbor he believed had informed, in a village where the militia had recruited his son. Before the ceasefire. His disclosure was complete and cost him nothing to make, but there was nothing in it: no one to implicate, no remains to find, no pattern to explain. Written reason: no political objective.

The written reasons are boilerplate — they recite the statute and move on — which is exactly why I want the pattern rather than the reasons. My own worry, and you can tell me I'm wrong: that what we've actually been running is a truth-purchase rule, where the thing that decides is how much new information the applicant hands over, and gravity is decoration. If that's right it's ugly, because it means the families are the ones paying the price of what we buy.

The application on the 14th. She was a driver for a regional opposition unit and is now a secondary school teacher. Seven months after the ceasefire and three months before the final accord — and note that sporadic fighting continued in the northern province for the better part of another year, which the defence will lean on — she was told by her regional commander to drive two fighters to a farmhouse. The objective was the father, who the unit believed had given up a safe house. He and his wife were shot, and so were their two children, aged six and nine; she waited in the vehicle and drove the two men back. She never went inside and carried nothing. Her disclosure is the fullest thing I've seen on this panel: it is the only evidence linking the regional commander, who is alive and denies everything, to any incident; it exposed one of the shooters, whose own application over the same incident we DENIED last year because he swore he wasn't there; and it recovered the family's remains, which had been moved. The two state-army applicants who drove a patrol to a farmstead where three adult civilians were killed five months before the ceasefire both got amnesty. The family's surviving son from the father's first marriage is a lawyer and is appearing against her, and he's going to say the ceasefire is a bright line and that we have never once granted anything on the far side of it. The chair said something in the last annual report to the effect that the commission shouldn't be seen to reward violence committed after the guns fell quiet. And the prosecutor has told me privately that he'll only charge the regional commander if she testifies, and her lawyer says a denial means she takes her chances at trial instead of helping a commission that burned her.

Give me the rule or rules our seven precedents actually support, and then tell me what we should do on the 14th so we can look ourselves in the eye.
