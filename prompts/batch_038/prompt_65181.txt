I am drafting the theory section of a paper on sequential diagnostic testing, co-authored with a biostatistician, and our departmental reading group has got stuck on an argument that starts from a theorem I have no intention of giving up and ends somewhere none of us can follow. The theorem is Good's result linking Carnap's principle of total evidence to the principle of rationality: for an agent with probabilistic credences who updates by conditionalization, and for any costless experiment whose possible outcomes are mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive, deferring the choice until the outcome is known is never expected to be worse than choosing now. Writing A for the act set, S for the states, and {e} for the partition of experimental outcomes, the claim is that the maximum over a in A of the sum over s of P(s)u(a,s) is less than or equal to the sum over e of P(e) times the maximum over a in A of the sum over s of P(s|e)u(a,s). The proof is a couple of lines, and my colleague regards it as unremarkable. What is not unremarkable, at least to me, is the following argument, which a graduate student in the group built on top of it and which I have been unable to dismantle for three weeks.

The anchor case is mine. A vineyard must decide before dusk whether to cover the vines. Covering costs 4; an uncovered frost night costs 15; the prior probability of frost is 0.5. Acting now, the best available act is to cover, at an expected value of −4. There is a free regional advisory, correct 80% of the time in each direction; conditioning on it, the best act is still to cover when it says frost (−4 against −12) but to leave the vines uncovered when it says no frost (−3 against −4), so the wait-and-see strategy is worth −3.5. Strict improvement. Now suppose a second free reading, dew point at dusk, refines the advisory's partition and — my colleague has checked the arithmetic and I will simply grant it — the same inequality is strict again, and a third reading refines that, and so on, with an endless supply of free, conditionally informative readings all obtainable before dark. Here is the student's argument. (1) Good's theorem holds, with the hypotheses just stated. (2) Therefore declining costless, relevant evidence is at best weakly irrational, and in any case where the inequality is strict it is strictly irrational. (3) The choice whether to run a further experiment is itself a choice among acts, so (1) applies to it as well: the expected gain from evidence about the bearing, reliability, or interpretation of further evidence is likewise nonnegative. (4) Iterating, at every stage the agent's expected utility is strictly increased by postponing the decision in favour of one more reading, and there is no stage at which the chain terminates. (5) So the rational Bayesian agent in this case never covers the vines; she reads until dusk and then it is too late. (6) But covering now is plainly rational in this case, and a decision theory whose deliverances include perpetual postponement in an ordinary frost decision does not deserve the name. (7) Hence (1), or the inference from (1) to (2), is false: free information can be expected to hurt, or the inequality simply does not license an ought.

If I am forced to choose I would rather keep the theorem than keep (6), but the student's riposte is that any Bayesian who keeps (1) owes an account of why such an agent ever acts at all, and I do not have one. So: which step do you reject, and is the argument invalid or valid with a false premise? I want to know why the step you reject looked innocent enough to survive three meetings — it did not look like a trick to any of us — and I want the price of each of the main candidate exits, in particular what a Bayesian surrenders if she denies (3) and what she surrenders if she denies (2), since those two denials seem to have very different consequences for the rest of the theory. Two further questions. First, does your diagnosis also cover the cases people produce as counterexamples to the theorem itself — an agent who doubts she will conditionalize on what she learns, an agent whose evidence fails to form a partition, an agent with unbounded utilities, an agent who will be unable to act on the result — or are those separate leaks that need separate patching? Second, and more practically: is the abstract regress in (4)–(5) the same problem as my vineyard case, or two problems? I suspect two, and if so my paper only needs to answer one of them, and I need to know which.
