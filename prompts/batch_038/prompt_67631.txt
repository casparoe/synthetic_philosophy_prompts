I teach a 100-minute first-year historical methods seminar, 16 students, one TA, and I can print as much as I want. Three of them do family history in their spare time and will happily tell you a record is a record.

I want an activity that makes them feel what history from below is actually up against, without me saying the phrase once until the very end. The thing I want them to run into on their own: pretty much every surviving scrap of paper about an ordinary person in the 1840s was written by somebody who was counting them, employing them, relieving them, moving them or prosecuting them — so the life you can rebuild from it is a portrait drawn by the people who had power over her, and the parts of her that were nobody's problem left no trace at all. I want them to construct a confident, moving biography out of that, win something with it, and then have the ground taken away.

Here's the dossier I've already made up. Invented woman, invented parish, real genres, so nobody can look it up. Sarah Ann Vickery, born about 1826, a midlands market town:

- one census line where she exists only as a relationship to somebody else's household head, age 24, birthplace given as a parish nine miles off
- a magistrates' examination about which parish she legally belonged to, in the clerk's third person, ending in an order to remove her
- a deposition where she names the father of her child, with his two-line denial underneath and no verdict recorded anywhere
- four entries in a workhouse admission and discharge register, 1849 to 1853, mostly dates and one word about why she came in
- the parish account book showing what she cost them over two winters, plus a disputed bill sent to the other parish
- a curate's letter to a charity describing a deserving case, which tells you a great deal about the curate
- one line in a quarter sessions calendar: name, offence, four words of disposition
- and then nothing. no letters, no diary, no photograph, no gravestone, the burial register gives an age and an abode

Then late, on purpose, two things that cut the other way: a mark she made at the bottom of a petition about a footpath, and her name as licensee of a beer house in a trade directory eleven years after the last official paper mentions her. I want those as a second movement — some records were made by poor people for their own reasons — but they mustn't rescue it into a feel-good ending.

So design the whole thing for me. Specifically: how to split the 16 and how to allocate documents asymmetrically so that two groups working from the same life end up with two different people (that collision is the moment I most want, so build to it); some judging or scoring scheme where the reward goes to whoever tells the most compelling story, because I want the incentive to be part of the trap and I want them to notice they were being paid to fill gaps with confidence; the exact order of handouts and reveals with minutes against each; what the TA is doing while this happens; one short beat where they rattle off from memory what they'd expect to survive about the biggest landowner in the same parish, since I reckon they'll produce a dozen items in two minutes and won't need to be told the contrast; and the debrief.

In the debrief I need them to see both halves, not just the pessimistic one — what the small scale buys you, i.e. how her removal and the disputed bill expose a whole machine of settlement and poor relief that a county-level narrative would sail straight past, and then whether one life can license any generalization at all. Also give me what to say to the genealogy kids when they insist the fix is just to go and find more sources, and seperate out for me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle each one.

Finally, be straight with me about which feature of the idea an exercise like this simply can't reproduce and I have to supply by hand — and if anything in my dossier strikes you as rigging the game too obviously, say so. I dont want them walking out feeling tricked.
