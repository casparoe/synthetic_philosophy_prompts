I'm a junior in a political theory seminar this term on membership and borders — the whole syllabus has been Walzer, Carens, Miller, Benhabib, a couple of Sarah Song pieces — and next Thursday is student presentation week. Everyone gets twelve minutes to lay out their final paper and then the floor opens for about fifteen minutes of discussion, which in this seminar means the professor plus eleven people who've all read the same four books and are quietly competitive about it. My paper is an internal critique of Walzer and im kind of nervous because she's sympathetic to him. The thesis, roughly: his defense of a community's right to exclude is supposedly limited by two conditions — closure is legitimate only if the polity is genuinely full (the lifeboat case), and it turns into tyranny when people living inside the territory are kept out of membership. My arguement is that those aren't side-constraints bolted onto the position, they *are* the position, and that once you describe an actual wealthy state's labor and settlement policies honestly, both are already violated, because such states continuously manufacture the exact category they claim not to have: long-term residents, seasonal workers, people with no path in. Meanwhile "full" does all the work with no test anyone outside the community could apply. So his own premises, pushed through, get you something like a defeasible right to enter, not merely a duty to take refugees or share spare territory. I also try to kill the club analogy — a club can exclude because nobody's life chances ride on being in it, while state coercion sets the terms of basically everything. In office hours she already warned me that internal arguments like this usually end up smuggling in cosmopolitan premises the target never held, and honestly that's the thing keeping me up.

The room: my professor works on legitimacy and democratic self-rule and is the type to ask whether coercion is doing quiet work for me, and whether I've actually shown Walzer anything he'd have to accept rather than just announced that he should. One student is deep in the special-obligations-to-compatriots literature and will insist that distributive justice is inherently a relation among people who already share institutions, so my "already violated" move just assumes the border I'm supposed to be arguing about. And at least two people will say some version of isn't-this-just-Carens-with-extra-steps.
