I study the literature on biological function, and I want to work through a real scientific dispute rather than another round of armchair intuitions about hearts and teleosemantics. The case is the ENCODE controversy.

The facts as I take them, so you need not reconstruct them: in 2012 the ENCODE consortium reported that roughly 80 percent of the human genome is functional, where functional meant biochemically active in some assay — transcribed, bound by transcription factors, or in an accessible chromatin state — and the result was widely reported as the death of junk DNA. Evolutionary genomicists, most prominently Dan Graur and collaborators with W. Ford Doolittle among the critics, objected that biochemical activity is not function: that pervasive transcription and binding occur as noise or as byproducts of selfish elements, that comparative-genomic estimates under a conservation criterion land under 15 percent and by some measures near 5 percent, and that the selection coefficients implied by the consortium's figure are incompatible with what drift permits at human effective population sizes.

Analyze the dispute through the two standard accounts of function. I want (a) an explicit mapping of which account each side was relying on, and a judgment about whether the disagreement was substantive or partly verbal once that mapping is on the table; (b) an assessment of the permissiveness objection to causal-role theories — everything has a causal role in something, and on some readings a heart's function would include making noise — asking whether ENCODE actually instantiates that objection, or whether a causal-role account properly relativized to an explanandum predicts the critics' reaction rather than the consortium's claim; (c) where the selected-effect account is embarrassed here: elements whose activity serves their own replication rather than organismal fitness, functions whose selected effects are weak and lineage-relative, and activity with no measurable fitness consequence either way; and (d) whether the episode confirms or complicates the stock verdict that biologists' function talk is etiological, given that the party invoking the causal-role notion was the one doing the measuring.

Mark clearly where each account goes silent instead of forcing an answer out of it, and close with what the case implies for reconciliationist moves — Boorse-style goal-contribution theories, or an avowed pluralism about function.
