I write a science-and-ideas column for a mid-size outlet — pieces run about 3,000 words and the readers are smart but not academic. Next month I'm doing a long sit-down with a computational neuroscientist who's become the loudest advocate for what he calls "replacement-scale uploading." His pitch, roughly: you don't scan a brain and run it somewhere else, you swap neurons out one at a time for synthetic analogs that do exactly the same job, and if each individual swap is functionally negligible then consciousness just carries across — he leans hard on the old dancing-qualia style argument to make the point. News peg is that his lab just got funding to do the swap in awake, behaving mice, and he's said publicly that if it works in mice the same logic scales to humans and there's no further interesting question. He's charming, he's been interviewed a lot, and he's very good at steering everything back to the engineering.

I've got 45 minutes. Write me ten to twelve questions that get into the weeds of the view instead of letting him give me the Ship of Theseus talk he gives everybody. Specifically I want:

- two or three questions that exploit a hole in his own protocol: he's said the human version would be done during sleep, partly because its easier surgically, which means the subject never gets an uninterrupted first-person view of the transition. Does the dancing-qualia move still do any work when nobody is awake to watch their qualia dance? Anticipate his answer — something like "functional continuity is what matters, not witnessed continuity" — and give me the follow-up that makes him cash out what functional continuity is adding over and above "you wouldn't be able to tell."

- one question on the halfway point, where half the brain is silicon and half isn't: can the two halves have different phenomenal character mid-procedure, and if they can, whose report about it is authoritative, given that the language areas sit on one side? Follow-up keyed to the answer he'll actually give.

- one on "functionally negligible" — negligible as measured by what? I want him forced to name an observable, and then pressed on whether that observable is behavioral or something else, because if it's only behavioral he owes me a reason why identical behavior is evidence about experience rather than a restatement of his own assumption.

- one on reversibility: if you can run the swap backwards and restore the tissue, does that show nothing was lost, or only that nothing detectable was lost? He'll say those come apart in words alone — push him on that.

- one that seperates his case from the scan-and-copy case, since replacement means the original gets destroyed piece by piece rather than duplicated, and I want to know whether he thinks the duplication scenario is a red herring or a real threat to his argument.

- and one straight question about what would change his mind, phrased so that "we'd see it in the mouse behavior" doesn't count as an answer.

Then close with two questions a general reader will actually care about, one of which gets him onto who the first human candidate would realistically be and why anyone would volunteer — not onto his grant. No softballs, nothing like "what drew you to this work," and don't put words in his mouth; I only want the questions, plus a one-line note under each saying what answer I'm fishing for and why it's a hard one for him.
