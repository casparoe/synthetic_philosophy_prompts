I'm a retired physiologist — thirty-five years on ion channels, then the last decade of it drifting into whole-animal work — and since I stopped going in to the lab I've been reading philosophy of mind for pleasure, badly and out of order. My wife and I both had colonoscopies in the spring, hers under propofol and mine under what they called twilight sedation, and neither of us remembers a single thing, and the nurse said that was the point. But my wife says I talked during mine. Apparently I complained, twice, in a voice she'd never heard from me, and when she mentioned it to the fellow afterwards he laughed and said that's normal, they don't remember it. I've since had two cataract operations with the same logic: awake, local drops, and afterwards my daughter tells me I said the whole thing was horrible — I remember being wheeled in, I remember the coffee in recovery, and the middle twenty minutes simply aren't there. My recollection is that this is completely standard and that the endpoint the field actually cares about is whether you can recall anything later. If you can't, the event is written up as though it never happened, and everybody is satisfied, including me.

Here's what's been bothering me, and it's a philosophy question rather than a medical one: what theory of mind does that practice presuppose? It looks to me like the profession treats "is there anything it's like to be this patient right now" as a question that only has an answer if somebody can report on it afterwards, which as a young man I'd have called behaviorism and been told I was being uncharitable, but which now looks like precisely what the practice does — and I can't tell whether its a considered position or an operational convenience nobody has ever had to spell out. Is there a real commitment in there, that an experience leaving no memory trace isn't a harm, or is a much smaller one? Somebody must have run that as a thought experiment, a pill that erases your memory of a painful operation and whether that makes the operation any better, and I have a feeling it was Parfit, but I couldn't find it and I don't want to hang anything on the attribution. The obvious rival story is that nobody in that room thinks the pain didn't happen: they think a competent adult consented to not minding it, so consent does the work rather than any theory of harm or of persons. Which of those two is the practice actually committed to, and is there any way to tell them apart from the outside, given that the observable behaviour is identical?

Then there's the inconsistency I can't dissolve. In the same building, where the patient can't report — a baby, somebody far gone in dementia, the animals in the veterinary unit down the road — the rule is the reverse: you infer pain from the face, the guarding, the heart rate, you treat on the inference, and nobody would dream of arguing that because the baby won't remember the needle it doesn't matter. The field also officially holds that pain is whatever the person having it says it is, which sits oddly with a practice whose success criterion is the person's silence. So we do one thing when the report is missing and the patient was never in a position to consent, and the opposite when the report is missing and the patient was. Reconstruct whatever single theory makes both coherent, if one exists; if none does, tell me which of the two the practice would have to give up and what it would cost them. And I'd like you to separate carefully what the practice strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably — I suspect a lot of it is philosophically underdetermined and would be licensed equally by a memory-based account of harm, by a preference view, and by plain cost-benefit thinking, and I want to know which parts aren't. Finally: if you took the problem of other minds seriously instead of dissolving it, what changes on a Tuesday morning? Depth monitors on everybody, higher doses, treating a non-report as an open question rather than a closed one — and what does that cost, ethically as well as financially, given that overdosing is not free either. I have coffee Thursdays with two old colleagues from the department, one of whom thinks I've gone soft in the head, and I'd like to arrive with something better than than an anecdote.
