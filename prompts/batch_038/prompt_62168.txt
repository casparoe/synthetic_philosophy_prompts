I help run a small calibration club where members post probability estimates for upcoming events and settle up with each other in small stakes at the end of the month. One of our more senior members has a stock move. Whenever anybody's numbers look non-probabilistic — somebody who gives 0.4 and 0.45 to two hypotheses they admit are equivalent, somebody who reports a range instead of a point, somebody who says they simply would not accept a wager at any price on a political question they care about — he says, in the same tone, that they can be Dutch-booked, and treats that as ending the discussion. He phrases it as a slogan: if your degrees of belief do not behave like probabilities, you can be Dutch-booked, and that is what is wrong with them. Last week the slogan got deployed against a member who publishes imprecise estimates, on the grounds that a spread of prices is precisely how you lose money; another member objected that a spread of buying and selling prices is exactly what protects you from being booked, since you can decline any bet whose price falls inside it, and the two of them argued past each other for an hour without either saying what they actually meant.

I would like you to act as a professional philosopher who works on formal epistemology and the foundations of decision theory, someone who has taught this material many times and knows which steps of the standard argument are theorems and which are philosophical glue. Please think carefully before writing, and begin with two or three sentences identifying what you take to be the most load-bearing ambiguities in the slogan — the places where the word "can" and the word "irrational" are doing quiet work — so that I can see how you are reading the question. Then give me the full precisification.

What I want is a menu, not a verdict. Take the sentence apart into the distinct claims it could be making, and work through them in a sensible order, saying for each one what it precisely asserts, whether it is a platitude that nobody disputes, a genuine mathematical result with nontrivial assumptions attached, a substantive and contestable philosophical thesis, or simply false. At minimum I would like the following to be sorted out, though you should add anything I have missed: the two directions of the betting-quotient result and what each direction actually licenses, including which assumptions the converse direction needs that the forward direction does not; the bridge premise linking an agent's stated betting prices to their degrees of belief, and what has to be true about the agent for that link to hold; the difference between stakes measured in money and stakes measured in utility, and what happens to the argument if utility is not linear in money; the scope of additivity, including what changes when the algebra of propositions is infinite and an agent is finitely but not countably additive; whether the argument requires the agent to be willing to both buy and sell at a single price; the assumption that the agent will in fact be offered the bets that the book requires, and that they will accept them, and what this does to the case of someone who refuses to gamble; the problem of the book exploiting a package of attitudes rather than beliefs alone, so that a guaranteed loss may not identify the beliefs as the culprit; and the question of whether a guaranteed loss is an epistemic defect or merely a practical one, which I suspect is the real point of contention in our club. Please also address whether susceptibility to such a book is necessary for irrationality, whether it is sufficient, and give me your best counterexample in each direction, including whatever you think of the agent who fails logical omniscience and the agent who is coherent by any standard but has expensive tastes.

Then bring it back to the argument we actually had. Tell me which of these readings my senior member needs in order to convict someone of publishing a range rather than a point, which ones he needs in order to convict someone of refusing bets altogether, and where each of those arguments breaks if a premise fails — and say plainly whether the two disputants in our club were disagreeing about a theorem or about a bridge premise. Finish with a short paragraph giving me the bottom line: what survives of the slogan once it is stated carefully, and how I should phrase it back to him at the next meeting so that it is both accurate and not a conversation-ender.

Two constraints on how you write this up. First, no references, no bibliography, no article titles and no page numbers; where a result or an objection has a standard name, use the name rather than citing it, and if you are not certain of an attribution, describe the position instead of assigning it. Second, and please take this seriously: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables. I want continuous prose only, organized into ordinary paragraphs, the way one would write a considered letter or a long email to a colleague — if you need to enumerate, do it in sentences within a paragraph. Aim for roughly twelve hundred to fifteen hundred words.
