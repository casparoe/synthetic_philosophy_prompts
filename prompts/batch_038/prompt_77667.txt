I have run a monthly film-and-discussion evening in the back room of our public library for about eleven years. I spent my working life in municipal engineering and have no training in philosophy; what I know I have picked up by reading slowly and by arguing with the fifteen or so people who turn up. Next month we are showing Kubrick's Paths of Glory, and I want a written reading of it that I can hand out beforehand, because I have got myself stuck on a question I cannot settle alone.

Some years ago I read Michael Walzer's Just and Unjust Wars, and more recently I have read some of the essays written against him, chiefly the argument that a soldier fighting for an unjust cause does not acquire a right to kill merely by putting on a uniform. I think McMahan is the name most attached to that view, though I may have that wrong. As far as I could follow, the whole debate runs horizontally. It asks what our soldiers may do to their soldiers, and their soldiers to ours. The equality in the phrase moral equality of combatants is an equality between two opposed groups, and the doctrine reads to me like a bargain with two signatories.

Paths of Glory seems to me to be a film in which almost nobody kills an enemy. The Germans exist as machine-gun fire coming out of a fortified position the picture calls the Anthill, and then, in the last scene, as one frightened captive woman singing a folk song at a room of jeering soldiers. Every death the film actually dwells on is a Frenchman killing a Frenchman: the lieutenant who panics on a night patrol and grenades one of his own scouts, falsifies his report, and then chooses the surviving witness as one of the three men charged with cowardice; the general who orders his artillery to fire into his own trenches to push a stalled attack forward; the firing squad at the end. And the central atrocity — a general ordering a hopeless assault because a promotion was mentioned to him, then shooting three survivors of it as an alibi — does not look like anything the theory I have been reading is built to name. The intended targets were enemy combatants. The order was vain and wasteful rather than indiscriminate. There is no category I could find for it.

So my question is whether the moral equality of combatants also runs vertically. Does the doctrine say anything about what commanders may do to their own troops, or is the soldier's standing against his own generals settled elsewhere, by a different set of arguments the theory simply assumes? A second point I cannot resolve: the three men are condemned for failing to advance. Nothing I read asks whether a soldier may refuse an order that is futile rather than unjust. Refusing to fight an unjust war is one problem; refusing to walk into machine guns for the sake of a general's career is another, and the film is plainly about the second.

Please write me the hand-out. It should explain the doctrine and the revisionist objection to it plainly enough that my group can follow without having read anything, and it should then test my horizontal-versus-vertical idea against the film seriously rather than flattering it. I want to be told where I am wrong. I half suspect that the closing scene with the German singer is doing sentimental work rather than philosophical work, and that an honest critic might say the film is not about the ethics of war at all but about a rotten institution — if that is the better reading, say so and say what the film is for instead. I would also like to know whether the fact that the picture never tells us which side is fighting justly is a weakness or a deliberate device, since it seems to shut the revisionist question out entirely.

Two practical requests. If what I am groping toward already has a name in the literature, tell me the name; I do not want a reading list or references. And please do not put lines of dialogue in quotation marks — I cannot check them against the print we are showing, and one of our regulars is a retired sergeant major who will notice anything sloppy about how an army behaves.
