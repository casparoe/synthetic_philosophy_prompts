I work on animal-welfare policy, and in a recent internal discussion a colleague argued that we should extend legal protection and husbandry standards far down the phylogenetic scale — to decapods, to insects, and on some accounts further still — by borrowing the smoke-detector principle from evolutionary medicine and anxiety research. His reasoning: a detector that cries fire at burnt toast is a trivial nuisance, while a detector that stays silent through a real fire is a catastrophe, so the rational setting is a very low threshold; likewise, wrongly granting moral standing to a creature that feels nothing costs us almost nothing, whereas wrongly withholding it from a creature that suffers costs that creature everything, so the evidential bar for sentience should be set very low. He also insists he is a gradualist: consciousness comes in degrees, shading off toward the thermostat, rather than switching on at some taxonomic rung.

I am not persuaded the two commitments fit, and I want a careful audit of the analogy rather than a survey of the sentience debate. Please do the following.

1. Give the mapping explicitly, ideally as a compact table: what plays the role of the detector, the smoke, the fire, the alarm, the cost of a false alarm, the cost of a missed fire, and the threshold-setting decision. Where a slot has more than one plausible candidate in the target case, flag it.
2. Separate the disanalogies that actually threaten the intended use from those that are merely decorative. I am especially interested in whether a device with a binary output is the right model for a property my colleague describes as graded, and what becomes of the argument if the "fire" is itself a matter of degree rather than an event.
3. State what the analogy quietly assumes and the target case lacks — for instance, calibration against known positives, a stable base rate, false-alarm costs that are bounded and borne by whoever sets the threshold, and an alarm that can be switched off once it has gone off.
4. Rule on the conclusion. Does extending protection far down the scale survive the disanalogies, survive in a weaker form, or was it being carried by the analogy all along? If the case for a low threshold is better made without the detector picture, say what carries it.
5. Offer a better analogy for moral-status attribution under this kind of uncertainty, if one exists, and say what work it does that the smoke detector cannot.

Keep the whole response to 700 words or fewer, table included. No background on the literature, no citations, no recommendations for further reading — just the audit.
