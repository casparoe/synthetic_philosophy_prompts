**Working title:** The Handoff: Delegated Dirty Hands and the Distribution of Moral Residue

**1.** The standard dirty-hands case is monological. In Walzer's Political Action: The Problem of Dirty Hands, a politician authorises the torture of a captive in order to uncover a plot; he does what is wrong because it is what must be done, and the guilt he feels afterwards is treated as evidence of his moral seriousness rather than as a reason to have refrained. Nagel's Ruthlessness in Public Life has the same shape: an office generates requirements that cannot be fully reconciled with the requirements binding the person who occupies it. In both, one agent stands at the centre of the analysis, and the two questions are what he should do and what he should feel once he has done it.

**2.** My claim is that the analytically interesting cases in the modern state are dyadic rather than monological, and that a family of them is missed by the two frameworks usually reached for — complicity on one side, and the problem of many hands on the other.

**3.** Call the structure the handoff. A principal wants an outcome obtainable only by an act that violates a serious constraint. He cannot perform the act himself, not because he is physically unable, but because the publicity conditions of his office — the evidential record, the chain of authorisation, the forms in which his decisions are minuted — make it impossible for him to be its author. So he creates or occupies himself with a position whose holder can be. He does not order the act in terms that would make him its author: he sets eligibility parameters, funds a programme, defines a target category, withholds an objection he is known to be in a position to enter, or leaves the operative choice to a subordinate whose incentives and information he has arranged.

**4.** Three features separate the handoff from mere diffusion of responsibility. First, the separation is designed. In Thompson's problem of many hands, the difficulty of assigning responsibility to any official is a by-product of the scale and internal division of labour of the modern state; nobody chose it, and the diagnostic task is to recover personal responsibility where the hierarchical and collective models lose it. In the handoff, the distribution is the point. The principal's aim includes the aim that the dirt fall elsewhere.

**5.** Second, the principal is not merely causally upstream. He is the only party who sees the trade-off whole, and the only party positioned to refuse it. The subordinate typically encounters a task rather than a dilemma. I want to say that this asymmetry of description does moral work that causal contribution alone does not, and that it is precisely what the many-hands framework has no place for.

**6.** Third, and here is the puzzle. The handoff drives apart two things that dirty-hands theory treats as travelling together. On the standard picture, the agent who performs the wrongful act incurs both the wrongdoing and the residue: he is guilty, he should feel guilt, and his feeling of it is what distinguishes him from the fanatic. In the handoff, the residue settles on the subordinate — he performed the act, he will be investigated, disavowed, prosecuted, and remembered as its author — while the wrong, if there is one, belongs principally to the principal, who weighed the reasons and chose. So the agent who ought to feel the sharpest anguish is the one whom the ordinary vocabulary of wrongdoing does not reach, and the agent who is appropriately described as ruined by the business is the one whose decision was least free and least informed.

**7.** I propose to call this residue transfer. It is not merely descriptive: if it holds, then Walzer's test of seriousness — does the agent acknowledge the wrong and bear it? — is applied to the wrong person, and a system can satisfy that test locally at every node while failing it globally.

**8.** Illustration. A state operates a targeted killing programme. Political leadership sets eligibility categories, funds the programme, receives general briefings; authority over any individual strike is delegated to a legal officer and an operations commander; execution falls to a crew that sees a feed. Suppose, for the sake of the example, that some strikes are wrongful. On a monological analysis we look for the agent who stood in the dirty-hands dilemma. There may be no such agent. The political principal never confronted the killing of a particular person; the legal officer applied criteria she did not set; the crew carried out a lawful order. Yet the programme killed wrongly, and someone chose that it should be able to.

**9.** The institutional corollary. A constitution may be designed so that no occupant of any office ever faces a genuine dirty-hands dilemma, because every decision that would generate one is decomposed across offices in advance. Each participant's moral position is thereby improved, and I argue the practice is worsened: the constraint that used to bite as a cost felt by someone now bites nowhere. Call this the dirt-dividing constitution. It is the opposite of the transparency ideal — an institutional arrangement whose virtue for each participant is its vice as a whole.

**10.** The objections I expect: that this is role morality redescription; that it is the doctrine of double effect wearing a political theory costume, since the principal intends the harm while the subordinate merely foresees it; that it collapses into ordinary complicity, the principal being a co-principal; and — the one I cannot yet answer — that residue is not a distinct moral quantity at all but simply guilt, which tracks wrongdoing, so that the inverse distribution in paragraph 6 is a stipulation dressed up as a discovery.

**11.** Conclusion: the dirty-hands literature asks what a good person must do to govern well. The handoff suggests a prior question, which is what a political system does to the distribution of answerability when it makes sure that no one inside it has to answer.
