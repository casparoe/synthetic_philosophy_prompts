I have spent the better part of two decades reading the rationalist blog and forum corpus on probability — the long arguments that probability lives in the map rather than the territory, the running battles with frequentism, the attempts to ground credences in coherence and in proper scoring rules — and I have recently been asked to turn that into something more consequential than a comment thread. A small institute is funding a three-year, four-person working group on the foundations of probability. I am not one of the four, but I have been asked to draft the programme document that fixes what they will spend those three years on, and the four will be selected partly on the basis of that document. So the agenda I write is not decorative.

Two features of the setup constrain everything. First, the composition: two philosophers of science, one theoretical statistician who works on calibration and scoring rules, and one physicist whose background is in quantum foundations. There is no machine learning researcher on the group, and the funder has said explicitly that they do not want the deliverable to be a new algorithm or a benchmark. Second, the funder's stated end, which I share, is narrower than settling what probability means. They want to know whether the numbers produced by careful forecasters in genuinely one-off domains — the probability of a particular pandemic, of a particular treaty collapsing, of a transformative AI system arriving inside some window — can bear the weight we put on them: whether such a number can at once be a coherent degree of belief, something whose accuracy could in principle be checked against long-run behaviour, and something that stands in the right relation to physical chance to license an agent in aligning their credence with it. A colleague and I have referred to this as the three-role problem. That is our coinage, not a term of art; please do not go looking for it in the literature, and please do not attribute it to anyone or to any school.

My provisional list of candidate questions for the group is as follows, in no particular order:

1. Whether the three roles above are jointly satisfiable for a single formal object, or whether one of them must be surrendered — and if one must go, which, and what the practice of forecasting looks like afterwards.
2. Reference-class selection for events that happen at most once: is there a principled account of which past events a one-off forecast is a forecast about, or is it judgment all the way down — and if it is judgment all the way down, does that undercut any interesting notion of calibration in exactly the domains where we most want it?
3. The status of imprecise-probability and set-valued accounts: do they genuinely solve the problem of having a sharp credence on the basis of thin evidence, or do they relocate it, and what do the known pathologies of such accounts do to a forecaster who has to act and update?
4. Whether frequentism is still a live option worth engaging, or whether that debate is finished and the group would be spending three years on a corpse.
5. Whether any notion of physical chance is needed outside quantum mechanics and statistical mechanics, and if it is needed there, whether a best-systems or otherwise Humean account can absorb it without leaving an unexplained constraint on rational credence.
6. Whether the coherence-based foundations — Dutch-book arguments, representation theorems, scoring-rule arguments — establish anything normative at all, or whether they smuggle in the very betting framework and decision-theoretic assumptions they are supposed to derive.
7. Something I have not listed that you take to be more upstream than all of these.

What I want is a defended agenda, not a survey and not a reading list. Concretely:

- Rank the questions for this group, and commit to the ranking. State the criteria you are actually using — how much else depends on the answer, how tractable the question is in three years for two philosophers, a statistician and a physicist with no ML capacity, whether it is currently neglected or already well worked, and whether resolving it would change what a forecaster or a decision-maker ought to do — and tell me how you are trading those criteria off against each other rather than silently weighting them.
- Give me the dependency structure. Which questions are upstream of which, which are downstream and therefore premature to attack first, and which of my items are, on your view, the same question wearing different clothes.
- Be blunt about what to strike. If you think my own favourite, item 1, is a pseudo-problem — or worse, an artefact of the way I have coined it, with no content until the coinage is unpacked — say so and say what should replace it. I would much rather hear that now than in year two.
- Tell me what would move items. For each of your top three, give me the kind of development over the next two or three years — a result, a published argument, an empirical finding about forecasting practice, a shift in how physicists talk about chance — that would push it up or down the list. I want markers I could check against, not a general statement that more evidence would help.
- If you think the entire framing is wrong — that this is not fundamentally a question about interpreting probability, but about something else, such as the epistemology of expert judgment or the pragmatics of what a number commits you to when you publish it — then argue for the reframing and give me a ranking under it. I still have to hand in a document either way, so please do not stop at the objection.

Finally, an epistemic request. I do not want a hedge on every line: give me the ordering you would defend if a colleague of yours attacked it at a whiteboard. Then, separately, tell me what would change your mind — which single argument, if it went through, would make you reorder the list, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail. Mark the two places in your ranking where you are least confident and say why you are least confident there, and flag which of your deprioritizations you would be most embarrassed by if it turned out to have been the wrong call.
