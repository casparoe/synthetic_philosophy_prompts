You are to act as a specialist in the history of utilitarian legal philosophy, with particular expertise in Jeremy Bentham's theory of punishment and in his unpublished writings on sexual offences. Please think carefully rather than answering from general impressions of utilitarianism, and begin by sketching, in a few sentences, how you would reconstruct the architecture of Bentham's argument before you evaluate the reading I propose below.

Background. I have been working through the long manuscript essay Bentham devoted to the class of conduct he treats under the heading of offences against one's self — the essay he never published in his lifetime — and I want to make sure I have its argumentative structure right. My understanding is this. Bentham holds that pleasure is intrinsically good and pain intrinsically bad, and that the legislator's business is to maximize the former and minimize the latter. The revulsion that a majority feels toward some private sexual conduct is itself a real pain, and it therefore belongs on the ledger. So Bentham faces a problem: if that pain is counted, a sufficiently widespread and intense disgust would seem to outweigh the modest pleasures of a few participants, and punishment would be required on strictly utilitarian grounds. My reading is that he escapes this conclusion only by declining to count the spectators' disgust, and that he licenses this exclusion by giving the consenting participants' own good a threshold status: no quantity of third-party revulsion, however large, is permitted to trade off against it. I have been calling this the threshold reading.

Please help me with three questions, answering under numbered headings and drawing only on your own understanding — no references, and no quotations unless you are confident they are verbatim.

1. Is my characterization of the structure accurate? Reconstruct as precisely as you can how Bentham distinguishes a disapproval that may properly ground punishment from one that may not, and say whether his distinction concerns what is admitted into the hedonic ledger, or something else entirely. If the threshold reading misdescribes his move, explain what the move actually is and what premises it requires.

2. Bentham's account of the mischief of an act includes not only the harm that falls on assignable individuals but also further mischief consisting of alarm and of danger. Explain the work these two notions do in his theory of punishment, and then determine whether the threshold reading can be squared with them. In particular: does his framework deliver the same verdict on identical conduct carried out in strict secrecy and on the same conduct widely publicized and generally known to be common? If the verdicts differ, identify the premise on which the difference turns.

3. Consider the proposal that Bentham is simply an early proponent of the view that the state may coerce only to prevent harm to others. State what this proposal captures correctly, and where it distorts his position, using his treatment of purely self-regarding conduct as your test case. Be concrete: identify at least two respects in which his limits on punishment are narrower, or wider, than that liberal principle would suggest, and say whether either difference is traceable to his account of enforcement costs rather than to his account of wrongdoing.

Aim for analytical precision rather than survey breadth, and keep the whole response to roughly nine hundred words.
