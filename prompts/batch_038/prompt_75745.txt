My grandma died in March and I'm the one clearing out the house, which is a hoarder situation but the gentle kind — nothing rotten, just more. Rooms of boxes, a shed, and a second shed behind the first shed. My aunt and I have been fighting about how to divide it up. She wants a system: label everything, sort it into categories, decide once and for all which of us gets what out of each box, and write the system down so anyone could check it later. I keep saying we'll never get to the end of it, we'll die first, so we should just take turns picking, one thing at a time, and let whatever happens happen. She says that isn't fair, because there's no rule to it, and a division you can't state the rule for isn't a division, it's just whatever happened.

That argument has been living in my head for three weeks, and it turns out it's basically the thing I half-remember from a logic class: the gap between a choice existing and a choice being describable. The old boots-and-socks example — with boots you can just say "take the left one," with socks you're stuck and have to appeal to something you can't point at. And the creepier bit I looked up: that if you don't have any choice principle, you can have a collection that is definitely not finite, that nobody could ever finish going through, but that you also can't count — you can't even start a list of it, because you can't pull out infinitely many distinct members one after another.

Can you write me a short story that lives inside that? Around 2000 words, more if it needs it. What I'm after:

- Don't name any of it. No character says "axiom of choice" or "set theory" or "uncountable." The math has to be load-bearing but underground — the reader should feel the shape of it without being handed a diagram.
- Make it about someone whose job or situation is exactly this: they have to take one thing from each of an unending collection of things, and someone above them insists the taking only counts if the rule can be recited afterward. Work the boots/socks asymmetry in as a concrete plot object — some containers are trivially fair and some are impossible, and the character notices the difference without understanding why.
- I want the can't-count-it idea to be the dread underneath the whole thing. A collection that is obviously not finished, that you could never finish, and that still can't be put in a sequence. If you can make that feel like a haunted house rather than a lecture, that's the win. Optional extra chill if it helps: the version where the collection can't even be split into two endless halves, so any division is lopsided no matter how hard you try. My aunt and I are living that one.
- Give both endings their due: either the character finds a rule that can be said out loud, or they take the thing without one and it costs them something. Pick one, but let the reader see what the other would have cost.
- Keep it grounded. Kitchen tables, dust, cardboard, someone who's tired and hasn't eaten properly. No cosmic sci-fi, no AI, no talking numbers, no dream sequences that turn out to be dreams.

Basically I want to read something that helps me feel like my aunt and I aren't just being stubborn at each other, but are operating inside two different ideas of what a fair division even is — and that one of those ideas might require more than the world actually contains.
