I am a second-year graduate student in a philosophy department that this year is co-running a seminar with the machine learning group on the metaphysics of computational systems, and for the past month we have been working through the question of whether trained neural networks really have representations in the sense that interpretability research presupposes. My term paper is supposed to stake out a position, but in seminar the two camps I care about have kept talking past one another: the people who think a feature is a genuine property of a trained system and the people who think feature talk is an instrument for predicting interventions, with nothing answering to it beyond that. Before I commit, I would like to see the disagreement laid out as an actual exchange rather than as a pair of summary theses. So please write a dialogue between two interlocutors, each a sophisticated and wholly committed proponent of one side, who have been given the same case file and are trying to work out what it establishes. Give them ordinary names and let each speak in their own register, but do not attribute either position to any real researcher, and do not cite or allude to specific papers by title; if a character wants to invoke a well-known argument, they should describe it.

The case file they are arguing over is this. A mid-sized transformer language model has been studied independently by two interpretability groups working on the same layer of the residual stream. The first group, using a strongly sparsity-constrained decomposition, isolates a single narrow, reliably firing direction which they gloss as *asserts that a historical date is approximate*, and which their patching experiments show to be causally implicated in the model's downstream hedging behaviour. The second group, using a different regularization regime and a different corpus slice, reports instead two directions — *invokes a calendrical system* and *source-quoted rather than authorial register* — and their patching experiments show each to be separately causally implicated, with the hedging behaviour recovered only when both are present. Neither account is a linear redescription of the other: the directions cross-cut, so that there are inputs on which the first group's direction fires and neither of the second group's does, and inputs where the reverse holds. Both accounts pass the standard causal tests, including a battery of held-out interventions designed by a third group with no stake in either. The model is, in short, doubly decomposable: one behaviour is supported by two non-coextensive sparse factorizations, each of them sparse, each of them monosemantic by the field's own criteria, and each independently sufficient under intervention.

I would like the two characters to have real resources, not just intuitions. The realist should be able to press the thought that at most one of the decompositions is the correct one, and to cash that out by appeal to something like an objective comparative naturalness among properties, drawing on the family of arguments that hold that reference is determinate because some candidate referents are more eligible than others; they should also be able to say that the winning decomposition being fixed by the training run and the optimizer rather than by our interests is exactly what makes it a fact, and that realism was never the claim that features are determined a priori by the data. The anti-realist should be able to reply that naturalness, in this setting, can only be operationalized by the methodological criteria the two groups already both satisfied — sparsity, causal stability, breadth of counterfactual support, intelligibility to us — so that the notion either collapses into those criteria or becomes unmeasurable and idle; and they should be able to press the further point that the appeal to the latent structure of the training distribution cannot break the tie, because a text corpus is a mixture of many generative processes rather than one, so there is no unique set of latent variables for the model to have tracked. Have them attempt to design a discriminating experiment, fail, and then diagnose explicitly whether their remaining disagreement is substantive or merely a contest over the word *feature* — though I do not want the easy deflation in which they simply agree that everything turns on definitions; if that is where they land, they must explain why that concession does not end the debate. Please keep it to roughly fifteen hundred words with at least eight exchanges each way, use no more than a sentence or two of scene-setting, give each side at least one point the other has to concede, and do not let either character get a closing speech that resolves the matter.
