My debate club has been fighting about money pumps for two months and we've gotten nowhere, so I want a diagnosis of the argument instead of another round of it. Setup: an agent prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. They start out holding C. I offer to swap in B for C plus a dollar, they say yes; then A for B plus a dollar, yes; then C for A plus a dollar, yes — they're back where they started with $3 less, and I can keep running it forever. Half the club says that's a proof that intransitive preferences are irrational, period. The other half says no, because a real person can just refuse the trades or plan ahead ("I'm not doing this sequence"), so the pump only bites on an agent who's idealized into deciding each trade separately with no plan and no memory of the ones coming. First half says thats cheating: choosing to commit to a plan is itself a choice, and the only reason to make it is the pump, so transitivity just sneaks in the back door.

My hypothesis: they're not really disagreeing about the pump. They're disagreeing about whether rationality grades a set of preferences at a moment or grades a person's choices over time, and about whether an agent forced to decide one trade at a time is even a coherent thing to evaluate. Is that right, or is there a substantive point one side has that the other hasn't answered?
