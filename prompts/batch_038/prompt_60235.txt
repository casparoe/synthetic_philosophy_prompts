I sit on my state judicial conference's committee that screens proposed model jury instructions and expert-evidence guidance, and in six weeks we vote on a petition to adopt a standing rule that "readiness potential" testimony is categorically inadmissible in criminal trials. The petition grew out of a real case: a guy convicted of aggravated assault whose defense expert testified that scalp EEG shows a motor buildup beginning roughly half a second before a movement while the subject reports the urge to move only about 200 milliseconds before it, so on that picture the punch was already getting underway in the defendant's brain before he was aware of choosing it. The state's response was that the paradigm is about people flexing a wrist whenever they feel like it while watching a lab clock, and that it says nothing about a fifteen-second barroom sequence involving a shove, a decision to come back, and a swing; they also pushed the accumulator line, that if the buildup is just noisy drift toward a threshold then averaging backwards from the movement manufactures the early-onset picture and there was never an unconscious decision there to find.

We're split two-two and I'm the tiebreak, so I have to circulate something before the meeting. Draft the short opinion I'll send around.

A few things I need it to do. Land on something we could actually vote yes or no on — ban, admit with limits, or a third option like a narrow admissibility rule paired with a cautionary instruction — and say what the operative language should hinge on. Our committee works under an internal "specificity rule": expert testimony about a defendant's state of mind has to be capable of coming out differently for this defendant, so findings that would apply to literally every human being don't count as evidence about him. Take a position on whether the Libet literature could ever satisfy that rule, or whether the rule is itself doing some question-begging work here, and don't dodge the fact that Libet's own people mostly thought the result left room for a veto rather than proving determinism. Also give the strongest honest case for whichever side you don't pick — I've recieved enough 5-0 memos that read like briefs to know the difference. And please keep it about readiness-potential style evidence specifically: don't sweep in lesion, addiction, impulse-control or adolescent-brain stuff, which is a seperate petition we hear in the spring.

Format: exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, point 1 being the bottom-line recommendation. No preamble, no closing remarks, and no citations, case names or bibliography — I can't check them and the committee doesn't want one.
