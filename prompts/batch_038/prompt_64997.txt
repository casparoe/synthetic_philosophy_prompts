I teach on the civilian faculty of a national defense academy and sit on the committee that revises the ethics module in the staff course for career officers. The module is supposed to be continuous with the law the officers actually operate under, and it is precisely at the seam between the two that I have run into a difficulty I cannot resolve. I would like a careful reconstruction of what the surrounding practice assumes.

Four features of that practice are, I take it, uncontroversial:

1. Captured members of a regular armed force are detained rather than punished. A soldier on either side who kills enemy combatants within the rules may be held for the duration, but is not treated as a criminal for having done so, and is released on the same terms as his opponent.
2. The international crime of aggression reaches only persons in a position to direct or effectively control the political or military action of a state. A private who fights in a war of conquest commits no crime merely by fighting, and nobody proposes to charge him with one.
3. Atrocity liability, by contrast, is individual and perfectly symmetric: the same prohibitions and the same punishments apply to the aggressor's rifleman and the defender's.
4. The states that accept (2) and (3) nevertheless award medals for valor to their own soldiers for conduct in wars later widely judged unjust, and present the recipients publicly as moral exemplars rather than as people who got away with something.

Last year, at a colleague's insistence, we added a paragraph telling cadets that some enemy combatants they are legally entitled to kill are nonetheless wronged by being killed — that a conscript who has been told nothing, or who serves under severe duress, may be excused without being someone it is morally permissible to kill. This year a cadet asked the obvious follow-up: if killing such a person wrongs him, does that not generate at least a presumptive duty to avoid killing him? The module's answer, which I helped draft, is that in combat there is no way to sort the excused-and-not-liable from the responsible-and-liable; every uniformed opponent presents the same evidential situation, so the practical permission to treat them alike survives the underlying moral distinction. The cadet replied that this reduces the permission to a merely evidential one, with two consequences he took to be fatal: the practice never actually asserted the moral symmetry it is usually said to embody, and feature (4) becomes inexplicable, since we do not normally decorate people for conduct we regard as excused-but-wrong.

Please work through this step by step.

(a) Reconstruct the account of the combatant's moral position that the practice as a whole presupposes. Does it require that combatants on both sides be moral equals — equally permitted to fight, equally liable to be attacked — or is it fully compatible with an epistemic excuse story, with a reciprocity or rule-consequentialist rationale, or with a view on which liability attaches to a role rather than to individual responsibility? Say which of these the practice actually needs and which merely happen to fit it.

(b) Separate strict presupposition from congenial fit. Identify the points at which the practice is philosophically underdetermined, such that several incompatible theories would license exactly the same institutional behavior, and say what further evidence would discriminate among them.

(c) Judge the fog-of-war answer on its merits. Can an evidential permission bear the weight the module puts on it, or does it quietly reintroduce the equality view it was meant to replace? And is the cadet right that decoration practices are inconsistent with treating one's own soldiers as merely excused?

(d) Note what the liability-based alternative would have to say about our own side: if liability tracks responsibility for an unjust threat, then soldiers fighting an unjust war are not liable to be killed by their opponents, while opponents defending themselves are. Trace that through rules of engagement, prisoner treatment, the leadership-only structure of aggression liability, and the awards system. Is the resulting view implementable at all, or does it collapse back into something indistinguishable from the practice we have?

I am genuinely unsure whether the cadet has located a real contradiction or a confusion, and I would rather not have the question settled by which side of the contemporary debate one happens to find congenial.
