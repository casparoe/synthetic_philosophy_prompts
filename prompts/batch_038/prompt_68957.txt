Last Thursday I spent three hours arguing with a friend — she is a historian of science, I am the secretary of a prize committee — about a priority dispute we have to settle in June, and neither of us moved, so I want a third party. The foundation I work for awards a single prize every four years for one discovery, and its charter directs the committee to decide not whose work is better but who has priority; in twenty-five years the committee has accumulated a handful of rulings that I am now expected to apply consistently. Here they are, as recorded in the minutes. In 2001, two groups with no contact published the same catalytic mechanism nine days apart; the prize was split, the earlier-dated paper named first, and the minute gives no reason beyond the date. In 2006, a method had been described in full in a 1968 doctoral dissertation deposited in a national library and catalogued for consultation; the student's supervisor published it in a journal in 1971 and never credited her; the prize went to the student, on the stated ground that the dissertation was already in the world and dateable, with the added remark that the supervisor's silence counted against him. In 2011, a senior researcher described an effect in an unpublished lecture and in correspondence; a junior researcher who had been in the audience produced the missing derivation and published it three years later; the prize went to the junior researcher, the minute distinguishing ideas that merely circulate from ideas fixed in a record and noting that she had supplied what was absent. In 2016, one group's notebooks showed its work complete two years before its rival's, but it posted its preprint second; the prize was split with the first poster named first, the minute saying that private notebooks are not a disclosure. In 2019, a journal referee reproduced a submitted result with a graduate student and published it five weeks ahead of the original authors; the prize went to the original authors despite the later date, the minute adding that the record rule presupposes honest entry.

This year's case is uglier, and I suspect it breaks whichever rule the minutes have been circling. Group A, in Lisbon, posted a preprint on 3 March under the server's embargo option: title, abstract, authors and date were public from that day, the abstract asserting the effect and promising the mechanism in the full text, which became readable only on 3 September and which, when it appeared, gave the mechanism in a more general form than anyone else's. Group B, in Toronto, published the effect together with a complete mechanism in a journal on 12 June. B's corresponding author was a postdoc in A's group until 2023 and sat in A's internal meetings last autumn where A presented the draft; B maintains the mechanism is hers from her doctoral work and produces notebooks from 2023; A maintains those notebooks postdate the meetings and cannot prove otherwise, and neither can B. There is also a Group C: a 2021 technical report from an institute in Pune stated the effect, with no mechanism, on the institute's public server, was never indexed and never given an identifier, and the server was decommissioned in 2023 — a cached copy and one co-author's printout are all that survive, its author died last year, and the charter says nothing about posthumous awards. Finally, A sat on the national panel that read B's 2023 grant proposal, in which the mechanism was described; panel papers are sealed by rule; A denies having engaged with the science of that proposal; nobody on the committee has any evidence either way. One of our members keeps invoking Merton's claim that independent multiple discovery is the norm rather than the exception, as though that settled something. My friend says B must be named first: the earliest readable account of the whole result is B's June paper; the 2011 ruling shows the committee has never cared who heard an idea from whom; and acting on the sealed-panel suspicion would mean manufacturing a finding of misconduct out of nothing. I say A: the March posting was public and dateable in exactly the sense the 2006 minute cared about; the 2019 ruling exists precisely for people who acquire an advantage under a duty of confidence; and C's predicament shows we already treat a disclosure as a disclosure even once the copy has become hard to find — though I concede C cuts both ways.

Tell me what the committee should decide if it wants to be consistent with itself rather than with our intuitions: sole award or split, who is named first, and what, if anything, becomes of C. Then say which of the five rulings is the closest analog and which of us is stretching one, and lay out the candidate rules the record jointly permits — earliest readable full disclosure; earliest dateable public deposit of any part of the result; earliest disclosure that is still verifiable when the committee sits; whoever supplied the decisive step, with date breaking ties; a record rule defeated by breach of a confidential role — and identify which rule each of us is actually running, and at which point in this case two of those rules pick different winners. Where the minutes genuinely underdetermine the outcome, say plainly what principle we would be adopting by ruling each way: that embargoed metadata is itself a disclosure; that accessibility is judged as of the posting rather than as of the adjudication; that taint may be inferred from materials we are forbidden to read; that a claimant who cannot be consulted is no longer a claimant. And finish by describing one further dispute, of a kind our precedents have not yet met, that would settle which rule the committee has really been applying — I want to put it to my friend as a test case, since if we cannot agree about this one, we may at least agree about what would decide it.
