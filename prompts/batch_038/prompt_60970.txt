I've spent about six months helping a tenants' group in my city put together a policy platform, and somewhere along the way I realized my own thinking about price controls is a mess — I can't state anything that generates all my gut reactions. So here they are, in roughly the order I formed them. Rent stabilization in a genuinely tight urban market with no vacancy decontrol: bad — it rewards the incumbents, locks out everyone arriving now, and slows construction, and I'd oppose it even though half my friends would benefit. A cap on water, gasoline, and motel rooms during a hurricane: good, even though I know it means lines and empty shelves and even though I'd normally say the price spike is doing useful work there. A wage floor set low enough that it doesn't seem to cost jobs: good. The exact same wage floor, but lobbied for by a big chain that can absorb it and wants to jack up costs for the smaller competitors who can't: bad — the workers are affected identically as far as I can tell and yet I flip. Banning key money, the under-the-table payment a newcomer makes to the outgoing tenant of a stabilized unit: ban it, even though I can see the payment is basically restoring the price mechanism and getting the apartment to whoever values it most. Laws capping concert ticket resale: bad, which I admit is structurally the same case and I cant explain the difference.

More of them. A cap on payday loan interest: bad, I think it just pushes borrowers toward worse credit — but a cap on overdraft and late fees: good, even though those feel like the same intervention to me economically. Agricultural price supports that keep small dairy farms alive: bad, even though I like small dairy farms and would happily pay taxes to subsidize them directly instead. And a company town where the same firm is the dominant employer and the dominant landlord: a wage floor plus a rent cap there is not just acceptable but more clearly right than either one alone in a normal city. My tentative principle, such as it is, is that a price control is legitimate when it stops someone from using a chokepoint they didn't create to extract a ransom from people who have no exit, and illegitimate when its just people trying to duck a price they don't like. I can already see this struggles with the hurricane case, since those sellers didn't create the hurricane either, and I don't think it explains the dairy supports.

What I actually want is the principle, or a small handful of candidates stated precisely enough that I could apply them to a policy I haven't seen before without agonizing, with each clause tied to the case that motivated it. Then run it against everything above and tell me which of my verdicts I have to give up — I'd rather have a simple principle that breaks one or two of them than an epicycle bolted on for each, so don't save all nine if the cost is a mess. In particular settle the key-money/ticket-resale pair and the two halves of the credit case. And tell me what it says about one I haven't landed on: the group floated letting a stabilized tenant sell their lease to a newcomer at whatever the market will bear, with the city taxing forty percent of the sale — is that a reform, or does it turn a protection into a piece of property and make things worse? Then give me two or three further cases your principle delivers a confident verdict on that I wouldn't have predicted. Lead with the bottom line in your first sentence — the principle you'd defend or the verdict you'd drop — and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating my question back to me. Take a position rather than surveying the options and leaving me to choose. American English, please, and don't bother with sources or a reading list; I just want the argument.
