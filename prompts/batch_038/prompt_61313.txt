I am a third-year undergraduate on a seminar in Enlightenment aesthetics and political thought, and we have just covered Rousseau's 1758 letter to d'Alembert opposing the establishment of a theatre in Geneva. For a separate Shakespeare module I must apply one philosophical text to one dramatic sequence, and I want to read the Mousetrap material in Hamlet — the player's weeping recital and Hamlet's reaction to it in Act II scene ii, the performance itself in Act III scene ii, and Claudius's failed prayer in Act III scene iii — as a test case for Rousseau's central claim that plays can only please us as we already are and can never reform anybody's morals.

My difficulty is that the sequence seems to refute and to vindicate him simultaneously, and I cannot see how to hold both halves of that judgement together. Hamlet stages a performance expressly as an instrument of moral knowledge and action; it succeeds, in that Claudius's conscience is caught; yet the outcome is not amendment but a prayer that fails and a resolve to murder that hardens, which is roughly what Rousseau says spectacle does — it excites the passions it claims to purge. I have been calling the gap between what an audience spends in pity and what it subsequently does the 'Hecuba deficit', and Hamlet appears to diagnose that deficit in himself long before Rousseau names it.

Could you give me a reading that takes both the letter and the play seriously rather than using one to decorate the other? I would particularly like your view on four points: whether the dumb show's failure to move Claudius, where Lucianus's spoken lines do move him, raises a problem that Rousseau's account of spectacle cannot absorb; whether Hamlet's direction to the actors — temperance, the mirror held up to nature — is in fact closer to Rousseau's own ideal than his rhapsody over a player in tears for a fictional queen, and whether that inconsistency undercuts his use of the theatre as evidence; whether Rousseau could class the Mousetrap as one of his free public festivals in which the crowd is its own spectacle, or whether that move would simply be a way of saving his thesis; and where the reading must be abandoned, since an honest critic should concede something about anachronism, about the difference between a commercial Parisian playhouse and a Danish court, and about the fact that Hamlet's motive is evidential rather than moral.

Please open with your bottom line in two or three sentences — whether the sequence refutes, vindicates, or merely complicates Rousseau's thesis — and then give the reading, in no more than 900 words. Paraphrase rather than quoting at length, and write in British English.
