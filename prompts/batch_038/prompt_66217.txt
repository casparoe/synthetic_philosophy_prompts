I cover science and ideas for a national magazine, and I'm reporting a feature on how psychiatry sorts people into kinds. Before any of my sources' slogans go into print, I want the specification behind them.

You are a philosopher of psychiatry who has also sat in on diagnostic-review committees. Think the problem through first: list the demands actually made on a classification of mental disorder, then separate the ones that are genuine requirements from the ones that are the local loyalties of a particular school.

Now write the desiderata — what any adequate taxonomy must deliver. Which phenomena must it explain, which cases must it get right, which distinctions must it not collapse, which findings from genetics, epidemiology, and clinical practice can it not contradict? Give the case behind each entry, mark which are non-negotiable and which are negotiable, and name the single requirement that eliminates the most candidates.

One conflict I want adjudicated rather than merely noted. An epidemiologist told me a category ought to be stable under its own publicity: once it is widely known and widely used, its boundaries must not drift in ways that quietly invalidate the studies run with it. A patient advocate told me the reverse — that a category nobody takes up, that no one builds an identity, a community, or a claim for services around, has failed however clean its statistics are. Can both be satisfied? If not, which one gives, and what does conceding it do to the rest of your list?

Anchor the entries in two cases I can assume readers know: the career of PTSD since it entered the diagnostic manual, and what became of people who identified as having Asperger's syndrome once DSM-5 folded it into autism spectrum disorder.

Close with no more than a paragraph on how the three live options in my story fare against your list: the symptom-checklist categorical manual, the dimensional or spectrum alternative, and a research-criteria program that bypasses clinical categories altogether in favor of circuits, behaviors, and measures. Reasoning, not references — no bibliography.
