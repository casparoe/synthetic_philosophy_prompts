I'm 71, retired after thirty-eight years in a neurophysiology lab — mostly conduction velocities, some of it on patients — and about two years ago I started sitting in on a Tuesday-evening philosophy group that meets in the back room of the public library. It's run by a woman I'll call Ines. She was a neurologist, took early retirement, spent something like five years in a monastery, and came back reading Parfit alongside the Buddhist material. Her position is no-self, anatta, held with total confidence and, I increasingly suspect, with some internal slack in it. I want a second opinion before Tuesday because this week the case is mine.

Her track record on identity cases, written down as best I can remember it:

- Whole psychology intact, brain put into a fresh body: "him, obviously. The body is the vehicle."
- Total retrograde amnesia after an accident, but he still plays the piano the same way, still can't stand the smell of coffee, still finishes his wife's sentences: "Same person. Memory is a record, not the recorder."
- A man who goes to sleep a militant, cruel, funny atheist and wakes up after a small stroke devout, gentle, and unable to account for the change: "That's a different man. Not because of *what* changed. Because of *how*."
- A ninety-year-old who has drifted over forty years into someone unrecognizable — different politics, different friends, no interest in the work that made his name — but changed a shade at a time, every day linked to the day before: "Same person, and I don't see how you could say otherwise." Then, a minute later, unprompted: "Though I wouldn't hold the twenty-year-old answerable for the ninety-year-old's meanness, and I wouldn't hold the ninety-year-old to all of the twenty-year-old's promises."
- One psychology copied into two bodies: "Neither of them is him. Both have everything he'd want preserved. 'Which one' has no answer, and there's no third thing they're sharing."
- Irreversible coma, body maintained: "The person's gone. What's left is a body being kept warm."
- Asked flat out whether the one reborn is the one who died: "Neither the same nor another." And when someone called that a dodge: "It's only a dodge if you assume there's a something that has to be one or the other. You don't need a self to inherit a deed. You need a chain."

Now my case, which unfortunately isn't a thought experiment. My mentor — I'll call him B. — is 92. Vascular dementia, slow onset over about a decade. He remembers nothing of the field, doesn't know me, and his temperament has inverted: he was the most combative, vain, oddly generous man I ever worked for, and now he is placid and easily frightened. His son and his daughter are fighting over something B. insisted on twenty-odd years ago, that his lab notebooks go to one particular former student rather than to the university. The son's argument is that the man who made that promise is effectively dead, that nobody can bind a person who no longer exists, and that in any case the notebooks are worth more to science. The daughter's argument is that he is the same man and his word stands.

And here's the wrinkle that made me think of Ines's list. The student the notebooks were promised to — call her L. — worked under B. for six years and has, honestly, become him. She runs a lab his way, argues his way, has his contempt for sloppy controls, even his trick of going very quiet instead of shouting. She remembers a great deal of the work. She has none of his private history — nothing before she arrived, no childhood, no first marriage. So there are two candidate continuers of B.: a body whose mind has gone quiet, and a mind that has taken up his entire way of being without the body or the early memories.

What I want from you:

1. Which of the two does Ines call B., if either? Don't hedge at me — give me your best single prediction and the reasoning behind it, then tell me which of her seven precedents is doing the work.
2. On the practical question, who gets the notebooks under her own principles, and does the promise bind the man in the chair? Note that her two remarks in the fourth case point in opposite directions here and she has never had to choose between them.
3. Spell out the candidate rules that fit all seven precedents equally well. I've got maybe four in my head and I'm sure I'm missing some. Say what each one predicts about B. and L., and where they come apart.
4. Is any of this distinctively Buddhist, or is she a Parfitian with a meditation cushion? I want to know specifically what anattā is adding that psychological-continuity-plus-non-branching doesn't already deliver — if the answer is "nothing," say so and tell me whether her rebirth remark survives that verdict.
5. Give me one further case to put to her next Tuesday that would force the surviving rules apart. Not the mirror image of her amnesia case — memories perfectly preserved, character inverted. She'd answer that one too easily and it only sorts out two of my four rules. I want the case that makes her choose.

I should say where I'm coming from: I spent my career designing experiments that could actually fail, and I've noticed that Ines's list has never once been asked a question she couldn't answer. I'd like to know whether that's because she's right or because we haven't pushed.
