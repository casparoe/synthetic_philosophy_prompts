I cover psychology and ideas for a general-interest magazine — mostly the science-of-mind beat, sometimes the wellness-industrial complex. Two things have collided this year and I could use a second brain. First, I got talked onto the advisory board of a small nonprofit that runs resilience training for 911 dispatchers and some ICU nurses; they've been teaching a Stoic-flavored curriculum for about four years, built around the dichotomy of control, and they have a one-year internal review plus a modest budget for outside consultaiton, so the board is now arguing about what to actually spend that year on. Second, I'm writing a 6,000-word feature about the same curriculum, so I need to know which of these disputes would carry a piece and which would just be throat-clearing.

The fights Ive mapped so far, roughly: (1) whether the two-way split is exhaustive at all, or whether the popular three-way version — fully up to us / not up to us / partly up to us — is a genuine improvement or a corruption of Epictetus, who seems to reserve what's up to us for assent, desire and aversion rather than for actions out in the world; (2) whether that quarrel is philological luxury, i.e. whether it makes any difference at all in a training room; (3) the reserve clause Marcus Aurelius recommends — do you need it in order to aim at outcomes while holding them loosely, or is it a patch over a tension the three-way version was invented to paper over; (4) whether control is relative to how the event gets described — a dispatcher can't control whether the caller survives, but can control the description under which she acted, whether she did what protocol and judgment called for — and if so, whether the skill actually being trained is choosing the description, which starts to look a lot less like ancient ethics and a lot more like ordinary cognitive therapy with a toga on; (5) whether the whole internalism licenses political quietism and blame-shifting inside institutions (my editors' favorite angle, and I suspect it's overrated); (6) whether any of this survives stripping out the providential determinism the ancients attached to it, and if it does survive, what's left that deserves the name.

What I want isn't a survey. Rank these for the board's actual year — limited consultant hours, a program that has to keep running while they figure this out — and then rank them again for my feature, and tell me where the two orderings diverge and why. Be explicit about the criteria you're using (how much downstream practice would change if the question got settled; whether it's settleable by argument or needs data out of the training rooms; whether it's over-argued relative to its actual stakes) and about the trade-offs between them. Flag anything on my list that's overrated or that just doesn't follow, and tell me if I'm missing a dispute that outranks all six. Then name the single crux — the one question where, if it goes the other way, most of the rest of the redesign changes — and sketch the argument that would move a skeptical program designer who thinks philosophy is overhead. I'll be reading your answer on a plane with no wifi, so please dont hand me a bibliography or a to-read pile; I want the reasoning, not pointers. And if you think the honest verdict is that the philosophy here is basically inert decoration on a program that's really doing CBT, say so and argue it — I'd much rather have a committed take than a hedge.
