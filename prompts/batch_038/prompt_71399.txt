I am preparing a draft for a graduate workshop in formal philosophy, and I would like a set of comments on it before I circulate it. The topic is verisimilitude. I should say up front what I am not after: I do not want a tutorial on the history of the problem, a summary of the literature, or a balanced survey of the options. I want a reader's report — the sort a sharp referee would write — on the accuracy, the argumentative structure, and the prospects of the specific proposal sketched below. Please write in American English.

Some context for where the draft sits. My working assumption, which I take to be uncontroversial, is that Popper's original quantitative definition was demolished in 1974 by Tichý, Harris, and Miller, who showed that on his account of verisimilitude as the difference between the true and the false content of a theory, no false theory can outrank a true one; that the subsequent field split into the content-and-consequence tradition (Miller, Kuipers, and the relevant-element programs of Schurz and Weingartner and of Gemes) and the likeness tradition (Tichý, Hilpinen, Niiniluoto, Oddie), which takes seriously the idea that a proposition's nearness to the truth is a matter of the similarity between the actual world and the worlds in which it holds; and that the latter tradition faces a well-known embarrassment, namely that the ranking it delivers depends on how the state space is carved up and on which similarity metric is chosen, a sensitivity that has been sharpened by impossibility results in the same family as Zwart and Franssen's. My draft tries to exploit exactly that embarrassment. Here it is, in compressed form.

§1. The likeness tradition is usually charged with two sins: arbitrariness (the measure moves when you refine the language, so verisimilitude is not a property of a theory but of a theory-plus-a-vocabulary) and trivialization (if you get to choose the metric, you can get any ranking you like). I argue these are one sin wearing two masks, and that both trace to a suppressed premise: that verisimilitude is a two-place relation between a proposition and the way the world is. §2. Consider a court asked whether a driver exceeded the limit. Two reports are offered. Report A says the car was moving and that it was blue. Report B says the car was moving at 61 miles per hour in a 60-mile-per-hour zone, but also that the driver's great-aunt disliked turnips. A is entirely true and entirely unhelpful; B is false in its second conjunct but far better as an answer. Any question-independent likeness measure must weigh B's turnip error against its speed accuracy, and whether B beats A depends on how finely the language distinguishes turnip-related possibilities — which is a fact about the report's vocabulary, not about the road. §3. The proposal: verisimilitude is a three-place relation among a proposition, a question, and a world. A question fixes a partition of logical space into the cells that constitute possible complete answers; a metric is then not chosen at all but read off the question, because only distinctions the question forces are distinctions that can be gotten wrong. This gives a resolution test: refining the language beyond the demands of the question cannot change the ordering, which blocks the arbitrariness charge; and it blocks trivialization, because the theorist no longer gets to pick a metric — she gets to pick a question, and picking the question wrong is a substantive error about what is being asked, not a mere re-parameterization. §4. The obvious objections, which I address and which I suspect are not all equally damaging: (a) the view makes verisimilitude interest-relative and so gives up on it as a measure of anything objective; (b) it cannot handle the case that motivated Popper, since scientific theories are not answers to a single determinate question but to a shifting bundle of them; (c) once the weighting over cells is allowed to reflect what is at stake, the account collapses into expected accuracy, at which point verisimilitude is no longer a relation between a proposition and the world but a function of an agent's credences and utilities; (d) the view cannot deliver the pre-theoretic verdicts about numerical magnitude, such as a false estimate of 9 planets outranking a false estimate of 9 billion, unless the question already smuggles in a metric on magnitudes, in which case the metric has not been eliminated but hidden inside the question.

My requests, in order of importance. First, accuracy: tell me whether §1 misdescribes or unfairly compresses any of the three programs it names, and whether the diagnosis of arbitrariness and trivialization as a single underlying problem is one the literature has already made, since if so I would rather cite it than appear to have missed it. Second, structure: does the argument of §3 actually follow, or does it equivocate between a question understood as a set of alternatives and a question understood as a weighting over them? The turnip case in §2 is supposed to motivate the move rather than assume it — tell me honestly whether it does, or whether a referee would say the example is rigged because report A's uselessness is doing the work that the partition is claimed to do. Third, objections: pick the one you think is most serious among (a) through (d), say why, and tell me whether my instinct that (c) and (d) are more dangerous than (a) and (b) is right. I want a committed judgment here, not a ranking with caveats attached. Fourth, verdict: is the three-place thesis defensible as stated, or does the paper need to be narrowed — for instance, to a claim only about question-relative likeness measures rather than about verisimilitude as such? Take a position and give me your reasons. Finally, tell me what to cut: I suspect §2 can be halved and that one of the four objections is padding, and I would rather hear which than keep everything.
