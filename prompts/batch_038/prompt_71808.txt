Write an essay on whether the paradox of phenomenal judgment is a decisive objection to epiphenomenalism about phenomenal consciousness. The epiphenomenalist holds that conscious states are caused by neural states but exert no causal influence back on the physical world. The objection notes that our reports, beliefs, and philosophical arguments concerning consciousness are physical events whose causal histories are complete without any contribution from consciousness itself. If that is so, it looks either inexplicably lucky that these judgments are accurate, or else they are not accurate — in which case the epiphenomenalist's own case, built on zombie intuitions, knowledge arguments, and reports of ineffability, is undercut by her own view.

Rather than treating this as a single argument, disentangle at least three distinct problems that the objection runs together, and assess each on its own:

1. The reliability problem. Given that some neural state causes both the experience and the report about it, is the correlation between reports and experiences adequately explained by common causation, without any causal traffic from experience to report? What, if anything, does that common-cause reply leave unexplained?

2. The reference problem. Even granting the correlation, how did the concepts deployed in our judgments — concepts that purport to pick out phenomenal properties themselves rather than their neural correlates — acquire the content they have, if the properties they are about played no part in shaping their acquisition or use? Examine whether any candidate answer (acquaintance, non-causal constitution of the judgment by the experience, purely descriptive or role-based fixing of reference) succeeds without smuggling causal efficacy back in.

3. The self-undermining problem. Does epiphenomenalism deprive the arguments for epiphenomenalism of evidential force? Compare the structure of this worry with the identification problem Benacerraf posed for mathematical platonism, where the difficulty is that a causal account of knowledge cannot be satisfied by causally inert objects. Assess how strong the analogy is. If the two problems are instances of a single difficulty, does that make the objection to epiphenomenalism less forceful, because it indicts a broad class of otherwise respectable ontologies and rests on a contested causal epistemology; more forceful, because both are symptoms of a constraint any ontology must satisfy; or neither? Say which case has resources the other lacks — for instance, whether the platonist can appeal to structural roles or indispensability in a way the epiphenomenalist cannot, or whether the epiphenomenalist can appeal to first-person acquaintance in a way the platonist cannot.

Keep in view what is at stake. Epiphenomenalism is not a free-standing curiosity but the price many think is forced on one by combining a strong reading of the causal closure of the physical with the conceivability of zombies or the knowledge argument, so a verdict about the paradox has implications for how those arguments should be weighed. Your essay should say something about those implications.

Close with a clear, defended verdict. A qualified or conditional conclusion is acceptable — that the paradox defeats epiphenomenalism only in conjunction with some specific thesis about concept acquisition, say, or that it is decisive against claims about our reports and arguments while leaving the metaphysical thesis untouched, or that it is inert because it depends on a causal epistemology its proponents have no independent reason to accept — but the essay must not end by declaring the question simply open or by splitting the difference between the two sides.

Throughout, distinguish what is settled or close to consensus among philosophers working on these issues from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested position as though it were the default. Argue from the substance of the positions; no bibliography or citations are required.
