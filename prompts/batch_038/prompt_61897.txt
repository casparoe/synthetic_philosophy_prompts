I have spent the last three years reading and re-reading the exchange between Christopher Browning's *Ordinary Men: Reserve Police Battalion 101 and the Final Solution in Poland* (1992) and Daniel Goldhagen's *Hitler's Willing Executioners* (1996), together with Goldhagen's 1992 review of Browning and the selections from the April 1996 symposium at the United States Holocaust Research Institute. I am drafting a dissertation chapter on the structure of that dispute, and I have reached a point where I can no longer tell what kind of disagreement it is. I would like your diagnosis, not a verdict on which side is right.

The peculiarity I want you to work with is evidential overlap. Goldhagen's book devotes much of its attention to the same Reserve Police Battalion 101, drawing substantially on the same body of postwar trial and interrogation testimony that Browning used; and in his 1992 review Goldhagen explicitly accepted several of Browning's descriptive findings — that the killing in these operations was carried out by men who were not SS fanatics, that the commander offered his men the chance to step out, that fewer than fifteen of roughly five hundred initially did so, and that no German was seriously punished for refusing. Where he parted company was the interpretation: Browning explains the battalion's conduct through conformity to the group, deference to authority, careerism, routinization and the dehumanizing effects of prolonged brutality — generalizations he imports in part from experimental social psychology — while Goldhagen argues that these situational factors cannot do the work unless the actors already held a culturally transmitted, eliminationist conception of Jews, and that the absence of punishment for refusal plus the willingness to exceed orders shows the men acted on beliefs of their own. Both, in other words, accept the events and reject each other's account of them, and neither has conceded anything in three decades.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to test and, if you can, refute, is that this is not a factual dispute at all but a cluster of three different disputes wearing one name. First, a partially verbal one about "ordinary": Browning means ordinary in a sociological register (not SS, not fanatics, not demographically unusual), while Goldhagen and much of the reading public hear it in a normative and psychological register (like us, hence exculpable), and a good deal of the heat comes from the two senses sliding into each other. Second, a methodological one about what level a cause must be specified at to count as an explanation of intentional action — the psychology of individuals in a situation versus the beliefs of a collectivity — and about whether the laboratory-derived situational generalizations Browning leans on are even transferable to a historically particular case. Third, an expressive one about what may be said: situational accounts are heard as diluting responsibility, cultural accounts as essentializing a nation, so each side may be defending a claim about propriety rather than about truth.

Please tell me whether that decomposition holds, or whether there is a genuine causal disagreement underneath it that I am explaining away — for instance about whether antisemitic belief was necessary, or whether the situational factors were sufficient, given that the two sides seem to be making incompatible claims about sufficiency rather than about correlation. Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a distinction, a new class of evidence, a reformulation of the question, a way of testing the competing accounts against cases outside the battalion, or something else. If your conclusion is that no progress should be expected, I want the argument for that, including your view on whether the fact that this debate was effectively settled in newspapers, prize committees and sales figures rather than in the archives is itself evidence about its structure. Argue from what the two positions actually claim; please do not give me a survey of the surrounding literature, and do not append references or a reading list.
