I'm working through a popular book on rational choice, and one of its chapters uses the starving donkey — the animal placed exactly midway between two identical haystacks — to make a case I half agree with and half distrust. Its summary of the material runs roughly as follows. The thought experiment is usually credited to Buridan and was intended as a reductio of the idea that every choice requires a sufficient reason: a perfectly rational agent facing two exactly equal options has nothing favoring either, so it must either starve or pick at random, and anyone who finds the starving absurd is obliged to give up the principle that the will always follows the intellect's verdict about which option is better. The chapter then says modern decision theory dissolves the puzzle, because ties between outcomes are perfectly well behaved — an indifferent agent may adopt any of the tied actions, and where randomization is available, going left on a coin flip is simply what rational agents do under ties. Its conclusion is that choice without preference is not a failure of rationality but a species of it, and that whoever insists there must always be a reason to act is smuggling in a requirement no decision-theoretic framework actually imposes. Finally, it suggests the case feels paradoxical only because we covertly imagine the donkey as having a preference it cannot articulate; take exact indifference seriously and there is nothing left to explain — the animal just goes to one haystack.

I can't tell how much of this is right, and I don't want to accept or reject it wholesale. Could you take it claim by claim and say which parts a careful reader of the medieval debate would accept, which are common simplifications, and which are misrepresentations — in particular, whether the chapter has the direction of Buridan's own argument right, and whether "indifference between outcomes licenses any of the tied actions" together with "randomization is the rational response to a tie" are things decision theory actually says or things being read into it. I'd also like to know what a defender of the requirement that every choice have a reason would say in reply to the chapter's treatment of that requirement, since it seems to me the chapter dismisses the requirement rather than answering it. Then tell me whether the closing conclusion survives once the earlier claims are corrected, or whether it depended on them. For what it's worth, I found the last paragraph persuasive but kept stumbling on the coin flip: a donkey indifferent between the haystacks should be indifferent between flipping and not flipping, and it still has to settle that heads means left rather than right.
