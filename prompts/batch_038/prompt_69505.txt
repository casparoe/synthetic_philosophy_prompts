I sit on a city council labor subcommittee and Thursday we vote on whether to send a fair-workweek ordinance up to the full council. The draft requires two weeks advance notice of schedules, premium pay for any shift changed inside that window, no on-call shifts, and a right to decline a shift without retaliation — plus a hardship waiver handled by a seperate three-person commission that can grant it however it sees fit, with no written criteria. Half my colleagues think this is a worker-welfare question and want it argued that way. I've been pushing that the actual injury is domination: a manager who never once changes your shift still leaves you unable to look him in the eye, and that's what a lot of the public comment is inarticulately about.

Write the memo I'll circulate, with a clear recommendation — pass, kill, or pass with changes — and the specific changes if you go that route. I want it to run the case through Pettit's non-domination frame for real, including whether the waiver commission just relocates arbitrariness from the store manager to the city (dominium vs imperium, if that distinction is actually doing work here) and whether an unstructured waiver counts as domination even though it will interfere with almost nobody in practice. Then tell me where that frame and a straight welfare analysis give different answers and where they give the same one, because my LessWrong-poisoned instinct is that a framing which doesn't change the decision is decoration. And give the negative-liberty objection a real hearing instead of a strawman: nobody's interfering with anybody, workers can decline or quit, and the shift-swapping the on-call ban would kill is something workers told us they value. Don't recite Berlin at me, and say where the republican frame goes quiet rather than forcing it to speak.
