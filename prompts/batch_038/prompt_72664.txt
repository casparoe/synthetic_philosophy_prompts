I am seven years into a novel whose hinge event is a flood: during a storm, an autonomous spillway-management system, optimizing for the survival of a downstream city of four hundred thousand, opens a set of gates and drowns eleven people in a village whose habitation appears nowhere in its terrain data. The third act is a public inquiry, and the book's antagonist — I call her the antagonist, though I have come to think she is the only honest person in the room — is a philosopher retained by the families. She has an argument. I gave it to her, and it works far too well: it destroys more of the world than I need it to, and I want to know where it breaks before I let her say it under oath.

Here is the argument as she states it.

1. One is morally responsible for a harm only if the harm is one's own doing: only if what one did makes the difference between its occurring and its not occurring.
2. One is morally responsible for a harm only if one could reasonably have foreseen it — foreseeing either the harm itself or the carelessness that produced one's failure to foresee it.
3. The spillway decision issued from a learned procedure its authors can no longer fully explicate. No one — not the engineer who wrote the objective function, not the authority that procured the system and delegated the choice to it, not the duty operator who had ninety seconds and no grounds to override — foresaw that these eleven people would be drowned, or could reasonably have been expected to foresee it.
4. Nor did any of their acts make the difference. Had the engineer declined the commission, another would have written the same function; had the operator overridden, she would have been dismissed and replaced by someone who did not; the authority's delegation would have been executed by its successor under the same seal.
5. Therefore no individual is responsible for the eleven deaths.
6. A harm for which no one is responsible is a harm no one can be required to answer for: nothing to blame, nothing to repair, no one who owes the families a reckoning.
7. Nothing in the foregoing turns on the system's being a machine, or on its being autonomous. Any harm produced by a large distributed technical system answers to the same description — a rare drug reaction, an aircraft's failure mode, a bridge's fatigue, a clearing house's seizure. In each, no one foresaw this instance and no one's act made the difference.
8. Therefore no one is responsible for any harm that a modern technical system produces; and since nearly every serious harm in such a society is such a harm, responsibility for harm is an empty category, and the inquiry I am writing is a piece of theatre.

Step 8 is the problem. I cannot publish a novel that says it, and worse, two of my closest readers are structural engineers and a third litigates product cases; all three will smell the rat at once, though none has yet been able to tell me where it is. I have stared at the thing too long to trust my own diagnosis, so I want yours, and I want it as a verdict rather than a menu.

Tell me which single step to strike, if one will do it, and defend the choice: name the distinction the step trades on, explain why it looked unobjectionable to me when I wrote it, and price the repair. Specifically — if 3 survives, as I want it to, because it is the engine of the book and I believe it is true of this case — what has to give: the difference-making requirement in 1, the foresight requirement in 2, the replaceability reasoning in 4, or the move from 5 to 6? You should know my own prejudice, so that you can talk me out of it if it is wrong: I would rather weaken 1 than 2, since 2 is what makes her sympathetic and 1 is what makes her cold. And if you judge that the argument is valid and merely expensive — that 8 is honestly what 1 through 7 cost, and that the only question is which premise a reasonable person should be least reluctant to abandon — say so plainly, choose one, and tell me what she is then permitted to claim in the inquiry.
