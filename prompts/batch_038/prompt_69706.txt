I spent thirty years running population surveys for a fisheries agency, and since retiring I've been the lay member on a hospital ethics committee, mostly to have something to do with my time. I'm used to disputes that get settled by better data. This one didn't and I still don't know who was right.

The case: a woman wrote an advance directive at 62 saying that if she ever reached advanced dementia she was not to be fed by tube, and by hand only if she accepted food. She's now 81, in advanced dementia, doesn't recognise her daughters, but is placid and seems content, eats what's offered, and the family want to keep spoon-feeding her.

1. The doctor on the committee said the directive settles it: she was competent and specific, and the whole point of a directive is that the earlier self binds the later one. If present contentment can always override, no directive ever binds, since a settled dementia patient is nearly always content.
2. The social worker said the directive describes a life the woman predicted, not the life she got — she wrote it expecting misery and humiliation, and there isn't any. Following it now isn't respecting her values, it's enforcing a guess.
3. The doctor said her present behaviour isn't a preference at all: she can't form or revise one, so there's nothing there to respect. You'd be weighing a considered judgement against a reflex.
4. The social worker said that implies a person without capacity has no interests, which nobody accepts — we still spare such patients pain. And the "earlier self owns the whole life" line only works if its the same subject; plenty of people think the psychological continuity here is broken enough that it isn't, in which case the directive is instructions about somebody else.
5. The doctor said take that seriously and you get the absurd result that we just maximise pleasant moments for someone who would have despised that life, and her promises, relationships and accomplishments stop counting for anything.
6. The social worker said you can't have both: in step 1 you treated her as one continuing subject whose whole life is judged by the earlier self's standards, and in step 3 you said the subject actually here now has no will worth the name. If she's one person, her present contentment has to count for something; if she isn't, the directive is about a stranger.

Could you go through the six moves one at a time and say who's right at each, not just who wins overall? And does the dilemma in 6 actually land, or is there a coherent position that escapes it? Lastly, mark clearly which parts of this are settled among people who work on it and which are genuinely open — don't hand me a contested view dressed up as consensus. No citations needed, I couldn't chase them anyway.
