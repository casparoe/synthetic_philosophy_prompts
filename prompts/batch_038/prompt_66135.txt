Watching two people I know go at it over a potluck table last weekend and neither one budged, so I'm outsourcing the refereeing. Both have kids in the same midsized public high school district. Four years ago the district stopped computing class rank altogether. Since then the teachers have drifted hard toward giving A's — by Priya's estimate the average GPA in an honors section is around 3.9 now, the department heads all know it, and nobody's doing anything. Here's roughly the exchange, in order:

(1) Priya opened by calling it a textbook Moloch setup: nobody in the building actually wants a transcript where an A means nothing, but any single teacher who grades honestly has thier students penalized next to the inflated ones, so the honest teacher caves too and the thing ratchets. She said the only real fixes are a binding everyone-at-once pact or some outside anchor.

(2) Grant said it isn't a trap in the sense that word implies, for two reasons. First, grades aren't a fixed good — they're just noisy signals — so if everybody inflates, nobody's relatively worse off and the "race to the bottom" is a race nobody actually loses. Second, the can't-stop story is empirically false, because the district already proved it can kill a competitive structure overnight when it dropped class rank.

(3) Priya said the fixed good is obviously there, it's just not the grade, it's the admissions slots and the scholarship money, which are purely positional — and that inflation is worse than useless, because colleges recalibrate by school profile, so the school that inflates hardest gets discounted, meaning the strategy is rational for the individual teacher and self-defeating for the school, which she said is the whole defintion of the trap. On class rank she said Grant proved nothing: removing the ranking didn't remove the competition, colleges just rebuilt the ranking out of GPA, course rigor, and letters.

(4) Grant said fine, but then it isn't a trap, it's a selection environment, and that's a different animal with a different fix. The inflating teachers aren't being outcompeted by other teachers at all — they're being rated by students, parents, and administrators, and the ones who grade tough get the bad evaluations, lose the honors sections, and don't get renewed. So the pressure comes from the evaluation system, and calling it Moloch will make you reach for a teachers' pact, which is guaranteed to fail because a pact doesn't touch the thing doing the selecting.

(5) Priya said that's Moloch with extra steps: the evaluation system is just people responding to parents who are responding to colleges, so it's the same structure all the way up. She added that his distinction between "defection gets punished by the other players" and "non-defectors just get replaced" isn't a difference in kind, it's only a difference in whether the players are agents or slots.

Can you go through these five moves one at a time and tell me who's right about which specific point, rather than who wins overall? Then tell me which of the three structures it actually is — a mutual-defection dilemma where the other players punish you, a positional race for a fixed good, or a selection environment where the non-defectors get replaced rather than the defectors getting punished — and whether those three are really as separable as both of them are assuming. And finish with whatever the two of them are jointly missing that I didn't think to ask about.
