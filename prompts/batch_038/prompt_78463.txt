I'm a junior in high school, and this year my school lets students design an independent study in place of a fourth science elective; mine is on free will and machines, and it's supervised by a teacher who spent some years working as a software engineer before he went into teaching. In two weeks I have to give a twenty-minute presentation to the department, and rather than surveying positions I want to build the whole thing around a single case. For this response, please act as a professional philosopher of mind who is also genuinely hands-on with LLM-based agents — someone who has actually run an agent loop and watched it fail, not someone working only from descriptions of other people's runs — and please think carefully rather than reaching for the standard talking points on either side. The case is the spring 2023 wave of autonomous agent projects, above all AutoGPT, released at the very end of March 2023 by an independent developer and picked up almost immediately as the fastest-growing open-source project most people had seen, along with the similar task-list projects that appeared within weeks. It wrapped GPT-4 in a loop: the user supplied a name, a role, a main goal and a handful of sub-goals, and the program then browsed the web, wrote and read files, and kept re-prompting itself. What people actually remember is not what it accomplished but how it failed. It got stuck in loops, retrying the same subtask over and over, apparently unable to keep track of what it had already done, quietly running up API bills, and needing a human to sit there approving each step; one widely repeated diagnosis at the time was that the finite context window was what made it go off the rails. The mainstream press mostly covered stunts such as ChaosGPT, an instance given the goal of destroying humanity, which reportedly researched nuclear weapons and posted something unpleasant about people.

The lens I want to apply is sphexishness. The standard illustration is the digger wasp: it paralyzes its prey, drags it to the mouth of the burrow, leaves it outside while it goes in to inspect the nest; if you move the prey a few inches during the inspection, the wasp locates it, drags it back to the mouth, and then, because the routine has been reset, goes in to inspect the nest again — and the cycle can be repeated indefinitely. Philosophers, Dennett above all and Hofstadter as well, turned this into a term for behavior that looks purposive but is a fixed routine, and then into a premise in arguments about free will. Two complications I ran into while chasing the story down, which I would like you to take seriously rather than set aside: the account that entered the philosophy literature appears to have reached Dennett by way of Dean Wooldridge's 1963 book Machinery of the Brain rather than directly from Fabre's observations, and the philosopher Fred Keijzer has argued that real wasps do eventually adapt if you keep perturbing them and that re-inspecting a disturbed nest may be perfectly adaptive in any case — so the canonical exemplar may be as much a philosopher's folk tale as an ethological finding. Before the analysis itself, please give me a short brainstorming section of five or six bullets listing candidate ways to map the 2023 episode onto sphexishness, each with the reason that mapping might fail, and then commit to one. In doing the mapping, be explicit about the grain at which you are describing behavior, because my suspicion is that this is where the analogy quietly changes meaning. An AutoGPT run is fluent and locally novel at the level of every sentence and every step — each output differs from the last, each looks reasonable in isolation — while being circular at the level of the trajectory taken over ten minutes. Call that pattern fine-grained fluency over coarse-grained sphexishness. The wasp, by contrast, is rigid at every grain at which you look at it. Tell me whether sphexishness is a property of a mechanism or of a description of behavior; whether it requires the exceptionless, same-perturbation-same-response determinism the wasp supposedly has and a stochastic language model lacks; and whether a system can be sphexish while, unlike the wasp, being able to state its own goal, enumerate its own sub-goals, and explain on request why it is stuck.

Then run the analysis in both directions, and be honest about where the lens goes silent instead of forcing it to speak. In one direction: which features of the spring 2023 episode sphexishness genuinely explains; which it misdescribes — for instance, if the loops really were a memory and context-length failure, then the agent lacked an adequate internal model of its own recent history, which is a different diagnosis from having no model at all and executing a hardwired routine; and which it cannot reach at all, such as whether rigidity a system inherits from its training data, or from the scaffolding a human wrote around it, is rigidity that belongs to the system. In the other direction: what the episode does to sphexishness as a concept. Does an engineering failure of this kind vindicate it as a live diagnostic for machine behavior, expose it as an argument by analogy resting on an anecdote that entomologists dispute, or reveal that it was never really about wasps at all but about the worry that human flexibility is itself a stack of competences assembled with no comprehension anywhere inside? Finally, connect this to the free-will half of my project. The concept gets used to mark the absence of something that matters — evitability, self-control, responsiveness to reasons — and I want to know whether escaping a loop is even the right test. Consider that people perseverate as well: rechecking a locked door, repeating a self-defeating routine they can describe perfectly and sincerely say they want to stop, usually getting out of it only because someone else intervenes or the situation changes. If the 2023 agents resemble compulsive human rigidity more closely than they resemble the wasp, does that strengthen the inference from sphexishness to the absence of free will, weaken it, or show that the inference was never licensed to begin with?
