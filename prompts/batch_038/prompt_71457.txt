I sit on the elections working group of a professional association, and our charter prescribes a two-round rule: members submit a ranking of the shortlisted proposals, the two proposals receiving the most first-place votes advance, and the membership then votes between those two. I am not a social choice theorist — I learned the statement of the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem and built a spreadsheet to see how our rule actually behaves — and I have run into a configuration for which I cannot find a name.

Take forty members and three proposals: Ridge, Marsh, and Quarry.

- Sixteen rank Ridge first, Marsh second, Quarry third.
- Fourteen rank Quarry first, Marsh second, Ridge third.
- Ten rank Marsh first, Quarry second, Ridge third.

If everyone submits that ranking, Ridge (16) and Quarry (14) advance and Marsh (10) is eliminated; in the second round Quarry collects 24 votes to Ridge's 16 and wins. Honesty produces Quarry.

Now suppose four members of the first group do something perverse and rank the proposal they love best dead last, submitting Marsh first, Quarry second, Ridge third. The first-round counts become Quarry 14, Marsh 14, Ridge 12; Marsh and Quarry advance; and Marsh takes the second round, 26 to 14. The four have traded their worst outcome for their second choice.

Three features are what I want vocabulary for.

First, the gain exists only above a threshold. A single member who buries Ridge leaves Ridge 15, Quarry 14, Marsh 11, so Ridge and Quarry still advance and Quarry still wins, 25 to 15. Two members change nothing. Three leave Ridge and Marsh tied at 13 for the second slot, so the result turns on the tie-break. Four is the smallest number at which the move reliably pays. Done singly the act is pointless; done jointly it is decisive.

Second, at this profile no one can improve their own outcome by altering their own ballot while the other thirty-nine ballots stand — I checked all three types of voter. Honesty here is immune to any individual's temptation, and yet four people together can do better than honesty gives them.

Third, I am not certain the word for what those four do ought to be pejorative. Marsh beats Quarry one-on-one, 26 to 14, and Marsh beats Ridge, 24 to 16. The proposal that wins when everyone is honest therefore loses to the proposal the four install, and Ridge — the favorite of the four, the one they deliberately bury — loses to both of the others. The insincere ballot corrects the output rather than distorting it.

So my questions. Is there an established term for the thing captured by the first two features: a joint change of ballots that benefits everyone who makes it, benefits none of them if only one makes it, and coexists with an honest profile that is immune to individual defection? What does the definition require — must every member of the group gain, or is it enough that the group does better as a whole? Does it matter that the four movers' act also hands a better outcome to twenty-two members who did not move, among them the ten Marsh supporters, who gain without participating and are not penalized for not participating? I suspect this has something to do with thresholds and with the general difficulty of getting people to act in concert, but I cannot tell whether the field would classify it as a property of the rule, a property of the profile, or an instance of some broader trouble about collective action.

I would also like to know what the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem has to say about this case, if anything. My understanding is that it guarantees, for any rule with at least three alternatives that is not run by one voter and can reach every outcome, that there is some voter and some profile at which that voter gains by lying. If so, it seems to leave my profile untouched, since nobody gains individually there. Is that right? And is there a stronger property a rule would have to satisfy to rule configurations like mine out — and what is known about the cost of imposing it?

Separately: how does what my four members do relate to the more familiar kinds of insincere ranking — putting a viable compromise above your real favorite, or putting your favorite last to keep them out of a runoff? Is the ballot they submit a case of one of those, with the coalition-dependence an incidental detail, or is the coalition-dependence precisely what makes it a different kind of case? And is there a name for the positive property my profile has: honest voting that is self-enforcing person by person but not group by group?

Finally, does the third feature bear on the classification at all? I would like to know whether the literature separates insincere voting that exploits a rule from insincere voting that repairs its output, or whether the notion is defined purely by the misreporting and the gain, with the identity of the winner beside the point.

If there is no established term for the configuration as a whole, please say so plainly and propose one, with a definition that lets me decide further cases: whether a group of two suffices, whether the gain must accrue to every member of the group, whether the threshold structure is essential or incidental, and whether the honest profile must be immune to individual defection for the term to apply. A bare label will not help me. What I need is the carving — what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where its edges lie.
