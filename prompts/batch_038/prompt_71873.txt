Third week of my powers-and-dispositions reading group and one of us — I'll call her Dana — turned up with an argument she says refutes the whole conditional-analysis project, and I've been staring at it since and can't find the bad step. We'd just done finks and masks, so everyone was already primed to think the simple analysis (x is disposed to M when C iff x would M if C) is dead, and Dana's point is that Lewis's patched version — hold the intrinsic character fixed and the trouble disappears — doesn't rescue anything either, it just relocates the trouble. Her argument, in roughly her order:

1. A dispositional ascription says nothing over and above a counterfactual: x is disposed to M in C just in case x would M if it were in C. That's what makes dispositions the sort of thing that can be tested and argued about at all.
2. For any disposition whatever, you can describe a possible addition to the situation that leaves x's disposition intact while stopping the manifestation. Her examples: a lodestone sealed inside a superconducting shell, so its magnetization is untouched but no paperclip outside it is attracted; a tuning fork with a bead of putty pressed onto the tines; a match struck inside a nitrogen glovebox.
3. Call this the parity claim, which is Dana's own coinage: whatever is true of the lodestone in the shell is true of every disposition, because if dispositions were anything more than the counterfactuals in (1) there'd be something left over that stays fixed when you add the shell, and by (1) there is nothing left over.
4. So for every x, C and M: there is a possible situation in which x is disposed to M in C, is in C, and fails to M. Fine so far, that's just masking.
5. But a counterfactual of the form "x would M if C" is true only if it holds across the situations that count, and (4) says that for every situation you nominate there's a further one where it fails. Which situations count can't be settled by anything in x, since by (1) and (3) x contributes nothing but the counterfactual itself.
6. Nor can it be settled by the notion of interference, because an interference is just whatever prevents the manifestation, so the ceteris paribus clause gets its content from the very failure it's supposed to explain. Dana calls this the circularity of the clause.
7. Therefore the analysis in (1) is either false, or true of everything and so trivial — a brick is disposed to sing when struck, since in all the worlds we're allowed to keep in view nothing prevents it from singing.
8. Therefore dispositions aren't features of the world. They're bookkeeping devices for recording which background conditions we've decided to hold fixed, and the decision isn't answerable to anything.

My instinct is that (4) into (5) is where it breaks: (4) is a claim about what's metaphysically possible and (5) needs it to be a claim about which worlds are close enough to matter, and those aren't the same thing. But I know that's exactly what people said about finks too — the point of the electro-fink was supposed to be that the interfering world is a near one, not a far one — so if I lean on closeness I owe an account of why the superconducting shell is remote and the fink isn't, and Dana will just say im asserting it. Also (6) bugs me tho: I want to say the shell is identified as an interferer by its own physical description rather than by the failure to attract, but I can't tell whether that isn't question-begging. So tell me which step to give up and what giving it up actually costs — I'd much rather keep (2) than keep (1), if that's a coherent preference, since I don't want to end up with powers as brute primitives we can only identify by what they do, which starts to look circular in a worse way than Dana's.
