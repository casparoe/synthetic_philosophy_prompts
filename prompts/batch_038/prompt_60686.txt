I am drafting the central chapter of my dissertation in political philosophy, and I would like the thesis stress-tested before I defend it.

The thesis is blunt: coercive military intervention whose aims include removing a sitting government is, as a class, morally impermissible, because as a class it leaves the targeted population worse off than non-intervention would have. My empirical warrant is the post-2003 record — Iraq, Libya after the 2011 protection mandate turned into a campaign for regime change, and Afghanistan across two decades — together with a structural claim I take to be doing real work: once a state commits air power and special forces to protect civilians, the mission is pulled toward decapitation of the regime doing the killing, so that protective intervention without regime change is not a stable category in practice.

I have already absorbed the standard counterexamples and set them aside. The Indian intervention in East Pakistan, the Vietnamese overthrow of the Khmer Rouge, and the Tanzanian removal of Amin each produced enormous humanitarian benefits, but in each case the benefit was a by-product of a state pursuing its own security interests, which does not tell us what an intervener acting as an intervener could achieve. I also classify the West African operations in Sierra Leone and Liberia as peacekeeping and the restoration of elected governments rather than as humanitarian regime change, and I am prepared to defend that line-drawing.

Against that background, I want a map of the thesis's exposure.

1. What would have to be true for it to be false? Please keep apart two failure modes I find are usually run together: the empirical generalization being wrong, and the inference from making people worse off to impermissibility being wrong.

2. Rank the resulting defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say for each whether it targets the class claim, the structural claim about mission creep, or the moral inference.

3. Which of these would a committed defender of my position concede as counting against it, and which would they dismiss — and is the dismissal principled or opportunistic?

4. Does the thesis risk anything at all? It is easy to absorb every apparent counterexample by reclassification: it was peacekeeping, it was geopolitics, it was occupation, it was not really humanitarian. Give me an honest verdict on whether my treatment of the 1970s cases has crossed into an immunizing strategy, and if it has, what a version of the thesis that could actually fail would have to give up.

5. What does the thesis predict that its rivals do not, and what would count as evidence for or against those predictions? I am especially worried about case selection: my sample may simply be the interventions that were worst chosen to begin with, and I want to know whether there is any way to test that other than trading anecdotes.

6. Which consequences is the thesis committed to that I would find hardest to accept? I suspect Syria is one of them. Tell me whether there are worse ones.

Finally, here is what would move me most: not a case where casualty estimates are merely contested, but one where an intervention met its own stated success conditions and where the population was demonstrably better off a generation on, by standards its own members would endorse. Tell me whether that is a standard my thesis can meet in principle — and whether it is a standard the policy of non-intervention could meet either.
