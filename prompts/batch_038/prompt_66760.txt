I am a fourth-year PhD student in the history and philosophy of science, and I sit as the graduate member of the Surgical Quality and Credentialing Committee at the teaching hospital where my department places its students. On Thursday the committee takes up the case of Dr. A., a pancreatic surgeon now in his eighteenth month with us. I have been asked to circulate a written opinion beforehand, and I would like help drafting it.

The facts before the committee:

- Dr. A. has performed fourteen pancreaticoduodenectomies here, with four in-hospital deaths (29%).
- The national registry we report to publishes a benchmark mortality of 2.8% for the procedure, and 4.5% for the subset done at high-volume referral centers.
- Our own institution's rate for this operation over the past eight years, pooling all surgeons, is 5.9%.
- Nine of Dr. A.'s fourteen patients were transfers from outside hospitals, seven had received neoadjuvant therapy, and five had documented arterial involvement. Our internal severity model, applied to those fourteen patients, predicts 2.1 deaths. That model was fitted on the national registry population, not on ours.
- Before joining us, Dr. A. spent eleven years at another academic center, where he performed 148 of these operations with two deaths (1.4%).
- The hospital actuary has fitted a hierarchical model with random effects for center and for surgeon. He reports a shrunken estimate of Dr. A.'s mortality of roughly 9% (credible interval 3% to 22%), and he told the committee this settles the disagreement, because the model weighs narrow and broad populations automatically: the degree of shrinkage is fixed by the ratio of between-surgeon to within-surgeon variance, so the data choose the comparison class instead of any of us having to.

Our bylaws require action when a surgeon's risk-adjusted mortality is significantly above expectation, but they never say expectation relative to which population, and they separately permit the committee to act on a surgeon's demonstrated performance. Two members are deadlocked over a methodological rule I recognized from my coursework: one insists we must use the narrowest class for which reliable statistics exist, the other insists that narrowing until the numbers cooperate is precisely how committees convict people, and points out that we could define a class — left-sided resections, weekday cases, surgeons trained at academic institutions — that makes Dr. A. look either exemplary or monstrous.

Separately, the consent subcommittee has asked us what mortality figure should go on the informed-consent form for Dr. A.'s next patient. Depending on which population you consult, the honest answer is 2.8%, 4.5%, 5.9%, about 15%, or 29%. Venn's consumptive Englishman crossed my mind when I read the request.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should run to roughly nine hundred words and be written for surgeons and hospital administrators rather than philosophers. It needs: a bottom-line recommendation with a time frame, chosen from the options actually available to us (no restriction; proctoring for a specified number of subsequent cases; restriction of the case mix he may accept; temporary withdrawal of privileges for this operation; re-review at a fixed date); criteria the committee could apply to the next surgeon who comes before it, so that this is not settled ad hoc; a serious engagement with the actuary's claim that shrinkage removes the need to choose a population, rather than a dismissal of it; an answer to the consent subcommittee; and an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side of your recommendation. Do not include citations, references, or a reading list — treat the figures above as given and as complete.
