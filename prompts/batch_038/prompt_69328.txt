A reading group I audit — mostly cosmologists, plus two philosophers — broke into an argument after a talk on eternal inflation, and I have never managed to work out who was right. I will call the two arguers N and R: N works on quantum foundations, and R is a graduate student writing on self-locating belief. The question was whether a cosmological model that predicts Boltzmann brains outnumber ordinary observers thereby disqualifies itself.

Both granted the same background, so please take it as given rather than litigating it. A universe with a phase that lasts forever has a nonzero temperature, and therefore a nonzero, exponentially suppressed rate for spontaneous fluctuations to assemble any configuration, including a briefly coherent brain complete with fabricated memories of an orderly past. Because smaller configurations are exponentially cheaper to fluctuate into existence than larger ones, an old Boltzmann-style fluctuation picture of our own region, applied to such a universe, produces vastly more of these stray observers than it produces observers like us who evolved in a genuinely ordered history.

Their exchange, as near to how it actually went as I can reconstruct:

1. R opened by saying that if the model is true, you are almost certainly one of the stray brains, and your memory of attending the talk, of reading the paper, and of following the argument just now is fiction. So the model cannot be believed: accepting it removes the grounds for accepting it.

2. N replied that this is not self-refutation but an ordinary empirical prediction that happens to come out false. The model puts nearly all of its weight on your having an incoherent, discontinuous stream of experience; you do not have one; that is a likelihood ratio against the model, and standard Bayesian reasoning handles it without any special epistemology.

3. R said the datum N is relying on is exactly what the model impeaches. You learn that your experience is coherent only by trusting the faculties the model says are malfunctioning, so you cannot use the output of that process as a test of a theory that says the process is broken.

4. N said that standard makes skepticism unavoidable. On it you also could not dismiss the hypothesis that the universe began five minutes ago with your memories already in place, or that you are dreaming, and yet we do dismiss those without embarrassment. The difference is that a fluctuation model makes a quantitative prediction you can check against what you observe, while the five-minute hypothesis predicts nothing except that everything will look normal.

5. R said the five-minute hypothesis does make a prediction, a crude one: that your experience will look like the product of a fabrication. And the reason N thinks the fluctuation model is checkable is that N is assuming his own experience is a fair sample of the experiences the model contains. That assumption is a self-locating principle, and it is doing all the work: without it, the model's containing more stray observers than evolved ones does not yet license the conclusion that you are probably a stray one.

6. N accepted the point and said it cut against R. Once the sampling principle is out in the open, the dispute is no longer about inflation at all; it is about whether to adopt a principle that is not part of the model, not confirmed by it, and considerably more controversial than the model it is being used to demolish. Drop the principle and the model predicts an enormous population of deceived observers without predicting that you belong to it.

7. R said N owes a principle too. Every observational prediction in cosmology is a prediction about what we should expect to see, and "we" is indexical; with no rule connecting the model's observer population to anyone's expectations, the model predicts nothing, so N cannot turn around and use its predictions to dismiss the brains. Either N has a principle and must defend it independently, or N has none and cosmology is silent.

They stopped there and neither budged.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each of the seven numbered items, say whether it succeeds, partly succeeds, or fails, and give the reasoning behind the assessment, not just the label. Then answer these specifically: does the likelihood-ratio reply in move 2 really absorb the objection in move 3, or does move 3 identify something it cannot reach; is N right in move 4 that a fluctuation model is testable in a way the five-minute hypothesis is not; and is R right in moves 5 and 7 that a self-locating principle is unavoidable and that N needs one as much as R does? Say who comes out ahead overall and why, and at what level you think the dispute should actually be settled — cosmology, probability theory, or the epistemology of testimony and memory. Finally, name at least one substantive issue that neither of them raised which would have changed the shape of the argument, and explain how.

Please write your answer in American English.
