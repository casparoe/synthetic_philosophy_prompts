I am seventeen and in Year 13, and for the past year I have volunteered two afternoons a week at a small local-history archive in a former mill town in the north of England. The archive is run by a charitable trust with one paid member of staff — an archivist who works twelve hours a week and signs off everything I do — a handful of volunteers, a considerable backlog, and about four linear metres of shelf space remaining. Most of what we hold concerns the town's largest employer, a cotton mill that closed in 1982, and almost all of it comes from the management side: board minute books, wage ledgers in which operatives appear as numbers rather than names, a disciplinary register, and photographs taken for the company newsletter. The workers' side survives in scraps — a union branch minute book covering only 1974 to 1979, a few letters, and a good deal that people remember but nobody wrote down.

Offers of material arrive roughly three or four times a month, and every week I also have to write catalogue descriptions for boxes accepted years ago by people who are no longer here. Neither task has any framework behind it. Please write me a decision procedure I can actually run, rather than an essay about why archives are not neutral.

Specifically, I would like:

1. An ordered set of questions to ask about each offer or each item awaiting description, with what counts as a sufficient answer at each stage, and a clear indication of which stages can end the procedure in a refusal — either refusing the material outright, or accepting it while recording that it cannot be used as evidence of something it does not evidence.

2. Some help with a distinction I only half understand. I have read that it matters where a silence enters the record: that there are several quite different points at which something can fail to reach a future reader — never being created in the first place, being created but not kept, being kept but not described or looked for, being described but not allowed to carry any weight in the story that is eventually told. Please reconstruct that distinction in a form a volunteer with no training can apply to a box on a table, and tell me which parts of it survive that simplification and which are quietly lost by it.

3. A short rule for the catalogue entry when the holding is silent: whether to record an absence at all, how to phrase it so that we assert the absence and not its cause, and what to do when the only available explanation would be a guess about why nothing was kept.

4. An explicit marking of which steps are judgements dressed up as procedure, and which questions must be handed to the archivist or the trustees because they concern values rather than facts — for instance, whether our obligations run primarily to the people the records are about, to the donors, or to future researchers, and what to do when those pull apart.

The constraints are these. I have about fifteen minutes per offer. The donor is often standing in front of me and is usually a bereaved relative who wants their relative to be shown to have mattered. The outcome has to be written onto a single sheet of A4 that the donor sees and keeps, in language they will not find insulting. I have no archival qualification, no legal training and no palaeography. The procedure must also say what to do when no answer can be obtained at all: no provenance, donor deceased, nobody left alive who can identify the people in a photograph.

Then please run the procedure on three cases:

(a) The private diaries and correspondence of the mill's last managing director, offered by his daughter, who is friendly with the trust's chairman and expects the diaries to form the centrepiece of next year's bicentenary exhibition.

(b) A family album of roughly two hundred unidentified photographs of mill interiors and of groups of workers outside the gates, offered on the condition that we also record an oral-history interview with the donor's mother, who is ninety-one and has advanced dementia; she sometimes names people who cannot have been present.

(c) A run of typed newsletters produced by a workers' action committee during the 1977 dispute, offered by a former member's son, in circumstances where the committee's own minutes are held by a national union archive two hours away under a seventy-five-year closure — so that anything we accept would be the only accessible trace of the committee's own voice.

At least one of these should come out in a way I will not like. In particular, the chairman has asked me to supply 'the workers' story' for the bicentenary from our existing holdings. If the honest result of the procedure is that we cannot supply that story from what we hold, and must not assemble it by inference from management records, then please let it say so plainly — and tell me how to put that in writing to someone who outranks me and controls the funding.

Finally, tell me what this procedure cannot do: where it will predictably misfire, what it will cost us in refused material or offended donors, and what a trained archivist would object to in it.
