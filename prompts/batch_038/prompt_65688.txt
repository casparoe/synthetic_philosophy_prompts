I co-teach a third-year option module on the history and historiography of mass atrocity — about 18 students, roughly half historians and half philosophy types who've done the situationism debate already. The week I'm prepping is the Browning/Goldhagen row: whether what explains the perpetrators is mostly the situation they were dropped into (obedience, peer pressure, careerism, the way the task was carved up) or mostly the culture and beliefs they brought with them, with character and with brutalisation-over-time standing as the third and fourth candidates.

Trouble is, every time I've run it as a straight discussion the seminar turns into picking a team. People arrive already loyal to one of the two books and read the evidence to fit, and the ones who'd say 'surely it's both' say it inside the first ninety seconds and then stop thinking.

So I want a sorting quiz — what is your actual theory of perpetrator behaviour — that they fill in before the session, and that sorts them by what they say about cases rather than by whose side they're on. Here's the spec:

Nine questions, all anchored in one invented scenario that you design for me: structurally similar to the historical ones but with no recognisable names, so nobody's allegiances get triggered. My instinct is a private security firm contracted to a mine in a made-up country, or a peacekeeping contingent, ordered to clear a settlement or hand a group of people over to a militia — but you pick. Then each question turns one dial on that base scenario rather than introducing a new case: whether stepping out costs you anything or nothing at all, whether everyone around you is doing it or half are refusing, how far down the chain the order comes, whether the men were screened for the right beliefs when they were hired, whether it's their first week or their eighth month, whether they can see and talk to the people they're acting on, whether the people who'll find out what you did are the same people who'll promote you. Invent your own dials, those are only prompts.

Two or three options per question, all of them things a thoughtful person might genuinely pick and none of them obviously the clever answer. No question may ask what a historian ought to say, and the names Browning, Goldhagen, Milgram, Zimbardo and Arendt mustn't appear anywhere in the quiz itself — only in the key.

Then the scoring key, on a seperate page: map answer patterns onto at least five leaves — situation, i.e. obedience and conformity; the cultural or ideological explanation, where the belief system does the work; character, i.e. these were the sorts of men who'd have done it anywhere; gradual brutalisation, where the explanation is what the doing did to them; and bureaucratic diffusion, where neither anyone's beliefs nor anyone's character carries any explanatory weight at all. Say how interactionism would show up in an answer pattern, and how you'd tell it apart from someone who just picked the middle option nine times out of laziness.

Also tell me which two leaves the quiz genuinely cannot seperate, and why — I suspect it's ideology and peer pressure, since in the real case they predicted the same behaviour and nothing in what the men actually did distinguishes them. If no question could ever pull them apart, say so plainly rather than bolting on a fake one.

And flag at least two combinations of answers the quiz makes available but that correspond to a position nobody actually defends. For each, say whether that cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive to hold, or merely because nobody's got round to it.

Write it so I can print it as-is: quiz, page break, key.
