I coordinate a Tuesday-evening adult seminar through our county library's continuing-education program, "Blame, Fault, and Fortune: Who Is Responsible for What?" Sixteen registered learners, mostly retirees and a few people retraining; it carries no credit, but I set an essay question each fortnight and mark all of them with a written rubric, because the group has told me the feedback is the reason they keep coming. My own training is not in history or philosophy — I've spent twenty-two years in freight logistics and read my way into this subject on my own — so I built the rubric last year with help from the writing centre at the community college and I follow it mechanically. Three essays came in this week against the same question and I want to mark them consistently, which means I need a careful line-by-line assessment of each before I start comparing them. Below is the question as I set it, the rubric, and the first of the three essays in full.

> **Essay 3 (600 words).** People have argued about "war guilt" for the First World War both before and after 1919, and again after 1961, when a German historian published an account of the imperial government's wartime aims based on that government's own records. In your essay, separate three questions the phrase tends to run together: (i) which states' decisions and actions causally contributed to a general European war breaking out in the summer of 1914; (ii) which agents, if any, are morally culpable for it; (iii) which parties were legally liable to bear its costs. Then answer this: was the bitter West German controversy of the 1960s primarily a dispute about (i), (ii), or (iii) — or was it a dispute about something none of the three captures? Say what turns on your answer.
>
> **Rubric (100 points):** 30 — the three questions are genuinely separated, not merely relabelled; 30 — accuracy and fair-mindedness about the historical and historiographical claims made; 30 — a committed answer to the final question, with reasons; 10 — organisation and prose. Bands: 90+ A, 80–89 B, 70–79 C, 60–69 D, below 60 F.

> **The essay submitted:** "The phrase 'war guilt' is doing at least three jobs, and most of the heat in the controversy comes from people sliding between them. First, the causal question: which decisions helped produce a general war in 1914? This admits of degrees. Russian mobilisation mattered, and Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia, and the rigidity of the alliance blocs, and French determination not to be caught alone again, and the assurance Berlin gave Vienna in early July that it would stand behind whatever Vienna chose to do. Answering this question requires praising or blaming no one; it is about what would have happened had some decision gone otherwise. Second, moral culpability, which is about agents: who decided, what did they know, what could they have done instead, what did they intend? Culpability does not scale with causal contribution. A state can make the larger difference and be the less blameworthy, if it acted out of fear and under constraint while another acted from calculation. Third, legal liability. The clause of the Treaty of Versailles that came to be called the war guilt clause was formally a statement about liability for loss and damage, and it served as the premise for the reparations claim; a version of the same clause went into the treaties with Germany's allies as well, which suggests the drafters were writing legal machinery rather than passing a moral verdict. Fischer's work belongs to the first two categories and not the third. Using the imperial government's own records of its war aims — above all the annexation programme drawn up in September 1914 — he argued that German policy had expansionist goals already in hand, and that the July Crisis was used to bring on a war the leadership believed Germany could win. His second book pushed the argument into domestic politics: it showed how saturated German society was with expansionist nationalism, and how the chancellor and the Kaiser were carried along by a public mood they could not control. If that is right, the responsibility was national rather than governmental — a whole society wanted what its leaders did — and it becomes hard to maintain that blaming Germany is really just blaming a dozen men in Berlin and Potsdam. So: was the 1960s controversy about causes, about culpability, or about liability? I think it was about culpability, and about something the three categories miss, which is the question of who or what 'Germany' is. When Fischer's critics accused him of betrayal, they were not disputing his documents; they were disputing whether a German may say that Germany willed a war. That is why the problem cannot be settled by adding up individuals. Collective blame is objectionable when it sweeps together those who wanted the war and those who opposed it, and it is unfair to people not yet born. Yet a war is not done by individuals at all: it is declared in a nation's name, fought by its institutions, paid for by its taxes. So the nation is the only thing that can be responsible for a war, even though it is the kind of thing that cannot be punished — which is presumably why the acknowledgements of responsibility made by Germany in later decades are received as meaningful rather than as nonsense. The nation persists, and responsibility persists with it. On this reading the Versailles clause was doing something none of my three categories describes. It was asking Germany, as a nation, to accept a standing verdict on itself, and the historian's documents could neither confirm nor refute that verdict; they could only make it harder to live with."

What I need back is a grade against the rubric — a number, a band, and points allocated across the four criteria — followed by the specific comments I could hand to the writer, each tied to a particular sentence or step rather than to the essay's general impression. Where you think a claim is wrong, say what the correct version is; where you think a step does not follow from the one before it, identify the missing premise that would be needed to make it follow. Please also tell me how confident you are in each individual criticism, and flag any point where the historiography is itself contested, so that I do not mark this learner down for holding a defensible minority reading. Finally, single out the one element of your assessment you are least sure of and explain what would settle it; I cannot verify any of this myself, and I would rather leave a comment off a paper than write a confident correction that turns out to be wrong.
