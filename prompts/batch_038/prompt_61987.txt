I spent about 35 years at a national lab doing seismology, mostly inverse problems — trying to work out what's down there from what wobbles up here. I'm retired now and I've fallen into a rabbit hole I probably should have hit decades ago: the socialist calculation debate. Mises's original challenge, Lange's answer in the mid-thirties, Hayek's replies, Lerner, the later market-socialist stuff. What drives me up the wall is that everyone arguing about it is talking past everyone else, and the actual claim being made keeps sliding around without anybody noticing.

So here's what I'd like you to write: the joint statement that a committed Misesian — someone who holds the impossibility thesis, that without genuine exchange in the means of production there just aren't any prices to calculate with — and a committed Langean market socialist — someone who holds that a planning board setting parametric prices and adjusting them by trial and error can reproduce, and maybe improve on, the competitive solution — could both sign after a real adversarial collaboration. Not a dialogue, not a transcript, and not you refereeing who won. The constructive document they should have produced a century ago.

What I want in it:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides can honestly sign. There should be more of these than people expect, including claims about what the Walrasian apparatus actually does and doesn't show, and about what actually happened in the planned economies.

2. Then the residual disagreements, stated exactly, each one phrased so that its own side would accept the wording. No straw men, no slant.

3. Here's my main injection, and I think it's the thing that would untangle the whole mess. Force both camps to say which of these three claims they're actually making, because I've never seen anyone keep them apart:
   (a) the data don't exist in gathered-up form anywhere — the planner's input is not merely unknown but unknowable in articulable shape, an identifiability failure rather than a hard problem;
   (b) the data exist but can't be collected and processed on the relevant timescale — a cost and latency problem, the world moves while you're measuring it;
   (c) the problem is well posed and the data are in hand, but the adjustment process doesn't converge — no uniqueness or stability, so the iteration wanders instead of settling.
   Each side should locate itself on those letters, say whether it thinks the other side's thesis is even intelligible under a different letter, and admit which letter it has been quietly borrowing from the other camp.

4. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would actually move somebody. For each, give the case, argument, or piece of evidence both sides agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance, pre-registration style, so a reader can tell later whose predictions failed. Candidates I'd want them to wrestle with — use them if they work, explain why not if they don't:
   - Kantorovich doing linear programming inside the Soviet system in 1939 for a plywood trust and getting out dual multipliers. Is that a refutation of Mises, because socialist planners found prices after all? Or a confirmation, because those were accounting shadows that only meant anything with world-market prices sitting outside the system?
   - The free-rider point: a single socialist economy embedded in a capitalist world can borrow prices from abroad, so no actually-existing planned economy was ever the test case Mises described. Does the Langean get to use that? Does the Misesian get to use it to explain away every historical example, and if so is his thesis still falsifiable at all?
   - Kornai's soft budget constraint and the shortage economy. Both should be able to sign something about what happens to trial-and-error pricing when enterprises aren't allowed to fail, and it should cost somebody something.
   - A very large modern firm allocating internally by quantity targets and algorithmic systems rather than internal prices. Evidence that non-price coordination scales? Or evidence that it scales only because the boundary is priced by the market the firm sits inside?

5. Finally, tell me straight which parts of this hundred-year fight you now think were verbal. I suspect that whether calling something market socialism is self-contradictory is mostly a fight over the word market, but I could be wrong. If some of it is verbal, say which part and what survives once the vocabulary is pinned down. If you think none of it is, argue that.

Aim for roughly 1500 to 2000 words, tight enough that both parties could sign it without wincing. And one more thing: I'm a scientist, not an economist, so if any of my framing above is technically off — the stability point, the duality point, whatever — correct me briefly inline instead of quietly going along with it.
