My brother and I got into an argument last week about whether "well, both sides have a point" is ever a respectable move or always a cop-out, and it sent me down a rabbit hole on Indian philosophy, specifically the Jain stuff about many-sidedness. My partner's doing a masters in South Asian studies so I've been picking up bits at dinner, but I'm mostly an amateur here. Anyway, I found a short piece in a general-audience magazine that tries to explain the doctrine, and I cant quite tell how much of it to trust. This is my paraphrase of the middle section, pretty close to their wording but I've cut some and fixed the spelling of a few terms:

"The Jains held that reality is inexhaustibly many-sided, and that any sentence we utter catches only one facet of it. They called this anekantavada, the doctrine of non-one-sidedness, and they turned it into a discipline of speech: before you assert anything, you prefix it with syat, which means 'maybe' or 'probably.' So a Jain will never simply say the pot exists; he says that maybe, in some sense, the pot exists. From this they derived a table of seven possible answers to any question at all — that it is, that it is not, that it both is and is not, that it is indescribable, and the combinations — which amounts to a logic in which nothing is simply true or simply false. Their favourite illustration is the parable of the blind men and the elephant, each of whom touches one part and confidently describes the whole animal wrongly. The upshot is a relativism two thousand years ahead of the postmodernists: there are no facts, only perspectives, and anyone who claims to have grasped the whole truth has thereby proved he hasn't. It's a lovely ethic of intellectual humility, and it's also self-refuting in an interesting way, since the claim that no claim is wholly true is itself put forward wholly."

Two things itch at me. One is the "maybe/probably" gloss — I have a vague memory of someone insisting that syat isn't an expression of doubt at all, and that reading it as "perhaps" changes what the whole doctrine is supposed to be doing. The other is the relativism charge: my impression is that Jains are realists about an objective world and were perfectly happy telling Buddhists and Vedantins that they were flat-out wrong, which is hard to square with "no facts, only perspectives." Could you audit this for me? I'd like to know which bits a scholar of Indian philosophy would sign off on, which are harmless magazine simplifications you'd let slide, and which actually distort the doctrine — and for the distorting ones, what the doctrine says instead. In particular: is the sevenfold scheme a logic in the sense the article implies, or something else (a metaphysics? a rule about standpoints?), and what's going on with the "indescribable" entry; does the self-refutation charge land, or is there a standard reply; and how does anekantavada relate to nayavada and syadvada, since the article seems to mush all three into one thing. Also, separately, whether the blind-men story is really theirs to claim, and whether the "ethic of intellectual humility" framing is what classical Jain authors were up to or something more recent. And I'd be curious whether my own read — that this is basically an epistemology about how we know things — is even right, or whether I'm missing that it's making a claim about what objects are like. If it helps, end with a short paragraph you'd be willing to put in the magazine's place. No need for references or bibliography, I just want to be able to explain to my brother why the piece annoyed me, or admit that it shouldn't have.
