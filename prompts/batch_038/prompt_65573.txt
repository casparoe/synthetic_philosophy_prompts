So I help run a small interdisciplinary reading group at my university — about half physics grad students, half philosophy people, plus two guys from the meteorology department who have Opinions — and the first session next term is on determinism vs. predictability. Last year's opener was a straight summary of Laplace's demon and you could hear people's souls leaving their bodies. This year I want to hand out something funny instead, as the icebreaker, and I think a fake job posting is the right vehicle because everybody in the group has scrolled past a hundred of them and knows exactly how they read.

What I want: a parody job advertisement, written completely deadpan in real academic-HR voice, for something like a "Chief Prediction Officer" or "Senior Analyst, Deterministic Forecasting" at an institute you invent (pick a plausible-sounding name, don't make it obviously silly). Give it the sections a real posting has — About Us, Key Responsibilities, Essential Qualifications, Preferred Qualifications, What We Offer — plus a short paragraph on why the previous postholder left and an equal-opportunity statement at the bottom. Around 600 words, hard cap 700, and just give me the posting as it would appear on a job board, headline and all. No afterword explaining the jokes, no bibliography, no named real researchers living or dead.

The humour has to come from getting the content right, because there are dynamical-systems people in the room and they will wince. The target is the slide from "the laws are deterministic" to "so the future can be predicted" — the posting should treat those as two entirely separate job requirements and be visibly irritated that applicants keep confusing them. So build the qualifications out of real material: positive Lyapunov exponent versus randomness; the fact that better instruments only extend the forecast horizon logarithmically, so a millionfold gain in measurement precision buys you a handful of error-doubling times and the fortnight-ish ceiling on weather isn't an engineering failure; that the butterfly effect is not a claim about butterflies; when the shadowing lemma makes a numerically integrated trajectory worth anything and when it doesn't; that a bounded chaotic system is not "unstable" in the colloquial sense; a nod to those Newtonian setups where the equations are non-Lipschitz at a point so one state licenses two futures; and the Oedipus effect, where publishing the forecast changes the thing it was a forecast of. I'd also like one or two deep cuts — a line about the previous analyst being let go after an incident involving a rounded-off initial condition in an early-1960s rerun, maybe an interview question about whether quantum indeterminacy getting amplified by chaos counts as a feature or a liability — but keep those light enough that someone who never took a dynamics course still gets the surface joke and just smells that theres more underneath. Exaggerate features the field actually has; don't invent fake results or fake theorems.
