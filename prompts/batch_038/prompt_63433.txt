Consider a class of cases I will stipulate rather than illustrate, because the stock illustrations tend to smuggle in negligence. Call them **opaque-agency harm cases**. In such a case:

(a) a lawfully deployed autonomous system — say a triage-and-discharge tool operating across a public hospital network — produces an output that causes serious, identifiable harm to a particular patient;

(b) the specific causal pathway from the system's design, training data, and operating context to that output was not foreseen, and was not foreseeable, by any human involved, given the information available to them at deployment and given competent use of the best verification methods then in existence — thorough testing, formal verification where applicable, adversarial probing, staged rollout, live monitoring, and a functioning incident-reporting channel;

(c) as a result, nobody acted negligently, recklessly, in bad faith, or from any defective motive or settled vice, and nobody failed in a role obligation they had undertaken;

(d) the system has no experiences, no interests, and is not an appropriate target of resentment, indignation, or blame.

Now the claim I want a number on. Call it the **Residual Gap Thesis**: in at least some opaque-agency harm cases, the harm is one for which nobody is morally responsible and for which nobody can properly be called to answer in a fault-tracking sense — the absence of a responsible party is a real feature of the case, not an artifact of restricting our attention to individual human beings.

Give me your credence that the Residual Gap Thesis is true, derived rather than announced. Decompose it into the sub-questions you think actually carry the weight, assign a rough probability to each with a sentence of justification, and show me how you combine them — whether multiplicatively, as a weighted disjunction, or by some structure you should explain. Use my list only as a starting draft, and cut or add terms as you see fit:

1. That clause (b)–(c) can be jointly satisfied at all — i.e., that the case is genuinely possible once competence is stipulated, rather than covertly redescribing faultless-but-real negligence or plain bad luck.
2. That foresight or control over the specific harm is a necessary condition on being blameworthy for it.
3. That distributed attribution across many contributors (developers, validators, procurement officers, clinicians who overrode nothing) closes the gap in such cases rather than relocating it.
4. That collective entities — the firm, the hospital trust, the regulator — can bear fault-based responsibility over and above that of their members.
5. That no-fault compensation schemes plus institutional duties to investigate, disclose, and redesign satisfy the same moral demand that blame would, rather than meeting a practical need while leaving the fault question untouched.
6. That some non-experiential account of moral address could make the system itself an appropriate recipient of accountability practices.

Then tell me: which single term your final number is most sensitive to; which term you are least confident about; and what argument or piece of evidence would move the overall number furthest. I also want to know how much of your credence rides on conceptual or semantic issues — including whether my stipulations have made the thesis nearly analytic — as against substantive moral disagreement, and what you'd assign once that distinction is factored in.

Finally, locate a dispute. Suppose one philosopher puts their credence in the Residual Gap Thesis at 0.9 and another at 0.15, and both are competent and fully informed about the stipulations. Say which of the six terms (or which consideration outside the list) they most likely differ on, and why you think the divergence lives there rather than elsewhere.

Be committed throughout. Take a definite side, give me numbers even where you suspect the question is only verbally determinate — stating which reading you have adopted — and do not close with a balanced summary of the options. End by saying which of the two philosophers above you would bet on, and naming the one consideration that does most of the work in getting you there.
