I've been reading rationalist blogs and lurking on the alignment forums for something like fifteen years, and I go to a local AI-risk reading group maybe twice a month. Last week two of the regulars got into an argument I couldnt settle, and I've been chewing on it since, so I want your help adjudicating. Please take the role of a professional philosopher of AI who has also spent real time around ML research — someone who knows the technical vocabulary but mainly cares whether the concepts are doing any work. Think carefully. Before you hand down verdicts, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming what "one problem" versus "several problems" could even mean, because I suspect a chunk of their fight was over an ambiguity neither of them named.

The topic was whether the alignment problem is a single problem or a bundle of genuinely distinct ones. Owen said single. Devi said bundle. Here's roughly how it went, numbered so you can grade each move:

(1) Owen opened with a test. There's one problem: get a system to reliably pursue what we'd want it to pursue, on reflection, with full information. Everything on the standard list — outer alignment, inner alignment, corrigibility, mesa-optimizers, interpretability — is either a special case of that or a difficulty about *implementing* the solution, not a second problem. He said the list is a research-program taxonomy, an artifact of how the field is carved into teams and grants, not a taxonomy of problems. Then he offered what he called the collapse argument: suppose a demon hands you a training procedure guaranteed to produce a system whose terminal goal is a faithful encoding of what humanity-on-reflection wants. Corrigibility follows, because a system that wants that goal wants to be corrected when it's mistaken about the goal's content. Interpretability follows, because it wants us to be able to audit it, for the same reason. So the whole subproblem list collapses back into specification.

(2) Devi said the collapse argument trades on a confusion between having the right goal and being right about the world and about itself. The demon gives you faithful goals, not faithful beliefs, and not a faithful model of its own model. And the corrigibility step is backwards: a system that genuinely wants X, and believes that being switched off means X doesn't happen, resists being switched off. That isn't a wrinkle the oracle smooths over, that's exactly the phenomenon the corrigibility stuff is about. So to make the collapse go through you have to build in by hand the deference-under-uncertainty machinery that was supposed to be the separate problem. It doesn't dissolve the list, it re-derives it.

(3) Owen said fine — then it's still one problem, "reliably pursue what we want, including when you're uncertain and when we intervene," and her list is just that one problem chopped into work packages. He accused her of quietly moving: she started by insisting these were separate problems and ended by describing components of a single one. His line was that she'd conceded the problem is unified and was now just defending the org chart. He added a practical worry: the many-problems framing is actively harmful, because it lets people declare victory on whichever piece happens to be tractable and file the rest under "engineering."

(4) Devi said no, separate problems have a mark, and the mark is independent failure — you can succeed at one and fail at the other, and the two failures are not the same failure. Case A: you nail the objective, the specification really is what we want, and the trained system still ends up optimizing something else internally, because what got selected for wasn't what got written down. Specification solved, something else broke. Case B: the system has good goals and isn't deceptive, and we get hit anyway because nobody could tell in time what it was doing or why — nothing went wrong at the level of goals at all, the failure is epistemic. If both A and B are possible they aren't one problem, and relabelling doesn't make them one. She said his practical worry cuts the other way too: the unified framing lets people claim the whole thing is handled by whatever specification trick they personally like, and treats interpretability as decorative.

(5) Owen said then give me a criterion that doesn't explode. By "independent failure," making a safe car has separate problems of brakes, steering, tires, crumple zones, and a dozen more; nobody says a car is forty problems. He also said he thought she was smuggling in a category error — that some of her items aren't problems but constraints. Corrigibility, on his view, isn't something you solve, it's a property the solution has to have. She'd turned design constraints into research problems, which is why her list looked longer than the problem actually is.

(6) Devi accepted that the constraint/problem distinction is real but said it does him no good: a constraint you don't know how to satisfy is a problem in every sense that matters, and the interesting question is precisely whether you can meet the constraint without giving up everything else. She said the car analogy fails because with a car you can test the brakes and the steering separately on a Tuesday, whereas with a trained system you often can't test the thing you care about without having already solved it. Then she pushed somewhere new: the whole exchange had skipped the prior question of whether there is any such thing as what humanity-on-reflection-wants. If the plurality of human values doesn't collapse into one thing to specify, then his demon is being asked to encode something that doesn't exist, and the "one problem" is really a coordination problem among many principals wearing a specification problem's clothes. Owen said that's a different subject entirely — politics, value pluralism — not the alignment problem, and that folding it in is how people talk themselves into the field being impossible.

They both dug in and the meetup broke up. Neither would grant the other had scored zero, which is part of why I can't rank this myself.

So: go through 1–6 one at a time and say, for each, whether the move lands, half-lands, or misses, and why. Don't give me "both sides have a point" as the whole answer — commit, including on any move you think was flat-out wrong, and say which of the two argued better *given what they were actually claiming*, not given what a stronger version of their claim would have been. Then give me a verdict on the headline question and say who's more right overall. Then separately: what are both of them missing? I have a hunch that "is it one problem or several" isn't a single question and that they were answering different versions of it in the same words — if you agree, lay out the versions and say which one is worth arguing about and which one is just deflationary bookkeeping; if you think I'm wrong and there's a determinate answer, say that instead and give it. No bibliography, don't invent references, I'm not after a literature review — just the reasoning. And don't hedge it into mush. A clear verdict I can push back on is way more useful to me than a careful shrug.
