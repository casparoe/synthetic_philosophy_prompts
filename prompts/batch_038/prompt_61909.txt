**Authority Without Survival: Advance Directives and the Epistemic Position**

The literature on advance directives has settled into a familiar shape. A competent person records, at t1, that she does not wish to receive a specified treatment should she later reach a specified condition. At t2 she is in that condition, lacks the capacity to decide, and shows no sign of distress; she may seem contented. The question is whether t1 or t2 speaks for her. The dominant way of answering it is to ask whose interests are at stake. Dworkin's answer is that a person has both experiential interests, in how her life feels from the inside, and critical interests, in its being the life she judges worth having as a whole, and that the latter are what make a life good rather than merely agreeable; so the directive, expressing critical interests formed while she could form them, governs. Dresser's answer is that the patient in front of us has a welfare now, that it is the only welfare anyone can act upon, and that a document composed by someone who could not imagine her present condition cannot outweigh it. Both accept the framing and disagree about the weights.

My claim is that the framing conceals the difficulty rather than resolving it, and that once it is replaced several standard results come out in an unfamiliar order. What the directive settles is not whose interests count but who is in a position to answer the question the directive answers. Authority, on the account I want to defend, tracks epistemic position: the earlier self binds the later self not because it is numerically identical to it, nor because autonomy has some peculiar forward-reaching grip, but because at t1 the patient is the only person available who can evaluate a whole life prospectively, and at t2 nobody can.

By *diachronic evaluation* I mean the capacity to hold together in a single judgement what a life has been, what it is now, and what it will be, and to rank candidate outcomes by whether they belong in that life. That is what a directive-type decision requires. Dementia removes precisely that capacity while leaving intact a considerable capacity for present enjoyment. The later self's contentment is therefore real but beside the point: it is a good answer to a different question.

This yields a consequence I find uncomfortable but want to keep. If authority tracks epistemic position, the strength of a directive varies inversely with the evaluative capacity the patient retains — which is the reverse of the intuitive gradient. Most people, I think, feel that a directive is most troubling where it binds a patient who still seems to be there, and least troubling where it binds one who has wholly gone. On my account the first case is weak and the second strong, because in the first the person on the spot has inherited exactly the epistemic position the earlier self occupied, while in the second it is vacant.

The first case is meant to show the account working. A renal physician of fifty-eight writes a directive refusing dialysis and refusing treatment for intercurrent infection in advanced dementia, having nursed her own mother through both. At seventy-nine she presents with urosepsis and acute kidney injury; dementia is moderate to severe; she is comfortable, hums along to the radio, and recognises her husband intermittently. Her husband says: she wrote that in the month after her mother died; it was not a judgement about her own life, it was grief; and she is not her mother. Two questions arise, and they come apart. Was the document an exercise of diachronic evaluation, or the deposit of an emotion? And is anyone now in a better position to answer the question than she was? My account says the directive is at its strongest here, since her capacity for diachronic evaluation has largely gone; but it also says that the husband's point is relevant, and relevant at the point of issue rather than at t2 — not because she can now disagree, but because the document may never have been the kind of act it purports to be. I concede this forces an addition: a condition on the quality of the t1 deliberation. Whether that condition is about authority or merely about evidence is something I have not resolved.

The second case is meant to show the account biting. A woman of strong religious conviction refuses blood products, and adds a clause providing that the refusal is to hold even if she later loses her faith, since she takes that faith to be her truest self and any later loss of it to be a failure on her part rather than a change of mind. She survives, loses the faith entirely, keeps full capacity, and needs a transfusion. On my account her directive should count for far less than the nephrologist's: she is here, she occupies the epistemic position the earlier self occupied, and she disagrees with it. On the precedent-autonomy account, by contrast, hers is a paradigm case for enforcement, since the point of binding oneself is to protect constitutive commitments against one's own later drift. My account and the standard account therefore diverge most sharply exactly where the standard account is most confident. I suspect I should pay that cost, but I have not yet worked out how to argue it rather than assert it.

One asymmetry runs against me and I want to state it myself. Take the two errors. Error A: honour the directive, she dies, and it turns out the directive rested on a systematic misprediction of what the condition would be like from the inside — a tendency for which there is a good deal of evidence in the case of healthy people imagining disability or decline. Error B: override it, she lives, and it turns out the directive was right. These are not symmetric in the way the decision is structured. Error A is a single unrepeatable act against a subject who cannot afterwards be corrected, compensated or consulted; Error B is an act against a subject who persists, who can be asked again tomorrow, and whose situation admits of revision at every later point. That tells in favour of the reversible course, which is the course that overrides. I resist this only in part. Where diachronic capacity is retained, the asymmetry is decisive, and the directive should operate as a strong default that the present self may revoke without having to argue her way out of it. Where it is not, the asymmetry loses its purchase, because there is no longer anyone whose standing to re-decide is being preserved: the reversibility of a decision is worthless to a subject who cannot use it.

So authority transfers with the capacity for diachronic evaluation. It is not inherited through identity and it is not lost merely because values change; it is lost when the capacity to hold a whole life in view is lost, and only then does the earlier self get to decide. On a reductionist view of personal identity this has the further advantage that the account needs no metaphysics: it does not matter whether the t2 patient is the same person, only whether anyone present can do the evaluating.

What I want from you, in rough order of importance:

First, whether the central relocation of authority from identity to epistemic position is a substantive thesis or a redescription of the competence standard that already governs practice. If it is the latter, tell me what would have to be added to make it bite, and ideally describe a case in which my account and a straightforward competence-based standard come apart.

Second, whether the nephrologist case actually discriminates between my view and Dworkin's. My worry is that both give the same verdict, in which case the case is doing no work and I should either modify it so that it does or cut it.

Third, whether the faith case is a genuine counterexample. How much of the ordering in the fourth paragraph would I have to give up to accommodate it, and is there a route that keeps the ordering while treating the clause as void — for instance a claim that no one can make her own future evaluative capacity irrelevant in advance, which would need stating on its own rather than being left implicit?

Fourth, the objection I most want pressed: I define the question a directive answers as a whole-life question, which guarantees in advance that the earlier self is better placed to answer it. Is this question-begging, and if so where in the draft does the framing get smuggled in? Could it be repaired by allowing that some directives answer inside-the-life questions and others answer whole-life questions, and making the interpretation of the document the first step rather than the last?

Fifth, whether the reversibility section earns its place at all, and in particular whether the claim that reversibility is worthless to a subject who cannot use it is too quick. It reads to me like the sentence I most want someone to attack.

Sixth, whether the closing remark about needing no metaphysics is a genuine advantage or an evasion — on the reductionist picture, is there anything left for the earlier self's authority to consist in?
