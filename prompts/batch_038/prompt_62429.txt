I'm writing a seminar paper on origin essentialism and I've accidentally built an argument I can't live with. My supervisor thinks it's fine and that I should just accept the conclusion. I'd rather not. Here it is.

(1) Origin essentialism: if a person x actually came from a particular egg and a particular sperm, then necessarily, if x exists, x came from that egg and that sperm. Different gametes, different x — or no x at all.

(2) No orphan origins (his name for it, not a standard term): gametes aren't brute either. That egg is essentially the product of the particular meiotic division that actually produced it, in that body, on that occasion. There's no world where that very egg shows up by some other route.

(3) Chain them: if it's necessary that the egg came from that division, and necessary that the division came from the state of the ovary and the hormone levels and everything upstream of that, then in every world where I exist, the whole ancestral sequence running back to my conception went exactly as it actually went.

(4) So in every world where I exist, a big stretch of the actual past is nailed down — everything in the causal history of my conception.

(5) The past isn't compartmentalized, though. That division depended on my mother's health that month, which depended on what she ate and how much stress she was under and the state of the economy and the weather; keep pulling and you've pinned down essentially all of it.

(6) A possibility for me has to be a possibility in some world where I exist. Worlds in which I'm never conceived aren't worlds in which I could have done otherwise.

(7) So there's no world where I exist and any part of the past is different.

(8) If the past is fixed in every such world and the laws are fixed too, then everything downstream is fixed in every such world, including what I did this morning. I couldn't have done otherwise, and neither could anybody else.

(9) Origin essentialism therefore collapses into global necessitarianism, which makes the entire free will debate trivial. So (1) is false.

Where I'm stuck: I'd happily keep (1) and (2), and I think I could even keep (4). Its (5) that smells like a vibe to me rather than an argument, but when I say that he just asks what's supposed to stop the spreading, and I don't have an answer — everything does seem connected to everything. I also can't tell whether (6) is doing something illegitimate, because it sounds obviously true out loud and yet (7) somehow doesn't.

So: which step or steps do you reject, and why did each one look fine before you rejected it? Then price the exits for me — if I give up (1) instead, what do I lose that I wanted origin essentialism for in the first place, and likewise for (2), for (5), for (6)? And if the thing can be patched into a valid argument, tell me what extra premises the patch needs and which of them is the weakest link.
