For the past few months I have been trying to settle whether death is bad for the person who dies, and I have been going about it in the least disciplined way available: recording what I actually think about a succession of concrete cases rather than arguing from a theory. The result is a list that I cannot unify. My working formulation so far is comparative and person-affecting — a death is bad for someone to the extent that her life would have gone better had she gone on living — and it handles some of what follows and plainly fails the rest. I would like the list repaired or replaced.

To keep the comparisons clean, cases 1 through 6 concern a single imaginary person, a researcher.

1. She dies painlessly in her sleep at thirty, of an undetected aneurysm, with every reason to expect four more decades she would have found absorbing. Bad for her, and seriously bad.

2. Two people each lose exactly twenty years. One loses the years from twenty-five to forty-five, which would have been the best of her life. The other loses the years from eighty-five to one hundred and five, which would have been comfortable, uneventful, and mildly pleasant. The first loss is much worse. So what matters is not the number of years but the quality of the life forgone. Consistent with my working formulation.

3. Now compare losing twenty years each of which would have been just barely worth living with losing three years that would have been outstanding. The sum is larger in the first case, and my formulation therefore says the first loss is greater. My verdict says the three years are the greater loss. This does not look like an extension of case 2; it looks like a contradiction of it, and I cannot say what the difference is.

4. The thirty years before her birth were as much nonexistence as the thirty years after her death, and on the face of it as large a loss, but I do not think her life went worse for her because she was not also born in 1960. I accept the asymmetry and cannot defend it. The best I have is that whoever was born in 1960 would not have been her, whereas whoever would have gone on living would have been, and I suspect that is a trick of grammar rather than a principle.

5. She dies at thirty having just finished a manuscript. Forty years later a collaborator publishes it as his own and fabricates evidence of misconduct on her part; her name is ruined. I think she is made worse off by this, and that it happens to her when it happens, four decades after there is anyone for it to happen to. I also think the slander would matter less if it came five hundred years later instead of forty, which I cannot account for at all: if there is no causal contact, temporal distance ought to be irrelevant.

6. Two women die in their sleep at the same age in identical circumstances, with identical futures lost. One was murdered. I think the murder is worse for its victim, not merely worse morally, though I cannot locate the extra badness if she never knew and had no desire frustrated in any way she could have registered.

7. A man loses his memory, his projects, and his recognition of his family between seventy and seventy-eight, and dies at eighty-five. I think very little was lost at eighty-five and that the serious loss happened at seventy-eight, when the organism survived and the person did not. Here badness tracks psychological continuity and not the amount of future life remaining.

8. An infant dies at six months: no plans, no memory, no psychological connection to any future self, though sixty good years lay ahead. I think this is among the worst deaths there is, and at least as bad as the death of a thirty-year-old facing the same sixty years. Cases 7 and 8 are the pair I most want examined, because the discount I happily apply in 7 is exactly the discount I refuse in 8.

9. An endless life would not be worth wanting, and yet it would be a mistake for the thirty-year-old of case 1 to refuse five hundred guaranteed years of mild pleasantness. Each increment looks like a benefit and the total does not, and I have no stopping rule.

10. Dreading my own death seems to me misplaced, since there will be nothing it is like to be dead; being relieved at a misdiagnosis seems entirely appropriate; and so does the sense that my death would be a loss. I do not know whether these attitudes constrain the principle or are noise to be explained away.

What I would like back:

- One or a small number of candidate principles, stated precisely enough to take a possible death and yield both a verdict on whether it is bad for the person who dies and a rough magnitude comparable across cases, with the clause-by-clause motivation from my list.
- For each candidate, which of the ten verdicts it reproduces, which it cannot, and the single verdict you would tell me to abandon, with the argument for abandoning it. If two of my verdicts are simply incoherent together, say so directly rather than buying consistency with a clause whose only function is to fit the data.
- What each candidate says about three cases I have not listed: a person in irreversible coma from birth, apparently content, who dies at twenty; a woman who dies at eighty having asked that her letters be destroyed, whose family ignores her and publishes them to her posthumous embarrassment; and two thirty-year-olds who die now, one of whom would have had ten miserable years followed by forty good ones and the other fifty years of even contentment.
- One further case of your own devising on which the leading candidates deliver opposite verdicts, so that I can test my gut where the theories actually diverge.
- A verdict on my working formulation: whether it can be saved by the addition of a clause, and if so which, or whether it must be replaced outright.

I am not asking for a survey of the literature, for references, or for attributions; I want the principles in your own formulation and the reasoning behind them.
