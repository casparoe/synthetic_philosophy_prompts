I'm in a law-and-philosophy reading group that spent the last month on the Nuremberg and Tokyo tribunals, and I volunteered to take the prosecution side on the retroactivity objection — you know the one, nullum crimen, the complaint that convicting people of crimes against peace for starting a war in 1939 punishes them under a rule nobody could have looked up beforehand. I built what I thought was a clean answer, was pretty pleased with myself, and then reread it and realized it detonates on my own side. Here it is.

(1) The only thing wrong with retroactive punishment is that it defeats fair notice: you can't be blamed for violating a rule that wasn't available to you to guide yourself by. (2) So the notice objection bites only against a defendant who was in a position to use the law as a guide and would actually have used it. (3) The major defendants weren't. They drafted, suspended, reinterperted and ignored German law as it suited them, and they knew perfectly well that their orders would be treated as criminal by every other state and by treaties Germany had signed itself; nobody in that dock was consulting the legality of aggressive war as a practical constraint on what to do. (4) Therefore the novelty of the aggression charge is beside the point: novelty only injures someone who was relying on the old rule, and they weren't relying on anything. (5) But nothing in (1)–(4) mentions Nazis. By the same reasoning a head of state, a general counsel who writes the compliance memo that blesses the scheme, or a trader who routes deals through a jurisdiction that happens to have no rule covering them — none of them can object to being convicted under a rule announced afterwards, because none of them was relying on the law either. (6) Meanwhile the defendants who do get the protection of the legality principle turn out to be exactly the ones who were law-abiding and ignorant, i.e. the people with the least capacity to shape or evade the rules. (7) So nullum crimen isn't a constraint on punitive power at all; it's a shield for the weak and a discretionary instrument against the strong, wheeled out whenever a prosecuting power finds conduct outrageous enough to want to reach it. (8) And that is the victors'-justice thesis, stated in something like the terms its defenders state it in. I set out to answer the charge and instead supplied it with an argument.

So: which step do I give up, and what does giving it up cost? I'd much rather keep (3) — it seems to me these are precisely the defendants against whom a notice-based complaint is at its weakest, and I don't want to retreat to some generic principle that lands identically on a head of state and a shoplifter. If (3) is the one that has to go, say so and tell me what I lose. Two loose ends I can't resolve myself. First, (4) is doing work I'm not sure I can defend separately, since the aggression charge was novel in a specific sense: the interwar treaties that outlawed war came without any individual penalty attached, and at Tokyo one of the judges apparently dissented on exactly that ground and thought the whole peace-crimes case failed outright. Second, I vaguely remember reading that the submarine-warfare count against Dönitz didn't end up adding anything to his sentence because the defence produced something from an American admiral to the effect that the US Navy had been doing the same thing in the Pacific from the first day — and if I've got that roughly right, it makes me suspect the notice story in (1) is standing in for something else entirely that the tribunal was actually tracking. I can't say what that something else is, or whether naming it rescues the argument or buries it.
