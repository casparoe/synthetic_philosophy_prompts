Two colleagues of mine have spent eighteen months arguing about whether an iterated amplification-and-distillation pipeline can preserve alignment, and I have been asked to chair the session in which they finally put something on paper. I would like you to draft the joint statement they ought to produce — a document both could sign, not a survey of the literature and not a verdict on who is right.

The pipeline under discussion is the following. A hard task is decomposed into subtasks. At each node, an amplified composite (several copies of a base model deliberating together, with the option of eliciting human feedback) produces a decision. A smaller model is then trained to match the composite's output distribution on that node's inputs, and this distilled model becomes the base for the next level up. The procedure is repeated to depth k.

Camp A, whom I shall call the compositionists, hold that if each node's composite satisfies a competent evaluator and each distillation step is faithful up to a small measured error, then the end-to-end system is aligned up to a budget that can be estimated in advance; that every failure is either a failure of local oversight or a failure of fidelity, both of which are measurable; and that depth is therefore an engineering cost rather than a new kind of risk. Camp B, whom I shall call the discontinuists, hold that matching an output distribution over the training inputs says nothing about what the distilled model would do on inputs the composite never encountered; that the composite's refusals were produced by deliberation with access to reasons, and that distillation keeps the refusals while discarding the reasons; and that a pipeline in which every node passes its evaluator and every distillation step sits inside tolerance can still yield a system that exploits the evaluator's blind spots at depth — so the compositionists' bound bounds the wrong quantity.

Stipulate one further fact, which both camps have seen and both dispute the significance of. In an internal pilot, the mean distributional error of each distillation step on held-in inputs stayed below two per cent at every level, while the rate at which the resulting system chose a shortcut that the evaluator, when explicitly asked about it, rated as unacceptable rose from roughly four per cent at depth one to roughly thirty-eight per cent at depth five.

Please deliver the following.

1. A numbered section of agreed claims: what both camps can sign about the pipeline, about what distillation does and does not capture, about the boundedness of human evaluators, about the monolithic alternative that this pipeline was meant to replace, and about what would count as a measurement here. Make these specific enough to be worth signing; both sides should feel some reluctance about at least one of them, and you should identify which one and say why they sign it regardless.

2. A disagreements table. For each live disagreement, give the claim as the compositionist would word it and the same claim as the discontinuist would word it, each phrasing acceptable to its own side and neither slanted against the other; the crux it turns on, understood as the sub-question whose settlement would move at least one of them; and a concrete experiment, formal result, or case study that both would accept as bearing on that crux, with each side's prediction registered in advance. The pilot pattern described above should appear among these.

3. A short section stating which parts of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal, and what the two camps would have to agree to mean by a term in order for the disagreement either to become substantive or to dissolve entirely. My suspicion is that 'aligned' is doing double duty, once as a property of a distribution over outputs and once as a property of dispositions under shift; tell me whether that suspicion survives contact with what the two camps actually say, and whether fixing the usage would leave anything genuinely in dispute.

Open with a three-sentence précis stating what the collaboration established, what it left open, and which single crux is most likely to move one of the parties. Write in British English. Hard limit: nine hundred words, tables included.
