I'm putting together a position paper for our philosophy of religion workshop and want a sanity check before I circulate it. Here's roughly the package I've landed on:

1. Skeptical theism, more or less — we're nowhere near in a position to judge which evils were necessary for which goods, so sliding from "I can't see a reason" to "there is no reason" is just no good.
2. For moral evil I take the free will line: libertarian freedom is a good great enough to justify God permitting its misuse.
3. For natural evil I lean soul-making — a world with real resistance and real loss is the only kind that produces persons of any depth, so pain and disaster aren't merely tolerated, they're doing work.
4. Universalism. Everyone is eventually reconciled, nobody is finaly lost.
5. Heaven is what it's traditionally said to be: unending, undisturbed joy, no suffering, no moral risk.
6. Divine hiddenness is fine and to be expected — genuine free response to God needs some epistmic distance.
7. And about certain particular horrors I'm happy to say I can't make any sense of what good they serve; I'd call them gratuitous in the ordinary sense of the word.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? For the real ones, rank them by how bad they are, say what has to go or get weakened, and what the weakened version costs me argumentatively — I'd rather keep a defensible theodicy than keep all seven. Then tell me which single revision would clear the most ground. I've got a nagging feeling some of these are doing double work in incompatible ways but I can't put my finger on where.
