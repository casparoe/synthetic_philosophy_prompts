I'm putting together a handout for a grad seminar on infinite ethics and I want a dialogue that's pinned to one concrete case, so nobody can coast on generalities about the long run.

Here's the setup to build it around. A civilisation has just been *proved* permanently stable — continuation probability is exactly 1 at every future date, no extinction risk, no chance of collapse, so nobody can defend discounting as a stand-in for risk. Time runs in years, t = 1, 2, 3, … forever, and exactly one new person is born each year. The same people exist under every policy below (person born in year t is the same person either way), so nothing turns on the non-identity problem. Welfare is measured on a scale where 10 is a good life.

Two advisors are testifying before a committee about which policy to adopt:

- STEADY: everyone ever born gets welfare 10.
- SPIKE: the person born in year 1 gets welfare 10 + 1,000,000; everyone born in year 2 or later gets welfare 9.

Advisor V evaluates welfare streams with a constant discount factor δ < 1, weighting the person born in year t by δ^t. Advisor U holds that the only defensible criteria are undiscounted limit-based ones of the overtaking / catching-up family.

Partway through, the committee throws a third policy at both of them:

- PULSE: welfare 11 in year 1, 9 in years 2–3, 11 in years 4–7, 9 in years 8–15, and so on, blocks doubling in length and alternating.

What I need from the dialogue:

- Both advisors are equally sharp. No strawmen. V needs a real answer to the charge that δ-discounting will trade an eternal one-unit loss to infinitely many identifiable people for a single one-off jackpot; U needs a real answer to whatever PULSE does to their criterion.
- Make them do arithmetic at least once. V should get pushed to state exactly how close to 1 δ has to be before STEADY comes out ahead of SPIKE, and the number should be right. Likewise somebody should work out what the running total of the difference between PULSE and STEADY does as the blocks pile up, and both of them should react to that result rather than wave at it.
- Genuine back and fourth: each one answers the move the other just made. No alternating monologues, no "an interesting point, but consider…" padding.
- Nobody name-drops philosophers or lectures about the literature or the history of the debate. Keep it inside the case. If one of them wants to reach for bounded utilities or an ultrafilter-style fix, they can, but it has to come out of the argument.
- By the end they should have localized the disagreement to some specific commitment — an axiom, a completeness requirement, a stance on incomparability — and said honestly whether it's the kind of thing that could be settled. But no cheap conversions: neither gives up their view.

Around 900–1200 words.
