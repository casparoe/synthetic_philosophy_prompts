I'm on the benefits committee at my company (about 400 people) and we're redoing our parental leave policy, and the whole thing has me stuck in a way I can't quite articulate. Here's what we have now: 20 weeks paid at 80% of your own salary with a cap on the weekly amount, and all 20 weeks are a single family pot — either parent can take any chunk of it, in pieces if you want, but whatever you don't use just evaporates, there's no cashing it out. The proposal on the table for next quarter is to carve 6 of those weeks out and make them non-transferable, one block reserved for each parent, use it or loose it, plus add a flat $2,500 "baby bonus" paid as a lump sum that the family can spend on literally anything — daycare deposits, groceries, a used car, or just leave in the bank.

The thing bugging me is that these pieces seem to be built on different assumptions and I don't have the vocabulary for what those assumptions are. Like: the weeks can't be traded between parents or sold, but the money can be used however the family wants. If the point of both is the kid's wellbeing, why do we trust the family to decide about one and not the other? And if a parent doesn't use their reserved 6 weeks, the child doesn't get more care and the family doesn't get a penny — the value just disappears, nobody benefits. What kind of thinking makes that okay rather than obviously wasteful? Is it that we're assuming a father doing the care is worth something in itself even if the total amount of care in the house doesn't change? Then there's the 80%-of-your-own-salary part, which means a week taken by the higher earner costs the household more, so the reserved weeks being "the same for both parents" sit on top of a base that already tilts everything toward whoever earns less — and separately, the birth parent gets 4 extra weeks of physical recovery leave that the other parent can't touch at all, which I keep hearing described as us treating both parents as equal carers. We also credit pension contributions while someone's on leave, and if you're a contractor or between jobs you get a flat rate instead of a percentage. I don't know if any of that hangs together.

So what I want isn't a recommendation, it's a read on what this arrangement is quietly committed to. Can you reconstruct the theory underneath it — what it assumes a family is (one unit whose welfare adds up, or two separate individuals whose relationship the policy is actually aimed at), what it assumes care is worth (a means to a well-raised kid, or something good in itself), who it assumes gets to judge what's good for a child (the parents? the company? the state by proxy?), and what version of fairness it's using (equal totals or equal splits)? Then please separate out which of those the design strictly requires from ones that just happen to fit it, because my hunch is that a lot of it is underdetermined — you could tell several very different stories and get the same policy out. My own guess, for what it's worth: the reserved weeks assume parents can't be trusted to bargain fairly with each other and that someone has to force a particular split to happen instead of letting the household optimize, but maybe that's wrong and it's really just a practical thing — that nobody takes leave unless it's earmarked for them by name and they'll feel entitled to it. Tell me if my guess is even the right shape. And then: if you swapped in a rival view — say, one where you only care about total wellbeing and would just hand over cash and let the family spend it, or one where the household counts as a single person — what would concretely have to change in the design, and would the change actually be visible in the numbers or just in the paperwork? Last thing: is there any version of a scheme like this that isn't a patchwork of incompatible ideas, or is every parental leave policy basically a compromise between three or four things that can't all be true at once?
