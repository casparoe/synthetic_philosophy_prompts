I'm two-thirds through a novel and I've backed myself into a corner with a character.

She's Dutch, from Rotterdam, has lived in Kanazawa for fourteen years, married to a Japanese man, and she makes her living writing a monthly column for Dutch firms sending people over — basically she's paid to explain Dutch bluntness to Japanese readers and Japanese indirectness to Dutch ones. So she has opinions, and I've let her voice them in eight scenes already, and now I've written a ninth and I cant tell what she does in it.

Her own stated theory, laid out in chapter three: there are three things people lump together as lying. The flat one — you say something you believe is false. The angled one — you say something true, positioned so a false belief lands anyway. The borrowed one — you say nothing and let somebody keep a belief they'd have dropped if you'd spoken. She says only the flat one is ever wrong. The other two cost you something, but they aren't wrong.

Track record so far. Situation, then her verdict:

1. A colleague hands her a bad manuscript and says tell me straight, I can take it. She says the middle sags and the ending is the best thing he's written. Verdict: right, and not even interesting. He asked, he got.
2. Her mother-in-law's soup is bad. She says "it's a bit salty." Verdict: not wrong, and not a lie either — angled, and in that house the angle was the entire conversation.
3. A neighbor brings over pickles. She says "they're very different from what I'm used to." Neighbor beams. Verdict: not wrong. Nothing false came out of my mouth and she heard what she needed to hear.
4. The neighbor's seven-year-old shows her a drawing. It's bad. She says "you worked hard on the sky." Verdict: not wrong.
5. Husband asks whether she told his mother what he said about the funeral. She didn't — his sister did. She says "I didn't." Verdict: not wrong, but it costs.
6. The roofer who botched the job asks "so we're square?" She says "we're square." She is not. Verdict: wrong, flatly wrong. A flat one, to a man entitled to it, about the the one thing he needed.
7. Her sister asks whether she saw the woman her sister's husband was with. She had. She says "I didn't see anything." Her sister finds out six weeks later and is furious she wasn't told. Verdict: wrong.
8. Her husband's younger brother, before co-signing a loan with the husband, asks if there's anything about the first marriage he should know. There is. She answers the question he actually asked — did he ever hit you — with "no," which is true, and doesn't volunteer the rest. The brother finds out two years later and never quite forgives her. Verdict: the worst thing I've ever done, and I never said a false word.

So 8 blows up the chapter three theory and she knows it, and I have her sitting with that in chapter eleven without resolving it.

Now scene nine. Her sister is visiting — Dutch, blunt, jetlagged — in the kitchen of the house in Kanazawa, with the mother-in-law's hearing aid on the counter two rooms off. Sister says: "Is he actually happy here or is he being polite about it? Give it to me straight, I'd rather know." She knows, because her husband told her in confidence three weeks ago, that he wants out, and he's asked her to say nothing while the family is halfway through arrangements that would be wrecked. She says: "He's happy. You worry too much." Flat one. The first one she tells somebody who loves her in the whole book. Three months later the sister finds out anyway and — this part is already written and I don't want to change it — is privately relieved she wasn't told in that kitchen, because she would have detonated the family that weekend.

What I need from you:

- Her verdict on the kitchen. Wrong, not wrong, or wrong in some particular way — and the actual sentence she'd say out loud about it a year later, because I need dialogue and I don't trust my own ear here.
- The rule she's really running. Not the chapter three one. I want the candidate rules that survive all eight items, and then which of those diverge on the kitchen and which don't — because right now I can't tell whether this scene is actually a hard case for her or just feels hard to me.
- Which item is the closest analog. I assumed 7, and now I'm not sure; 6 and 8 both feel like they're carrying weight.
- If the eight items genuinely don't settle it, say so plainly and tell me what I'd be committing her to each way — what has to be true about her for the kitchen to come out wrong versus not wrong.
- One more scene, the smallest one you can build, that would decide it, plus what she'd have to say in it under each candidate rule, so I can check I'd recognize the difference on the page.

One note: please don't hand me tatemae/honne as the explanation. She uses those words in chapter three and then says she hates them and they explain nothing, and I mean that — it's the whole reason she has a job.
