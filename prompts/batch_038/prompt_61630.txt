I spent thirty-five years as a physical chemist measuring things, and in my own field every quarrel about a number was settled the same way: take more data, narrow the error bar. Since retiring I have been reading Keynes — the *Treatise on Probability* and the chapter of *The General Theory* on the state of long-term expectation — and last spring I served as the scientist on a national panel reviewing the safety case for a deep geological repository for spent fuel. The regulator required a probability distribution for radionuclide release on a ten-thousand-year horizon. A colleague, an engineer of strong philosophical tastes, blocked it with the remark that what we were being asked for was uncertainty, not risk, and that the two are not interconvertible. He said it again when our elicitation exercise returned 5th/50th/95th percentiles spanning four orders of magnitude — proof, he said, that the experts were only dressing up the absence of a probability — and a third time when someone proposed averaging those numbers to produce the deliverable.

I should like the sentence unpacked. What are the distinct claims that "this is uncertainty, not risk" could be making, arranged from the near-definitional to the most radical? For each, state precisely what it asserts — who or what is said to lack a number, whether the shortfall is contingent or permanent, and whether the failure is evidential, semantic, or metaphysical — then whether it is true or at least defensible, and whether it would in fact have licensed my colleague's refusal at each of the three points where he used it. I am particularly interested in which readings survive the observation that the elicitation did yield numbers, however wide, and in whether Keynes's notion of the weight of argument permits one to concede that a number exists and still decline to report it as decision-grade. Finally: which of these readings is Keynes's own, how does it differ from Knight's, and did my colleague borrow one man's vocabulary in order to reach the other's conclusion?

Please give me a numbered menu rather than a continuous essay, with a one-line verdict attached to each reading, and close by naming the reading you believe was actually doing the work at that table.
