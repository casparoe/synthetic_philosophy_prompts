I TA a graduate reading group called "Cooperation, Computation, and the Limits of Contract," and next week we close with the program-equilibrium literature — the setting in which the players in a one-shot game do not choose actions but instead submit programs, each of which is handed the other's source code and outputs an action for the base game. The session always goes the same way. Half the room are economists who arrive already convinced that this dissolves the prisoner's dilemma — hand over your code, condition your behaviour on theirs, and cooperation is sustainable in a one-shot game with no communication, no repetition, and no enforcement — and the other half spend the hour discovering, to visible dismay, that the price of all this trustless trust is an informational apparatus far more demanding than anything the mechanism was advertised as replacing. I get about fifteen minutes at the end, I will read something aloud rather than lecture, and I would like that something to be a piece of sustained satire rather than my usual summary slide.

What I want is a parody corporate policy document — call it a Source Code Disclosure and Mutual Conditional Cooperation Policy — issued by an invented and deliberately unglamorous firm (a cold-chain logistics operator, a specialty reinsurer, a regional clearing house; somewhere the stakes are boring enough that the absurdity can be purely structural). Roughly eight hundred words, formatted as a short preamble followed by numbered clauses in deadpan legalese, with the voice of a document that has been through Legal three times and is not interested in being funny. The humour has to come from getting the theory right and then stating its known weaknesses as administrative features, so please build the following beats into the clauses rather than around them: that the Policy's operation depends on everyone's code being common knowledge, and that this is treated as a filing requirement; that there is no such thing as a unilateral deviation, because a counterparty's program is a function of yours, so the firm must explain to employees why "acting on your own" is not a recognised category; a strict byte-for-byte identity clause under which cooperation obtains only between exact counterparts, together with the incident procedure for what happens when someone reformats a file or a build system inserts a trailing newline; a review clause requiring a formal proof that the counterparty's program will cooperate before cooperation may be extended, with a stated resource bound on proof length and a policy on what to do when the proof exists but is too long to find in time; a recursion-termination clause resolving every infinite regress of my-code-reads-your-code-reads-mine by defecting with small fixed probability, phrased as a prudent deadline-management measure; the exclusion of non-halting submissions and the designation of some office or officer as the authority on halting; an express statement that any feasible and individually rational outcome is fully compliant, mutual defection included, so that nothing in the Policy is to be read as encouraging cooperation; the problem of patches, promotions, and hotfixes, under which any change to an employee's own code invalidates every equilibrium they were party to and requires re-disclosure; and a closing self-attestation clause observing that no finite procedure can verify whether a given submission is a best response, and therefore certifying compliance on the employee's word. That last clause should function as the reductio — the reader should feel, without being told, that the apparatus has just reintroduced precisely the thing it existed to eliminate.

Two constraints. First, no real citations, no quotations from actual papers, and no named real researchers or living persons as targets; invent the firm, its officers, its arbitration body, and its revision history, and refer to the theory only in the plain descriptive terms sketched above. Second, and this matters: the target is the informational apparatus and the sales pitch, not the folk theorem. My advisor, who wrote her dissertation on folk theorems in repeated games, will be sitting in the room, and I want a piece she would laugh at rather than correct — so let the result that feasible and individually rational payoffs are attainable stand as stated and unimpeached, and aim the satire at everything that has to be true of the world for it to be usable. Finally, keep the surface legible: someone who has never heard the phrase "program equilibrium" should be able to enjoy it as a document about an organisation that has legislated itself into paralysis, with the second layer available to those who recognise the machinery. Please do not append a glossary, a list of the jokes, or any explanation of what is being ironised.
