I'm about two-thirds of the way through a novel whose narrator has a recurring dream, and the device is load-bearing enough that I've been stress-testing it. In the dream she's reading a letter — the same letter, same wording, every night — and partway through she deliberately wakes herself, jots down the sentence she stopped on, goes back to sleep, and the letter picks up exactly there, like a paused audiobook. My editor flagged it as possibly too cute and asked whether it's even the kind of thing that could happen, so I forwarded the question to two friends: Wes, a sleep medicine fellow, and June, an old roommate of mine who's finishing a philosophy PhD. It turned into a four-hour arguement about whether dreams are experiences at all, and I mostly sat there taking notes because each of them sounded convincing in turn. Here's roughly how it went.

(1) June said the device is incoherent from the start, on Malcolm's line about dreams: what makes it true that someone dreamed is the impression they have on waking, so a dream isn't an experience occurring during sleep but a present-tense impression plus a story — which means my narrator cant check on her dream mid-dream any more than I can fact-check a sentence before I write it. (2) Wes said that's pure word games, that he wakes people out of REM all the time and gets dense, detailed scene reports, and that recorded eye movements often track what the subject says she was looking at, so there's plainly something going on while the person is asleep. (3) June said the eye-movement data doesn't touch her point and gave the Dennett version: the narrative gets composed on demand at or near waking, the way someone under post-hypnotic suggestion invents a reason for what she just did, and the vividness of a report tells you nothing about how long an experience lasted. (4) Wes said then explain why people roused after a long REM period much more often call their dream long than people roused after a short one — if the story's improvised at the moment of waking, why does its length track the sleep stage it came out of? June said because the improviser has been busy for fifteen minutes and is drawing on whatever material is lying around, that a made-up story can be made long or short on cue, and that people are terrible at judging how long they dreamed anyway. (5) Wes said her view can't handle the letter dream: if the report is fabricated at waking, then my narrator's trick requires the fabrication to be saved somewhere and resumed, and a last-minute confabulation doesn't leave a file on the disk. June said the file is just the memory of what she reported the night before, so the continuity is a continuity of reports, not of scenes; Wes said then she ought to be able to feel the difference between remembering what she said and remembering what she saw, and June said that's exactly the discrimination people can't make in the dream case, which is Malcolm's point about the grammar of remembering a dream. (6) Then they went meta. June said the dispute is about the concept, not the brain — no electrode reading could settle whether what we call dreaming is an experience, because experience is doing grammatical work in the sentence. Wes said that's an immunizing move that makes her position untestable by construction and lets her wave off the whole research program that generated the vocabulary she's analyzing, and he pointed to patients with REM sleep behavior disorder who thrash around enacting what they later describe, and to people reporting dreams coming out of anesthesia, and said on her account all of that is a report of nothing. (7) June conceded there's obviously something happening during REM and said what she denies is narrower: that the something has the narrative shape and sensory fullness the morning report assigns to it. Wes said that's a surrender dressed as a clarification; she said no, it's the entire issue, because her claim is about whether a dream report describes a past event or performs a present one. (8) Somewhere in there Wes floated a third thing neither had been arguing for — that a dream might be neither an event nor a nothing but a state, like an itch or a low-grade mood, something you can be in the middle of and sample without ending, and that they were both trapped by the event/nothing dichotomy. June didn't take it seriously and I couldn't tell whether she should have.

So: go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step, not just who won the night — which of the eight are genuine replies, which are dodges, which are question-begging, and what both of them are missing, including whether Wes's state-not-event idea actually rescues anything or is just a relabeling. And as a kind of footnote: does my letter dream survive as written on either account, and if it doesn't, what's the smallest change to the device that would let it stand?
