I run a two-person consultancy that rewrites bylaws for member-run organizations — professional societies, housing co-ops, credit unions, faculty senates, small unions. Almost every engagement starts with the same question from the client: "so what voting method should we actually use?" and honestly right now my answer is "instant runoff, everyones heard of it, it's on the ballot-reform people's list." I know that isn't a real answer, and I know enough social choice theory to know I can't give one, but I need something better than either hand-waving or dumping Arrow's theorem on a room full of retired dentists and co-op treasurers.

So: write me a decision procedure I can run with a client to land on a voting rule for a specific office or decision. Ground it in what the theory actually establishes — what Arrow's theorem does and doesn't rule out, what Gibbard–Satterthwaite does and doesn't imply about strategic voting, which criteria are genuinely in tension versus which just feel like they are, and why the cardinal methods get to sidestep part of this — but the output has to be a sequence of questions I can ask in order in a two-hour workshop. For each question, tell me what counts as a good enough answer, what the branches are, where the procedure terminates in a recommendation, and where it stops and hands the choice back to the room because it's a values question rather than a technical one. Assume the clients are smart but have no math past high school, so every criterion the procedure turns on has to be phrasable as a concrete scenario they can react to ("if your favorite can't win, would you rather…"), not as a definition with a name attached.

Constraints on the situations it has to cover: group sizes from about 12 to 5,000; single-winner elections, yes/no votes with amendments, and rank-the-whole-slate decisions; a lot of these bodies have a chair with a casting vote, which I want the procedure to treat explicitly rather than pretend away; and several are small boards where everybody knows everybody else's preferences and strategic voting is not hypothetical. It should also have something to say about how many candidates are realistically going to run, since that changes everything.

Then run it on three cases:
(a) a 4,000-member professional society electing its president, plurality for decades, with a reform caucus pushing IRV and a fringe candidate everyone expects to siphon votes off the incumbent;
(b) an 11-person housing co-op board choosing among four sites for a new building, where the chair holds the casting vote and two members are openly trading support;
(c) a 300-person faculty senate that wants one rule to use both for officer elections and for ranking competing budget proposals, and insists the two uses be consistent with each other.

At least one of the three should come out with a recommendation I'd be embarrassed to deliver — I want to see the procedure actually bite instead of laundering my priors. And be explicit, step by step, about which parts are real implications of the theorems and which parts are judgment calls I'm dressing up as procedure. Finish by saying what the procedure can't do: which client demands are unsatisfiable no matter how its run, and what I should say to them when they make one.
