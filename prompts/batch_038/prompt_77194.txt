Analyze the retirement of Asperger's disorder as a distinct diagnosis — folded, along with autistic disorder and pervasive developmental disorder not otherwise specified, into a single autism spectrum disorder in DSM-5 in 2013, and similarly under ICD-11 — as a case study in Hacking's looping effects of human kinds. Begin with the mapping: what the DSM-IV category did to the people who received it, how their self-descriptions, communities, and advocacy then fed back into what the experts were classifying, and what the revision itself tells us about whether DSM-5 was responding to a kind that had changed under description or trying to interrupt the loop that had changed it. Say what to make of the manual's transitional provision directing that people who already carried an established DSM-IV diagnosis be given the new one, which strikes me as an implicit acknowledgment that reclassifying an interactive kind imposes costs on the classified that reclassifying a natural kind does not.

Then take up two features the standard account does not obviously handle. First, much of what happened to the label afterward was not symptom-driven at all: once details of Hans Asperger's conduct in Nazi-era Vienna became widely publicized in 2018 and were fiercely disputed, use of the eponym collapsed in clinical and community settings even while the dispute over those details continued unresolved. Call this a loop that runs through the namer rather than through the named, and say whether it belongs inside the looping-effects framework, requires a distinct mechanism, or is not a looping effect at all. Second, consider the split outcome: the category was abolished from above, yet Asperger's and Aspie survived as self-descriptions while a great many who had used them migrated to autistic. Does this support what I will call identitarian lock-in — the claim that once a kind is inhabited as an identity, revising it means overriding the self-descriptions of the very people it is about, so that interactive kinds acquire a ratchet natural kinds lack — or does the migration show that the ratchet is far weaker than that framing implies? Be careful to distinguish what lock-in actually predicts from what mere habit, community politics, or service eligibility would predict.

Finally, mark where the framework goes quiet. The episode was also driven by things having little to do with self-description: diagnostic reliability, eligibility for services and accommodations, insurance coding, and how prevalence is measured. Say what those drivers look like from inside a theory built around the classified coming to be what they are called, and end with a verdict on whether this episode confirms looping effects, constitutes an anomaly for it, or is a kind of case Hacking never really contemplated. Please write your answer in American English.
