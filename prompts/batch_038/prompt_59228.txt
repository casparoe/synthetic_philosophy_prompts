I am in the third year of a doctorate in political philosophy, and the first substantive chapter of my dissertation is supposed to ask whether a duty to remember can survive the death of everyone who actually witnessed the events in question. Before I evaluate anybody's answer, I need to know what an answer has to do. The two poles I keep reading — Margalit's The Ethics of Memory, with its account of thick relations and shared memory, and Rieff's In Praise of Forgetting (2016), with its attack on what he treats as a moralised cult of remembrance — almost never state their own success conditions, so the debate proceeds by each side asserting intuitions the other has no reason to share. Please write the specification.

I want the desiderata that any adequate account of the duty to remember, and of any competing duty or permission to forget, has to meet. For each one, give me the case or consideration that generates it, described concretely enough that I could test a candidate view against it; slogans will not help me. The following are seeds rather than a checklist — add what I have missed, and tell me which of my seeds you think should be struck out:

- The bearer problem: who or what can be the subject of such a duty, given that memory of this kind runs on institutions, endowments, curricula, anniversaries and monuments that outlive the individuals who are said to owe the remembering.
- The generational problem: how a duty can bind people to whom no wrong was done by anyone now living, and who had no part in the events remembered.
- The distinction between there being a record and there being remembering: a complete, accurate, publicly accessible archive that nobody consults and nothing commemorates.
- The distinction between remembering that something happened and remembering it under a description that presents it as an injury still owed — and whether an account must treat the second as normative rather than merely psychological.
- The relations among remembering, resentment, forgiveness and forgetting: must an account say whether forgiveness requires, permits, or forbids continuing to remember as grievance?
- Veridicality: must the content remembered be true? Consider a sincere but materially distorted survivor narrative, and the case where correcting the record seems to destroy whatever the remembering was for.
- Efficacy: does a memory that changes nobody's conduct still satisfy the duty?
- Impartiality and scope: can the duty admit of degrees and of ranking victims, and how must it handle the case where one community's commemoration is experienced by another as a continuing humiliation?
- Justificatory structure: is remembering owed because of what it prevents, or is the instrumental framing itself a category mistake?
- Feasibility: whether the ought-implies-can constraint applies, and what becomes of an imperative addressed to a third generation that cannot possibly carry it.
- The distinction between private mourning and public commemoration, and between the ethical and the moral in something like Margalit's sense.
