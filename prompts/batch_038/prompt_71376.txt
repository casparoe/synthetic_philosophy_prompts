Hey — I'm putting together a session for my grad seminar on the social contract tradition, and the working title is something like "what happens to Hobbes if the agents can read each other's source code." I want your help thinking it through. Write as a professional philosopher who is also genuinely at home in the technical literature — someone who has thought about program equilibrium (the solution concept Tennenholtz introduced around 2004, where players submit programs that get run with the other player's program as input, so an equilibrium is a profile of programs such that nobody can swap in a different program and do better, holding the others' code fixed) and who has also thought about promises, trust, and the state of nature, and can move between the two without straining.

Here's the setup I have in mind. N agents, each committed in advance to a fixed public program, playing a one-shot prisoner's dilemma against every other agent simultaneously; each program can read the others' code before outputting a move, and can condition on what it reads. So a cooperator-recognizing program and its twin both cooperate, and neither can gain by switching to a plain always-defect program, even though defection is dominant in the ordinary sense of the base game. Hobbes's complaint — that agreements, absent some power behind them able to make good on the threat, amount to so much noise and are of no strength to secure anybody — is supposed to get you from that to the necessity of a sovereign. Source-code transparency looks like it delivers enforcement with no sovereign anywhere in sight, which is exactly what makes it philosophically interesting to me.

Now the twist, which is the part I actually care about and which I don't think you'll find worked out anywhere: suppose the transparency is lossy. Each agent sees only a compressed, noisy summary of the others' programs — good enough to separate an unconditional defector from a conditional cooperator, but not good enough to distinguish two different conditional-cooperation policies that agree on, say, 95% of the histories they could ever encounter. I'll call this blurred program equilibrium. I know there's a standard grumble that exact-string-matching cooperators are brittle, since one extra whitespace character in the opponent's code and everybody defects; I'm after something more systematic than that grumble. Blur as a continuum, not as a typo in the source.

So: give me the most important implications of program equilibrium, and of my blurred version of it, for the claim that covenants need a sword. I want implications for the philosophy, not a game theory tutorial — what it does to the idea of a binding promise; to the idea that mutual assurance requires a third party to stand behind it; to whether the differnce between a real covenant and a lucky coincidence of interests is a difference in the world or only in our description of it; to whether obligation is possible without a sovereign; to what a self-interested agent could rationally commit itself to and how. And tell me what blur does: my hunch is that partial transparency can be worse than none at all, because it licenses confident mistakes about which policy you're actually facing, and an agent that's sure it's facing a cooperator has every reason to defect. Push back on me if that's wrong — I'd rather be corrected than flattered.
