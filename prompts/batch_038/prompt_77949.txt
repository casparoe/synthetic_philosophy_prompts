---
(a) Apply (ii) to P conditionalized on B. Then P(A→B | B) = P(B|A) = 1, since we're already assuming B. Likewise, conditionalizing on ¬B gives P(A→B | ¬B) = P(B | A&¬B) = 0. So by the law of total probability, P(A→B) = P(A→B | B)·P(B) + P(A→B | ¬B)·P(¬B) = 1·P(B) + 0·P(¬B) = P(B). So the probability of the conditional just is the probability of its consequent, which is clearly absurd — it makes the antecedent do no work whatsoever.

(b) But (ii) says P(A→B) = P(B|A), so we get P(B) = P(B|A), i.e. P(A&B) = P(A)P(B): A and B are probabilistically independent. That contradicts how we chose them. So Lewis has shown that the equation P(A→B) = P(B|A) is flatly inconsistent with the axioms of probability — no probability function can obey both. That's what "triviality" means here: the equation only holds when it says nothing. One thing worth stressing is that the derivation never assumed anything about what makes A→B true, not even that it's truth-functional. All it used was that A→B has a probability and that the equation holds. So the result isn't an objection to the material-conditional analysis in particular, it's an objection to giving indicatives truth conditions at all.

(c) Since the equation is inconsistent with probability theory, Lewis has to give up (ii) rather than (i) — you can't abandon logic, but you can abandon a thesis about conditionals. His positive view is that "if A, B" is really the material conditional ¬A ∨ B, so indicatives are straightforwardly true or false, and are typically true whenever A is false. The triviality result is then a reductio that pushes you back to the truth-functional analysis. The leftover puzzle — why "if A, B" seems unassertable when we know ¬A, but fine when we merely think A is unlikely — Lewis handles pragmatically: we only assert a conditional when we have some independent reason to think there's a connection between A and B, so the assertability conditions come out stricter than the truth conditions, and that's why our probability judgements about indicatives track P(B|A) even though that isn't what they mean.

(d) The derivation obviously depends on (i). If A→B weren't a sentence of L it wouldn't have a probability and step (a) couldn't even be written down. The "for every conditionalization" clause in (ii) is a technical convenience, a way of making sure the equation isn't just a coincidence about one particular P; the contradiction is already latent in the single equation P(A→B) = P(B|A).
