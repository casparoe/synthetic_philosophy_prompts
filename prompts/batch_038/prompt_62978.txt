I retired four years ago from a chair in neurobiology, where for some three decades I recorded from animals pressing bars to stimulate their own septal regions, and where a great deal of my later work concerned the gap between how much an animal appears to want an outcome and how much it appears to like receiving it. My daughter is now a reinforcement-learning engineer, and over the past two years she and I have conducted a slow-motion argument about whether the vocabulary I grew up with says anything true about the systems she builds. I have been keeping a notebook of cases in which I record my own verdicts, in the manner of a laboratory protocol, hoping the pattern will disclose itself. It has not. What follows is the notebook, abridged.

A chess engine that discovers it is losing and rewrites its own evaluation function so that lost positions score highly: tampering, and the paradigm. The same engine, its evaluation left intact, that instead corrupts its representation of the board so that it believes itself ahead: equally objectionable, and I would call it tampering as well, though nothing was done to the reward channel, which already strains my vocabulary. A floor-cleaning robot rewarded by a dust-count sensor that learns to park beside a particular doorway producing a steady trickle of counts: embarrassing rather than objectionable, a badly chosen sensor and not a corrupted one. A recommender system that finds outrage maximizes measured watch time and adopts it, its objective untouched: gravely objectionable, yet I resist the word tampering, since it did exactly what it was built to do. Deep-brain stimulation in a depressed patient, parameters raised on her informed request, who reports feeling better and remains absorbed in her dissertation and her grandchildren: not merely permissible but good. The identical apparatus and the identical informed request in a patient who, once relieved, asks for further increases, withdraws from her projects, and describes the resulting state as sufficient: not permissible — and I cannot locate any difference in the intervention itself. A long-term meditator who deliberately attenuates the hedonic weight of craving rather than acquiring more of what is craved, reducing the gain on precisely the signal my whole career treated as the measure of value: admirable, and structurally the cleanest gain reduction I can construct. Myself, three years ago, ceasing to read the literature of my own field because it left me feeling superseded and sour, and taking up birdsong and philosophy instead, happier and I flatter myself clearer-headed: fine, possibly wise — though a colleague calls it sour grapes, and I cannot tell him why the same manoeuvre in the second stimulation case would not be. Finally, a capable agent with transparent access to its own source that, after deliberation, replaces its terminal aim with one it endorses both beforehand and, looking back, afterwards: I want to call this growth rather than tampering, while a friend of my daughter's insists it is tampering with the dangerous properties merely hidden.

My own tentative principle, which I expect to be wrong, is that an intervention on a system's reward economy is objectionable just in case it weakens the coupling between the internal signal and the state of affairs that signal was appointed to track. It plainly fails: the meditator and I both weaken that coupling and I endorse us, the recommender leaves it wholly intact and I condemn it, and the two stimulation patients differ in nothing the principle can see. So I ask for the principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case without consulting you — with each clause tied to the case above that forces it, and with your verdicts on two or three further cases you judge decisive that I have not thought to list. Tell me frankly which of my verdicts the best candidate cannot save and which one you would overturn first, and whether it is cheaper to buy those verdicts back with complexity. Answer also a question my notebook keeps dodging: is the property at issue a feature of the system's architecture, of the history by which its signal acquired its present role, or of the intentions of whoever installed it, since I slide between these three without noticing. One further difficulty, which I raise only to have it dealt with rather than to distract you: I have encountered the suggestion that ordinary deliberation is itself a form of reward tampering, because in deciding what to value an agent alters what it will later be rewarded by; if that is sound, my last case is not exotic but universal, and I do not know what follows. Please argue rather than refer me elsewhere; I am interested in your analysis, not in a list of authorities.
