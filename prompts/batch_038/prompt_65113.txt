In September I was elected to the conduct panel of a community arts organisation of about three hundred members — an amateur theatre company and a photography group sharing a building. Our entire written standard on consent, unchanged since the organisation was founded, is one sentence: "No means no." Two cases last year showed how thin that is, and I have now been asked to draft the revision ahead of our annual general meeting in March.

Case one: a member reported an encounter with another member during which she said nothing at all — she describes having frozen — and sent a message the following day saying she had not wanted it. The panel split 3–2. The majority held that the policy's only operative text was never satisfied, since no "no" was uttered; the minority held that a frozen non-response obviously counts as one. Case two: a photographer asked a sitter to pose for what he told her were portfolio images, then entered them in a national competition under her real name. She agreed readily to the sitting and objected to nothing during it; her complaint concerns the use, not the session. He invoked the same sentence: she never said no to the competition. Two members invoked it against him: she did say no when he raised the competition weeks later, and in their view that ended the matter even though the prints were already hanging.

So the slogan has been used within a single year to defeat one complaint, to defeat another, and to sustain a third. Several panel members treat it as though it settles such questions, and one argues that any elaboration "just shifts the burden onto the accused."

Before I write anything, I want to know what the sentence could actually be claiming. Please lay out a menu of the distinct theses it might stand for. I can see at least these directions and expect there are more: a rule addressed to the person on the receiving end of a refusal about how to interpret it in the moment; a rule addressed to an adjudicator afterwards about what counts as non-consent; a claim about what is sufficient for a refusal to be valid (no force, no tears, no repetition, no witness required); a claim about what is necessary for consent to be absent; a claim about revocability, that a later "no" withdraws an earlier "yes" even where the consequences have already started to unfold; a claim about standards of evidence rather than standards of conduct. For each reading, tell me whether it is a platitude nobody disputes, a substantive and defensible claim, or something false or at least highly contentious — and which of the three cases above it would decide, and how.

Then tell me which reading each of the three invocations most likely relied on, since I suspect they are not the same one, and identify any pair of readings that cannot coherently be adopted in a single policy text — for instance, whether the most protective reading of "no" leaves the panel unable to say anything at all about the complainant who froze, or whether a reading strong enough to reach her undermines the "shifts the burden onto the accused" objection in a way I could actually argue.

I am not after a reading list, case law, or scholarly references; I need the conceptual map, precise enough that I can defend a replacement text to a room of volunteers who have been repeating this sentence for twenty years. Please close with a short paragraph on what the sentence is genuinely good for — some function it performs well and would likely lose if rewritten as a set of conditions.
