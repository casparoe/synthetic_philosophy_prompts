I have been the volunteer registrar at a county historical society for eleven years. I have no academic training in history or in anything adjacent; I took the job after retiring from a bank, where I kept records for a living, and I have done most of my thinking about what our rules assume while sitting at the intake desk. I would like you to tell me whether that thinking holds up, and where it fails.

These are the rules we work under.

First, nothing is taken in except as part of a collection, and every collection must have a creator: a named person, a named family, or a named organization that made or gathered the papers. An item that cannot be attached to such a creator goes into an uncatalogued miscellaneous file that no researcher has ever requested in eleven years.

Second, a collection stays as it arrives. We do not rearrange it, we do not lift an interesting letter out of one family's papers to file it alongside the subject it concerns, and we do not fill gaps by purchasing documents at auction, however good the document and however large the gap.

Third, every acquisition decision goes into the accessions log: date, donor, description of what was offered, and, when we decline, a sentence explaining why. Our refusals are described in more detail than most of the lives we agree to keep.

Fourth, we may destroy duplicates and material judged to have no local bearing. The destruction is logged, but nothing is given away elsewhere and no copy is retained.

Fifth, our oral history interviews are catalogued with the interviewing project as creator, on the reasoning that the project is what accumulated the tapes. The person who spoke appears in a note field, and note fields are not indexed in our system.

Last spring the mill on the river closed and we received its payroll ledgers, covering roughly 1911 to 1958. About nine hundred people are listed in them by name, some for forty consecutive years, with wages, deductions, the weeks they were laid off, and in a few cases a terse remark in the margin. Our form has one creator field, so the ledgers were entered under the mill. The nine hundred names can now be found only by someone who already knows to ask for that volume and read down the page.

My own conclusion, which I arrived at slowly and without any authority behind it, is that those nine hundred people also made a record. A woman who gave her name to a timekeeper for four decades, and whose name was written down in her presence, did something that resulted in that page; the page is not only a document about her, produced by an entity with a legal name. If that is right, our catalogue does not merely overlook her. It is built so that she cannot be entered.

So I would like the following from you.

What does the creator requirement presuppose about what a record is, and about who is capable of making one? Which competing account does it exclude, and does it exclude it by decision or only as a side effect of how the form happens to be laid out?

Our letterhead says the society exists to preserve the record of this county and of the people who lived in it. Can any intake procedure of the kind I have described produce that, or is the gap between the sentence on the letterhead and the rules in the manual the real subject here? Put differently: are we failing at our stated purpose, or succeeding at an unstated one?

What does the accessions log presuppose about what the archive is? On our own practice, is the archive a record of the county, or a record of the society's decisions about the county? Those are not the same object, and we behave as though they were.

Are the rule against buying and the rule permitting destruction consistent with one another, given that both appeal to the integrity of what arrived on its own?

Where is all of this underdetermined? I want to know which of my conclusions are forced by the rules and which are merely comfortable with them, and where several quite different ideas about what an archive is for would generate exactly the same five rules, so that I would be wrong to think the rules settle anything.

And if you took my claim about the ledgers seriously, what would have to change: in the form, in the log, in the destruction policy, in the way we describe an interview? I would like the costs stated as well, including whatever we would lose that the current rules protect.

If what I have described belongs to a line of argument with a name, give me the name once, at the end. I want the reasoning first, and I do not want a list of things to read.
