I am in the final year of a philosophy degree and am currently taking a module on non-classical logic; I also serve, rather improbably, as assistant secretary of our university's debating society, which is how I came to have the minute book for the episode I want to put to you. Two members of my reading group, Rhys and Beatrice, got hold of that episode and argued about it for the better part of an hour without either giving way. The facts are these. Our Standing Orders provide, at Order 12, that a member who is absent from three consecutive ordinary meetings ceases automatically to be a member at the close of the third, without vote or notice, and that a member of the Committee who so ceases thereby vacates their seat; and, at Order 31, that the Committee may readmit a former member by a resolution carried by not fewer than six affirmative votes — the Committee's full complement being fixed at nine — and that such a resolution may specify that the readmission takes effect from a date earlier than that of the resolution itself. Callum Reeve missed the ordinary meetings of 7, 14 and 21 January, and so ceased to be a member on 21 January; Nadia Okonjo missed those of 28 January and of 4 and 11 February, and so ceased on 11 February. On 19 February the Committee met, attended by its seven sitting members together with Callum and Nadia. It passed two resolutions: one readmitting Callum with effect from 1 January, and one readmitting Nadia with effect from 1 January — in each case a date earlier than the very lapse the resolution was meant to cure. Each resolution was carried by six votes to three, the six including both Callum and Nadia, the three being sitting members. Nobody at the meeting raised the question of whether Callum and Nadia were entitled to vote at all.

Rhys opened by saying that the minute book now records a genuine true contradiction: of Nadia, for the period from 11 to 19 February, both that she was a member and that she was not a member are true, and likewise of Callum from 21 January onwards. He thought this mattered more than the usual textbook examples, because here the contradiction arises not from a language talking about itself but from perfectly ordinary administrative machinery, which he took to be much harder for anyone to quarantine. Beatrice replied that the rules conflict, but that a conflict among rules is not a contradiction in the world: membership is a status the rules constitute, so when the constitutive rules point both ways what one has is a defective rulebook, and the correct description of Nadia's position on 12 February is that nothing determines it, not that both determinations hold; she added that stipulations are not the sort of thing that could be true and false together, so the case never gets off the ground as a dialetheia. Rhys answered on two fronts. First, the appeal to indeterminacy is unavailable here, since nothing in the case is vague — the dates are exact, the vote counts are exact, and on these facts 'member' has no borderline application at all; one posits gaps for borderline cases, and this is not one. Second, and decisively, her position is unstable: if the retroactive readmissions do not make Callum and Nadia members before 19 February, then neither was entitled to vote, each resolution mustered only four valid affirmative votes against the required six, both failed, and the Committee has been improperly constituted ever since, so that every motion it has carried in the interim is void. She must therefore either accept the glut or accept that both readmissions failed; there is no comfortable third position. Beatrice said she would take the second horn cheerfully — both resolutions failed, the readmissions are void, and the Society should hold fresh votes under amended standing orders — and then turned the argument round: even if she granted him his true contradiction it would be of no use to anyone, because the Society still needs one answer about whether the 19 February resolutions stand, and the claim that Nadia both was and was not a member is exactly as unusable for that purpose as a confession that nothing settles it. She further said that he cannot have the glut without paying for it, since in classical logic a single contradiction entails everything, and she doubted he wished to hold that the whole minute book, and every other proposition whatsoever, is true because two standing orders misfired. Rhys called this a caricature: the paraconsistent move is precisely to deny that a contradiction entails everything, which is why the view is named for consistency rather than for triviality, and no one proposes that every proposition is true. Beatrice said that if that is the move, he owes her an account of what it costs, because denying that is not free — some inference he relies on constantly has to be given up — and she wanted him to name it and to say whether he was truly willing to surrender it for the sake of a botched committee minute. Rhys did not name one, and said instead that the cost, whatever it turns out to be, is worth paying, because the alternative makes the validity of a public act depend on a circle that nobody could have detected at the time and leaves a society permanently unable to repair its own membership by the only procedure its rules afford it. Beatrice's last word was that this is a complaint about the drafting of standing orders, not a discovery about the nature of truth.

Could you go through that exchange move by move and tell me who is right at each stage, rather than merely who wins at the end? In particular: does the objection that a conflict among norms is not a contradiction in the world actually block the dialetheia, or does it only redescribe it? Is Rhys right that an appeal to indeterminacy is ruled out by the absence of vagueness, and if not, what else in the case might license a gap? Are the two horns he offers Beatrice genuinely the only ones, given that both resolutions failing and both resolutions succeeding each seem to hang together? What inference, exactly, does treating this case paraconsistently cost him, and is Beatrice right to think that cost objectionable? And is Rhys's final point — that the void reading condemns the Society to an unrepairable circle — a reason about truth or only about institutional design? Please also identify what both of them have missed; my own suspicion is that there is at least one reading of the episode that neither canvassed and that would reshape the whole argument, and I should like to know whether that suspicion is well founded and what that reading would be. Finish with a verdict on who was more right overall, and on whether the episode tells us anything general about true contradictions or only something local about retroactive provisions.
