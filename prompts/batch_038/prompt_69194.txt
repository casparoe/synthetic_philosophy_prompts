ok so I'm a junior and I'm editor in chief of my school's paper this year, which sounds a lot cooler than it is. 8 issues a year, about 12 editors, a faculty advisor who basically just signs the form at the end and doesn't read anything unless there's a problem, and a principal who is not exactly our fan. Nobody on the staff has done this before and we turn over completely every year, so there's no institutional memory, just whatever we write down.

I got interested in this idea called the unilateralist's curse. My understanding, possibly wrong: if a bunch of people each have a shaky estimate of whether some action is a good idea or a bad one, and any single one of them can just go ahead and do it, then it's the person with the most optimistic estimate who ends up acting, so you get way more bad actions than if everyone had to agree or vote first. And it gets worse the more people are in a position to act and the noisier everyone's estimates are. A guy on a forum told me this means you should basically never act without consulting everyone, which I'm pretty sure isn't right either, so tell me where my version above is off before you build anything on top of it.

Because this is basically my whole life right now. Every issue, three or four times, I'm sitting on something only I've fully seen and I have to decide fast: run it, kill it, pull it, sit on it — myself, or drag it to the editorial board and lose a day or two and probably the source. All four section editors get the same tip folder, so any of us could act on the same thing. Last year I called two things wrong in print and everyone remembers. And if we screw up bad enough the paper gets put under prior review, which has happened at other schools around here.

Can you write me an actual decision procedure I could tape inside the cabinet in the paper room and run through in like ten minutes? Not an essay about journalism ethics — an ordered thing. Questions in order, what counts as a good enough answer to each so I know when to stop and move on, where the procedure just halts with "don't do this alone," and where it halts because the question isn't a factual one and I have to hand it to a human (and say which human — advisor, board, the person it's about). The order should matter; I want the questions that kill a decision fast to come first so I'm not doing twenty questions on something that was dead at question two.

It should actually build in the stuff the curse cares about, not just be a generic ethics checklist: how many other people are positioned to act on the same information, how trustworthy my own judgment is on this particular kind of call, whether the action is reversible and how far, and how lopsided the harm is if I'm wrong in one direction versus the other. Tell me explicitly how the bar moves when each of those changes — like what's different about being the only person who knows versus being one of four who know. And, important, a lot of "consulting" at my school is just texting a friend who's going to agree with me, so build in something about what counts as a real check versus a fake one that just makes me feel covered.

Then run it on three things and show the work, not just the verdict:

1. anonymous tip plus some half-legible budget papers about booster club money going into the football program. the source will only talk to me and might ghost if we sit on it for a few days. running it means naming a coach who also teaches a class I'm in. board meets thursday night, we print friday.

2. a sophomore wrote a first-person piece about being on anxiety medication. she says her parents don't know and don't need to, and she's eighteen in a couple months. three other editors read it, think it's brave, want it in.

3. layout for the sports spread is already sent and I just noticed a photo where you can read part of a kid's phone screen and it's something pretty private. I can swap the photo in ten minutes without telling anybody, or flag it and there's a real chance the paper goes out with the photo because the printer deadline is genuinely hard.

One of these is going to come out with an answer I really dont want to hear. Don't soften it or hedge around it — say which one and why the procedure lands there.

Last part: tell me where the procedure breaks. What kinds of situation it can't handle, which of the steps are secretly just my judgment wearing a clipboard, and what would make me want to throw the whole thing out and start over. And please don't tell me to go read something, I have a chem test tomorrow.
