I chair the street-naming committee in a mid-sized American city, and on Saturday I have to give the remarks at the sign-unveiling for a street that is being renamed. Since 1911 it has carried the name of Elias Vardell, a physician who endowed our public hospital and who, as a state legislator, drafted the provision that kept the mill district's children out of the schools his endowment also helped build. The street will now be named for Ada Rowe, a nurse-midwife who delivered four generations in that district and who left no papers of her own — we know her almost entirely from other people's records, Vardell's among them.

Two of Vardell's descendants will be in the front row; they petitioned against the change and lost, narrowly. The people who ran the campaign will be there too, along with a large number of residents whose only strong feeling concerns the paperwork. I have four or five minutes, about 600 words.

Here is the difficulty. I voted against the renaming. I don't regret the vote and I don't want to imply I've come around — but I chair the committee, the decision is made, and I mean to speak for it sincerely, without resentment and without performing a conversion I haven't had. Please build the speech on the distinction between honoring someone and remembering them: a street name as a standing act of endorsement performed in the present tense by a community speaking in its own name, and therefore revocable for reasons that need not touch what the person actually did, whereas the historical record is not revocable at all. Observe one constraint strictly: the speech may not contain a single sentence amounting to a verdict on Vardell, in either direction — no talk of a man of his time, no pairing of brilliance with cruelty, nothing about his character or his place in history. Every justificatory move has to be made out of facts about us: who is speaking, in whose name, and what a present community can stand behind. It should be warm to the descendants without asking them to agree, and it should give the campaigners something better than vindication.

After the speech, add a note of about 150 words in which you commit to this framing rather than laying out alternatives: say why honoring and remembering really are separable in a case like this one, and flag where the speech takes liberties — above all, whether a refusal to pass any verdict on Vardell is itself a covert verdict, and whether the distinction can bear the weight I'm putting on it.
