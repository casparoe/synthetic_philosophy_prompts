I'm taking a causal inference course this term and we did Simpson's paradox last week, and I've sort of talked myself into a position I want stress tested before I write anything dumb in my final paper.

The position: there's nothing causally deep going on in the paradox. Every genuine reversal decomposes into two mundane ingredients — (1) the effect measure you happened to pick isn't collapsible (odds ratios, hazard ratios), and (2) the covariate is imbalanced between the groups being compared. Both together and you get a flip, either one missing and you don't. So on my view the whole thing is a fact about measurement scales plus a fact about sample composition, and the business about needing a causal graph before you know what to condition on is overreach — an interpretive gloss people add because they find the arithmetic unsettling.

Two things I want.

First, the exposure map. What would have to be true for this to be wrong? Rank the defeaters — which are actually fatal versus just embarrassing — and tell me which ones the graph-and-causal-structure people would themselves concede count against *them*, since I suspect their side has soft spots too (cases where the right adjustment set is genuinely underdetermined, say). Also be honest about whether my two-clause formulation has been qualified into something that can't fail. "(1) or (2)" sounds like it covers every possible reversal by construction, and if it does, is that a problem for a claim of this kind, or is it fine because I'm offering a decomposition rather than a prediction? Related: what does my view actually predict that the causal view doesn't? If the answer is nothing, just say nothing.

Second, run my own numbers at me. I made this up for a problem set and I can't tell whether it saves me or kills me. Randomized, 1000 per arm, balanced by design. Z is post-randomization — call it whether liver enzymes spiked in week one, which the drug causes in a lot of people.

Z=1 (spike): drug 350/700 recover (50%), placebo 110/200 (55%)
Z=0 (no spike): drug 90/300 (30%), placebo 280/800 (35%)
Crude: drug 440/1000 (44%), placebo 390/1000 (39%)

So the drug loses inside both strata and wins overall, with a collapsible measure, in a properly randomized trial. I'd file that under clause (2) — imbalance in Z, 70% vs 20%. But my prof says the actual fix is not to condition on Z at all, and that my account has no way of telling you that. Is she right? Does my view have any resources for deciding which variables to condition on, or is it just silent there — and if its silent, is that fatal or merely out of scope?

Last bit: I claimed in section that non-collapsibility can never flip a sign on its own, it only shrinks the marginal estimate toward the null, so even an odds-ratio reversal still needs imbalance to do the work. Is that provable or am I about to get burned?
