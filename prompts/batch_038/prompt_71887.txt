For the closing reading of a two-day workshop on personal identity — the room is a mixture of neuroscientists, clinicians and philosophers, and the session immediately beforehand will have run through the familiar arguments about gradual replacement — I would like a pantoum of five quatrains built around the following scenario, which I have invented and which the audience will be encountering for the first time.

A patient enrols in a phased migration programme: one neuron a day is displaced by an artificial neuron that is, on every measure available from outside, functionally identical to the cell it replaces. As a condition of consent, the patient must make the same two-entry log each morning before breakfast — one entry answering the question whether they are still themselves, one answering whether anything changed overnight — and a clinician reads the entries back aloud. Across three years the wording never varies once; what varies is everything else, including the fraction of the brain that is no longer biological. At the halfway point the contract permits a halt and a reversal, with tissue re-grown to displace what has been installed, and the patient lets the window close without using it. The poem should turn on that unused option, and on why its availability cannot deliver the reassurance the paperwork implies: from the inside there is no way to tell a reversal that would restore the patient from one that would install a stranger who reports continuity, because the log would read identically either way.

Formally, keep the pantoum's rules strictly — lines two and four of each stanza reappear as lines one and three of the next, and the poem closes by returning its own opening line in the final position. The two lines carried forward throughout should be the log entries themselves, word for word identical on every appearance, so that the fixed report and the changing substrate sit in the same stanza; each should be short enough to sound like a form being filled in rather than a line of verse, and each should be capable of reading as either reassuring or damning depending on the stanza it lands in. The non-repeating lines are where the substitution, the clinic, the arithmetic of the halfway point and the closed window belong. Do not force a rhyme scheme onto those new lines: the exact repetition already supplies the form's music, and I would rather have good images than strained end-rhymes. The closing return should leave it genuinely open whether the sentence still means at the end what it meant at the beginning; the final stanza must not settle the question by introducing some new claim that answers it.

Please do not name any of the literature or use its technical vocabulary — no talk of qualia, psychological continuity, or the like. Someone who knows the arguments should recognise them in the structure and the imagery alone. Return the poem and nothing else, in British English.
