Every major professional team sport in North America regulates the price of player labour in a way that would be remarkable in almost any other industry. The National Football League and the National Hockey League impose a hard ceiling on each club's total payroll; Major League Baseball permits unlimited spending but taxes payroll above a threshold; the National Basketball Association sets a ceiling that clubs may exceed only through exceptions written into its agreement with the players' union. All four route incoming rookies through a draft in which the clubs that performed worst select first — subject, in some leagues, to a lottery that deliberately weakens the link between finishing last and picking first. All four redistribute a share of national broadcasting revenue from richer clubs to poorer ones. Yet none of them caps what an owner may pay a head coach, spend on a practice facility, or devote to medical staff, scouts, or analytics; those inputs are bought at whatever price the market sets.

The leagues' own vocabulary for all of this is parity or competitive balance: the claim that the product is worth more to fans, broadcasters, and clubs alike when any team can win on any given afternoon. Critics sometimes put the objection differently — that the leagues have settled on a preferred shape for the distribution of talent and compensation, and that they hold that shape in place by blocking transactions both parties want to make, season after season.

I would like to know what theory of justice, if any, this arrangement presupposes.

First: is the cap best read as an endorsement of a patterned or end-state view, one that fixes a shape for a distribution and must keep intervening to preserve it, or does it presuppose no view about justice at all, because a league is a voluntary association whose internal rules are terms of a joint undertaking rather than the basic arrangements of a society to which principles of distributive justice apply? Is that distinction doing genuine work here, or is it a way of evading the question?

Second: separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely fits well with. Does the reverse-order draft, for instance, require the belief that the natural distribution of athletic talent is undeserved, or only that raw talent is a poor input into a product whose value depends on uncertainty of outcome? Point to the places where the arrangement is underdetermined, so that rival theories license the same rules.

Third: is the practice internally coherent? Weigh the asymmetry between capped player payroll and uncapped spending on coaches and facilities, and the tension between a draft that sends talent to the worst-performing clubs and a free-agency market that sends it to the highest bidder. Do these presuppose incompatible things about when outcomes may be engineered and when they must be left to transaction, or can a single account unify them? Note also the second-order patch: because a reverse-order draft gives clubs a reason to lose, the leagues added lotteries that blunt the reward for losing. What does that sequence — a rule, then an incentive corrupting the rule, then interference to protect the rule — suggest about whether the shape of the outcome is the goal or merely a constraint?

Fourth: in at least two of these leagues the ceiling was not imposed on players over their objections but written into a collective bargaining agreement by their own union, in exchange for a guaranteed share of league revenue and, in some cases, for genuine free agency. Does the fact that the restriction on transactions is itself the product of a collective agreement change what it presupposes?

Finally, tell me what these leagues would have to look like, and what would have to be true of their justification, if they dropped the parity rationale in favour of a purely historical view on which a distribution of talent is just so long as it arose from consensual signings and nothing further is required of it. Say what would be lost, and whether that loss is a loss of justice or of something else.
