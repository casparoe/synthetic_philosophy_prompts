Been reading a lot of path dependence stuff lately (QWERTY and the usual suspects) and I keep hitting a wrinkle I can't find a word for. The standard story is that an inferior thing wins early and then sticks because switching costs or network effects make it individually rational to keep using it. But there seems to be a second mechanism that isn't like that at all: once the incumbent wins, it also supplies the yardstick that challengers get measured against, so the comparison is stacked before it starts, without anyone stacking it.

Cases that look like this to me: someone with no borrowing history can't get a loan, because the score that measures creditworthiness is built out of borrowing history; a firm that invests mostly in software and brands looks less profitable than one that invests in machinery, because the accounting rules were writen when capital meant plant, and markets trade on those numbers; a new programming language scores badly on benchmark suites that are written in the idiom of the languages already on top. In none of these is the incumbent's edge about switching costs or about people needing to coordinate — the winner authored the exam.

Is there an established concept for this? I know about Goodhart's law, adaptive preferences, and "dominant design," but none of them seem to be it exactly — tell me how my thing differs from each, and from plain lock-in. If there's no name, give it one, and define it sharply enough that I can sort real cases from fakes: what would a challenger look like that gets judged on criteria the incumbent did not supply?
