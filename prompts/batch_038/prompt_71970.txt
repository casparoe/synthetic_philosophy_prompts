I have just spent most of an evening arguing with a friend about the balance sheet of the British Raj — roughly the Ferguson side against the Tharoor side — and the argument ended somewhere strange that I am unable to characterize. I am hoping you can.

The strange thing is how little of the disagreement turned out to be about facts. We agreed on nearly all of them: that the railways were built, that they were laid out to move troops and carry goods to the ports, that Indian textile production collapsed under British rule, that revenue and raw materials flowed out and manufactured goods came in, that the great famines occurred under colonial administration, that India's share of world output fell sharply over the period, and that the British left behind courts, a civil service, a common administrative language and a continental rail network. Neither of us disputed any of that. What we could not agree on was what any of it was.

Take the railways. His description: an instrument of extraction that incidentally also carried passengers. Mine: the largest infrastructure investment India had yet seen, which also happened to serve the movement of troops and raw materials. Same lines, same causal roles, same passengers, same troop trains. The entire disagreement lives in which function gets treated as the point of the thing and which as a side effect — and neither of us produced an argument for that ordering, because each of us thought it was simply given by the facts we already shared.

Or the famines. I said the railways made it possible to move grain into a starving district; he said the same railways made it profitable to move grain out of a starving district while people in it were dying. Both true, both about the identical technology. Yet one puts the railway on the asset side of the ledger and the other on the liability side, and we never actually argued about which of the two things the railway did, because it plainly did both.

So here is what I want from you. Is there an established name for this kind of disagreement — one in which the disputants share all the descriptive facts, and the whole dispute is carried by an unstated choice about what the thing in question is to be compared with, or by a ranking of its several real functions into primary and incidental? I expect people will say it is just framing, but that word makes it sound like a rhetorical trick or a bias, whereas here both descriptions seem entirely accurate. It also feels adjacent to the notion of essentially contested concepts, though I doubt that railways or famines are concepts of that kind, and to some general thesis about descriptions outrunning facts, which is too broad to be of any use. Tell me which of these is nearest, what precisely separates what I have described from each of them, and whether this is a real disagreement or a pseudo-disagreement that dissolves the moment you insist on saying the comparison out loud. If nothing established fits, coin a term, define it, and tell me what falls just outside it — for instance, whether a purely empirical quarrel about whether the railways raised or lowered famine mortality would be a case of this, since I suspect it would not be.

Please do not give me a menu of candidates with the pros and cons of each and leave the choice to me. Take a position, commit to it, and give your reasons. And give me your verdict on the argument itself: once this feature of our exchange is named, does it favor his side or mine?
