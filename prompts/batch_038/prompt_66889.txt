Our interpretability reading group has been passing around a short argument about whether trained networks have representations in the realist sense — whether claims about features, circuits, and internal world models can be true or false, rather than merely convenient. The argument is meant to be especially uncomfortable for people who want to say yes, because it borrows their own semantic assumptions and then overshoots. It circulated in this form:

(1) For a physical structure S to represent a content c, it must be a determinate matter of fact that S carries c rather than some rival c*; there must be something that makes it the case that c, and not c*, is what is there.

(2) That determinacy can come from only two sources: intrinsic aboutness, or the assignment of content by an interpreter.

(3) Structures inside a trained network have no intrinsic aboutness; they are weight configurations whose only semantic significance is borrowed from what we say about them.

(4) Nor can an interpreter's assignment supply determinacy here, because for any candidate structure the interpreter faces a family of ascriptions that fit the evidence equally well — the same activation profile admits a narrow gloss (this particular individual), a broader one (familiar faces), a broader one still (faces as such), and a purely functional one (whatever licenses this kind of downstream behavior) — and nothing in the network, its training history, or its deployment privileges one over the others.

(5) So no structure in a trained network determinately represents anything; feature and circuit talk is at best an instrument for predicting behavior, not a description of the system.

(6) Nothing in (1)–(4) mentions silicon, engineering, or artificial training. The same reasoning transfers premise for premise to structures in brains: they too present only causal-dispositional profiles, and they too admit equally good ascriptions at different levels of grain.

(7) Therefore nothing in a brain determinately represents anything either: nobody has beliefs or desires in any fact-of-the-matter sense, and there is no determinate content to what anyone says.

The group's verdict is that the argument defeats itself, since (7) is intolerable and (7) is generated by the argument's own premises, so realism about network representations must survive somewhere. I find that diagnosis too cheap. It looks like a descendant of familiar underdetermination and rule-following worries, and those are not usually dissolved by noting that they generalize.

I would like to know where the chain actually breaks. Which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Specifically: is the trouble an equivocation, and if so over which term, and in which direction does the ambiguity run — toward the anti-realist or away from her? Is (2) an unexhausted dichotomy, and what must the leading alternatives give up in order to break it — causal or informational accounts on which covariance plus learning history fixes content without any interpreter, teleological accounts on which content is fixed by a selecting history, or deflationary and interest-relative accounts on which determinacy is only ever determinacy relative to a level of grain we choose for a purpose? Or is the failure at (6), because brains and trained networks are genuinely disanalogous — gradient descent is itself a selecting process and might ground a teleology of its own, or biological lineage does work that an optimization run cannot — in which case what follows if they are in fact analogous, and should the anti-realist simply embrace (7) and insist the debate was never about networks in particular?

So that you know where my sympathies lie: I want (5) to come out false, since otherwise an interpretability result is never a discovery about the system but only about our way of describing it. That said, I would give up (5) before inheriting (7). Please price each available exit, and say whether any of them makes (5) false without also making (7) false.
