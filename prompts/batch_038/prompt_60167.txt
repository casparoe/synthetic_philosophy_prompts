I'm a staff software engineer at a mid-sized cloud infrastructure company, and in about three weeks I'm giving a forty-minute talk at a local rationalist- and effective-altruism-adjacent meetup that several of my colleagues attend regularly. I have no philosophy background: I took one ethics elective fifteen years ago and retain almost nothing from it. What I do have is a spreadsheet I've been refining for a year that estimates animal suffering caused by different diets, and the talk is going to defend a conclusion I expect to annoy the room — namely, that for someone whose sole aim is to reduce total vertebrate suffering, shifting from chicken and farmed fish to pasture-raised beef is probably a better marginal move than going fully plant-based, and that the standard advice to simply eliminate animal products is not obviously the suffering-minimizing option. I plan to be explicit up front that I am not presenting this as a settled result but as the output of a stated set of assumptions, and that I would rather be attacked on the assumptions than on the mood of the conclusion.

The argument has four legs, and I should tell you where I already suspect the soft spots are. First, animals per unit of food: my model puts roughly two orders of magnitude between a poultry-heavy diet and a beef-heavy diet at equal protein, since a broiler is slaughtered at about six weeks and a single steer yields a very large number of meals. Second, welfare per life: I score a broiler's life as substantially worse than a beef cow's, mainly on stocking density, lameness rates, and the fact that cattle spend most of their lives outdoors while broilers spend essentially none of theirs. Third, land use: I assumed that most of beef's notoriously large land footprint is rangeland that would not otherwise produce human-edible crops, and that the grain portion comes largely from low-elasticity byproducts, so the marginal cropland effect of an additional steak is small. Fourth, crop deaths: plant agriculture kills a great many small field animals per hectare during planting and harvest, and those deaths scale with cropland area, so a diet that shrinks total cropland shrinks them too. I normalized two ways — expected suffering-minutes per 1,000 kilocalories, and per dollar spent — and the two rankings disagree, which is embarrassing. The whole result is dominated by one parameter, the relative moral weight I assign to a chicken versus a cow; shifting that weight by a factor of five flips the conclusion, and I suspect that parameter is quietly carrying a moral intuition I cannot independently justify. I also left out a premise on purpose: my model implies that if wild animals' lives are on balance dominated by suffering, then habitat expansion and rewilding are net harmful, and I do not want to say that aloud in this room. There is a related trap I intend to walk into rather than around: if broiler lives are worth living, then falling demand means fewer such lives, and I currently handle that by assigning existence itself very little positive weight, which is a move I cannot defend from first principles.

The audience is around thirty people and is not homogeneous. Perhaps ten are engineers like me who already do informal cause prioritization and will attack the model as an engineering artifact — parameter sensitivity, unit errors, the choice of normalization, whether my uncertainty ranges mean anything at all. Two are graduate students in animal science, one working on broiler welfare and one on rangeland ecology, and both will know immediately whether my numbers are real or invented. One is a wildlife ecologist who studies farmland biodiversity and has already said in the group chat that he regards the entire framing of wild animal suffering as a category error; he will target the fourth leg hardest. Several are long-term vegans who have heard versions of this argument before and will say I have built a quantitative rationalization for wanting steak. One attendee does carbon accounting professionally and will try to drag everything into CO2-equivalent terms, away from suffering and toward climate. Nobody in the room is a moral philosopher and nobody will be impressed by name-dropping; they will be impressed by whether I know which of my own numbers are load-bearing.

Please write my Q&A prep sheet, in American English. I want the eight to ten objections this particular audience will actually raise — not the generic objections to vegetarianism that could be generated for any room — ordered by how much damage each would do if I fumbled it. For each objection: phrase it roughly the way the person raising it would phrase it, since the ecologist and the broiler-welfare grad student do not sound alike; identify which leg of the argument it targets; give me a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds without a slide; and state plainly where that reply is soft, or where it depends on a number I could not defend if pressed. Then tell me which two concessions I should make in the first five minutes to buy credibility, which single objection would be fatal if I had no answer for it, and what the best response is when I genuinely do not know. Finally, if any of my four legs is simply mistaken as stated — if, for instance, the rangeland-versus-cropland assumption does not survive contact with how beef is actually produced, or if the per-hectare field-death figures I used are not the kind of quantity that can be responsibly estimated at all — please say so directly rather than treating it as one opinion among several, because I would much rather find out from you now than from the rangeland ecology student in front of thirty people.
