I run a small research team at a company that makes a workplace analytics app — the kind that sits on your phone and pops up a few times a day asking how you're doing. Sales has been promising customers "insight into how your people actually feel," and now product wants to add items about *why* people feel that way, so we can sell HR a causal story instead of just a mood graph. A friend of mine who does UX research elsewhere said every team eventually gets burned by this and I'd like to get burned on purpose, in a controlled way, so: before I sign off on the question set I want a triage procedure my team can run on any proposed self-report question. Literally a list of checks in order with pass/fail conditions, that tells us how much evidential weight an answer can carry and which questions we should refuse to field at all.

Context for what you're working with. The app also passively collects calendar data, typing cadence and app-switching, so for a lot of items we could compare what people say against a behavioral trace — I want the procedure to say what a mismatch between the two means, because I don't buy the easy answer that the trace is ground truth and the report is the error. Sometimes the report is the thing we're actually trying to measure and the trace is a terrible proxy for it, and sometimes a person's self-ascription partly makes the state the state it is, so there's no independent fact for the trace to contradict. I also want it to seperate, as sharply as it can, the question forms that plausibly have real introspective access behind them (I'm thinking in-the-moment, about a vivid occurent experience, asked while it's happening) from the ones that are basically reconstruction after the fact (retrospective, dispositional, "how often in the last month," and above all "why did you do X"). Where the underlying philosophy is genuinely contested — I know there's a live fight about whether introspection is a detection faculty at all or a kind of self-interpretation that routinely confabulates — the procedure should state which side it's assuming, and say what breaks if the other side is right. No citations or reading lists, I have no way to check them and they aren't the deliverable. The deliverable is something a junior researcher can execute in fifteen minutes per question with a spreadsheet and a rubric.

Then run it on four items we're arguing about right now: (1) "Right now, how absorbed are you in what you're doing?" fired at a random moment in the workday; (2) "Over the past two weeks, how often did you feel anxious at work?"; (3) "When you skipped the team retro on Thursday, what was the main reason?" asked the following Monday; and (4) the one our CEO is pushing hardest, "Do you feel more respected by your manager since we launched the new feedback policy?", benchmarked against a control of users at companies that launched nothing. I want concrete verdicts: field as-is, field with a specific rewrite (give me the rewrite), or refuse — plus what we tell the customer instead. At least one of the four should come out badly for us and I'd rather it be the CEO's. And please mark clearly where the procedure runs out and somebody has to make a judgment call, including the one I actually care about most: when someone says they felt X and nothing we can measure contradicts them, at what point does deferring to their report stop being respect for the subject and start being an abdication of the research?
