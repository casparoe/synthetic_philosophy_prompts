I left school at sixteen and have never sat an examination in statistics, philosophy or anything else. For five years I have read on my own in the evenings, mostly about how people form expectations about their own projects and how often those expectations are disappointed — the material usually discussed under the headings of the planning fallacy, the inside view and the outside view, and the forecasting of one's own life by reference to what happened to other people. I now convene a study group of seven of us in a village hall: a joiner, two retired teachers, a district nurse, an apprentice electrician, a bookkeeper and myself. We have no library, no internet access during meetings, and between us no academic training at all, so everything we discuss has to be settled from the material in front of us.

Reading aloud together has told us nothing about whether any of us understands this material, because we all nod at the same sentences. So I have decided to set the group a proper written paper, timed and marked by me, and I want your help drafting the first question.

The question must turn on a difficulty that I have not seen faced squarely in anything we have read, and that I expect nearly all of my group to get wrong. The difficulty is this. We are told to take the outside view: find a reference class, use its base rate, and resist the siren song of our private knowledge of our own case. But the narrow reference class one is told to prefer is very often defined by a feature that the members of it reported about themselves, in advance, from the inside view. So entering the flattering narrow class does not actually escape the optimism; it merely launders it. And if one starts from the broad, unflattering base rate and then adjusts upward for the very feature that defines the narrow class, one has counted that feature twice. I want a question on which a candidate cannot coast past this by reciting the distinction between the inside and the outside view, because the question is about what to do once the two views have been mixed together inside the same table of numbers.

Please write the question around the following scenario and figures, which I have invented for the purpose. Keep my figures unless you think one of them is arithmetically incoherent, in which case tell me so and propose a correction.

A wooden boat restoration society has kept records for twenty years of full restorations begun by its members. There are fifty recorded projects. In each case the owner gave a completion date at the outset, and the society recorded whether the boat was finished by that date.

- Of the fifty, ten were finished by the original date.
- At the outset, each owner filled in a short form. Twenty of the fifty wrote that they had prepared exhaustively: a complete parts list, suppliers identified, hours budgeted. Nothing on the form was checked or audited by anyone.
- Of those twenty self-described exhaustive preparers, nine finished by their original date.
- Of the remaining thirty, one finished by the original date.
- Records exist only for members who were still in the society when their project ended and who returned the closing form.

The candidate for the question is a member about to begin a full restoration. On the opening form she has written that she has prepared exhaustively. She has also completed two smaller projects — a dinghy and a launch — each inside her original estimate. The society's records do not distinguish an owner's first project from a repeat.

The question should have five parts, worth twenty marks in total, to be answered in one hour in roughly eight hundred words, with pencil and paper and no calculator. I suggest the following, though you may adjust them:

- Part (i), four marks: which of the two rates, ten in fifty or nine in twenty, is the better starting point for her estimate, and why.
- Part (ii), five marks: another member reasons, "I begin at ten in fifty, because that is the honest outside view, and then I raise it because I prepared properly." Diagnose exactly what is wrong with this, and state a condition under which raising a base rate for case-specific information would be legitimate.
- Part (iii), five marks: should her two smaller completed projects change the number, in which direction, and what limits how far it may be changed.
- Part (iv), three marks: suppose she is afterwards told that three of the nine successes among the self-described preparers had hired a professional boatbuilder for part of the work, a fact she does not have about herself. What becomes of the nine-in-twenty rate for her purposes.
- Part (v), three marks: given the size of the sample and the way the records were kept, how much confidence should she place in the figure she has arrived at, and what is the mistake in treating it as exact.

Please give me the following five things.

1. The question exactly as it will be printed and handed out, including the scenario, the figures, the five parts with their marks, the time allowance and the word allowance, and a line telling candidates that everything they need is on the sheet.
2. A model answer of about seven hundred words, written so that a member of my group could in principle have produced it within the hour.
3. A marking scheme. For each part, list the separate moves that earn marks and what each is worth, and add a sentence on what distinguishes an answer that takes full marks from one that takes half.
4. A list of the errors that ought to cost marks. I expect it to include at least: quoting ten in fifty as though it were the objective figure and the other rate a rationalisation; counting the preparation twice; treating nine in twenty as precise because it came out of arithmetic; narrowing the class until it contains the candidate alone; reciting the vocabulary of the inside and outside view without applying it to any figure. For each, say how many marks it forfeits and say why it is an error rather than a difference of opinion.
5. A short note to me as examiner, saying what a fluent answer that has merely memorised the distinction would score, and at which part the marks would stop coming.

Two constraints on style. Nothing in the question or the model answer may cite a book, a paper or a researcher by name, and nothing may use a technical term that the scenario has not itself defined; we have no way of checking any of it and no reason to trust a name. All the arithmetic must be possible in the head or on paper in a few lines.
