My sister's boyfriend Dev does data work for a grocery delivery app — his whole job is basically turning what people tap on into "what people want" — and last week he and our friend Rhea (high school teacher, no econ training either, but she's sharp) got into a long argument at my kitchen table that I couldnt referee, and I'm still turning it over. It started because Dev said something like: nobody really knows what they want, we just watch what they pick, and after enough picks you can predict the next one, so "preference" isn't a thing inside your head, it's just a name for a pattern in what you do. Rhea said then his claim is the kind that can't ever be wrong, which for her is the same as not being a claim. If whatever you pick is by definition what you preferred, then "people prefer what they choose" is on the level of "bachelors are unmarried." A guy bets his rent money on a horse and loses — you say he preferred the gamble. She says she wants to quit her job and then doesn't quit — you say she preferred staying. Nothing anybody does could contradict it, so it isn't telling her anything about the world, it's a labeling system.

Dev said no, it can fail, and here I thought he had his best point: it predicts your picks have to hang together. If you take the apples over the oranges when you can afford both, you can't take the oranges over the apples next week when you can afford both — someone who does that has no pattern to recover, and his models break on them. So the claim is that people's choices cohere in a way a random chooser's wouldn't, and that's a real, checkable thing. Rhea said fine, but that's much weaker than what he started with, and also mostly false where it matters, because people pick differently depending on how the options are worded, which one shows up first, whether the price is per item or per package. Dev said yes, and that's literally what he's paid to exploit — but he said the exploit working proves there's a regularity there to exploit. Then Rhea brought up her uncle, who has paid for a gym membership for four years, gone maybe six times total, cancelled twice and rejoined twice. Under Dev's rule the correct description of that man is that he prefers paying $60 a month and not exercising to keeping the $60. Rhea said that's obviously a bad description of him: the truth is he wants to be in shape and also wants to stay on the couch, and the membership is him buying the feeling of being somebody who goes to the gym. Dev said sure — and "buying the feeling of being somebody who goes to the gym" is the preference, she just said it in more words. Rhea said that's the circular move again, he relabeled her description instead of arguing with it. Dev said the relabeling is the entire theory: preferences aren't a separate inner thing you hold choices up against, they're just the summary of the choices. Rhea said if that's so he has no business saying his app gives people what they want, only that it gives them what they pick, and those are not the same, because a 1am scroll through miserable stuff is picked and nobody thinks its wanted. Dev said that's her smuggling in a moral premise — deciding ahead of time that some picks don't count. She said no, he's the one who decided ahead of time that all picks count, and that's the smuggled premise, except hers can be argued with (you can point at a person who's furious about what they keep clicking) and his can't, because any evidence she offers just gets absorbed as one more preference. Near the end Dev half-conceded something: he said at work they do separate "the click" from "did they reorder after," and they treat the reorder as the honest signal because the click gets inflated by tricks. Rhea said that proves her case, since it means there is a fact about what someone wants apart from what they pick, and they've simply found a better proxy for it. Dev said no, the reorder is still a pick, they just weight some picks more. Then they ran out of steam and started talking about the game.

Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each step, not just who won overall? I'd especially like you to take on: whether Rhea's "it can't be wrong" objection actually lands, or whether Dev's hang-together answer defeats it; whose description of the gym uncle is the better one, and whether Dev is right that adding words isn't disagreeing; whether it's fair of Rhea to say Dev has a hidden moral premise, and fair of Dev to say the same about her; and whether the click-versus-reorder bit at the end settles anything or just restarts the same fight one level down. Also tell me what both of them were missing — my hunch is that partway through they stopped arguing about the same thing, and that there's a third position neither of them said out loud, something like "the pattern in the picks is real and useful and also isn't the same thing as what a person wants," but I couldn't get it past Dev and I'm not sure it survives his objection about the reorder. You don't need to name economists or point me at books, I mostly want the reasoning untangled and a verdict on each round.
