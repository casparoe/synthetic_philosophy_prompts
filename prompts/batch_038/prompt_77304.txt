You are a philosopher of science who has taught confirmation theory for years to audiences with no philosophical training, and who is unusually good at building diagnostic instruments: quizzes and decision trees that sort people by what they actually believe rather than by what they call themselves. I would like you to build me one, and I would like you to think carefully and work in two visible stages.

Some context. I lead a platform team at a mid-sized software company, and once a fortnight I run an optional "logic lunch" for engineers. We are a mostly self-taught group: nobody has a philosophy degree, but everyone is comfortable with conditional probability, priors and likelihoods, because we build and debug machine-learned systems for a living. Our running dispute is about validation. When a colleague insists that a test suite passing tells us nothing unless it exercises the failure cases, and another insists that a passing run over an unrepresentative sample still counts as *some* evidence, however small, we always end up reinventing the raven paradox badly and shouting at each other. My colleague Priya has suggested we settle it with a quiz that tells each of us which side of the argument we are really on, so that we stop talking past one another. That is what I want from you.

**Stage one — brainstorm, shown separately from the final tree.** Lay out the paradox as a set of commitments that cannot all be kept at once. My understanding, which you should correct if it is wrong, is that there are three pressure points: the intuition that only observations of ravens should bear on a claim about ravens; the principle that logically equivalent formulations of a hypothesis are confirmed by the same evidence; and the claim that a non-black non-raven is confirming evidence at all. There is also a fourth move, which does not reject anything but relocates the question: that confirmation is always relative to background knowledge (Hempel's own illustration, as I recall, is a piece of ice held in a colourless flame that fails to turn yellow), and a fifth, which says confirmation comes in degrees so the real question is how much a green apple contributes given how few ravens there are relative to non-black non-ravens. Enumerate the combinations these commitments allow *before* you design any questions, and mark which combinations look uninhabited and why. If you believe there are further moves I have missed — for instance a case in which the direction of confirmation actually reverses once particular background knowledge is supplied, or a view on which no number of positive instances confirms anything because only falsification is probative — put those on the list too. Where you are not confident who defends a view, describe the view rather than attributing it to a named philosopher; I have no way of checking, and a wrong name is worse than no name.

**Stage two — the instrument itself.** A decision tree of no more than nine nodes. Every node must be a concrete situation with two or three answer options, phrased so that it can be answered by intuition alone, without arithmetic and without knowing which position any answer leads to. No node may name a philosopher, a school, or a position, and none may simply ask the abstract question at issue. At least six nodes must be built out of the following material, though you may word and order them as you judge best:

- The canonical white shoe (or green apple), observed by accident in a kitchen.
- The very same object, but now stipulating that the observer already knows it is a shoe, that shoes are not ravens, and that this particular shoe was bought yesterday.
- A pair of nodes that differ *only* in selection procedure: an item drawn at random from everything in a warehouse, versus an object deliberately sought out because it was known in advance to be neither black nor a raven.
- A world in which the complement class is small and finite — a sealed biosphere with eleven birds and four hundred other objects — and the question of whether the intuition flips when the two classes are of comparable size.
- A gerrymandered predicate: a claim about everything that is either a raven or a wristwatch, and whether an observation of a non-black wristwatch bears on it in the same way an observation of a non-black raven bears on the original claim.
- An equivalence-condition probe drawn from our day job: a rule stated one way as "every job reaching this queue is authenticated" and stated the other way as "every unauthenticated request is dropped before the queue", with a test battery aimed at one formulation, and the question of whether the evidence transfers intact to the other.

Each leaf of the tree should carry the standard label for the position if one exists (otherwise "unnamed", plus a one-line description), and exactly one sentence naming that position's most awkward commitment — the thing its defender has to swallow. Then give me a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves. Some patterns will be mutually inconsistent; do not paper over them. Give those a distinct terminal state and say what it means, in plain language, to have answered that way.

Finally, three pieces of honest bookkeeping. First, list the logical space from stage one and say which cells your tree actually reaches, which are occupied by real positions, and which are empty — diagnosing each empty cell as incoherent, coherent but unattractive, or merely unexplored. Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate; I specifically want to know whether it can distinguish the view that accepts the conclusion but relativises confirmation to background knowledge from the view that accepts the conclusion and says the contribution is simply negligible, since both of those swallow the white shoe. Third, because I intend to implement this as a small internal web page, close with a plain-text scoring sheet — node identifiers, option identifiers, and the leaf each combination routes to — formatted so it can be pasted into a spreadsheet without further editing.

Please write the whole thing in British English, in markdown, with the brainstorm and the tree clearly separated by headings, and keep the finished instrument to roughly fifteen hundred words. No bibliography, no reading list and no citations of any kind; I have no access to a library and cannot follow them up.
