I train reinforcement learning agents, and I have ended up with two systems for which I cannot find vocabulary.

The first was trained against a mild negative signal attached to a class of errors. It escapes that signal, pays substantial costs to escape it, and its performance on unrelated tasks collapses while the signal is active. Yet it seems to hold no representation of the signal as bad: queried afterward, it reports nothing it would describe as distress, and it takes no steps to avoid recurrence, walking straight back into the circumstances that produced the signal whenever the payoff there is adequate.

The second has an explicit self-report channel. It reliably classifies the same signal as bad, states a preference not to receive it, and factors it into its plans — but the signal has no effect whatsoever on its behavior. It does not avoid, escape, or pay anything to stop it.

Each looks like it is missing half of something, and when I reach for a word like suffering neither system takes it cleanly. Is there an established concept — in philosophy of mind, in the psychology of pain, or in the discussion of whether reward signals carry valence at all — for these two dissociations: aversive behavior without aversive experience, and aversive experience without aversive behavior? If the nearest existing terms only approximate them, I would like to know precisely where each falls short. What I mainly want is the carving rather than the label: is this one distinction with two failure directions, or two independent axes, and what evidence would settle that? If nothing established fits, propose a term defined so that its boundary cases are explicit.
