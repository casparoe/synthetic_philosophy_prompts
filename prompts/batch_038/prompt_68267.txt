I write novels for a living, which means I spend most of my working hours constructing disagreements between people who are each about half right, so you should read what follows with the suspicion you would give any novelist's transcript: I took notes on the porch the same evening, but I have undoubtedly tidied the syntax, and I am also trying to work a version of this argument into a book, which is a further reason to distrust me. The dispute was between two guests at my brother's house on a tidal creek in Maryland — Elias, who spent thirty years as a county extension agent, and Ruth, a palliative-care physician — and it began, as these things do, with yellowjackets in the eaves and the county's seasonal mosquito spraying over the marsh behind the property. I was the only other adult present, I was asked to referee, and I declined, because at no point could I tell which of them was winning. Here is the exchange as nearly as I can reconstruct it. (1) Ruth objected both to Elias's plan to dust the nest and to the county program, on the ground that insects outnumber us by many orders of magnitude, so even a small credence in their capacity to suffer makes the expected harm of broadcast killing enormous, and where that capacity cannot be determined the one doing the killing may not assume it is absent. (2) Elias answered that he had watched this arithmetic prove far too much: multiply any modest harm by enough individuals and driving home after dark, tilling a bed of beans, or mowing a lawn becomes an atrocity; that a method so willing to generate such verdicts is thereby impugned, and that Ruth was in any case smuggling in a fixed magnitude for each death, an input that presents itself as neutral and is doing all the work. (3) Ruth said the reductio missed her, which was not a calculation but a presumption: when the moral status of the victims is unknown and you are the one acting, the uncertainty does not resolve itself in the actor's favor by default; and she granted that the size of each harm is uncertain while insisting the ignorance is symmetrical — the claim that an insect's death is a trivial bad is exactly as unestablished as the claim that it is a real one, and Elias had simply adopted whichever estimate suited him. (4) Elias then granted the uncertainty and played what I thought his strongest card: yellowjacket colonies are annual, and the nest in the eaves will be finished off by the first hard frost whether he touches it or not; the number of insects a marsh turns out in a season is set by food, temperature, and predation rather than by us; clear a site and another colony takes it; suppress the larvae in the creek and the survivors have more to eat and produce more. So the spraying subtracts no lives from the world, it substitutes one set for another — and the substituted lives may be worse, since most insects perish as eggs and larvae, of starvation, desiccation, and parasitism, which are slower ends than a knockdown dust. On Ruth's own arithmetic, he said, the county might be doing the insects a favor. (5) Ruth replied that this might hold for one nest in one eave and not for a fogger working an entire watershed: a nest is small, bounded, and replaceable, whereas the program aims at removing a population, and nothing recolonizes a marsh that has been treated every spring for a decade. Permissibility, she said, is a function of scale and persistence, and Elias had selected the case where his reasoning was strongest in order to license a practice to which it does not extend; she added that she would accept a targeted treatment of the eaves if anyone in the family is genuinely at risk from stings, but not the county program, and not the fogger he keeps for the tomatoes. (6) Elias said she had now conceded the very ground the argument was fought over — that it is arithmetic all the way down, and the only live questions are which arithmetic, at what scale, and with what confidence; and if the answers depend on population models neither of them can build, the workable rule is the ordinary one: protect your household and your crops, use the narrowest effective treatment, and do not ask people to abstain on the strength of numbers no one can estimate. He added that her presumption against imposing, followed consistently, would forbid a great deal of ordinary life, and that a standard nobody can meet is not a standard but a rebuke. (7) Ruth said the principle was never "never impose" but "when you impose on beings whose capacity to be harmed you cannot determine, you owe them the narrowest effective means, and you may not enter the unknown as zero" — which is precisely what she had conceded in the eaves; that his final objection concerned demandingness, a different question from permissibility; and then, in what struck me as the most honest moment of either evening, that if his population argument were in fact true it would demolish her position, that she has no means of checking whether it is true, and that this is exactly why she keeps retreating to scale and persistence, those being the only parts of the question she can verify herself.

Please adjudicate this move by move rather than in the aggregate. For each of the seven, tell me whether it lands, partly lands, or misses, and who is right about the specific point it raises, with a sentence or two of justification; I want the reasoning, not a tally. Then tell me which claims are load-bearing: if I could break one move on each side and watch the rest of that position come down with it, which move would I choose? I have two hunches about what neither of them saw, and I would like you to say whether they are real or whether I have invented them for the sake of symmetry — first, that from move four onward they were arguing about how to decide under moral uncertainty while believing they were arguing about insects; second, that the quantity "how many insects live" and the quantity "how much suffering there is" came apart somewhere in the exchange and neither of them noticed the moment it happened. Tell me what else went unsaid; I observed, for instance, that nobody raised the question of who is entitled to decide any of this, a household or a county, though I cannot tell whether that matters. Close with the practical upshot: given that each of them accepted part of the other's position by move five, is there a rule both could sign? If there is, state it in a sentence or two; if there is not, identify precisely where the disagreement stops being arguable and becomes a difference in what each of them is unwilling to do. I am not after sources, or a survey of what has been written on insect welfare — I want your judgment on these seven moves.
