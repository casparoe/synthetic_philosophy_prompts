I am drafting the technical section of a thesis chapter on Sen's liberal paradox, and I would like to settle the diagnosis of the impossibility before I commit it to prose.

The result is almost invariably introduced as a sibling of Arrow's theorem: the nondictatorship condition is swapped out for a condition of minimal liberty, and one is then left to ask which of Arrow's remaining conditions has to be weakened to make room for the liberal clause. My reading, which I would like you to examine closely rather than merely restate, is that the culprit is independence of irrelevant alternatives. In the Lady Chatterley's Lover case, the social ranking of the pair over which the prude is decisive appears to get dragged about by the lewd's ranking of those very states, and symmetrically for the lewd's pair; on that diagnosis, a variant of independence that screens off whatever one individual thinks about the contents of another's protected sphere ought to restore the possibility of a Paretian liberal while leaving the weak Pareto condition wholly intact.

Could you work through the prude/lewd profile explicitly — the three states and each individual's ordering over them — and identify precisely at which step independence is doing the damage, so that I can see what a screening-off reformulation would have to rule out? Two further points would be useful. First, how much of the derivation actually depends on requiring the social preference to be a complete and transitive ordering, as opposed to something merely choice-generating, since I have been assuming the stronger requirement throughout. Second, whether the weak Pareto condition really is the part of the argument that survives untouched, or whether retaining it imposes some constraint I have not yet appreciated.
