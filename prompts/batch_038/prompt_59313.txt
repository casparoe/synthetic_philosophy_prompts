I sit in on a reading group that mixes historians and statisticians, and last week two of the regulars — call them A and B — spent an hour arguing about whether long-run declines in violence are real. Afterwards each of them separately asked me to say who had won, and I couldn't. The exchange, as I wrote it down:

(1) A: The world is measurably less violent than it used to be. The recorded homicide rate in parts of Western Europe fell by roughly an order of magnitude between the late medieval period and today, and the decades since 1945 contain no war between great powers on the scale of the two before it. That is a finding, not an opinion.

(2) B: It's a finding about rates, and rates are per capita. The denominator has grown more than tenfold over the same span. Total harm need not have fallen at all, so what you've shown is that the average person is safer — a different and much weaker claim than "there is less violence."

(3) A: Per capita is the correct normalization. Violence is a disposition of a society, not a headcount. Two societies with identical murder counts and populations differing tenfold are simply not equally violent, and any measure that calls them so is measuring something else.

(4) B: Agreed on the arithmetic, but notice what you've bought: your measure now describes risk to a sample of one, while the moral question counts victims. And there's a second problem. The homicide category isn't stable across the series. Deaths medieval courts recorded as homicide we would now classify as accident, self-defense, medical error, or lawful state action. Projecting today's taxonomy backward isn't neutral measurement; it embeds our values in the baseline, and the direction of the bias is toward showing a decline.

(5) A: Fix the definition before you look, apply it uniformly, report the residual uncertainty. That is ordinary measurement practice, and a trend is then a fact about numbers rather than about our concepts.

(6) B: Ordinary measurement practice assumes stable instruments. Here the instruments are legal-administrative record systems whose own category boundaries we can only partly reconstruct, so uniform application is unavailable in principle, not merely difficult. And separately: even a clean series wouldn't license the inference people actually draw — that the decline will continue — because the process generating the numbers hasn't been the same process for eight centuries.

(7) A: Then you've conceded the trend and switched to the forecast. Those are different questions, and the first one has an answer.

(8) B: The trend claim only ever interests anyone because of the forecast. And if you admit catastrophic risk, the present era's expected total harm may exceed that of any past era, since the tail is new — which your per-capita series cannot represent at all.

(9) A: An unrealized probability isn't violence. Mixing occurrences with tail risks makes the series meaningless rather than more honest.

Please adjudicate move by move: rate each of the nine from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence of justification, and say explicitly where a move succeeds against the position its author thought he was attacking versus a weaker one. Then tell me which of B's objections A can absorb without giving up the core claim, which one genuinely defeats it, and whether "the world is becoming less violent" has a determinate truth value once every definition is pinned down — or whether pinning down the definition is itself the contested step. If both are missing something that would change the verdict, say what it is.
