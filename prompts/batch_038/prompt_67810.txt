In our reading group on logic and databases, one member — an engineer who builds data pipelines — has an electrical gloss for the principle of explosion, and it has become the group's default vocabulary. A single contradictory record, he says, is a short circuit: in classical logic the crossed wires blow the fuse for the whole house, so every light goes out at once; a paraconsistent logic is what you get when you install circuit breakers, so a fault in the kitchen takes out the kitchen and nothing else. Two claims are then run through this picture. The first is that explosion is a discovery about how contradictions behave — they propagate damage — and that classical logic is therefore simply unsafe wherever contradictions are to be expected. Our running example is a municipal permitting database in which the same parcel is recorded both as inside and as outside a flood zone, and the proposal on the table is to route such records into a quarantine table that the rest of the system reads around. The second, more ambitious claim is that the breaker picture is metaphysically neutral: installing breakers is engineering hygiene, so one can migrate to a paraconsistent logic without taking any position on whether there are true contradictions, and the dialetheism debate is beside the point for anyone who only wants a working system.

I would like an audit of the analogy itself, not of the slogans around it. Specifically:

1. The explicit mapping. Take each element — wire, current, the short, the fuse, the breaker, the remainder of the house's circuits, the load being served — and say whether its counterpart in the logic is a genuine structural match, a loose one, or nothing at all.

2. The disanalogies, sorted into those that bear on the two claims and those that are merely decorative. Three candidates I cannot adjudicate myself: explosion has no time, no locality, and no degrees of severity, whereas a short circuit has all three; a breaker is a device that detects and isolates, and it is unclear what in a logic plays the role of the detector, especially when we do not know which of our premises are the inconsistent ones; and triviality looks like a global property of a consequence relation, so treating it as something switched off room by room may be a category mistake rather than a simplification.

3. What the picture imports from the source that the target lacks. I suspect at least two things: that a contradiction is always a fault to be repaired rather than possibly a correct description of how things are, and that consequences flow causally from premises in the way current flows from a source, rather than being fixed by the closure conditions of a consequence relation.

4. Whether each of the two claims survives. On the second especially: does the breaker really leave the metaphysics untouched, or does describing a dialetheist's true contradiction as a tripped breaker already mis-describe it as a malfunction? And at the level of the concrete case — does quarantining the flood-zone records amount to adopting a paraconsistent logic at all, or to a policy about which records to trust, with the logic unchanged?

5. The places where the analogy fails in ways an engineer would treat as damning, and the place where it seems to succeed. I have a half-formed suspicion that the Curry-paradox route to triviality never passes through explosion, so that adding breakers can leave a house that still burns down; and that a relevance logician's rejection of explosion is not a breaker but a rewrite of the wiring code, constraining what may follow from what. Against that, the analogy looks genuinely apt for those paraconsistent systems that preserve classical reasoning for formulas not marked as inconsistent, where the breaker really does trip only in certain rooms — and I want to know whether that partial aptness rescues the neutrality claim, or whether it is precisely the case where the analogy works worst, since those systems were not designed to describe true contradictions in the first place.

6. Whether a better analogy is available — the contagion-and-quarantine variant of the same picture, or something else — and whether any single analogy can carry both claims, or whether the engineering claim and the metaphysical-neutrality claim need different pictures.
