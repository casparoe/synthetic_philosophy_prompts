I work on the policy team of a mid-sized social platform: American-owned and headquartered, but with roughly a third of our users in Europe, where several countries criminalize certain kinds of group-denigrating speech and impose removal duties on services like ours. A colleague who joined us from a civil-liberties organization has circulated a memo arguing that we should push back on both the legal duties and our own voluntary takedown standards. His central premise is a line he attributes to a 1927 concurrence by a Supreme Court justice, which he renders as "more speech, not enforced silence." When I looked it up, the sentence in full reads: "If there be time to expose through discussion the falsehood and fallacies, to avert the evil by the processes of education, the remedy to be applied is more speech, not enforced silence."

The memo puts the sentence to two different jobs. First, it argues that criminal penalties for group defamation are unjustified because the harm they target can be met with counterspeech, which we should fund and amplify instead. Second, it argues that when our own moderation team removes a post we could have answered, we are ourselves practicing the "enforced silence" the line condemns, so the platform should adopt something close to a no-removal rule for any speech that asserts a proposition, however false or demeaning.

Two things make me uneasy and I cannot yet articulate them. One is that the sentence as written is a conditional with an explicit antecedent about there being time, and my colleague's version drops it — while in the cases we actually handle, the targeted users tell us the counterspeech never reaches the people who saw the original post. The other is that the two jobs look different in kind: one concerns what a state may punish, the other what a private company should choose, and I am not sure the sentence travels between them.

My European colleagues press a third objection. On their account this speech is not a false proposition awaiting refutation but an act that degrades the standing of the people it targets, so counterspeech is not even a candidate remedy — the memo commits a category mistake. My colleague replies that this merely relabels the harm in terms chosen to make his argument inapplicable.

Could you lay out, as a menu rather than as a single diagnosis, the distinct claims this sentence and its truncated form might be making? For each reading, please: state it precisely, specifying whose remedy it is, in place of what, aimed at which harm, and asserted by whom against whom (a limit on state power, a duty of listeners, a claim about efficacy, a claim about how knowledge is produced, a claim about who may be trusted to judge); classify it as a platitude, a contestable empirical thesis, or a substantive normative thesis, and say what would have to be true for it to hold; and say whether it supports the first job, the second, both, or neither, noting how much of the support depends on the antecedent clause the memo omits. Then tell me whether the transatlantic disagreement is about the same claim or about different ones: which readings a defender of criminal group-defamation laws could accept without inconsistency, and which they must reject. And assess the dignity-harm exchange directly — is it a genuine counterexample, or a redescription?

I am not after case citations, doctrine history, or a reading list; I want the claims themselves. For each verdict you give, say how confident you are that it is right, and then single out the one point in the whole analysis you are least sure of, together with what you think would settle it.
