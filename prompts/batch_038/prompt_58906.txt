I cover science and research policy for a magazine, and over the years I've built up a pile of judgments about when it's rational to keep pouring money into something you've already poured money into. My editors think I'm inconsistent. I suspect they're right, but I want to know exactly where.

Here's the list.

1. Half-built stadium, anchor team leaves town. $900M already spent, $400M to finish, and finishing will lose money every year after that. Scrap it. The $900M is just gone and pretending otherwise is how cities wreck themselves.

2. Expedition, six weeks of fixing rope and burning supplies, summit push turns around at 2pm with weather closing in. Descend, obviously. Here "we've come this far" is literally the sentence that kills people, and I want whatever principle I end up with to say that loudly.

3. Two non-refundable tickets to two trips, I'd enjoy the cheaper one more. Go on the one I'd enjoy more. What something cost me is not a reason.

4. Light rail in a city I cover. First segment built for $900M, ridership half of what was promised. Extension costs $400M and, evaluated by itself, would lose money — but segment one also loses money alone, and the two together roughly break even. Extend. Very confident about this one.

5. A lab gets a ten-year, $10M grant tied to one specific long-shot program. Six years in, an easier project with better expected payoff appears. Stick with the program.

6. Same lab, same six years, same alternative — except they funded it out of their own overhead. No promises made, nobody to answer to, no one will ever know. I still feel they should stick with it. I know how that sounds. I feel it anyway.

7. A researcher who's spent 25 years defending a theory that's now clearly losing. She shouldn't just migrate to whatever's winning; she should first go back and check whether the anomalies that pulled her into the thing have actually been answered. Walking away from that reads to me like a betrayal of her earlier reasons, not just of her earlier self.

8. A researcher who's spent 25 years perfecting a technique that a cheap new method just made obsolete. Switch overnight. I'd think less of her if she didn't.

9. Hypocrisy data. When my editor killed an investigation I'd put 18 months into, I was furious and wrote a memo about the value of long reporting. Two years later I killed my own book project — same shape, comparable time sunk — after about four minutes of thought, and I don't regret it at all.

My tentative principle, which I'm sure its broken somewhere: past costs count only where they've changed the forward-looking situation — by creating obligations (5), by making the remaining pieces complements (4), or by leaving you holding evidence or judgment nobody else has (7, and 8 as the negative case). Bare "we've already spent it" gets zero weight (1, 2, 3).

Where it visibly struggles: 6, where nothing has changed except the money; and 9, where the same person got opposite treatment depending on who was holding the pen.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of them, that gets all or most of these? State it precisely enough that I could hand it to a junior reporter and get the same verdicts back, and tell me which case is doing the work for each clause. Then run it on three I haven't given you a verdict on — a fifth-year grad student whose method stopped working halfway through the dissertation; a space mission extended after its target turned out to be much less interesting than hoped; a family that refuses to sell a house they badly overpaid for. And be blunt about which of my verdicts the best version of the principle forces me to give up. If the answer is that 6 or 9 just has to go, say so — and then tell me why I find them compelling anyway, because that part I'd actually like explained.
