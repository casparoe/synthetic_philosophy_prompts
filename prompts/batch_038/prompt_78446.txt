I have just started work as a junior cataloguer at a mid-sized auction house, after a year of graduate study in aesthetics that I had assumed would be professionally useless, and it turns out to be the only part of my training that keeps coming up. My first assignment was helping to prepare a sale of Continental pictures, and I was handed our house's guide to attribution terminology along with the estimates. The ladder runs, roughly: the artist's name alone, meaning the house believes the work to be by his own hand; 'attributed to,' meaning probably by him but with reservations; 'studio of,' meaning made in his workshop by another hand under his supervision; 'circle of,' meaning by an unidentified contemporary closely associated with him; 'follower of,' meaning by someone working in his style but at some remove; 'manner of,' meaning in his style but of a later date; and 'after,' meaning a copy of a known work, at any date. I was told these are not flourishes. They are contractual. As I understand our conditions of business, the limited guarantee of authenticity attaches only when a lot is catalogued under the artist's name in the first sense; if you buy a 'circle of' picture and a scholar later establishes that it is a twentieth-century invention, you have no comparable recourse. And the rungs are not stable: a picture can be sold under a name in one decade and re-catalogued two rungs down in another, without a single molecule of it changing, as connoisseurship, technical imaging, and the relevant catalogue raisonné move.

What puzzles me is how much of the value the ladder appears to carry, and how little of it seems to be about the object. Two panels of the same date, subject, size, and, as far as I can tell standing in front of them, quality, differ in estimate by a factor of ten or more purely because of which rung they sit on. My grandmother left me a small oil on panel catalogued as 'after' a well-known Bolognese master; it sold for the low four figures, and a friend who has no interest in art told me it was lovely and that I should have kept it — which made me realise that if I had never seen the label I would not have known whether I was looking at a modest painting or a treasure. Meanwhile the ladder treats a candid nineteenth-century copy as an honest category with a real price, and provides no category at all for a modern imitation made to deceive, which would be worthless and criminal; yet the two can be visually indistinguishable, and the copy is the one that has a rung. And the workshop case troubles me most: a panel prepared by the master, painted by his pupil from the master's design, corrected by the master's brush in the final pass, and sold under his name to a contemporary buyer who expected exactly that arrangement, now sits several rungs below a fully autograph work — as though 'by Rembrandt' had always meant what it means to us, when the studio system it came out of did not draw the line that way.

So I would like a reconstruction of the theory this practice presupposes, and I want it to be honest about how much of a theory there is. Start by saying what view of the value of a work of art the vocabulary only makes sense under: what property is being graded, and why does it come in degrees rather than being simply present or absent? Then say which rival accounts the practice quietly rules out — in particular, a view on which a picture's aesthetic value is exhausted by what can be seen in it, a view on which value lies in the achievement or process that produced the thing rather than in its causal lineage to a famous individual, and a view on which value is simply whatever a community's institutions currently confer — and test each against the features I have listed, especially the copy-versus-imitation asymmetry and the fact that the guarantee tracks expert consensus rather than the object. Is the ladder a theory of value at all, or is it an epistemic and risk-allocation device — a calibrated way of disclosing uncertainty and assigning the cost of error to whoever is best placed to bear it — that happens to look like a theory of value from the bidding floor? I want you to distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly presupposes, what it merely fits comfortably with, and where it is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license exactly the same wording. Then address the coherence question directly: can the ladder survive the history of workshop production it is being used to grade, or does that history show the top rung to be a nineteenth- and twentieth-century import applied anachronistically? Finally, tell me what would have to change at the house — the vocabulary, the warranty structure, the way estimates are set, whether we would need an equivalent ladder for extensive restoration and for composite objects assembled from two originals — if we replaced the presupposed view with the one my friend implicitly holds, that you are buying a thing to look at and the rest is gossip.
