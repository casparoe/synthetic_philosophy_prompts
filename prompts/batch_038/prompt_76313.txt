I'm TA-ing an upper-level ethics seminar and we're in the "should the numbers count?" stretch — Taurek first, then the replies. A chunk of the class has settled into the view that refusing to add up harms across people *is* Taurek's coin flip, i.e. that anyone who won't aggregate owes you an equal-chance lottery and nothing else. I want to hand them a case that breaks that equation, and breaks it in the direction they aren't expecting: a non-aggregationist who is *more* willing to let the many go than Taurek is, not less. I don't want to just assert this in section — I want a scenario they can argue with on the page.

So: build me a thought experiment in which three views deliver three different recommendations about the same forced choice. (1) Taurek-style non-aggregation with equal concern for everybody and no special ties, so the coin-flip default is what's live. (2) A plain total-welfare aggregator, who has to come out for saving the five. (3) A strict pairwise-comparison view — the Scanlonian balancing method, but run in the "numbers skeptic" mode that Otsuka presses, where you compare each person's complaint against each other person's one at a time and never sum or vectorize — which has to come out *for the one*, so the five lose even though what they lose adds up to more. Constraints, which are where the difficulty is:

- one person vs. five. The solo harm has to be worse *for that person* than each of the five's harm is for *each of them* (that's what drives the pairwise verdict), while five of those harms have to clearly add up to worse than the solo harm (that's what drives the aggregator). Both comparisons should look obvious to an ordinary reader on their own — no torture-versus-hangnail grotesqueries, no resting the whole thing on a contested claim about interpersonal cardinal comparison. I want the ordinal verdict and the cardinal verdict to each feel unforced.
- everything certain. No lotteries, no probabilities, no risk, no ex ante / ex post gap to exploit. If the pairwise theorist could rescue their verdict by retreating to an ex ante argument, the case fails.
- realistic. A workplace, a small town, a rural utility, a hospital — whatever. No trolleys, no lifeboats, no transplant harvests, no magic drug with contrived dose fractions, no sci-fi.
- the decision-maker is a stranger to everyone, promised nothing, did not cause the situation, and has no role-based duty to any of them. I want Taurek's partiality escape hatch and any "but you're the one who put them there" duty both closed off, so we're comparing the theories on the merits.
- the scenario itself in one paragraph, 150 words max, written so a student who's read none of the literature can see the choice clearly.
- and don't smuggle the verdicts in. Nobody in the scenario announces a theory, no "everyone agrees that..." stipulations, no rigged details that only exist to tip one of the three. If a recommendation isn't generated by the view as it's independently understood, the case doesn't count.

After the scenario, run the check: for each of the three views, give the recommendation and the two or three steps that get you there, in that view's own vocabulary. Then tell me honestly which single feature of the case is doing the work — I suspect it's the split between the ordinal and the cardinal comparison, but say so if it's something else. Then dial-test it: what does each view say if the group is four, three, and two, and if it's fifty? I specifically want to know whether the pairwise verdict is *stable* across all of those sizes, because if it never budges no matter how many people are in the group, that's my whole point for the class — and if there's a size at which it flips, tell me where and why. Finally, how does each losing theorist attack the case: what does the aggregator say is rigged, what does the Taurekian say, and is either objection a genuine design flaw or just their disagreement restated? If you think the spec can't actually be met without rigging something, say that instead and tell me which constraint to drop.

Format: prose for the scenario and the walkthrough (no jargon without a one-line gloss, since it's a handout), then a table with columns Position | Verdict | Principle invoked | Step that decides it | Best attack from this position, and then a short JSON object of the case's dials so I can spin variants — fields: setting, solo_harm, group_harm_each, group_size, harms_share_a_type (bool), uncertainty, decision_maker_relation, aggregate_of_group_harms_exceeds_solo_harm (bool), pairwise_verdict_stable_across_group_sizes (bool), flip_point_if_any, feature_doing_the_work.
