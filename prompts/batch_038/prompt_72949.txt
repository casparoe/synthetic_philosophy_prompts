You are a professional philosopher who works on the logic and metaphysics of time, and I would like to ask you to do a piece of careful work for me. I am a high school student writing an independent study paper on the sea battle argument, and I have found that my own judgments about when a claim about the future is true refuse to line up into anything I can state. Please read all of my verdicts first, then brainstorm at least three candidate principles that might underwrite them before you commit to one.

1. Tomorrow there will be a naval battle, and the admirals have as yet decided nothing: the claim "there will be a naval battle tomorrow" is not true, and its denial is not true either. Anyone who asserted either flat out would be going beyond what there is to say.
2. Still, "either there will be a naval battle tomorrow or there will not" is true. I am confident of this one.
3. Same situation, except that the two fleets have in fact already been ordered to engage and the order is secret: now "there will be a naval battle tomorrow" is simply true, even though my evidence is exactly as weak as in case 1.
4. A fair coin will be tossed tomorrow: neither the claim nor its denial is true. A particular atom in a lump of uranium has about a 51 percent chance of decaying within the hour: I want to say the same thing, neither true nor false.
5. But if the chance that this atom decays within the hour is 0.999999, I do want to say the claim is true.
6. A claim about what I will freely choose for lunch tomorrow is not true now, even though I know my own habits extremely well.
7. If the battle happens, then it was true yesterday that it would happen; if it does not happen, then it was not true yesterday that it would, and it was not true yesterday that it would not either.
8. Cases 1 and 3 together seem to show that what matters is not likelihood but whether something already present settles the matter; cases 4 and 5 seem to show that likelihood is what matters. I cannot make these fit.

For your answer, please state the principle you think survives precisely enough that I could apply it myself, with a clause for each case above and an account of what does the work in that clause. Then give me its verdict on a case I have not considered — whether a claim about a battle two hundred years ago is true when no record, trace, or memory of it survives — and on one further case of your own choosing that best separates your principle from the runners-up. Finally, tell me honestly which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon: I would rather have a spare principle that costs me 5 or 7 than an elaborate one that preserves everything. No bibliography or list of sources is needed; I want the reasoning itself.
