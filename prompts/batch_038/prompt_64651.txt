For the past thirty-one years there has been a weekly reading group in our department that everyone simply calls the Tuesday Group, because it has met on Tuesdays since before anyone currently in it was born. It was founded by Helena Brandt, a mathematical logician who retired in the spring; next month it holds its last meeting, not because anyone decided anything, but because the four of us who kept the room booking alive have scattered and the department has told us the booking ends with the term. I have been asked to give the closing remarks — about seven minutes, so roughly nine hundred to a thousand words — to a room of sixty: a dozen logicians and philosophers of mathematics who were there in the early years, most of the current graduate students, several computer scientists who came for the programming-language sessions a decade ago and never left, and, which is the awkward part, two people from the dean's office who are considering whether to fund some kind of successor and who will be listening for exactly the wrong signal. Helena herself will be in the front row. She is eighty-one, hears everything, and has stopped pretending to enjoy speeches.

I was Helena's student, and the reason I can give this talk at all is an index card she handed me in my first week, which I still have. On it was the MU puzzle from Hofstadter's book: you begin with the string MI and you want to reach MU, using only four rules — (1) if a string ends in I you may append a U; (2) you may duplicate everything that follows the initial M; (3) you may replace any occurrence of III with U; (4) you may delete any occurrence of UU. I filled two notebooks trying, over three weeks, and was thoroughly ashamed of myself until Helena sat down and showed me that you count the I's: you start with one, doubling never turns a count that isn't a multiple of three into one that is, subtracting three doesn't either, and the other two rules leave the count alone — so no string reachable from MI can ever have a number of I's divisible by three, and MU, with none, is on the far side of a wall that no amount of industry will breach. I want that argument in the speech, in spoken prose, correct, taking no more than about a minute, with no board, no slides and no handout, so that the computer scientists and the people from the dean's office follow it on one hearing. It was, for me, the reason I eventually left the academy for industry, which I have always privately described to myself as jumping out of the system — though Helena had a standing objection to that whole way of talking: she thought nearly every claimed jump out of a system turned out to be a move inside a larger system nobody had bothered to write down. The speech must carry that objection rather than defeat it, and it must admit that I do not know whether my own departure was a jump or simply a much longer derivation inside rules I could not see. It must not resolve that question.

Beyond that, the constraints are mostly prohibitions. The puzzle may not become an inspirational poster: no talk of breaking the rules, thinking differently, or having the courage to step outside, and no use of the MU puzzle or of incompleteness as a metaphor for Helena's career, for grief, for institutional decline, or for anything else human — she despised that move and half the room knows she despised it. It may not promise that the Tuesday Group lives on in whatever the dean's office funds, and it may not be bitter that the Tuesday Group is ending; and it must not sound, even for a sentence, like a rebuke to two administrators who are being kind. It needs one moment that makes the room laugh and works for people who know no logic at all. Please attribute no quotation to anyone, real or invented, and name no book, paper, or theorem other than Hofstadter's puzzle where it comes from. Give me the result in four sections, in this order: **Reading the room** (100–150 words on what each subgroup needs from seven minutes and what that therefore rules out); **Closing remarks** (the speech itself, 900–1,050 words, with bracketed delivery cues only where timing genuinely matters); **The invariant, checked** (about 150 words laying out the counting argument step by step, so I can confirm the spoken version has not garbled it); and **Where this will go wrong** (about 200 words on the line I am most likely to flub, which passages risk reading as a dismissal of the people who might fund a successor, and what I should be prepared to say afterwards in the corridor to whoever comes up to me about it).
