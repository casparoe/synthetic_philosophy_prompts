I spent some thirty-eight years as an experimental physicist, most of them running a low-temperature instrumentation group, and I have taken up philosophy only since retiring — bringing with me, I hope, a working suspicion of hand-waving and a taste for cases in which the load-bearing assumptions of a position are made to carry weight in the open. I should like to commission a short story that tests one such position. I find that a carefully built imaginary apparatus exposes what a doctrine is really committed to more efficiently than any paraphrase I could manage myself.

The doctrine is Molinism. It takes its name from Luis de Molina, the sixteenth-century Jesuit, and its content is this: that God possesses a "middle knowledge" (scientia media) standing between his natural knowledge of every possibility and his free knowledge of the world he actually brings about. Middle knowledge is knowledge of the counterfactuals of creaturely freedom — of what any free creature would freely do in any complete set of circumstances — and, crucially for my purposes, these conditionals are supposed to be true prior to and independently of any divine decision to place that creature in those circumstances. God does not make them true by decreeing; he reads them, and then decrees in their light.

Please build the story around the following invention of mine. You may develop it as you see fit, but keep it internally coherent and do not ask your readers to swallow more physics than the plot requires. A collaboration has constructed a superconducting interferometer in which the visibility of the fringes depends, in a manner the group cannot eliminate, on a decision that a human subject has freely made *not* to take. The apparatus thus yields a reliable readout of what that subject would have chosen in a specified situation — but only for situations that never in fact obtain. Whenever the group subsequently arranges the situation, the subject chooses otherwise, or the records prove insufficient to establish that the situation was genuinely the same one; and after any such attempt the apparatus falls permanently silent about that subject-and-circumstance pair. The team's verification problem is therefore structural, not merely practical.

Their response is the engine of the plot. They begin to use the readouts providentially, in miniature: selecting which circumstances to bring about in order to obtain, by way of someone else's freedom, an outcome they have already seen on a printout. I should like the story to make plain what it feels like to be governed that way.

Please give me four characters of roughly equal intelligence, none of them a strawman: the experimentalist who built the device and who insists that a true conditional must have a truthmaker somewhere in the world's furniture; a philosopher of religion who defends the Molinist picture and who presses back with the claim that no actual entity could ground a conditional about an unactualised situation, which is precisely why the instrument's behaviour is to be expected rather than anomalous; a theologian in the Dominican line who thinks the whole enterprise misconstrues what God knows, holding that divine knowledge of such matters runs through God's own moving of the will rather than through a prior survey of it; and the subject herself, who must not be reduced to a probe — she has read the printouts, she has her own account of what her choices mean, and she has a view about whether being foreseen in this way costs her anything. The grounding objection and the objection that a choice foreseen and then circumstanced is no longer free should both be voiced by their most capable advocates, and answered, and answered again. Do not resolve the metaphysics by a twist, a deathbed insight, or an authorial wink; the ending should be earned and open.

Two further requirements. First, please write the whole thing in British English, in spelling and in idiom. Second, aim for between 1,800 and 2,500 words of fiction, followed by a separate note of no more than 300 words. In that note I should like you to distinguish what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this material: which parts of the Molinist framework are common ground even to its critics, and which claims — about the status of the counterfactuals, about their grounding, about the compatibility of foreknowledge with freedom, about the Dominican alternative — remain live disputes in which you would be wrong to present one side as the consensus. Mark clearly where the story takes liberties with the doctrine for the sake of the fiction, and where it leans on a reading of it that its defenders would not accept. I ask for that note because I have been reading long enough to know that a confident tone is not evidence, and I would rather be told where the ground is soft.
