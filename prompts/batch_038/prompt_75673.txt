"Gould and Lewontin's 1979 spandrels paper argued that a lot of what looks like an adaptation is really just a byproduct of building an organism at all, and they named these byproducts spandrels after the triangular spaces between the arches of St Mark's in Venice, which the builders were pretty much forced to fill in with mosaics. Their bigger claim was that most traits in any organism are spandrels rather than adaptations, because giving a body any particular shape automatically throws off a bunch of incidental features alongside the useful ones. That paper is also where the term exaptation comes from, meaning a spandrel that later gets recruited for a job, like feathers that started out doing something else entirely and ended up powering flight. So the upshot is that adaptationism is basically untestable, and if you're wondering whether something like the human chin or our taste for music is an adaptation, the default answer should be no, it's a spandrel until somebody proves otherwise."
