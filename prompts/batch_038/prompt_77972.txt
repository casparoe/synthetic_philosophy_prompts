So I'm a third-year PhD student and I have to hand in my dissertation prospectus in about six weeks. The committee wants roughly 3,000 words plus a one-page timeline, and they've told us up front that what they most want to see is a question that is both open and actually finishable, not a topic. My half-formed idea, which I've definately been kicking around too long: we had a reading group last semester and spent three straight weeks on eliminativism about belief and desire, and I came away with the strong feeling that both sides were talking past each other in a way nobody was naming.

The standard framing is whether "belief" and "desire" refer to anything real, and the standard moves are the familiar ones — folk psychology is a stagnant, explanatorily bankrupt theory (it doesn't handle sleep, mental illness, learning, skill acquisition, any of it), so its central posits are like phlogiston or vital spirit; or, content attribution is so holistic and indeterminate that there just aren't states of the kind beliefs are supposed to be; or, against both of those, the whole worry is misplaced because belief-talk is an instrument for prediction and interpretation rather than a description of inner mechanism, plus the realist rejoinder that without genuinely intentional states there's no psychology worth the name. My suspicion is that the debate never converges because "belief" is doing at least three seperate jobs, and each argument only lands on one of them. Roughly: (a) belief as a state type individuated by its causal-functional role in producing behavior; (b) belief as a state individuated by having truth or accuracy conditions fixed by its relations to the world; (c) belief as a *status* you take on when you avow something, assert it, or make a promise on the basis of it — the thing that makes you accountable and that other people can hold you to. I want to claim that the empirical/pessimism arguments mostly target (a), that the indeterminacy arguments bite (or fail to bite) specifically on (b), and that (c) is left almost untouched by both, which is strange because (c) is exactly where the self-refutation objection lives — you supposedly can't argue against belief without, on some account, occupying (c).

Turn this into a prospectus. I want a precise statement of the question — something with a determinate answer, not "is eliminativism true?" — and a real argument for why the existing positions leave it open, i.e. why neither the eliminativists nor the realists/instrumentalists have the resources to settle it as things stand. Then the method: I'm picturing a matrix of the main arguments against each of (a), (b), (c); then a defense of the claim that a *stable* eliminativism has to target all three at once rather than one; then working out what the eliminativist actually loses or gains by trying. Tell me why that method beats the obvious alternatives (straight conceptual analysis of "belief," or just waiting for the neuroscience to come in). Then say what a successful result looks like, including what I'd be committed to at the end of it, and give me an honest failure condition. My current guess is that the real risk is that the three-way split isn't clean — that on close inspection each argument already presupposes all three components, in which case the taxonomy is decorative and I've burned a year on it.
