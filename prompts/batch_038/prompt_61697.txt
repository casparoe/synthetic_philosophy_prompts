I'm a retired physicist — forty years of low-temperature measurements and equipment nobody uses now — and since I stopped going to the lab I've been slowly working through old books on the philosophy of science. Right now it's Mach, The Analysis of Sensations, in the Williams/Waterlow translation, which is a strange experience, because I grew up seeing "Mach number" written on blackboards and had no idea the man wrote sentences like this. Here's the opening of the "Introductory Remarks: Antimetaphysical":

"The great results achieved by physical science in modern times — results not restricted to its own sphere but embracing that of other sciences which employ its help — have brought it about that physical ways of thinking and physical modes of procedure enjoy on all hands unwonted prominence, and that the greatest expectations are associated with their application. In keeping with this drift of modern inquiry, the physiology of the senses, gradually abandoning the method of investigating sensations in themselves followed by men like Goethe, Schopenhauer, and others, but with greatest success by Johannes Müller, has also assumed an almost exclusively physical character. This tendency must appear to us as not altogether appropriate, when we reflect that physics, despite its considerable development, nevertheless constitutes but a portion of a larger collective body of knowledge, and that it is unable, with its limited intellectual implements created for limited and special purposes, to exhaust all the subject-matter in question. Without renouncing the support of physics, it is possible for the physiology of the senses, not only to pursue its own course of development, but also to afford to physical science itself powerful assistance."

Can you help me get a grip on what he's actually claiming here? Three things I'm stuck on.

First: when he says physics is unable "to exhaust all the subject-matter in question," is that a metaphysical claim about what there is, or only a claim about the reach of a formalism? My hunch — and tell me straight if I'm reading a twenty-first-century habit back into 1886 — is that its basically a domain-of-validity statement, the way we'd say a calibration curve is good inside the range it was fitted over and meaningless outside, with no claim whatsoever about what's going on beyond the range. Does the wording bear that, or does Mach need something stronger to get the rest of the book off the ground?

Second: the very next section runs on about his table and his friend — "But, for me, it remains the table at which I daily write" — and then about bodies as a sum-total of permanency. Is the "for me" doing argumentative work, or is it just colour on an illustration? If it's doing work, what exactly is it licensing him to say about bodies that he couldn't say without it.

Third: where would a working scientist of the Weinberg/Feynman stripe — the sort who thinks philosophy of science has never once helped anyone at the bench — have to plant their flag against this passage? Give me the sharpest version of that objection, not a strawman, and tell me whether Mach has already answered it inside these four sentences or whether he's exposed.

Please don't send me off to read other people's commentaries; I want to understand this passage. I'm perfectly happy for you to say where the text is too thin to settle something.
