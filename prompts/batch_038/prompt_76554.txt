I write a weekly column on science and ideas for a broadsheet, and I have been commissioned to produce a long feature on whether large language models understand anything — the sort of piece that must survive both the philosophically literate readers and an editor who has lived through too many breathless announcements. A year on this beat has left me with a fairly definite position, which for my own purposes I have come to call the modal bottleneck view. It has three strands. First: a system's symbols refer to things in the world only if that system has itself transacted causally with those things, or with others of the same kind, through some non-symbolic channel — perception, manipulation, locomotion. Second: input consisting wholly of discrete symbols produced by other systems cannot substitute for such transactions, not because there is too little of it but because of a principled gap — co-occurrence settles the relations among symbols and never which extra-symbolic items, if any, those symbols are relations to; it is the familiar predicament of the reader who tries to learn an unfamiliar language from a monolingual dictionary of that language, and goes round in circles. Third: consequently, a system whose entire commerce with the world is mediated by other systems' symbols cannot understand the subject matter of those symbols, however accomplished its use of them, and whatever else we choose to call its accomplishments. I should stress that on my view none of this turns on silicon, or consciousness, or the presence of an inner light: a biological being confined to the same informational diet would be equally cut off.

What I need from you is a map of this view's exposure — what would have to be true for it to be false. Please work through the candidate defeaters rather than listing generic objections, and be prepared to say which are decisive, which merely awkward, and which are not difficulties at all. At a minimum I want you to engage with the following, though you should add anything I have overlooked: the thought that reference is fixed by chains of transmission running through a community rather than by acquaintance, which would appear to hand a text-only system precisely what my first strand denies it; the human cases that seem to embarrass the view — the congenitally blind person's command of colour vocabulary, and the fact that nobody has perceived inflation, that nobody alive perceived the fall of Constantinople, and that ultraviolet radiation has never been perceived by anyone at all, so that if acquaintance is necessary my view looks committed to saying that no one understands those terms; the structuralist reply that distribution over a sufficiently rich vocabulary pins reference down up to isomorphism, together with the question of whether anything more than that could coherently be wanted; the dilemma over criteria, since if the operative test is behavioural indistinguishability then a text-only system might in principle satisfy it, whereas if the test is intrinsic reference then the view makes a claim no observation could touch; and the empirical question about multimodal systems, where I want to know what difference the addition of images, sound and action would have to make for it to count as confirmation of my view rather than as simply more data of the same kind.

Please also consider a pair of cases of my own devising. In the first, an archivist is confined from birth to a sealed room and learns everything there is to know about mountains, weather and crowds through correspondence with people who have met them; in the second, the archivist's many correspondents are replaced by a single fabulously well-informed intermediary, so that the causal chain from mountain to symbol runs through one mind instead of a thousand. Does my view give the same answer in both cases, and should it? Finally, I would like an honest assessment of whether the modal bottleneck view has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and, given that part of what it says is conceptual rather than empirical, whether that is a genuine defect or simply what a claim of this kind looks like. Rank the defeaters from fatal to awkward; say which of them a committed defender would themselves concede as counting against the view and which they would dismiss; state what the view predicts that its rivals do not; and tell me which single difficulty I ought to regard as most serious if I am going to have to defend the piece in print.
