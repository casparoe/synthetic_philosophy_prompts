I have spent a year trying to write the framing chapter for an interdisciplinary volume on moral revolutions, and what has stopped me is not the history but the logic of the disagreement. The chapter has to explain why three competing accounts of how practices like the Atlantic slave trade and dueling came to an end have failed to converge, and I no longer believe they are competing in any clean sense.

The first account is the interest-based one. In Capitalism and Slavery (1944) Eric Williams argued that British abolition of the slave trade in 1807 and of slavery in 1833 tracked the declining value of the West Indian sugar complex to British accumulation, and that the humanitarian story favored by the imperial historians he attacked was self-congratulatory ideology. Seymour Drescher's Econocide (1977) replied that on the eve of 1807 the British trade was expanding and profitable, that abolition ran against the material interest of the dominant classes, and that it was forced by extra-parliamentary mass petitioning. Note the awkward consequence: the most effective empirical attack on the interest explanation is routinely read as a vindication of the causal power of public moral opinion, so the historiography does not line up with the philosophical dispute that borrowed from it.

The second account holds that the practice ended because the reasons against it were sound and came to be widely enough apprehended; the abolitionists were right, and their being right did causal work.

The third is the honor account in Appiah's The Honor Code (2010), built on dueling among British gentlemen, footbinding in China, and the slave trade: a practice collapses quickly once the surrounding code is rewritten so that continuing it marks one as shameful or absurd rather than as a gentleman, with moral argument mattering chiefly as an instrument of that reclassification.

The stall has a specific texture. All three draw on the same cases, and each can absorb the others' evidence. Interest explains why a status shift was affordable; honor explains why arguments bite only when they reach the audience whose esteem is at stake; argument explains why the change took the normative direction it did rather than some other. There is also an unresolved asymmetry about testimony: the interest camp treats actors' stated moral reasons as rationalization by default, the argument camp takes them at face value, and the honor camp reads them as symptoms of a status game. Nobody has proposed a rule for when an agent's account of her own reasons counts as evidence. Meanwhile the comparative material points both ways. Dueling disappeared in Britain decades before it did in France, Germany, or Russia without any comparable difference in economic structure. In the footbinding case, an argument in the economic-historical literature holds that the practice persisted in regions where girls' hand-spinning and weaving labor was commercially valuable and receded where machine-spun yarn destroyed that income, which would give the interest camp a village-level mechanism for the very collapse the honor camp explains as a change in what counted as respectable.

My working hypotheses, offered as hypotheses and not as conclusions: (1) the three accounts address different explananda, namely feasibility and timing, thinkability and normative direction, and the mobilization of individual action against free-riding, so the question of which one caused the change is ill-posed; (2) the disagreement is really about what an explanation must supply, since on a difference-making or counterfactual standard all three are trivially causes, which is why no side ever loses; (3) the durable part of the dispute is a disagreement about the epistemic status of first-person testimony rather than about the past at all. Alternatively the debate is not stalled but merely underpowered, and the live question is one of causal ordering: whether the status classification or the cost structure moved first.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on which account is true and not a survey of the literature. Sort the disagreement into its components and say, of each, whether it is verbal, a difference about the explanandum, a difference about standards of evidence, a substantive empirical question, or bedrock. Support the sorting by what each side is actually committed to when it makes its case. Then tell me what would count as progress given that diagnosis: a distinction, a class of cases, a test about the sequence of changes, a formal model of status and incentives, a reformulation of the explanandum in advance, or something else. If your conclusion is that no progress should be expected, argue for that explicitly, and say whether the dispute would nonetheless change character if the question were fixed beforehand as: why did practice P cease to be performed by anyone in region R by year Y?
