I lead a small team building an internal evaluation harness, and I've written up an argument for our design review that now strikes me as proving far more than I intended. The setup: we score model outputs two ways — a step-level reward model that grades the intermediate reasoning in a worked solution, and an outcome verifier that only checks whether the final answer is right. The team is split on which to invest in, and I wrote the following to settle it in favor of the outcome side. Every premise seemed obvious when I wrote it, and the conclusion now seems obviously wrong, so something has slipped.

(1) What we ultimately want from the system is correct final answers; everything else is valued only as a means to that.
(2) A step-level reward is therefore at best instrumentally valuable: it is worth anything only insofar as it raises the probability that the final answer is correct.
(3) But conditional on the final answer being correct, how well the system got there makes no further difference to what we receive — a right answer from sloppy reasoning is worth exactly as much to us as a right answer from careful reasoning.
(4) So the value of a good process is entirely absorbed by the value of the correct outcome, with nothing left over to be credited to the process itself.
(5) It follows that process supervision is not a competing account of what we value but a credit-assignment convenience: it matters when the outcome signal is sparse or delayed, and in that role it is a stand-in we would drop if we had a cheap, exact, always-available outcome verifier.
(6) And whenever the two disagree — steps the reward model likes but a wrong answer, or the reverse — we should follow the outcome verdict, because that is the only one tracking what we actually want.
(7) Conclusion: "the model reasoned well" is not a property worth evaluating on its own, and we should stop building tooling that treats it as one.

I can't accept (7). We use the step-level model constantly, and it catches failure modes the outcome verifier is structurally blind to. But I would also rather keep (1) than (7), since (1) is just a statement of what our product is for — and I don't want the answer to be that I should start valuing reasoning intrinsically, because that sounds like it would commit me to preferring a beautifully argued wrong answer.

Which step do you reject? I want to know why that step looked plausible when I wrote it, what the correct version of it would say, and what I pay by giving it up — including whether rejecting it hands me back part of (7) after all, since the step-level model demonstrably rewards fluent-but-invalid reasoning too, and I have no clean answer to that.
