I'm running the second-year political philosophy module this term and the Arendt week has landed on me — 75 minutes, 22 students, a mix of people who genuinely read the assigned "Lying in Politics" and people who skimmed three pages of it on the bus, plus a handful of joint-honours journalism students who will immediately try to turn the session into a debate about this week's news cycle. I'd like you to work as someone who teaches this material regularly and thinks about political epistemology for a living — an Arendt scholar, not a study-guide voice. Before you plan anything, spend a handful of lines brainstorming what's actually hard about this idea for a twenty-year-old, i.e. where the intuitive grip gives out and what they'll reach for instead. Think carefully about that part first, then plan.

Here's my worry. The thing I do not want to teach is the post-truth homily — governments lie, spin displaced facts, Arendt saw it coming — because that's exactly what they'll take away if I'm careless, and it makes the essays worthless. What I want them to leave with is a stranger pair of claims. One: the deception she finds in the Pentagon Papers material mostly isn't the concealment of a truth that was already sitting there, because a lot of it concerned futures and policy options that weren't facts yet and could have gone otherwise — so the lie isn't covering something up, it's building a replacement texture. Two: the credibility slide, where the standard quietly stops being true-or-false and becomes whether the story holds with the audience, so the deceiver ends up calibrating himself against other people's believability and eventually deceiving himself too. And underneath both, the counterintuitive bit from the earlier "Truth and Politics": factual truth carries no necessity with it, it's contingent and fragile in a way rational truth isn't, and truthfulness is not automatically a political virtue because truth has something coercive about it. I want them to feel the gap between the two essays rather than smearing them into one moral about lying.

So give me a teaching plan, not an explanation. Specifically: the order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what — can I reach credibility before they've grasped that factual truths are contingent, or does that have to come first, and what has to be in place about the Pentagon Papers case before the objection "well isn't that just lying" stops having any force; the single case that should carry the session, with what it must have and what it must lack, and tell me why not the obvious picks (Watergate, whatever scandal is running this week, a made-up press secretary); the misconception map with the exact point in the sequence where each one gets headed off — my guesses are that she's a relativist or thinks all politics is lying, that the Papers just prove governments lie so there's nothing new here, that credibility is only about PR so this is really a 1970s media story, and that the self-deception stuff means she's doing psychology rather than politics; one question I can put at the end whose answers would tell me whether the distinction actually landed; and what I should say out loud that I'm leaving out and why. No readings, no bibliography, and don't summarise the essays back at me — I've read them, I just can't seperate them cleanly enough to teach them in 75 minutes.
