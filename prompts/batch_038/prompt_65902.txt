I record a fortnightly podcast on decision theory and its practical fallout, usually in front of an audience of twenty-odd graduate students, and next month's guest is a researcher who has spent the last decade defending evidential cooperation in large worlds. I want the questions to assume familiarity with the view rather than to re-litigate its basics, so here is my working understanding of it: in a universe that is spatially vast or perhaps infinite, there are enormous numbers of agents who are acausally isolated from us but who find themselves in decision situations relevantly like our own; if my choice is evidence about what agents in those situations choose, then cooperating now raises the probability that they act in ways that benefit me, and because there may be so many of them the expected value of cooperating can swamp the local payoffs. Our guest adds two refinements of his own that I want pressed rather than explained away. First, he holds that the relevant similarity class is fixed by similarity of decision procedure rather than by shared ancestry or physical resemblance, so that a machine intelligence of wholly different origin can be a trading partner while a chimpanzee cannot. Second, in answer to the charge that agents like him are fickle — cooperative now, indifferent as soon as cosmology comes back with a small universe — he defends what he calls recalcitrant cooperation: the claim that an agent should keep cooperating even after acquiring strong evidence that no such partners exist, on the grounds that a policy which switches off the moment it stops paying is not a policy anyone could have relied on in the first place.

Please draft ten interview questions in the order I would actually put them, and for each give me three things: the line of objection it instantiates; the reply he is most likely to give, stated as concretely as you can manage; and a follow-up that still bites if he gives that reply. I would rather have depth on the following than breadth, and I want at least one question devoted to each. Why the inference from my cooperating to their cooperating is licensed in this case, when a causal decision theorist will say it is the same error that leads an evidentialist to give up smoking in the lesion case, where the urge to smoke and the illness share a common cause and the cigarettes change nothing — and, relatedly, why the same reasoning does not oblige him to act on every other acausal correlation he can construct. How he sets the probability that relevantly similar partners exist and are numerous, and what he answers to the suspicion that agents who have so much as heard of this argument are a tiny and unrepresentative sliver of the reference class, so that their behaviour is poor evidence about anybody else's. What the argument actually tells an agent to do, given that he cannot know the values of his partners: how a trade is struck without communication, why promoting one bundle of values rather than another is the right guess, and what he says to the worry that the expected-value sum is dominated by a handful of very large and possibly hostile partners rather than by the friendly ones. Whether the symmetry that recommends cooperation with possible partners also recommends appeasing possible aggressors, since my complying would be evidence about the decision procedure of anyone inclined to pressure me from across the light cone, and how a recalcitrant cooperator is supposed to be distinguishable from a sucker. Whether recalcitrant cooperation is compatible with evidential decision theory at all, or is an admission that the real work is being done by a commitment device rather than by evidential reasoning. Whether the arithmetic here is a species of fanaticism — negligible probabilities multiplied by astronomical partner counts — and how he blocks that objection without also blocking the argument. And whether the whole view is hostage to cosmology, such that a finite or very young universe would refute it, and conversely whether any observation could count against it.

Two of my regular co-hosts will be on the panel and I want each of them served. One is a causal decision theorist who thinks the project mistakes a statistical artifact for a reason to act; the other is an astrobiologist who wants to know whether anything she could measure bears on the question one way or the other. Write no warm-up material about how he came to the topic and nothing that invites him to restate the position for the benefit of the audience; every question should be one he cannot answer well without conceding something substantive somewhere. Keep the register conversational enough to be read aloud, but do not pad the questions with framing or throat-clearing — I will handle the framing myself.
