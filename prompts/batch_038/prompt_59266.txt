I spent thirty-four years as a soil microbiologist, working mostly on nitrogen cycling in managed grassland, and I retired eighteen months ago. Since then I have been reading philosophy of science and, more recently, the literature on the safety of advanced AI systems, which strikes me as methodologically reminiscent of field ecology: everyone has a model, and nobody has the controlled experiment. I retain an emeritus office at my former institute, and last spring I was asked to chair a standing panel that reviews requests from laboratories to deploy agentic software — systems that are handed tools rather than merely asked questions: placing reagent orders, scheduling shared instruments, editing data-analysis pipelines, drafting correspondence to collaborators, and launching compute jobs. The panel has five members: two experimental scientists with no machine-learning training, our information-security officer, a postdoc who works on language models, and myself. We meet monthly, we receive six to ten proposals a quarter, and each receives twenty-five minutes; our output is a written list of conditions the requesting laboratory must satisfy, which the institute's director signs or returns. We have no capacity to inspect weights, no capacity to run adversarial evaluations of our own, and no authority over what a laboratory procures — only over whether a given deployment may proceed and on what terms.

What I lack is a disciplined way to classify the safeguards people propose to us. The safety literature distinguishes measures that presuppose a system broadly shares our aims and needs help getting them right from measures intended to hold even if the system does not share our aims at all — control, roughly, as against alignment — and I want that distinction converted into something my panel can run without expertise we do not possess. I am asking, therefore, for a numbered procedure applicable to any proposed safeguard: a one-line question at each step, in the order the steps should be asked; a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer to each; a rule for deciding whether the measure would still function under the assumption that the system is misaligned, that it knows it is being assessed, and that it can read its own deployment conditions; and explicit indication of the points at which the procedure stops and hands the matter to a human being, because the remaining question concerns institutional appetite rather than fact. It should also tell us how to treat a measure that is partly one thing and partly the other, and how to treat the case that troubles me most, namely that the check upon an agent is very often another agent of unknown disposition. Please then run the procedure on four proposals before us next month. First: every consequential action is passed to a second language model that may veto it, with the monitor's instructions written by the deploying laboratory. Second: tool use is rate-limited, and any purchase above five hundred euros requires human approval, the approver seeing a one-paragraph summary of the request composed by the agent itself. Third: the agent is fine-tuned on feedback from institute staff and issued a charter of research-integrity values in its instructions, on the grounds that it will then want what we want. Fourth, my own favourite, which I expect your procedure to demolish: the agent must state its reasoning in plain language before any consequential action, and the panel treats a satisfactory explanation as evidence of good intent. For each of the four, give the classification, the conditions you would impose if any, and the failure mode the procedure has exposed; do not soften the fourth for my benefit.

Keep the entire response under nine hundred words. Lead with the numbered procedure rather than with background or definitions, mark each step as either mechanical or a matter of judgement, and close with no more than three sentences on what the procedure cannot settle — in particular, where it quietly converts an estimate of probability into a decision about how much risk we are prepared to accept.
