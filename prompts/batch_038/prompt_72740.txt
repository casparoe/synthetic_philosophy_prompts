For the past few months I have been helping a small research group that ranks possible long-run futures in order to evaluate present-day policy, and the piece I have been left holding is the hardest one: how to compare outcomes that are infinite in extent. Our toy models assume that the future contains countably infinitely many people, indexable 1, 2, 3, … by generation, each with a welfare level expressible as a real number, and our baseline world — call it Eden — has everyone at +10. I have been asked to deliver a single ranking rule that the whole group can apply mechanically, and the only honest route I have found is to write down what I actually think about particular cases and then ask what, if anything, those verdicts add up to. Here they are.

1. **Lift.** Everyone at +11 instead of +10: strictly better than Eden. Not equally good, not incomparable.
2. **Addition.** Eden plus one extra person living at +1, everyone else unchanged: strictly better. Eden plus one extra person at −1: strictly worse. I flag that the added person is worse off than everyone already alive, since a colleague says that ought to sink the comparison; I disagree, and I take this verdict to rule out any principle on which only the welfare of people who exist in both worlds counts.
3. **Front.** The first 10^100 generations at +11, everyone thereafter at +10: strictly better than Eden, even though nothing differs in the long run.
4. **Tail.** The first 10^100 generations at +10, everyone thereafter at +11: strictly better than Eden, and strictly better than Front.
5. **Front-with-a-cost.** The first 10^100 generations at +11, everyone thereafter at +9.99: strictly worse than Eden. This is where I am most exposed. I cannot say how small the permanent per-person cost must be before the front-loaded gain wins, I insist the flip is sharp rather than vague, and the only place I can put it is exactly at +10 — which sounds arbitrary even to me.
6. **Sparse agony.** Everyone at +10 except that every 10^100-th person is at −1000, forever: much worse than Eden, and also worse than a world in which everyone is at +9.99 and nobody suffers at all. The second clause is the verdict I most want to keep and least know how to defend; it says that how badly the worst-off lives go matters in a way that neither summing nor averaging captures.
7. **Clump versus Stripe.** In Clump, generations come in blocks of 10^6 that alternate between all-at-+11 and all-at-+10; in Stripe, generations simply alternate +11, +10, +11, +10. Exactly half of all generations are at +11 in each. My verdict: equally good, because reshuffling who gets what cannot change value and the indexing is my artifact rather than part of the world. I realize this sits badly with 4, where I ranked Tail above Front precisely because of *when* the good lives fall.
8. **The gamble.** A cosmic coin is tossed until heads first appears; if that happens on toss n, one person's welfare is raised by (−1)^(n+1)·2^n/n and everyone else stays at +10. Since the chance of first heads on toss n is 1/2^n, the terms of the expected-value series are (−1)^(n+1)/n, which converge only conditionally. My verdict: I cannot rank this gamble against the certainty of +10, but I refuse to call the two on a par, because I think the gamble has no determinate value at all. Worse, I think that if a bookkeeper merely re-labels which toss-count carries which payoff, leaving the physical situation untouched, the value can be made to come out anywhere — and I find that absurd without being able to say which of my other verdicts it contradicts.

My own tentative rule is this: compare worlds by the limit of the average welfare of the first n people as n grows; if both limits exist and differ, that settles it; if they exist and are equal, settle it by the eventual net difference in the running totals; if either limit fails to exist, declare the worlds incomparable. I believe it delivers 1 through 5, flunks 6 and 7, and gives the right answer to 8 for what I suspect is the wrong reason. What I want from you is reflective equilibrium rather than a survey of the literature. Give me one principle, or a small handful of rivals, stated precisely enough that a patient graduate student could apply it to an unfamiliar case without further judgment calls, and for each clause tell me which of the cases above forced it. Then tell me what your preferred rule says about three cases I have not listed: (i) the first 10^100 generations at +11 and thereafter welfare alternating between +10.5 and +9.5; (ii) everyone at +10 except a sparse infinite set at +11, with all the rest at +9.99; and (iii) a world with infinitely many people at +11 and infinitely many at +9, compared with Eden. Invent one or two further cases of your own that you regard as genuinely diagnostic. Above all, be honest about casualties: which of my eight verdicts does your rule overturn, and if I may keep only one of the three that seem to be fighting each other — 2, 6, and 7 — which should I sacrifice for the greatest gain in simplicity? Two of my colleagues offer escape routes I would like assessed: one says we should abandon real-valued welfare for hyperreal or surreal magnitudes, the other says we should give up on ranking infinite futures and rank policies only by their finite effects; say whether either escape is genuinely available to me given the verdicts above, or whether each merely relocates the problem. Finally, say briefly whether any of this changes if the infinity is spatial rather than temporal — infinitely many people coexisting rather than endlessly succeeding one another — since those two colleagues insist that the spatial version is the only one worth taking seriously.
