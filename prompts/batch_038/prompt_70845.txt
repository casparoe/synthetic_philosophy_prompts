I have spent some years reading the arguments about whether an alignment claim should be understood as an average-case or a worst-case guarantee — benchmark scores against a chosen input distribution versus a bound that is supposed to hold for every input, including the ones nobody thought to sample — and I have noticed that almost all of this literature is argumentative prose. Nobody seems to have dramatised it. I would like you to write the piece of satire that does.

The form: a parody Service Level Agreement, of the kind a vendor of frontier models would issue to a large institutional customer, running to between 800 and 950 words, set out as numbered clauses with capitalised defined terms, closing with a Schedule of service credits expressed as percentages of the monthly fee and an execution block signed on the vendor's side by its Executive Vice-President for Measurable Outcomes. The voice should be pure commercial boilerplate: dry, precise, self-satisfied and entirely unembarrassed.

The customer, for concreteness, is a national flood-defence authority that proposes to let the model decide unaided when to open a particular set of barriers — a decision taken perhaps twice a decade, with no opportunity to pool outcomes across repeated trials. Invent the vendor; do not name a real one.

The vendor's entire position, which the document should never announce but simply enact, is that safety properties are averages over a measured distribution and that the remedy for a bad outcome is a partial refund. Work the following into the fine print, so that a reader who has thought about the distinction can reconstruct the whole argument from the clauses alone:

- A definitions clause in which Representative Workload, Normal Operating Conditions and Out-of-Distribution Input are defined circularly, the covered set being in effect whatever the vendor happened to measure.
- An exclusions schedule carving out adversarial prompting, novel circumstances, errors that compound over long horizons, and any input for which the customer cannot demonstrate precedent in the Benchmark Suite. Let this schedule grow conspicuously longer than the warranty it qualifies.
- A results clause reporting that the vendor observed no failures across some stated number of sampled rollouts and requiring the customer to accept that figure as sufficient evidence of reliability. Do not tell the reader what confidence bound such an observation actually licenses; let the number do the work unaided.
- Something that captures the asymmetry between a metric that averages and a loss that does not: credits accrue per incident, the town is lost once.
- A change-of-checkpoint clause permitting the vendor to substitute a retrained model mid-contract with the averages recalculated, and a further clause conceding, in the tone of a courtesy to the customer, that the Benchmark Suite is itself an object of the vendor's training process.

Two points I want handled carefully, because they are what separate satire from a straw man. First, the document must contain at least one clause that is simply correct: the vendor should observe, in impeccably reasonable language, that no artefact produced by statistical learning can be certified against all inputs, that a demand for a guarantee of that kind is a demand for a proof the physical world does not supply, and that this is not a defect peculiar to the vendor's product. The reader should feel that the vendor has a real point, and that the point nonetheless does not help him at all. Second, keep the irony entirely deadpan — no winking, no narrator, no closing twist, no authorial aside. The humour should come from the document being technically coherent and completely useless.

No real company, no named researcher, no quotation from any actual text, and no references or reading list; the piece must stand on its own. It should be readable by someone who has never thought about the subject, and painful for someone who has.
