ok so my friend and I have been arguing about this for like six months and I want to know what the target even is before I read anything else.

I help run a mutual aid fund in my town. Roughly 9k a month, donated by locals, and we decide where it goes at a Tuesday night meeting. My friend is completely convinced the answer is always obvious: spend it on whatever helps the most people, done. I cant shake the feeling that's wrong, but every time I try to say why I come out sounding like I want to let a bunch of people go without so one person gets spared, which isn't what I think either. So I want the requirements list for a theory about whether the numbers count. What does such a theory actually have to be able to do to be any good? Which situations does it have to get right, which lines does it have to keep from blurring, which things is it not allowed to just declare off limits — and for each requirement, the reason it's a requirement and not just a vibe. And I want you to sort them: which are non-negotiable, and which are really just one camp's gut feeling wearing a rule costume. I'm pretty sure some of the demands people make in this area are the second kind, and knowing which ones would save me a lot of time.

Here's what the theory has to survive, and please use these, they aren't shaped like the ones I've seen:

1. Same 9k. One family needs it for their kid's jaw surgery — no fund, the kid lives with pain and wrecked teeth forever. Or 225 households get 40 dollar grocery cards. Nobody starves without the cards, they're just way less strapped for a couple weeks. What has to be true for the 225 to either win or lose?
2. Same pot, but now it's one elderly woman whose house is 48 degrees and needs a furnace, versus 60 families getting utility arrears cleared. The one is worse off than any of the many. Does the answer flip, and does the theory have to explain why it flips or why it doesn't?
3. A donor offered to buy a defibrillator and train people on it. Put it at the senior center — 400 people use it, much higher per-person chance of cardiac arrest — or at the high school gym, 4000 people through it weekly, each with a much smaller chance. There is no particular person we're choosing between here, only odds. Does the theory still apply, and if it says "not my department," is that an answer or a dodge?
4. Five households each need something completely different — a wheelchair ramp, glasses, an insulin fridge, a roof patch, bus passes — and one household needs the surgery. There's nobody to line up one-to-one against the surgery kid. What does a theory that compares complaints pairwise do with a case that refuses to pair?
5. Pantry night, 40 people standing outside in the cold, one family in the office with something genuinely urgent. No procedure exists, no time to make one, somebody just decides. If the theory's answer is "flip a coin," what exactly does it owe us in that room?
6. My friend's version: one stranger versus the entire town. He says if my view gives the same verdict there as it does on the grocery cards, my view is finished. Is he right? Does an adequate theory have to contain some point where it says fine, at this many, it just settles it?
7. I take two ibuprofen now to head off a migraine that would be much worse tonight. I add up across time inside my own life constantly and feel zero conflict about it. If the theory says you can't add up claims belonging to different people, why can I add up claims belonging to different moments of me? Does it have to answer that, or can it shrug?
8. If the theory says saving the one and helping the 225 are both permissible, and says other stuff about slightly different pairs, can it end up ranking things in a circle? My friend says circular means somebody could walk you through a series of trades that leaves everyone worse off. Is avoiding circles a hard requirement or can a theory own the circles and explain them?

Also, the distinctions I think it has to hold onto without smearing: numbers don't matter at all versus numbers matter but don't get the final say; sparing someone a big harm versus handing someone a nice benefit; a named person in front of you versus a statistical somebody who only might exist; what one individual owes versus what an organization holding a budget owes; "you may help the one" versus "you must help the one." And I'd like to know whether the claim that claims don't add up and the claim that you should flip a coin are one claim or two, because people I've read seem to slide between them.

One thing that makes this concrete for me: the theory has to be usable enough that I can defend the decision at the Tuesday meeting, and then explain it to the family we turned down, without either group being treated as a tally mark. So "adequate" here means adequate for that, not for a journal.

Then tell me which single requirement kills the most views, and — this is the part I actually want — whether some subset of these requirements can't all be met at the same time. If the list itself turns out to be a proof that nothing can satisfy it, say so straight out rather than hedging.

Last, short version only, three sentences max each: how Taurek's no-aggregation view with the coin flip, Kavka's the-numbers-should-count reply, Scanlon's pairwise balancing of complaints, a straight maximize-the-good view, and the threshold hybrid where numbers count up to a point, do against the list — and which requirement each one dies on if it dies.
