I'm a retired experimental physicist — most of my career was precision measurement, thermometry and calibration work — and I've only come to philosophy and welfare economics late, so treat me as smart but not an insider. Please take the role of someone who knows social choice theory and the philosophy of measurement equally well. Think carefully, and before you settle on an answer, brainstorm the candidate diagnoses out loud and then kill off the ones that don't survive.

The puzzle: interpersonal comparisons of utility have been argued over since Robbins threw them out of scientific economics in the 1930s, and as far as I can tell nobody has budged anybody. The Harsanyi aggregation crowd proceeds as though cross-person comparisons are available; the Robbins and Little line says every such comparison is a smuggled value judgement; Sen's partial comparability and the applied equivalence-scale people sit somewhere in between; and after nearly a century the camps look exactly where they started.

My hypothesis — push back hard on it, don't flatter it — is that this was never an ethical disagreement, it's a metrological one wearing ethics clothes. In my line of work you can compare the readings of two instruments only if the scale has a non-arbitrary origin and unit, which is why comparing the temperature of two beakers involves no moral stance whatsoever. That's precisely what expected-utility theory refuses to hand you: von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities are fixed only up to positive affine transformation, so every person's zero point and every person's unit are free parameters. But if that were the whole story, the dispute should of been closed by a representation theorem decades ago, and it wasn't. So my diagnosis is either wrong or badly incomplete.

What I want from you: is this a verbal dispute, a hidden empirical one, a clash of bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for, or two groups answering different questions under one label — one asking whether a common scale exists, the other asking whether we're entitled to assume one? Show where each side's actual argument turns, whether the affine-freedom point is decisive or a red herring, and tell me straight what would count as progress here: a theorem, a measurement result, a distinction, or nothing at all and why.
