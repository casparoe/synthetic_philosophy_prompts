I teach a 75-minute upper-level seminar on space and time, about 14 students, most of whom have had one metaphysics course and have read the Newton bucket stuff plus a bit of Einstein but nothing much beyond that. I want an in-class activity that makes the substantivalism vs relationism debate stop being a definitional stalemate and start costing people something. No lecture beforehand, and nobody should hear the labels substantivalism/relationism, or the name of the argument about the hole, until the debrief — during play you should only be allowed to say things like the points and the distances.

The constraint that makes it hard: absolutely no physical apparatus. No spinning buckets, no swivel chairs, no hanging anything from the ceiling. My room is a normal seminar room with a table, and I want the whole thing to run on paper, pencils, index cards and dice. So whatever makes them feel the difference has to come out of record-keeping and betting, not demonstration.

Here's the shape I have in mind, feel free to improve on it. Split them into bookkeepers of two kinds. One kind keeps a ledger where every particle has a permanent name and every entry is a statement about which named point of the grid a particle sits at on each tick of a clock. The other kind keeps a ledger that literally can't express that — they may only write down separations and bearings between particles, plus how those change tick to tick. Give each bookkeeper a little apparatus to generate worlds, say a grid and a bag of tokens and a die, so both kinds can produce a full history of some small world. Then, and this is the part I most want you to design carefully, have the room make predictions with points on the line: some scoring scheme where you earn or lose points depending on whether you correctly say what the world does next, and where both kinds of bookkeeper get rounds where they win and rounds where they lose. I especially want the rounds where relationism loses to be built in, because the usual classroom version only ever shows substantivalism looking extravagant. Make at least one round hinge on whether a world is turning or merely being carried around, where the distance-keepers find they have nothing to write down and the grid-keepers know exactly what happens to the shape of the water.

Then the sting. At some point, without telling anyone, the facilitator quietly relabels the grid — a shift or a shuffle that leaves every single separation and bearing untouched — and the two groups have to bet real points on which of two histories is the actual one, or on whether anything at all changed. I want the room to physically discover, in their own handwriting, that one ledger says the world is different now and the other says nothing happened, and that there is no procedure they can run, inside the game or outside it, to settle who's right. They should run into that before anyone names it. And I want a moment where a group tries to escape by declaring that the two relabeled histories are really the same world — let them make that move, and then make them pay for it, so the debrief can show exactly what gets given up there.

So: full rules, what I need to hand out, a scoring scheme that works without money, a tick-by-tick timeline for the 75 minutes with rough minute counts, and a debrief that maps specific things that happened at the table onto the actual positions and the actual argument — including honestly that my index-card shift is not the same thing as what general relativity does, and saying what the gap is. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this: where students will game the ledger rules, where they'll get bored, which round is likely to collapse, and what I should say to keep it moving. And be frank about which feature of the debate a paper game just cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply with my own mouth afterwards.
