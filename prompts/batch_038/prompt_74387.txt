I run a small recorded conversation series — forty-odd regular listeners, tracked in a spare room on a microphone that reliably picks up the refrigerator — and I have been reading rationalist blogs and their comment threads since roughly the Overcoming Bias days, which is in fact how I came to G. E. Moore's *Principia Ethica*: not through a philosophy department, but through a living-room reading group that spent six weeks arguing about the naturalistic fallacy and lost half its membership to attrition. Next month's guest is a philosopher who defends a broadly Moorean axiology as a live systematic position rather than as a chapter in intellectual history. As I understand him, his view has four load-bearing parts: that the principal intrinsic goods are certain states of consciousness, above all the appreciation of beautiful things and personal affection between people; that one discovers such goods by considering a candidate thing in isolation and asking whether its existence would be worth having even if nothing else whatever existed; that the value of a whole is not in general the sum of the values of its parts, so trade-offs must be assessed case by case rather than aggregated; and that these facts are apprehended by a faculty he is content to call intuition. He is charming and slippery, and he has a habitual deflection: when a case is put to him whose verdict he does not like, he remarks that the case is under-described and declines to weigh it.

Please draft nine to eleven questions that get past the deflection and engage the substance. I would like at least one question on each of the following, and I want the pressure to be real rather than merely topical. First, the isolation procedure itself: if organic unities is right that value emerges from relations among parts, in what sense can any part be considered in isolation without altering it, and does the method smuggle in exactly the relational structure it claims to bracket? Second, the two-worlds test — one world as beautiful as can be conceived, another as ugly as can be conceived, neither of them ever to be seen by anyone — and what his answer commits him to: if the beautiful one is better though unappreciated, what is left of the claim that value resides in states of consciousness; if he says appreciation is doing all the work, why is his view anything more than a refined experientialism in which the natural world is merely instrumental? Third, friendship as a supreme good held together with impartial maximization: what his account says about partiality, about the worry in the neighborhood of the one Bernard Williams pressed against consequentialist treatments of acting for a loved one, and about the fact that he appears to value the friend as an object of appreciation — so that if the friend grows dull or disfigured the good itself seems to shrink. Ask him whether that is a bite-the-bullet moment or a misreading. Fourth, comparability under non-additivity: if wholes are not sums, what procedure does he actually have for ranking two candidate futures, and does the doctrine make the view either inert or merely a vehicle for his own preferences? His answer here will probably be that Moore never claimed value was additive, so build the follow-up accordingly. Fifth, the epistemology: what distinguishes a deliverance of intuition from a report of taste, what he says to systematic and culturally patterned disagreement, and whether his axiology is best read as the theoretical rationalization of a particular early-twentieth-century literary sensibility. Sixth, implications he may not welcome — that his view seems to oblige him to preserve beautiful things no sentient being will ever encounter, that it ranks the aesthetically impoverished lives of the poor low in a way he will not want to endorse, and that if the good is a conscious state of the right character, it is unclear why an artificially induced or simulated one is any worse. And finally, a question on what would distinguish his thesis from the purely psychological claim that people happen to like beauty, together with one on what evidence could count against the view at all.

For each question, give me a single line stating where the pressure is and what concession it is designed to extract, plus a follow-up keyed to the answer he is most likely to give; for at least three of them, supply a second follow-up for use if he retreats to the under-description move. Arrange the sequence so that the conversation escalates rather than jumps around, since we have about forty-five minutes of questions and I want the epistemological worries to land after he has already committed himself to specific verdicts. Include exactly one question on which I expect him to score a genuine point against me — I would rather concede gracefully on the recording than pretend the isolation test has no force — and mark it as such. Do not write biography questions, do not ask him to define the naturalistic fallacy, and do not write anything a well-read listener could answer from the flap copy. If any part of my summary above misstates Moore, or states as settled something that is contested, flag it before the question list so that I do not embarrass myself on tape, and for each question note briefly whether Moore himself grappled with the difficulty or whether it arises only for a contemporary defender. I do not want a bibliography or a reading list attached, and please do not invent a title for his book or put any words in quotation marks: describe positions rather than quoting them.
