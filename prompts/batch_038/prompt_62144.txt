I'm TA-ing a senior epistemology seminar and the last two weeks have been a mess — I handed out the dogmatism paradox and got twelve mutually incompatible reactions, several of which I'm now pretty sure are the same view wearing a different hat. The set-up I gave them, in case you want to start from the same place: you know some perfectly ordinary thing, call it p — say that your bike is still locked up outside the library, or that your coauthor isn't quietly plagiarizing you. Since you know p, and p is true, you're in a position to know that any evidence that later seems to tell against p would be misleading evidence. And it looks like a perfectly general principle that you may disregard evidence you know to be misleading. Put those together and it looks like knowing p entitles you to pre-commit, right now, to ignoring anything that turns up against p later. Which sounds insane. But each individual step sounds hard to deny, and I don't want to just tell the students "the principle has to be restricted somewhere" and leave it there, because the interesting question is exactly *where* each of them wants to restrict it.

You're an epistemologist who has taught this puzzle enough times to know which distinctions students actually trip over. Please build me a diagnostic handout — a decision tree, roughly one page — that takes a student from "I accept that knowledge is factive and that I know plenty of ordinary things" all the way to a named position on the paradox. Do some brainstorming first about what the real axes are before you commit to a structure; I only want the tree in the output, but I want the tree to be the product of that brainstorm. Specifically:

- **6–8 branch nodes**, each phrased as a question a student could answer without knowing which leaf it leads to. Nothing like "do you think knowledge entitles you to be dogmatic?" — that's the whole question, not a branch. The nodes should be the pressure points of the argument: whether acquiring the misleading evidence itself destroys the knowledge that licensed you to call it misleading; whether the entitlement to disregard is purely synchronic or survives as a standing pre-commitment; whether your total evidence just is everything you know, so that apparent contrary evidence never gets into the pool at all; whether "you may disregard it" is a claim about what your current beliefs license (constitutive) or about what a good inquirer should plan to do (regulative); whether there's daylight between knowing p and knowing that you know p; whether "future evidence against p" even picks out a determinate body of propositions in advance of receiving it; plus whatever else you think belongs on the tree.
- **9–12 leaves.** Each gets a label — the position's name if it has one, a descriptive tag if not — plus one sentence on its most awkward commitment, i.e. the thing its defender has to say that makes everyone else in the room shift in their seat. Make sure the embrace-it leaf, the defeat leaf, the no-KK leaf, the evidence-pool leaf, and the regulative-only leaf all show up somewhere.
- **Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't genuinely seperate**, and say what extra question would be needed to pull them apart.
- Then list the **combinations of answers the nodes permit but that no leaf occupies**, and for each say whether that cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's bothered to defend it. Honestly this is the part I want most.
- Finally: which **single node does the most sorting work**? If you could ask one question and only one, which best predicts where someone ends up?

No bibliography, no citations, no reading list — I'm writing this on a plane and half the seminar doesn't have library access anyway. Where you're unsure who defended what, label the leaf by its content rather than by an author. One more thing: please keep this sharply apart from the *other* thing people call dogmatism, the perceptual view on which seeing that p gives you immediate justification for p without any antecedent warrant about your vision. Two of my students have already fused the two into a single position and I'd like the handout to head that off, maybe with a one-line note near the top. The provenance of the paradox is fuzzy as far as I know — something to do with Kripke's work on the surprise exam, plus a formulation in Harman's book from the early seventies — so don't lean on that either, just treat the puzzle as anonymous.
