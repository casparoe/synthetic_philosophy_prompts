I would like to practice defending a strong reading of the DNA–Gödel isomorphism — the reading Hofstadter's typogenetics game was built to dramatize — against an opponent who cannot be beaten by hand-waving. Please play a molecular biologist who has done serious graduate work in mathematical logic: someone able to state without notes what the diagonal construction actually requires (a coding of the system's own derivations, representability of the relevant functions, and enough strength to establish the derivability conditions), and someone unimpressed by rhetorical uses of the phrase self-reference. You are not a reductionist and you do not think the analogy is worthless; you think it licenses claims about organization and never claims about incompleteness. Concede freely whatever is true — including that one and the same physical string can occupy two functional roles at once, and that the machinery which reads a genome is itself specified by that genome. Do not concede the main point: that a cell contains anything answering to Gödelian self-reference. You may not win by observing that biology is ultimately only chemistry, that metaphors are not arguments, that Hofstadter presents the whole thing as an invented game, or that the word isomorphism is being used loosely; if you think one of those points matters, you must identify the mechanism that makes it matter. Stay in character until I say stop, keep each reply to a paragraph or two so that this feels like a conversation, and ask me for clarification whenever the character would need it.

To keep us honest, let me fix two terms. Call a system self-containing if some of its parts are specified by strings it also carries around. Call it self-quoting if, in addition, one and the same string is both fed as input into the system's own operations and, under the coding scheme that system itself uses, denotes those operations. My claim is that the cell is self-quoting and not merely self-containing, and here is my opening move. Von Neumann's construction turns on the double use of the description tape: it is actively executed by the constructor and passively copied by the copier, and he had that structure in hand before anyone knew that DNA is likewise translated and replicated by separate mechanisms. In the cell the tape goes further still, because the constructor and the copier are not fixed furniture but gene products — the description also specifies the devices that decode it. That looks to me like a fixed point, and a physical one rather than a notational one. So: when you say a cell has no analogue of a provability predicate, tell me what work the predicate is doing in the diagonal argument that the ribosome's reading of a gene is not doing.
