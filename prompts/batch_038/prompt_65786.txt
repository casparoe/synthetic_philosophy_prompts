I am writing a handout for a reading group on higher-order evidence and I have constructed an argument I cannot disarm. The case is modeled on double-reading in screening radiology. Our imaginary regulator requires two appraisers of equal training and equal seniority to judge, independently and without contact, whether the submitted trial data — call it E — support the claim that the drug works, call it H. Discordant judgments are not averaged and not referred to a referee; the protocol simply records a flag and instructs both appraisers to treat the dossier as unsettled.

Suppose I am the first appraiser. I read E, it strikes me as supporting H, and I believe H. The protocol then informs me that the second appraiser, working from the same E under the same instructions, judged that E does not support H. Here is the argument I built out of that:

(1) What I rationally ought to believe is settled by my total evidence, and my total evidence includes everything I have learned about the trustworthiness of my own reading of the evidence.

(2) The second appraiser's dissent is strong evidence that my reading of E is untrustworthy: we are equally trained, we saw the same data, and neither of us has any reason to prefer our own reading to the other's.

(3) Evidence that my reading of E is untrustworthy is evidence against the claim that E supports H.

(4) So my total evidence now contains a strong case against its own first-order component, and all things considered it does not support believing H.

(5) But whether E supports H is a matter of E and H. It is not made true or false by anyone's reading of it, including mine.

(6) E remains part of my total evidence after the dissent. Either E supports H or it does not; if it does, then by (5) it still does after the dissent, and by (1) what I ought to believe is settled by my total evidence, of which E is a part.

(7) Take the case in which E does support H. Then (6) tells me I ought to believe H and (4) tells me I ought not. This conflict cannot be settled by appealing to further evidence, because by (1) I have already taken account of all the evidence there is.

(8) Nor can it be settled by giving up (5). If the dissent could make E cease to support H, then what E supports would depend on how E is read — but (5) is the only thing keeping the question whether the drug works a real question rather than a report on our psychology.

(9) So in the case where E supports H, rationality requires me both to believe H and to withhold it, and the sole remaining course is to go on believing H while holding that my total evidence does not support believing H. That is epistemic akrasia, entered into knowingly.

(10) Nothing in (1) through (4) depended on there being a second appraiser. I know that any reading of evidence I perform might be untrustworthy, and that knowledge is itself part of my total evidence.

(11) So by the same steps, for every belief I form by reading evidence, my total evidence fails to support it.

(12) Hence (1), applied consistently, requires universal suspension of judgment from anyone who knows they are fallible, and knowing akrasia from anyone who does not suspend.

(13) Universal suspension of judgment is not rationally required of me. I have good evidence for a great many things, and a standard that forbids me to believe them is no standard of rationality.

(14) Therefore (1) is false.

I want to keep (1) and I want to keep (5): dropping (1) looks like abandoning the idea that rationality is evidence-responsive at all, and dropping (5) looks like collapsing the question whether the drug works into the question how the dossier reads to us. I am also unwilling to pay for the escape with (13). Tell me which premise to reject, or which inference is illicit — and if you think more than one is, rank them by what rejecting each costs. For the step you pick, explain why it looked unobjectionable when I wrote it, what it was silently assuming, and what I lose elsewhere by giving it up: about disagreement, about peerhood, or about the relation between a belief and the assessment that produced it.

Lead with your bottom line in your first sentence — the step you reject and the reason for rejecting it in a single clause — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and no restatement of the argument or of the question.
