I cover science and ideas for a monthly, and I've been working on a piece about what happens to people's judgement when the odds aren't written down anywhere — climate, pandemics, new tech, that sort of thing. Went along to a behavioural economics thing in London last month and came away mildly cross, because everyone kept using "ambiguity aversion" as though it were one tidy phenomenon, and I couldn't tell whether they meant the same thing by it each time. So I did the obvious stupid thing and spent the weekend writing out every case I could think of where I had a gut reaction about a bet with unknown odds, and sorting them into yes and no. Here they are, roughly in the order I hit them.

The classic urn: 90 balls, 30 of them red, the other 60 black or yellow in some split nobody will tell me. Bet pays if the ball I draw is red vs pays if it's black — I take red, no hesitation. Same urn, red-or-yellow vs black-or-yellow — I take red-or-yellow, also no hesitation. I'm aware those two together are supposed to break something. Second urn, also 90 balls, but this one was filled by someone who genuinely doesn't know what's in it, versus the first where there's a definite split I'm just not being told — and here's the thing, I'd pay the same for a red bet in either, which I didn't expect, since the second feels "more unknown" in some sense that the first doesn't. Two bookmakers: one I know to be sharp offers me evens on a match I know nothing about, a mate offers me evens on a match I follow closely — I take the mate's, obviously, but I'm not sure that's ambiguity at all rather than just my own knowledge. An expert says a coin is "roughly fair" and won't be pinned down, versus a coin I've flipped 200 times myself and got 101 heads — I take mine. But if the expert's vagueness is pure coyness, the coin actually being exactly 50/50 and them just enjoying being mysterious, I still feel the pull towards mine, which seems like it can't be about the real odds at all. Stakes: for £2 I'll take either ball out of the urn and honestly don't care; for £2,000 it's a flat no on the unknown, and I can't see how that's anything other than the size of the number doing the work. Insurance: I'd pay more to cover a house where the flood people publish a clean "1 in 200" than one where they say they haven't got enough data — so I'm buying the known number even when the unknown one might turn out smaller. Two urns, identical unknown composition, one filled by a stranger and one by my brother, who I'm confident wouldn't stitch me up: I prefer my brother's. And then the ones that go the other way, which is what really scuppered me — I actively prefer the mystery holiday, the unlisted restaurant, the band with no reviews. Structurally the same deal, unknown payoff distribution, except there I'm paying for the ambiguity rather than avoiding it. Finally the pair I keep going round on and can't fix: I would not buy a house whose flood risk is unquantified at almost any discount, I'll eat at a place with no hygiene rating without a second thought, and I'd put a large chunk of my savings into a business idea whose odds of working nobody on earth could state. All three are unknown-probability outcomes with real money on the line and I give three different answers. Oh, and one more that might be a different animal entirely: a bet on whether some technology exists in ten years, where I can't even list the possible outcomes properly, feels categorically worse to me than an urn where I just don't know the colour split, even though in both cases the probability is unavailable.

So — is there a principle, or a small handful of competing ones, that actually generates these verdicts rather than being retro-fitted to them? I'd want it stated precisely enough that I could run a new case through it myself, and I'd want you to say which of my cases motivates which clause, because I suspect the honest answer is that "ambiguity aversion" is three or four things wearing one label and I'd like to know where the seams are. Then give me its verdict on two or three cases I haven't listed — here's one I'd genuinely like settled: a doctor offers me either a treatment with a well-established 70 per cent success rate from trials on people nothing like me, or a treatment with no trial data at all but a mechanism that looks plausible; which does the principle say I should take, and does it also say I'm being inconsistent if I don't? And be straight with me about the cost. If a neat principle saves all of my verdicts only by acquiring six clauses and a special provision for family members, I'd rather hear that a simpler one overturns the urn-brother case or the mystery-holiday case, and which of my verdicts you'd sacrifice and why. I want to know which of the two you'd actually recommend I put in the piece.
