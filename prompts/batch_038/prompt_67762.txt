I am preparing a ninety-minute workshop for a mixed audience of historians, philosophers of science, and researchers who work on complex systems, and I need a pre-reading handout that sorts participants by what they actually believe about contingency in history rather than by what they call themselves. My concern is that without such an instrument the discussion will collapse into a stale determinism-versus-free-will fight, which I do not think is the real axis of disagreement, and that everyone will announce themselves a champion of contingency without noticing how much they disagree with one another.

Please write the handout in American English, and keep it to roughly the length of four printed pages.

Part one: the space of positions. Lay out four to six axes along which views about historical contingency genuinely come apart. State each axis as a claim a participant could accept or reject in ordinary language, and attach to each one a concrete case that separates the two answers. I want the following axes included, formulated in your own terms:

- An axis separating the claim that some small antecedent made the difference to what happened from the claim that what happened was unlikely beforehand. These come apart, and the handout should make the gap explicit, using a case in which a process is exquisitely sensitive to small perturbations while the distribution over large-scale outcomes remains stable — the roulette-wheel structure, so to speak. A participant who conflates the two should be able to see, from the handout, that they have conflated them.
- An axis separating contingency that operates by gradual amplification of tiny differences from contingency that operates by discrete branch points after which either path becomes self-stabilizing. The two have different implications for how much counterfactual speculation is legitimate, and I want that difference spelled out.
- An axis separating pivots that turn on somebody's decision from pivots that turn on events nobody chose: weather, harvest failure, epidemic, the timing of a death, the accident of who happens to be in a given office.
- Whatever further axes the space genuinely requires, including at least one concerning whether claims about how things might have gone describe the world or only the selectivity of the narrative form we impose on it.

Part two: the cells. For each combination the axes generate, say whether anyone occupies it. Where a position has a name in the literature, give the name; where it does not, mark it unnamed rather than inventing an attribution. Real occupants may include, for instance, the paleontological view that replaying the history of life would produce a wholly different outcome, the rejoinder that developmental and functional convergence makes outcomes broadly repeatable, the long-run structuralist view that events are foam on the surface, the view that early-adopted standards lock in and become unshakeable, and the deflationary view that contingency talk is an artifact of storytelling. For every empty cell, diagnose the emptiness: is the combination incoherent, merely unattractive, or genuinely unexplored and worth a paper? Close this part by naming the two or three occupants that come closest to collapsing into one another, and say what would have to be true for the collapse to be complete.

Part three: the instrument. Write eight to ten items that a participant can answer in about fifteen minutes. Each item should present a concrete situation with two or three answer options. No item may name a person, a book, a school, or an ism; participants should be answering questions about what would have happened, not about which label they prefer. Draw on families of case such as the discovery made independently twice within months, the battle whose timing was set by weather, the assassination that failed by a margin of inches, the technology adopted early for reasons that no longer apply, and the reform that arrived a generation late because of one death. You may mix well-attested episodes with invented ones, but every invented episode must be marked as invented. At least two items should be constructed so that a participant whose professed self-description and whose answers come apart are detectably inconsistent with themselves, and the scoring key should say what to make of that. Include the scoring key itself: a mapping from answer patterns onto the positions laid out in parts one and two, plus a note on which patterns are unlikely to co-occur.

Part four: limitations. Flag every pair of positions your instrument cannot really separate, and identify the axis responsible for the failure. Then address two ideas that matter to me and say honestly whether the items can detect them. First: a case in which one person's choice was decisive only because everyone else expected it to be decisive, so that the fragility of the outcome depended on people's beliefs about how settled it was — a second-order contingency that my axes may or may not capture. Second: a case in which the small cause did change the immediate event, but the large-scale outcome arrived anyway within a decade by an entirely different route. If your items cannot distinguish a participant who holds either view from one who does not, say so plainly instead of padding the quiz. Also state whether the design of the instrument itself presupposes an answer to any of the axes — for example, whether counterfactual claims have truth values at all — and mark that presupposition for participants.

Do not include a bibliography, a list of further reading, or any citations, and do not invent titles, dates, or attributions. Where a real episode carries weight in an item, use one you are confident about and describe it only in as much detail as you can keep accurate; where you are unsure, invent the episode and label it. Any position you are not confident is actually defended by anyone should appear as unnamed rather than as a guess at who holds it.
