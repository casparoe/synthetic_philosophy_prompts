My friend and I argued for the better part of two hours about Boltzmann brains last night, and I still cannot locate the break in the chain. He laid it out like this.

(1) If a theory says observers of type A will be vastly outnumbered by observers of type B, and A-experience and B-experience are indistinguishable from the inside, then anyone who accepts the theory should expect to be a B.

(2) Statistical mechanics says that in a sufficiently long-lived universe, ordered low-entropy systems arise by fluctuation, and the probability of a fluctuation falls off steeply with its size — so a bare brain accompanied by a coherent-seeming experience is enormously cheaper than a whole ordered cosmos with evolved observers and a real past.

(3) Any cosmology that is both eternal toward the future and governed by ordinary thermodynamics therefore predicts that essentially all of its observers are fluctuation-observers.

(4) A fluctuation-observer's memories, apparent records, and apparent telescope data are chance configurations, bearing no correlation to any actual past.

(5) So conditional on any such cosmology, my memories — including my memory of the data that seemed to confirm it — are almost certainly false.

(6) That data is my only route to the cosmology.

(7) Hence any such cosmology is self-undermining: the better it accommodates my evidence, the more strongly it implies that the evidence is worthless.

(8) Nothing in (1)–(7) depends on how large the fluctuation population is, only on its being larger; and nothing depends on cosmology in particular, since (7) is just the general point that a theory can entail the unreliability of the evidence that supports it.

(9) So the upshot is general: observation-selection reasoning cannot support any theory whose observer population is skewed toward systematically deluded observers, and thermodynamics-plus-eternity is merely the first casualty.

He also says the usual escape — "then a cosmology that predicts I am a fluctuation-observer is disconfirmed by my experience of being one" — is unavailable, because by (8) the identical move would license discarding the evidence against every theory with such a skew, at which point evidence stops constraining anything.

I want to keep (2); I have no appetite for revising statistical mechanics. I would also rather keep (6), since dropping it looks like surrendering inference to the best explanation across the board. So: which step do you reject, why was it so easy to accept when I heard it, and what does the rejection cost? In particular, if the casualty is (1), am I obliged to give up the typicality reasoning I use elsewhere — treating the apparent fine-tuning of my own universe as evidence for a large ensemble of universes — or is there a principled line that preserves typicality there and forfeits it here? And is there any way to run this argument so that it indicts the cosmology without, by (8), indicting every empirical theory at once?
