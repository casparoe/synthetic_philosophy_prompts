I have spent fifteen years reading other people's reviews of novels, including occasionally my own, and I have just come across an essay in a literary quarterly that claims to explain why none of that reading can amount to knowledge. Its case runs like this:

"A judgment of taste cannot be transmitted by testimony. When a critic writes that Middlemarch is profound, the sentence is not a report about the novel but an expression of the critic's own pleasure wearing the grammar of a claim, so there is nothing there for a reader to take up beyond the fact that a certain sort of person was moved. This is what philosophers call the acquaintance principle: the judgment must rest on experience of the object itself. It follows that someone who has read no criticism is no worse placed than someone who has read all of it; that criticism is at bottom elaborate advertising; and that the situation is not improved by locating better critics, since a disagreement about taste is not a disagreement about anything."

The prose is confident and I suspect parts of it are right. I can see that the claim that Middlemarch is very long passes cleanly from one person to another, while the claim that it is profound seems not to pass at all. Yet I read criticism constantly, and it reliably alters what I find when I finally open the book.

Please audit the passage claim by claim. Which of its statements would a philosopher working on aesthetic testimony accept, which are defensible simplifications, and which rest on a conflation? I am particularly unsure whether the acquaintance principle and the view that judgments of taste express nothing but the speaker's feelings are one thesis or two, and whether the conclusion about criticism survives their separation. Finally, tell me what the essay assumes without arguing for, and where the genuinely unresolved question lies once the overstatements are stripped away.
