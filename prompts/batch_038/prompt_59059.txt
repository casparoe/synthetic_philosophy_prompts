I've been arguing with my flatmate about split-brain patients and I think he's got me in a corner I can't get out of. Here's his chain, roughly as he put it:

(1) A subject has unified experience when their experiences are co-conscious — when there is something it's like for that subject to have them together.

(2) Experiences are co-conscious only if the neural processes realising them are integrated, in the sense that information carried by one process can be used to guide the behaviour and the reporting driven by another.

(3) In a patient whose corpus callosum has been fully severed, the two hemispheres are not integrated in that sense. Flash a picture to the right visual field and they name it; flash a picture to the left visual field and they sincerely say they saw nothing, while their left hand, reaching behind a curtain, picks out exactly the object that was shown.

(4) So the commissurotomy patient has two streams of experience, not one.

(5) But nothing about (2) or (3) is special to surgery. In an intact head too there is information in one process that another process cannot use: peripheral vision can't guide a fine reach; you can't report what your visual system did to recover depth; a blindsight patient carries visual information that never reaches their report; and what your semantic memory turned up half a second ago isn't available to the machinery now parsing the syntax of this sentence.

(6) So by (2) the intact person isn't one subject either — each of us is a loose federation of little subjects, most of which can't see most of the others.

(7) And once the number of subjects is unbounded and vague, "there is exactly one subject here" is false of everyone, so there are no unified subjects at all and the unity debate was about nothing.

He says he's perfectly happy with (7), it's just eliminativism, nothing to see. I'm not happy with it. I'm quite sure I'm one subject, and I'd sooner keep that than keep (2). Trouble is (2) looks like the only bridge I have from brains to persons, so if I burn it I can't resist (4) either — I'd be stuck saying the patient is one person, which the left-hand-picks-out-the-object business seems to contradict outright. And when I suggested that he's just stretching "integrated" until it means whatever he needs it to mean, he said no, integration plainly comes in degrees, that's the whole point, and the degrees are what make (7) follow. I couldn't tell whether that was a reply or a restatement.

So: which step is doing the dirty work, and what does it cost to cut it? Is there any way to hold that the split-brain patient really is disunified while the rest of us really aren't, without leaning on (2) or something like it? And should I be worried about (5) on its own terms — do the intact-brain examples even belong in the same category as a severed commissure, or has he quietly shifted what has to be integrated?
