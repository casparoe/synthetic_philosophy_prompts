So I'm on the board of a worker co-op — about 40 of us, we run a commercial kitchen and a delivery operation — and Thursday we're voting on whether to start doing our internal accounting in labor-hours instead of dollars. One member is pushing it hard and he circulated a write-up of the old calculation debate to make his case, lifted from some YouTube essay I think. Im sympathetic to the proposal tho, I just want to know whether his history holds up before I have to defend it in a meeting. Here's roughly what he sent around:

"Mises's 1920 argument was that socialism is impossible, full stop: with no private ownership of the means of production there are no market prices for capital goods, so a planning board literally cannot tell whether a given project is worth undertaking. Hayek then added the knowledge problem — the information needed to plan is scattered across millions of heads and can never be gathered into a database, which is why his objection was never really that there are too many equations to solve. Lange answered that a socialist planning board could simply do what the market does, setting prices by trial and error and instructing managers to minimize average cost and produce where marginal cost equals price, so socialism could calculate after all, and Mises basically conceded the point. The debate was then considered settled in Lange's favor for a couple of decades, until the collapse of the USSR made it look like Hayek had been right all along. But the real verdict came later: Walmart and Amazon now coordinate more economic activity internally, with algorithms, than the Soviet state ever did, so we know the calculation is doable in practice — socialism was just missing the hardware."
