**Question 3 (30 points; suggested length 700–900 words).**

(a) State the Church–Turing thesis as exactly as you can. In stating it, identify what the thesis is a thesis *about*: what is claimed to coincide with what, and what is the status of each side of that coincidence?

(c) Some writers argue that if a machine could be built which decided the halting problem — for example by exploiting an idealised spacetime in which one observer's unbounded history lies entirely within the past of another observer's finite one, or by a device that sets and reads a physical magnitude to unlimited precision — then the Church–Turing thesis would be refuted. Evaluate this argument.

The student's answer:

---

(a) The Church–Turing thesis says that a function on the natural numbers is computable in the intuitive sense — that is, there is a finite procedure for obtaining its values which a person could carry out with paper and pencil, following fixed rules and without any appeal to insight or intuition — if and only if it is computable by a Turing machine. Put another way: the class of effectively computable number-theoretic functions coincides exactly with the class of recursive functions.

It matters what each side of that biconditional is. The left-hand side is an informal notion, a piece of pre-theoretical practice: what logicians and mathematicians had in mind when they said a method was "mechanical." The right-hand side is a precise mathematical object defined in 1936. The thesis is therefore not a theorem internal to any formal system, because one of its terms has no formal definition; it is a claim that a precise concept has successfully captured an imprecise one. Note also what it is *not* about: it is not a claim about what physical systems can do, nor about how fast they can do it. It concerns procedures.

(b) There are two sorts of support. The first is the convergence of independent explications: the general recursive functions (Gödel, following a suggestion of Herbrand's), the λ-definable functions (Church and Kleene), Post's combinatorial systems, and Turing's machines were all developed separately and all turned out to determine one and the same class of functions. This is a striking coincidence that cries out for explanation, and the thesis is that explanation. The second is stronger. Gandy showed that any discrete deterministic machine satisfying four quite weak idealised physical constraints — among them a bound on how much information can be packed into a given region and a bound on how fast one part of the machine can influence another — computes only recursive functions. Since real machines obviously satisfy those constraints, the thesis has something like a derivation from principles about mechanisms rather than resting on a catalogue of coincidences.

Neither amounts to a proof, and none could: proving it would require quantifying over informal procedures, and there is no formal theory whose objects are those. But the thesis is falsifiable in principle. A counterexample would be a specific function together with a specific finite procedure for computing it, plus a proof that the function is not recursive. No such thing has been produced in nearly ninety years.

My classification: it is a conceptual claim, fixed by a successful explication of an informal notion, rather than an ordinary empirical hypothesis. Empirical hypotheses are tested by sampling instances; here we are not sampling, we are finding that a definition keeps working.

(c) The argument in the question gets things backwards, though interestingly so. The devices described would indeed settle the halting problem, if they could be built. But building them would not show that effective calculability outruns Turing-computability. As I said in (a), the thesis is about procedures that a person could carry out, and neither device is such a procedure: in the first case the person would have to survive an unbounded amount of their own proper time, and in the second they would have to set a magnitude to infinitely many digits. So the thesis is untouched.

This shows, I think, that the thesis is not empirical after all but true by stipulation: once we accept Turing's analysis, "computable" simply means "Turing-computable," and the equivalences between the λ-calculus, recursion theory, and Turing machines are no longer surprising but trivial. It also follows that there is no such thing as a counterexample to the thesis in any sense whatever. The halting problem makes the point nicely: the function that returns 1 for the index of a machine that halts and 0 otherwise is perfectly well defined and can be written down in a single line, yet it is provably not Turing-computable. Definable functions therefore outstrip computable ones. The Church–Turing thesis must accordingly be read as a claim only about what procedures reach, not about what functions exist — which is why it is unfalsifiable without being empty.
