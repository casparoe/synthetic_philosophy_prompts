In my logic tutorial last week two people ended up going head to head over whether Carroll's tortoise dialogue has anything serious to teach us, and I still can't work out which of them I agree with. Here's roughly how it went:

(1) Dana opened by saying the tortoise is a joke with a target and nothing more — it's a reductio on treating logic as one more empirical science to be studied from outside, and the correct moral is that you just cannot make sense of deduction if you insist on every inferential step being backed by a further premise. Any attempt to mine it for a theory of how deduction gets justified simply repeats the tortoise's own category error.

(2) Sam replied that Dana was throwing away the actual content. The regress only bites because the tortoise keeps treating a rule as if it were a premise — as one more item in the list of things accepted. But accepting that from A and A→B you may move to B is a different kind of state from accepting A and accepting A→B, and once you keep those apart, the dialogue doesn't show deduction is unjustifiable, it shows you misdescribed it.

(3) Dana said fine, but that just relocates the problem: how do you apply the rule? To move from the two things you've accepted to the conclusion you have to actually deploy the rule, and deploying it is a further act that itself would need a rule to tell you how to do it, so we're straight back in the regress — which is more or less what people say about Kripke's rule-following sceptic, except there it's about meaning rather than validity.

(4) Sam said the comparison to Kripke is revealing rather than fatal, because his own response to the regress was that rule-following is a practice, not an interpretation: rules aren't self-interpreting, they're just further items requiring interpretation, so you stop when you reach what people actually do, and there's no third thing needed underneath.

(5) Dana said that can't be right either, because it makes being right a matter of matching the crowd, and on that account there's no such thing as a rule you could be following correctly while everyone else got it wrong — and anyway, Sam's practice is itself just a description from outside, and the tortoise's whole point was that you can't get from description to use without adopting the rule, so Sam has re-imported the very step he claimed to explain.

(6) Sam closed by saying Dana has confused two questions: the tortoise is asking for an external justification of deduction, which no one can give, but the practice answer is answering an internal question about what it is to follow one rule rather than another, and refusing to hear that distinction is not a victory, it's a refusal to engage.
