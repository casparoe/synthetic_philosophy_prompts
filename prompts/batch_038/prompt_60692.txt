I write numerical integration code for coupled nonlinear systems, and I keep losing arguments about what chaos does and does not rule out. Before I read any philosophy of it, I want the specification. Write the desiderata for an account of predictability in deterministic systems: what any such account has to deliver to count as adequate, with the case or result behind each requirement so I can check whether I accept it.

Make sure the list covers at least these, and add any I've missed:

- Systems where individual trajectories outrun our error growth but aggregate statistics stay reliable — why forecasting a state and forecasting a distribution are different jobs.
- Shadowing. The orbit my integrator produces is not close to any true orbit of my equations; numerical analysts say it is close to some true orbit of my system started slightly differently, or of a slightly perturbed system. Does an account have to say whether that is enough for my simulation to count as knowledge, and what happens in systems where shadowing fails?
- Unpredictability that isn't sensitive dependence at all: some discrete systems are conjectured to admit no shortcut, and a few are known to be able to emulate a universal computer, so predicting them costs a full simulation. What separates that from chaos?
- Finite precision: my state space is finite, so any computed orbit must eventually repeat. Defect of the implementation, or a fact about prediction?
- Reversibility: the underlying dynamics run equally well backward, so where does the asymmetry between predicting and retrodicting come from?

Also name the distinctions the account must not collapse (determinism vs. predictability; unpredictability from initial conditions vs. from model error; growth rates measured over finite windows vs. in the limit; epistemic vs. ontic), and say which requirements are non-negotiable and which are preferences of one camp.

What I most want: whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable. I suspect a squeeze — tying unpredictability to a predictor's finite computational resources makes almost everything unpredictable, while tying it to an idealized predictor with unbounded resources and exact initial data makes chaotic systems perfectly predictable — leaving no stable content in between. Say whether that's real or whether there's a way through.

Format, in this order: **Purpose** (what the account is for — you fix this, but state it, ~150 words); **The desiderata** (numbered, each with its motivating case — the bulk of the answer); **Non-negotiable vs. camp preference**; **Joint satisfiability**; **Scorecard** (brief: how an information-theoretic account that counts bits of precision lost per unit time, a computational-cost account, and a model-error account do against the list); **Where this list is most likely wrong** — which requirement you'd drop under pressure, what argument would change your verdict on the squeeze, and where your reasoning is shakiest.
