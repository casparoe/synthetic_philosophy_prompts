Analyse the International Astronomical Union's decision of August 2006 — the adoption of a formal definition of *planet* at the General Assembly in Prague, and the consequent reclassification of Pluto as a dwarf planet — as a case study in vagueness and the sorites paradox. I do not want the astronomy, and I do not want the standard retelling of the controversy in which one side is accused of sentimentality and the other of pedantry. I want a disciplined mapping of the episode onto the machinery of the vagueness literature, followed by an honest assessment of how much of the episode that machinery actually captures, and a verdict on which reading of what the Union did is best supported.

To keep the exercise self-contained, take the following as stipulated. The definition adopted requires a body to be in orbit around the Sun; to have sufficient mass for its self-gravity to overcome rigid-body forces so that it assumes a shape close to hydrostatic equilibrium; and to have cleared the neighbourhood around its orbit, meaning that it dynamically dominates its orbital zone. A body meeting the first two conditions but not the third is classed as a dwarf planet, and is expressly not a planet: an alternative resolution that would have made dwarf planets a subclass of planets was put and defeated. Pluto satisfies the first two conditions and fails the third; Ceres likewise. The definition was triggered by the discovery of Eris, a trans-Neptunian body at least as massive as Pluto and, on the measurements available at the time, possibly marginally larger, together with the expectation that further Pluto-class bodies would keep turning up. The definition governs only bodies orbiting the Sun; exoplanets are handled by a separate working definition keyed to a deuterium-fusion mass limit, so the Union is now operating two non-coextensive criteria under one word. Mercury is not in fact in hydrostatic equilibrium, yet is included as a planet. A further objection is that the third condition makes planetary status depend on where a body sits: a Mars-mass object in the distant outer reaches of the Solar System would be too small to dominate so vast a zone and would fail the test. Only a few hundred of the Union's roughly ten thousand members cast votes on the decisive resolutions, at the closing sessions of the assembly, and the procedure has been widely criticised on that ground. Finally, two further facts matter. First, there is precedent: after the discovery of Ceres in 1801 the number of accepted planets had grown to some two dozen by mid-century as more bodies were found between Mars and Jupiter, and the astronomical community then quietly began cataloguing them separately as asteroids, without anyone taking that to be a discovery about the world. Second, when a committee was set up in 2005 to consider the question, it canvassed three possible definitions — a cultural one, on which a planet is whatever enough people say is one; a structural one, keyed to being massive enough to be rounded; and a dynamical one, keyed to being massive enough to expel other bodies from its orbit — and the 2006 resolution is a hybrid of the second and third. An informal show of hands at a planetary-science conference some years later rejected the definition by a wide margin, though nothing much should be made of the number.

With that in hand, answer the following.

1. Establish whether there is a sorites series here at all. Identify the ordering dimension, the base case, the inductive step, and the tolerance principle on which that step depends. Consider a dimensionless clearing measure of the kind astronomers have actually proposed — roughly, the square of a body's mass divided by its orbital period, estimating how effectively it can scatter smaller objects from its zone over the age of the Solar System. Ordering the Solar System's bodies by such a measure yields two clusters: the eight planets in one band, the rounded-but-not-dominant bodies in another, separated by several orders of magnitude, with nothing known in between. Does that bimodality defeat the tolerance premise, or merely its application to the bodies we happen to have sampled? Bear in mind that the definition must also govern undiscovered bodies and bodies in other planetary systems, and say whether the vagueness at issue is therefore better described as actual or as dispositional.

2. Locate the vagueness. Is it in the extension of the word, or in the third condition of the definition itself — such that the resolution relocates the problem rather than dissolving it? Separately, diagnose the location-dependence objection: is it a problem about vagueness, or about context-sensitivity and relational properties, where a body's status shifts with its surroundings? These are different philosophical complaints, and I want to know which the procedural facts support.

3. Apply three readings, stipulated here for the purposes of the exercise. On the *discovery* reading, the boundary was already sharp and unknowable, and the vote at best tracked it. On the *precisification* reading, the vote selected one admissible sharpening from among many, constrained by ordinary usage and by penumbral relations between candidate cases. On the *engineering* reading, the vote replaced a loose working concept with a new one built to serve dynamical astronomy, so that the subsequent dispute with the geophysical camp — who would call any rounded body a planet — is a negotiation about which concept to adopt rather than a disagreement about what the Solar System contains. For each reading, state what it says the Union did, what it makes of the dissenters (error, a competing sharpening, or a different concept), what it predicts about the 1801–1851 asteroid precedent, and what it owes by way of an account of the legitimacy of a boundary fixed by a few hundred voters. Then commit to the reading you find most defensible, and say what would falsify it.

4. Run the analysis back the other way. What does this episode show about the theories of vagueness themselves? Does the fact that the 2005 committee put a frankly conventionalist definition on the table — and that it was set aside rather than refuted — tell in favour of any particular account? Does the treatment of Mercury, an explicit exception to the very criterion the definition invokes, fit supervaluationist admissibility constraints, or is it evidence that scientific kind-terms of this sort are not vague in the relevant sense at all but merely contested and underdetermined? Where a theory is silent — about voting, about institutional authority, about which properties a category ought to track — say so plainly rather than pressing it into service.

No citations, bibliography, or reading list; argue from the stipulated facts and from your own grasp of the philosophical options. Do not end on a both-sides note.
