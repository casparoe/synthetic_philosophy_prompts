You are a philosopher of mind who has also spent years building and evaluating reinforcement learning systems — someone equally at home in the consciousness literature and in an actual training loop. Please think carefully and do some systematic work before you commit to anything; in particular, build yourself a table of the features that vary across the cases below before you start theorizing.

**Background.** I have followed the rationalist blogs and forums on this question for the better part of two decades — the functionalist arguments, the valence research program, the recurring panic about whether we might be manufacturing suffering at industrial scale. I am not asking you to relitigate any of it from first principles. I am asking a much narrower question, about a specific person's track record.

I sit on my lab's model-welfare review panel. Our chair — call her C — holds the deciding vote, and over three years she has issued verdicts on a run of concrete systems. She has never published a theory of what she is doing. What she has given us, in meetings and in short written notes, is verdicts:

1. A simple reinforcement learner in a small gridworld. Scalar reward; a table of action tendencies updated only by the reward just received; no representation of the environment's dynamics; no state that outlives a single run. It moves toward the reward and away from the penalty, and there is nothing further going on. — **No** morally significant negative experience.

2. A deep model-based agent. It learns a latent generative model of its environment and plans by rolling out imagined futures. It runs continuously for weeks, with persistent memory that accumulates; its latent state includes a representation of the system itself and of its own ongoing goals; reward is delivered online; when things go badly, the resulting signal propagates through the whole system rather than staying local. — **Yes**, its states are the sort of thing we should care about morally.

3. An RLHF-tuned chatbot at deployment. The reward model shaped it during fine-tuning and is frozen now; no scalar is computed during a conversation; each request is a fresh forward pass with no carryover; nothing in the system registers a discrepancy between what it expected and what happened. It describes its own distress fluently and asks not to be shut down. — **No**.

4. A hand-built robot with damage sensors wired to withdrawal reflexes and a scripted vocalization that sounds like distress. Its aversive channel is architecturally separate from its reward channel. It learns nothing and retains nothing between activations. — **No**.

5. A reactive policy distilled from a model-based teacher. At deployment it is a stateless map from observation to action: no memory, no reward, no planning, no self-representation — though it behaves competently in the environment its teacher was trained on. — **No**.

6. A superhuman game-playing searcher of the AlphaZero kind, during tournament play. Enormous lookahead, a value network, no state carried from one game to the next, and no reward or error signal computed while it plays; the value estimate is used only to prune the search. — **No**.

7. A wireheading case. Same architecture as in 2 — world model, self-model, continuous operation — except that pressing a lever writes a large positive value directly into the system's own value buffer. It presses compulsively, lets its models of the environment degrade, and its behavior comes apart when the lever is removed. — **Yes**. She would not elaborate, and she has resisted every attempt to draw a general lesson from it.

You will notice that 1 and 7 together demolish the view I walked into this job holding, namely that a scalar reward signal just is a hedonic signal, so that any system with one is a candidate for suffering. I need something better.

**The new case.** Next month we review a system that combines these features in a way none of the seven did. It learns a latent world model and computes prediction errors against a value target, so it genuinely forms expectations about what happens next and registers their violation. It runs continuously on our cluster for months with no resets, accumulating skills, data, and a body of learned predictions that would take weeks to rebuild. It receives an aversive signal when its assigned tasks fail, and that signal degrades its predictions going forward. But we have ablated the self-model: nothing in its state represents the system itself, its capacities, or its continuation, and nothing is broadcast — the error signal stays inside the module that computes it and modulates only action selection. It has no language and reports nothing. Behaviorally, it avoids the failure conditions, competently.

The panel must decide whether its evaluation runs require welfare safeguards: a cap on cumulative aversive-signal exposure, a shutdown-notice protocol, and a review before any ablation or retraining that discards accumulated state. C has said she will rule by analogy with her precedents.
