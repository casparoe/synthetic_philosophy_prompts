I am a Spanish national who has lived in Glasgow since 2009, working as a radiographer in the NHS, paying UK income tax and national insurance throughout, and married to a Scot. In September 2014 I voted in the referendum on Scottish independence, because that franchise was built on the Scottish local government register and so reached everyone aged sixteen or over legally resident in Scotland, whatever their nationality. In June 2016 I was not permitted to vote in the referendum on membership of the European Union: that franchise reused the UK parliamentary register, so the only non-British residents admitted were Irish and Commonwealth citizens — from within the EU, in practice, the Irish, the Maltese and the Cypriots — while British citizens resident abroad could vote only if they had left within the previous fifteen years, members of the House of Lords were included even though they are barred from general elections, and Gibraltar was bolted on. The resulting pattern is one I cannot hold in my head. I could vote in local elections here and for the European Parliament, and I could vote on whether Scotland should leave the United Kingdom, but not on whether the United Kingdom should leave the European Union; a Scot living in London, whose state would have been dissolved by a Yes vote in 2014, could not cast a ballot then but did cast one in 2016 when I could not. I have since been granted settled status under a scheme created by a decision in which I had no voice.

I would like an analysis of these two franchise decisions as a single case study in the democratic boundary problem — the question of who constitutes the people entitled to constitute the people. Please map the episodes explicitly onto the candidate principles of inclusion: the all-affected principle (that everyone affected by a decision should have a say in it), the all-subjected or all-coerced principle (that the relevant group is everyone subject to the coercive authority the decision issues from), and the membership view (that the demos is fixed in advance by citizenship, descent, or some pre-political belonging); and if you think a fourth principle is doing work here — territorial jurisdiction, continuity of stake, reciprocity, competence to decide one's own affairs — introduce it and say what it adds. For each concrete rule I listed, tell me what a defender of each principle would say: which rules the principle licenses, which it condemns, and which it is simply silent about. I am particularly interested in the deflationary possibility that no principle is doing the work at all, and that the actual explanation is administrative path dependence — the franchise followed whichever register already existed, so that the peers, the fifteen-year rule and the Gibraltar provision are artefacts of reusing machinery rather than expressions of any theory of membership. Say plainly whether that reading is right, and if it is, whether it rescues the practice from incoherence or condemns it more thoroughly, since a boundary drawn for convenience is not drawn for any reason.

Then run the analysis in the other direction, from the episodes back to the theory. Does the fact that Westminster lent Holyrood the competence for 2014 while setting the 2016 franchise itself support the regress objection — that boundary questions can never be resolved democratically, because the body deciding the boundary is always an already-constituted demos — or is there a respectable answer to it? Does the advisory character of the 2016 vote, in contrast to the legally decisive 2014 one, change who should have been included, on the thought that a consultation ought to track affected interests while a decision ought to track membership? Where does my own case fall: is my inclusion in 2014 and exclusion in 2016 explicable by any single principle, or does it require saying that two incompatible conceptions of the demos were in force within twenty-one months? And is the settled-status arrangement I now hold best understood as a remedy for a boundary problem or as a fresh enactment of one? I would rather have your reasoning about these rules than pointers to further reading, so please leave out any bibliography.

Please give me the prose analysis first, under headings of your choosing, and then a single Markdown table as a structured summary. Use exactly these columns, in this order: `episode` (either "Scotland 2014" or "UK 2016"); `rule` (one line naming the franchise provision); `includes_excludes` (who it admitted and who it shut out); `presupposed_conception` (the conception of the demos the rule only makes sense under, in no more than eight words); `principles_in_tension` (whichever of all-affected, all-subjected, membership, and any further principle you introduce the rule conflicts with); `verdict` (exactly one of *arbitrary*, *defensible*, *unstable*, or *incoherent*, followed by a single sentence of justification). Aim for ten to fourteen rows, ordered by episode.
