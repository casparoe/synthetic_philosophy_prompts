I am writing a chapter for an edited volume on negative causation, and I want to be pushed into committing to a position rather than treated to a survey. Please write an essay of roughly 2,500 to 3,000 words on the question set out below. Assume the reader is a professional philosopher who already knows what a counterfactual analysis of causation is, what the problem of causal exclusion looks like, and why absences are thought to be metaphysically embarrassing; spend no words on orientation.

**The cases.** A municipal pool is staffed, as regulation requires, by two lifeguards: one on the deep end, one on the shallow end. Each is under a duty to keep the whole pool under observation, and either, acting alone and in the ordinary way, would have seen the child who is now in difficulty and rescued her.

*Two phones.* In the first case, both guards are independently looking at their phones. Neither sees the child. She drowns. The counterfactuals appear to come out the same for both guards: had the deep-end guard been watching, the child would have lived; and had the shallow-end guard been watching, she would have lived. On a naïve counterfactual account, neither omission is a cause, which seems to let two culpable failures cancel each other out.

*The deputy.* Now vary the case. The deep-end guard is on his phone, as before. The shallow-end guard is perfectly alert — but she has been directed, ten minutes earlier, to keep her eyes fixed on the diving board for the duration of a lesson, and she obeys. The child drowns. Here the intuitions diverge: the phone-guard's failure looks like a cause of the death, and the deputy's looks like something else — perhaps an excused or merely derivative failure. Yet the counterfactual profile is identical in both cases, and the deputy's compliance is, if anything, the more reliable link in the chain, since she is the one who would certainly have acted but for the instruction.

Add one further wrinkle and keep it in view throughout: the supervisor's instruction was itself a breach of pool regulations, so there is a further omission upstream of the deputy's, and the question arises whether the deputy's failure is a cause of the death in virtue of transmitting causal oomph backwards along a chain of omissions, or whether that chain threatens a regress in which every omitted compliance with every applicable rule becomes a cause of the death.

**The question.** Philosophers have developed substantial machinery for handling pre-empted and overdetermined *positive* causation: accounts that look to the intrinsic character of the actual process rather than to counterfactual dependence, quasi-dependence, the view that causation is a relation that holds in virtue of what actually happens all the way along the sequence. My question is whether that machinery can be transplanted to omissions, where — on the face of it — there is no process, no sequence of events, and nothing at all that happens when the guard fails to look. I want to know whether the pre-emption and overdetermination cases decide between two broad strategies, and which one you end up endorsing.

*Strategy one: keep causation univocal.* Negative and positive causation are the same relation; the analysis, not the relata, must be revised. On this line the omission cases are just another pre-emption puzzle, and whatever solves it for billiard balls solves it for lifeguards.

*Strategy two: demote omissions.* Omissions are not causes in the strict sense, and the appearance that they are is generated by something non-causal — by normative or statistical expectations that make some absences salient and others invisible, by the difference between causing harm and being morally responsible for it, or by a reduction of the omission to whatever positive bodily activity the agent was engaged in instead. On this line the counterfactual misfires in *Two phones* and *The deputy* are symptoms of a category error rather than puzzles to be solved by better analysis.

What I want argued, specifically, is whether the asymmetric case of the deputy tells us anything that the general objections to absence-causation — promiscuity, exclusion, the sheer ubiquity of everyone's failure to rescue everyone — do not already tell us. If it does, say exactly what; the deputy case is the one place where a norm-based account and a purely causal account seem to make different predictions, since something other than moral appraisal may be doing the work in distinguishing the phone-guard from the obedient one. Consider whether an intervening agent's compliant action can carry that weight on its own, without smuggling evaluation into the causal relation, and say what your answer implies about the upstream regress.

**Deliverables.** Write in British English. Reach a definite verdict; do not end on a balanced note, and do not retreat to "it depends on what we mean by cause" unless you are prepared to say which of the two strategies that retreat belongs to and to defend it as a substantive position rather than a stipulation. Do not provide a bibliography or citations, and do not name philosophers except where a name is doing real work in locating a view you then argue with. Prose throughout: use lists only where a comparison genuinely has fewer than five rows.

Please organise the essay under exactly these headings, in this order, with approximately the lengths given:

1. **Verdict** (about 150 words). The thesis stated flatly, in two or three sentences, followed by the single claim that, if false, would most damage it.
2. **The cases stated precisely** (about 400 words). For each of the three guards involved across the two cases, and for the upstream supervisor, give the relevant counterfactual and say what the intuitive causal verdict is. Be explicit about where the two come apart and where they do not.
3. **Strategy one** (about 600 words). Its best reconstruction, applied to *The deputy* rather than to a generic example, and the most serious thing that goes wrong for it when nothing happens.
4. **Strategy two** (about 600 words). Its best reconstruction, likewise applied concretely, and the most serious thing that goes wrong for it — in particular, whether it can explain why the child's death is *the* thing to be explained by these omissions rather than any of the countless other outcomes nobody prevented.
5. **What actually decides it** (about 600 words). The considerations that survive the previous two sections, the regress question, and whether the asymmetry in *The deputy* is a causal fact or a normative one.
6. **What my verdict costs** (about 250 words). The concessions I am thereby obliged to make, and the strongest argument I now have to live with.
