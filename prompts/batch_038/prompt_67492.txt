They threw out Nash's independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives axiom and put a monotonicity principle in its place — if the set of possible deals grows so that one player's best case improves while the other's doesn't, the player whose options improved shouldn't end up with less. Their rule is beautifully concrete: mark the disagreement point and the utopia point, meaning what each side could get if they had things entirely their own way, draw a straight line between the two, and take the last efficient deal that line runs through. In the example below both players land 60 percent of the way from breakdown to their ideal, which is what fairness amounts to here: an equal split of the pie. It's the only rule that is efficient, symmetric, unaffected by rescaling the payoffs, and monotone, which is why its the deal arbitrators and courts actually hand down — and unlike Nash's formula it can't be gamed by threatening to take options off the table."
