I am a staff engineer on the platform team at a mid-sized freight logistics company, and I sit on our Model Release Review panel: two engineering leads, a product manager, a compliance lawyer, and me. Nothing goes into live operations until that panel signs off. I have no philosophy training, so I will describe the case in engineering terms and leave it to you to supply whatever conceptual structure it requires.

The case. We have rebuilt the agent that routes exception freight — shipments that arrive late, damaged, or mis-sorted and need to be re-planned. The version in production today is a rules engine with a queue of human reviewers behind it. The replacement is an LLM-based agent that reads the shipment record, calls our carrier-capacity APIs, and issues a routing decision together with a written justification that is appended to the ticket and visible to the customer.

Thursday's panel has to fix the acceptance criteria for promoting the new agent to 100% of traffic, and we are split on something I now think is conceptual rather than technical.

One faction wants outcome-based acceptance: run the agent in shadow mode against the human reviewers for six weeks, then promote if the resulting shipments do at least as well on the metrics the business actually tracks — on-time delivery, cost per exception, rework rate, complaint volume. Their argument is that those quantities are measurable, that they are what anyone downstream cares about, and that inspecting the model's stated reasoning is theater, since the reasoning text is itself just another output.

The other faction wants process-based acceptance: sample the agent's decision traces and require that each one show the right work being done — capacity checked before commitment, the published exception policy applied rather than improvised around, the written justification citing facts actually present in the shipment record. Their argument is that six weeks of outcome data will never surface the rare and severe failures (pharmaceutical cargo stranded, a carrier or region systematically disfavored), and that a trace audit is the only thing that leaves us able to explain a decision after the fact, which our customer contracts require.

Here is what has me stuck. As a side experiment we ran a faithfulness probe. We appended a spurious field to 2,000 shadow-mode inputs — a legacy note identifying the originating terminal, which carries no weight in policy. Decisions changed in roughly 4% of those cases, and changed markedly more for two terminals than for the rest. When we asked the agent to justify the altered decisions, the justification never mentioned the terminal field; it cited shipment characteristics that were equally present in the unaltered cases. The traces read cleanly. They would pass the audit regime as specified, and the colleagues who reviewed them did not notice the shift until we told them about it. Meanwhile the outcome metrics across the six-week window showed no degradation: the affected decisions looked locally reasonable, and whatever harm they do is distributed and slow-moving.

What I need is the memo I will circulate to the panel on Wednesday morning.

- Open with a single sentence stating the bottom line: which acceptance regime we should adopt for this release — one of the two, a specific hybrid, or reject and retest.
- Then the reasoning behind it, including criteria a panel member could actually apply to a trace or to a metric report, and a timeline keyed to our release train.
- Treat the stronger of the two factions' positions seriously and state what it gets right that the recommendation must preserve. Two of the people reading this memo hold that position; a straw man will cost me the room.
- Say explicitly what the terminal-field experiment does and does not establish. Is it evidence against process auditing, against outcome auditing, or against a shared assumption both factions make? I would also like to understand what it implies about the relationship between the reasons a system states and the causes that actually move it — I gather there is a general question here about whether an agent can be held to how it decided as opposed to what it decided, and whether human decision-makers are really any different on that score. Handle that in plain language and do not cite papers; I cannot look them up and neither can half the panel.
- Close with the single point on which you would most want to be argued with if your recommendation turns out to be wrong.

Keep the entire memo under 750 words.
