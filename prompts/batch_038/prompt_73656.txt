I cover science and ideas for a magazine, mostly AI these days, and I'm stuck on an image I can't decide whether to trust.

Here's the setup. A researcher I interviewed last week works on a pipeline where a model writes the rubric its own outputs get graded against, then gets shown its grades and rewrites the rubric, then gets graded again, round after round, with no human in the loop past the initial prompt. She loves Escher's Drawing Hands — the 1948 lithograph where two hands climb out of a flat sheet of paper, each drawing the other into existence. Her pitch is that this is literally what her system is: left hand is the model producing answers, right hand is the model judging them, and the whole thing is a tangled hierarchy with no ground floor, so people who demand an "external validator" have made a category error. There is no outside, she says, and asking for one is like asking who draws Escher. She also mentioned that the print turns up in a well-known programming textbook as a picture of two interpreter functions that call each other, which she took as backup.

My editor's reaction was that this is either the most interesting thing she's read all month or a slogan doing work it can't support. Her counter-image: it's a student grading their own exam, and we all know how that ends.

So: audit both analogies. You're a professional philosopher — ideally one who's thought hard about levels, self-reference and hierarchy, and who has read enough ML to know what a rubric-based eval pipeline actually is. Treat this as a paid consulting job, not a seminar.

Think carefully, and start messy. Before you organise anything, give me a quick brainstorm — eight-ish unpolished bullets throwing out candidate correspondences. What plays the sheet of paper? What plays the cuffs, that weird boundary where the flat drawing turns into a solid hand? Escher's actual pen? The viewer standing in front of the print? The ink? Then build that into a proper mapping table, and after that tell me:

1. Where each mapping breaks — and this is the part I really need — which breaks are load-bearing for the conclusion she wants and which are merely decorative. Nobody cares that one system is graphite on paper and the other is GPUs; that isn't a disanalogy doing any work. Sort them for me.
2. What the Escher picture quietly imports that an eval pipeline lacks. In particular: both hands are drawn by a real hand that is not in the picture, and there's a flat sheet the hands rise out of that never becomes part of the loop. Does her reading depend on ignoring that, or is that exactly what the print is about? And in her system, what would count as the level that stays outside — the training run, the loss function, the person who pressed go, the fact that the grades are just numbers sitting in a file? If she can name one, has she lost?
3. Whether the "there is no outside" conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it the entire way. I want a straight verdict, not both-sides mush — its for a magazine, not a journal.
4. Whether the textbook's use of the print for two mutually calling functions is the same move as hers or a different one. Does the fact that smart people have used the image that way make her use safer, or just more contagious?
5. If Drawing Hands is the wrong picture, what's the right one? Give me one better analogy for a model that authors and keeps revising the criteria it's measured by, and say what that analogy licenses which the hands don't.
6. And audit my editor's version too — the student grading their own exam seems to presuppose the teacher, i.e. the very thing my source is denying. Is that just the same argument with the conclusion pre-loaded, or does it get independent traction from somewhere?

I can't fact-check anything and I have no way to look things up, so no bibliography, no reading lists, no page numbers, and no confident attributions of technical terms to particular authors — if you aren't sure someone actually coined a phrase, just describe the idea. Plain English wherever possible, because I have to paraphrase this to a general reader without embarrassing myself. And please don't seperate the whole thing into forty numbered headings; a table is fine where a table helps, but I'd rather read an argument than an outline.
