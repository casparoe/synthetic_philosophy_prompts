Hi — I write a science/ideas column and I'm stuck on a piece I have to file Thursday. What I need is someone to actually take a side, not give me the balanced mush I could write myself.

Setup: I've been covering a little spinout, call them Aponia, that has a neuromodulation patch aimed at the affective side of pain. Their pitch is that it leaves the sensory-discriminative stuff fully intact — where it is, how intense, what quality, you can still point at it and rate it on a scale — and turns down only the part that makes pain matter to you. Essentially deliberate, reversible pain asymbolia for chronic pain patients who've run out of options. Their line in the press release is that they don't remove pain, they remove suffering. The trial endpoint isn't an intensity scale, it's a bothered-ness scale, and the numbers apparently look great. I spoke to a woman in the trial with an old injury thats fused her lower spine: she said she can still feel it when she bends, she knows exactly where it is, and it just doesn't bother her — she described it as a radio playing in the next room.

Two kinds of pushback and I cant tell which one is right. The clinicians say: if it doesn't bother her, why did she enroll, and if it does bother her then the device failed — so either your endpoint measures nothing or your marketing is a lie. A philosopher I interviewed says the whole thing is a category confusion: what's left once you take the unpleasantness away isn't pain-minus-badness, it's simply not pain, so the patients are reporting on a different kind of state than the one the patch was supposed to modify, and the slogan is doing all the work.

So here's what I want: argue, straight and one-sided, that Aponia's patients are genuinely better off and that their reports can be trusted — that the badness of pain sits in the affective/motivational dimension rather than in the felt sensory quality, so that dialing the affect down is a real gain in wellbeing and not a trick played on the patient's own introspection. About 1,100 words.

Things my editor will ask about, so handle them rather than nodding at them:

- the unreliability worry: that "it doesn't bother me" is produced by the deficit itself, so the report can't count as evidence about the state;
- the not-really-pain objection above — including whether your argument has to win that fight or can bracket it;
- the protective function: clinicians keep pointing me at congenital insensitivity to pain, where people chew through fingers and walk on broken ankles, and saying if you remove the concern you remove the reason to guard the limb, which is a cost the patient's own reports will never register;
- the alienation worry: whether there's something diminished about an embodied agent whose own tissue damage no longer moves her, even when it feels fine from the inside.

Also be explicit about which of these three your argument actually needs: (1) these patients are better off; (2) unpleasantness is the bad-making feature of pain as a general claim about pain, not just about this population; (3) it's permissible to offer this, and maybe preferable to opioids. My hunch is you can buy (1) without (2), and if that's right it changes my lede, so tell me plainly.

No bibliography and please don't cite studies at me — I've got a factchecker and I mostly can't verify anything you'd name anyway. If you want to gesture at evidence, describe in plain language what a finding or an experiment would have to look like to settle the disagreement. And don't hedge: strongest version of the pro-Aponia case, with the two or three places you think it's weakest flagged at the very end in a sentence or two each.
