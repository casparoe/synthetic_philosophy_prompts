I convene a graduate seminar on the philosophy of norm change, and for our opening meeting I would like to distribute a dialogue that stages the seminar's central dispute with more precision than my own lectures manage. I should be grateful if you would draft it.

The dispute concerns what does the explanatory work when a long-established practice such as duelling disappears from a society that had practised it for centuries. On the honor view — of which Appiah's account of honor codes is the best-known statement, and which owes something to Williams's insistence that radical reflection tends to erode a society's ethical knowledge rather than to improve it — the practice was sustained not by anyone's beliefs about its justification but by a structure of entitlement to respect, and it ended when that structure gave way. On the argument view, it ended because people were persuaded: the reasons against duelling (that it settles nothing about who is in the right, that it rewards nerve and marksmanship rather than justice, that it is available only to a narrow caste) came to be generally accepted, and that change in belief did the causal work.

For the seminar I stipulate the following definition, and both characters must work within it. Call a norm honor-structured when (i) the sanction for violating it is loss of standing with some body of persons; (ii) the agent cannot discount that body's opinion without ceasing to belong to it; and (iii) the sanction attaches whether or not the agent believes the norm justified. The crux I want the dialogue to turn on is clause (ii). The honor theorist should maintain that what changed was the reach of the sanction: the body whose contempt was once lethal ceased to be one a gentleman could not escape, and that this is a structural fact rather than a conclusion. The argument theorist should maintain that clause (ii) already concedes the point, since which body's opinion is undiscardable depends on what a person takes to count as a reason, and that the relevant body was redrawn — enlarged, and its criteria altered — by argument itself.

Setting: the two are members of a doctoral committee who must settle on a sentence for a student's prospectus stating what explains the disappearance of duelling. Give them names. Neither may cite a book, article, or philosopher; the dialogue must be self-contained and may use only such historical generalities as I have stated here.

Requirements:

1. Roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words, eight to ten exchanges, minimal stage direction, and contemporary analytic prose rather than pastiche.

2. The two must be of equal ability. At one point each must restate the other's position in a form the other accepts as fair, and then object to that form.

3. The sincerity gap must be confronted directly: men who accepted every argument against duelling nevertheless fought, and men who found the practice absurd were unwilling to be the first to stop. Each side must say what this shows, and neither may be permitted to explain it away.

4. At one point a character must introduce a third explanation — that duelling served as a credential for a hereditary gentry and officer class, and faded as those positions came to be allocated by examination and seniority — and the other must say whether it rivals, presupposes, or merely enables the two main views.

5. One character must accuse the other of shifting the explanandum among three distinct things: a change in what individuals judged, the cessation of the practice, and the loss of the norm's authority. The accusation must be answered rather than deflected.

6. Do not resolve the disagreement, and do not let either side win. End instead with each making a different prediction about the same body of evidence — for instance, about where the practice should have survived longest and where it should have died first: closed corporations, regiments, and small garrison towns on one hypothesis; the schools, pulpits, and newspapers that taught the arguments most thoroughly on the other. I intend to ask my students which prediction the historical record favours, so the two predictions must be genuinely distinguishable and must not collapse into a single claim.
