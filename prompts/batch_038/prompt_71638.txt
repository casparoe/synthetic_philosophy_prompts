I'm a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or history, and I've run into a disagreement I can't get any purchase on, so I'd like a structural diagnosis rather than a side taken.

In my orbit — tech policy writing, strategy memos, long argument threads — any discussion of a rising power challenging an established one, of whether to negotiate with an adversary who has broken agreements before, or of whether a slow response to a new threat was negligent, immediately generates historical analogies: the appeasement of the 1930s and what it supposedly teaches about concessions; the general pattern of a rising power colliding with a ruling power and the claim that such pairs usually end up fighting; the domino reasoning that justified escalation in Southeast Asia; the accidental slide into catastrophe in 1914. A second group objects that this whole practice misleads, that an analogy is a similarity judgment nobody has argued for, that the supporting case tables are tiny and coded toward the conclusion the coder already held, and that the famous analogies are famous precisely because their outcomes were dramatic, so the sample is selected for memorability. The first group replies that all reasoning is comparison, that refusing to analogize is refusing to think, and that the critics deploy analogies constantly while preferring the ones that suit them. As far as I can tell this has run for decades in print on both sides and nobody has moved.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than flatter, is that the two camps aren't disagreeing about history at all. One treats an analogy as an inference with premises and a conclusion that can be better or worse supported; the other treats it as a retrieval or framing device whose job is to make a possibility salient rather than to justify a belief. On that reading the stalemate is a category error — two different questions sharing the word analogy. I can already see pressure on my own hypothesis: the critics who complain about how cases were coded sound like they're making an empirical claim that better data could settle, and the defenders who say comparison is unavoidable sound like they're making a claim about evidence, not salience.

So diagnose it. Is this a substantive disagreement, a verbal one, two questions under one label, a hidden empirical question about base rates doing all the work, or bedrock differences about what counts as a case that neither side can argue for? Characterize the strongest version of each position from what its adherents actually do and say — I'm not after names or reading lists. Tell me whether my nearest-neighbor framing helps or just relabels the confusion. Tell me which parts could in principle be settled, and by what: a distinction, a better dataset, a formal account of analogical inference, a change of question. Say plainly if my hypothesis is wrong, and if there's a third position that explains the deadlock better than mine, give it to me. If your honest conclusion is that no progress should be expected here, argue for that rather than hedging.
