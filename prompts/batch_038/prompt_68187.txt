I am assembling a seminar exercise on theories of rational choice under risk, and I need a decision problem built to a tight specification. The purpose of the exercise is to force students to check by hand whether a theory of risk attitudes can accommodate ordinary caution about small stakes without committing itself to absurdity about large ones, so every verdict below must be derivable from the theories as stated rather than asserted in the description of the case. Please do not cite sources, recommend readings, or attribute results to named economists or philosophers; I want your own construction and your own arithmetic, and I want to be able to reproduce every number you give me.

First, fix the framework so we are working with the same definitions. Risk-weighted expected utility evaluates an act whose distinct outcomes, in increasing order, are α₁ < α₂ < … < α_n by the expression REU = α₁ + Σ (α_i − α_{i−1}) · r(P(outcome ≥ α_i)), summed over i from 2 to n, where r is a continuous, strictly increasing risk function with r(0) = 0 and r(1) = 1. Throughout, use r(p) = p², which is convex and therefore risk-averse in this framework, and use a utility function that is linear in money, so that every bit of risk aversion in the case comes from the risk function rather than from curvature in utility.

Specifications for the case:

1. Setting. One paragraph, realistic and contemporary, a single individual decision-maker, no exotic devices, no perfect predictors, no physics. No casinos, no lotteries, no insurance contracts, and nothing that is a relabelled version of the Allais paradox or the St. Petersburg gamble. The probabilities must be grounded in some actual randomising institution or procedure, or in evidence you explicitly describe as symmetric; a bare coin toss the story gives no reason to expect will not do.

2. The two decisions. The same agent faces two separate one-shot decisions in the same setting. The modest one is a two-outcome, fifty-fifty gamble with stakes in the hundreds of dollars and a slightly positive expected value. The large one has stakes in the tens of thousands. The verdicts the case must generate are: decline the modest gamble, accept the large one, and decline the modest gamble whether the agent happens to be several thousand dollars richer or several thousand poorer than she is. The description may not smuggle these verdicts in by characterising the agent as temperamentally cautious, and no act may be individuated by reference to the theory under test.

3. The expected-utility check. Show that no expected-utility maximiser with a concave utility function over money can display that combination. Reconstruct the reasoning in your own terms and apply it to your own numbers: explain why declining the modest gamble at every wealth level in the stated range forces a utility function that flattens out so quickly that the large gamble must be declined too, and state explicitly where in that argument the assumption of concavity is used.

4. The risk-weighted check. Compute the risk-weighted value of each act and of the status quo under r(p) = p² with linear utility, showing each term of the sum, and confirm that the intended verdicts fall out. If your chosen stakes do not produce them, adjust the stakes and not the risk function, and tell me exactly what you changed and why.

5. The background-risk stress test. Now suppose the agent is simultaneously carrying an independent background exposure that she cannot shed: zero or near-zero expected value, with a spread at least as large as the modest gamble's, and specified concretely in your setting. Recompute the comparison between declining and accepting the modest gamble on top of that exposure, giving the full arithmetic for the resulting multi-outcome act, and report whether the verdict flips. Then state what an expected-utility maximiser with concave utility would predict for the same comparison, and explain in one or two sentences why the two theories diverge or agree here. If the verdict does flip, treat that as a live cost of your design and say so plainly.

6. Diagnosis. Identify the single feature of the case that does the work in generating the pattern, and say what happens to each verdict if you remove it — for instance, if the modest gamble is rescaled so that its stakes are a substantial fraction of the agent's wealth.

7. Objections. Give two distinct attacks that a committed defender of expected utility would make on your case, and assess each: does it succeed, and if not, what resources does the risk-weighted theorist have to answer it? At least one of the two must engage directly with the result of step 5.

Organise your answer under headings corresponding to the seven specifications, keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph, and keep the total under about twelve hundred words.
