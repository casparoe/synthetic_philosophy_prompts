I'm auditing a philosophy of history seminar at a community college extension this semester, purely for fun, and about half the sessions collapse into the same old fight: one camp says an event is only genuinely explained when you can subsume it under some general law, the other camp says that's absurd and that what historians actually do is tell a story where each step makes sense of the next one. I've been keeping a separate notebook of things that come up in that fight, and there's one pattern I keep hitting that I can't find a word for, so I figured I'd ask here rather than derail the seminar.

Here's the pattern. A historian offers a chain-of-links account of a big outcome — say, why the French monarchy went under in 1789: a fiscal system that couldn't raise enough, a run of bad harvests, the paralysis of the court when it tried to reform, the decades of corrosive writing about despotism, and then the decision to call the Estates-General, which handed everyone an arena. Every link is well documented; nobody is making anything up. But when I try to pressure-test the whole thing by asking what would have had to be different for the collapse not to happen, the answer keeps moving. Take out the fiscal link and the harvests plus the political paralysis carry the load; take those out and it's the ideological one; take that out and it's the contingent choice to convene the Estates-General in that particular way. Each time, the historian produces better evidence for the link they've shifted to than they had for the one I attacked. I see exactly the same move with the outbreak of the First World War — knock over the alliance-system link and you get mobilization timetables, knock those over and you get the naval race and the cult of the offensive, knock that over and you get domestic politics in Vienna and Berlin. The explanation never loses. And it never loses without cheating, which is the part I find interesting. It's more like the story has enough slack in it that any one strand can be cut without the whole thing sagging.

So that's the thing I want identified. My roommate says I'm just describing every argument ever made, which I don't think is right, because it's not the usual complaint that narrative history is a just-so story told after the fact — nothing here is invented, and the links are all perfectly specific rather than vague. Its also not the standard ad hoc rescue, because the "rescues" are better attested than the original claim, and it doesn't feel like the theorist is protecting a cherished thesis either, since they'll happily sacrifice whichever link I come at. Is there an established term, or at least a literature, for the case where an explanation is testable at every individual link but unkillable as a whole because the links are mutually substitutable? If there is one, I want to know precisely what it covers and where it stops, because my hunch is that the nearest existing labels are usually aimed at something slightly different — the world having many routes to the same outcome, rather than the arguer having many routes to the same conclusion — and I want to know whether that difference matters or whether I'm splitting hairs. If there isn't one, coin it and define it properly, and give me the edges: what would a case look like that seems like this but isn't, i.e. an explanation that survives a lot of attacks for a legitimate reason; what makes the substitutability a defect instead of just a fact about any complicated causal story; and is there some practical test I could run on a real historical argument to tell whether I'm looking at this or at something genuinely robust. And while you're in there — does this land as a point for the covering-law camp, a point against them, or does it cut at both?

Please write the whole thing in American English. I care about the carving more than the label, so don't hand me a bare term and stop. End your reply with one final line beginning exactly `Verdict:` followed by the term itself (established or coined) and nothing else on that line, so I can drop it into my notes.
