Last weekend I spent four hours arguing with a friend — a philosopher of science who has read enough complexity theory to be genuinely dangerous — about whether the tractable/intractable boundary is a discovery about computation or an artifact of our notation. I came away unable to say precisely where he overreaches, which is exactly why I have decided to put the argument on my final exam.

I teach a graduate seminar, cross-listed between philosophy and computer science, on the conceptual foundations of computation. The students have had one term of complexity theory; with two exceptions they have no formal epistemology or metaphysics. The final is closed-book, sixty minutes per question. I want one essay question worth twenty-five marks.

My friend's argument, in the strongest form I can manage for him, runs in four steps. (1) A running time is never a property of a problem simpliciter: it is a property of a problem relative to a machine model and to a choice of encoding for the inputs. (2) The standard defence — that all reasonable machine models simulate one another with only polynomial overhead, and hence that the class of polynomial-time decidable problems is invariant — does not discharge the worry but relocates it, because "polynomial overhead" is itself a tolerance we chose, and no argument has ever been given for why that particular tolerance, rather than linear or quasilinear overhead, marks a joint in nature. (3) The tolerance can be seen to matter: representing numerals in unary rather than binary, or presenting a graph as an explicit adjacency structure rather than by a compact generator, changes which problems fall on which side of the line. (4) Therefore a claim of the form "problem X is intractable" is a claim about our representational choices, and even a proof that P ≠ NP would establish a theorem internal to a convention rather than a fact about computational reality.

Please write the following three things.

First, the exam question itself, about two hundred words, entirely self-contained. It must present my friend's argument to the students in your own words, since they have not heard it, and then require them to do three things: reconstruct the argument as valid by supplying its suppressed premises; identify the single step at which it fails and say why; and formulate and then apply a general criterion for when a complexity-theoretic claim is an artifact of representation rather than a fact. The question must be unanswerable by reciting the definitions of P and NP, by surveying the state of the P versus NP problem, by taking a position on whether P equals NP, or by repeating the usual assurance that polynomial time is robust across machine models — that assurance is precisely what is under dispute.

Second, a model answer of roughly eight hundred words, argued from first principles and from results the students have actually had. No bibliography, no citations, no named literature: I want the reasoning, not the references.

Third, a marking scheme out of twenty-five, built around five must-hit moves of five marks each. For each move, state what it consists in and add one line naming the characteristic way students fumble it. Follow that with a short list of errors that should cost marks outright.

To calibrate the A/B line — and please correct me where you think I am wrong. A B answer will correctly observe that running times are model-relative and that the invariance of polynomial time under changes of machine model is a real theorem, and will conclude that my friend's argument therefore fails. But it will treat "encodings that are polynomially related" as a definition rather than as a substantive assumption that could have turned out false; it will slide between worst-case complexity and practical feasibility as though the thesis identifying polynomial time with feasible computation were a claim about how long any given algorithm takes on any given instance; and it will not notice that step three cuts both ways. An A answer will see that the argument proves far too much — if representation-relativity of that kind dissolved the content of complexity claims, it would equally dissolve the known strict separation of polynomial from exponential time, which is a theorem with content — and will explain why the unary-numeral case is not a counterexample but an instance of the criterion working correctly, since what makes an encoding reasonable is that it is polynomially related to the standard ones and does not pad the input length exponentially relative to the information it carries. I would also like full credit available to a student who states what would have to be true for my friend to be right — the condition under which representational choice would genuinely bite — even if the student then argues that condition is not met.

Keep everything answerable without access to any text. Do not require knowledge of the known barriers to proving lower bounds, though a student who invokes them correctly should not be penalised for it.
