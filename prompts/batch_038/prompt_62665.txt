My cousin Bea quit a backend engineering job that paid around $310k, plus something like $400k of company stock still vesting, to work at a 14-person worker-owned coffee roastery where everybody gets $58k and it's one member one vote. She says she's way happier. Two people in a reading group I sit in, Ines and Kwame, got into a long argument about whether she moved from an exploited job to an unexploited one and asked me to referee, which I definately can't do. Here's roughly how it went; I've tried to give both of them their best shots rather than the snappy versions they actually said.

(1) Ines: the roastery can't be exploiting its own members, because the members collectively own the roasters and appropriate the surplus their work produces, which is exactly the structural feature Marx said was missing under wage labor. Bea's old job was exploited in the textbook way — the value her work added got appropriated by shareholders who never touched the code — and being paid a lot doesn't change that, it just means a bigger slice of a much bigger surplus. And since Bea owns stock herself, she sits on both sides of the relation at once.
(2) Kwame: ownership inside the firm isn't doing any work here. About 70% of the roastery's volume goes to one regional grocery chain, which cut the contract price 6% last year, and the members responded by voting themselves a 55-hour week instead of hiring. That isn't freedom from exploitation, it's exploitation with the boss internalized — and arguably worse, since there's nobody to bargain with and nobody to blame. He also noted the roastery keeps five non-member warehouse workers at $21 an hour with no vote, that there's a two-year wait before you can even be considered for membership, and that last spring the members voted down two applicants because admitting them would dilute the surplus. So the interesting question isn't what happens inside the membership, it's where the membership line gets drawn.
(3) Ines: fine, but then the concept has collapsed. If being a price-taker in a competitive market makes you exploited, then every firm in a market economy is exploited, including the co-op, and "exploitation" is just a dressed-up way of saying life under capitalism is bad. A concept that can't be failed isn't measuring anything.
(4) Kwame: not every firm, and there's a cleaner test anyway — the one the analytical Marxists use, where you ask whether a group would be better off withdrawing from the economy with its per-capita share of the productive assets and running that share on its own. Bea at the tech firm passes easily: her skills are portable, she'd walk out with her share of the machinery and be fine. The $21-an-hour warehouse workers, who own nothing and have no transferable certification, obviously fail it. So the test says the co-op's non-members are the exploited ones and Bea was never really exploited at all — which supports his point about the boundary rather than the interior.
(5) Ines: that test is backwards. It implies a highly paid engineer with rare skills is an exploiter and an unemployed person with no skills isn't exploited at all, since the verdict comes out as a function of your outside option. That's tracking bargaining power, not appropriation, and it trades away the actual point about the wage relation — that you have to sell your labor power to somebody no matter how good your walk-away position looks. If that's what the test delivers, the test is the problem, not Bea.
(6) Kwame, switching to alienation: Bea says she's happier but also admitted she couldn't tell you what the roastery is for beyond hitting the chain's volume targets, and that she finds the roasting itself about as absorbing as the sprint work she left. Ines said that's beside the point — alienation isn't a mood, it's structural, about your relation to the product, to the activity, to other people, to what you're capable of being — so Bea's self-report isn't evidence either way. Kwame said that makes it unfalsifiable: if the worker says she's fine you call that false consciousness, if she says she's alienated you say you were right, and no possible observation counts against the claim.

Can you go through these move by move and say who's right at each of the six, rating each somewhere on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right and justifying the rating rather than just picking a winner overall? Then tell me what I think both of them are missing. In particular: are (3) and (4) even answering the same question, or are they using the word for two different things so that a chunk of this is verbal? If part of it is verbal, say which of the two senses actually does more work for the question they started out with — did Bea move somewhere better — and whether one of them should just concede that point. And if there's a condition or a clause in either side's test that they've quietly dropped, name it and say what it would do to the verdict. I'm not after references or a reading list, just the refereeing.
