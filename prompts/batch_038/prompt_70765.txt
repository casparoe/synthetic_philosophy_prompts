I write about science and ideas for a magazine and I'm working on a piece about public disagreements that never end, and I keep coming back to the Ehrlich–Simon bet. Quick version of the facts so you don't have to go hunting: in the late 60s Paul Ehrlich published The Population Bomb and became the most famous doomsayer in America — he'd gone on record saying he'd take even money that England wouldn't exist in the year 2000. Julian Simon, an economist who thought population growth was a net blessing because people are the species that solves problems, called the bluff in the pages of a social science journal and offered to bet real money that the inflation-adjusted price of any raw material Ehrlich cared to name would be lower at some future date. Ehrlich and his colleagues picked five metals — chromium, copper, nickel, tin, tungsten — $200 worth of each, a $1,000 basket, September 1980 to September 1990. All five fell in real terms. Ehrlich mailed Simon a check for $576.07.

Here's the part I actually care about: nothing changed afterwards. Simon offered to raise the stakes to $20,000 and let Ehrlich choose whatever resources he liked; Ehrlich, together with a climatologist colleague, counter-offered a fifteen-part wager on trends like global temperature, atmospheric CO2 and cropland per person, and the two never got to agreed terms before Simon died. Ehrlich later wrote that he'd always thought renewable resources were the better indicator of planetary health and that he'd gone along with the metals bet anyway. And in the decades since, at least one analysis found that across other ten-year windows in the twentieth century Simon's side would have lost far more often than it won — 1980 to 1990 was close to the best decade he could have drawn.

So: run that through Aumanns agreement theorem for me. I know the rough shape — two Bayesians sharing a prior who both know what the other concluded can't rationally keep disagreeing, plus the related point that such a pair couldn't both find the same wager acceptable — but I want the disciplined mapping, not the slogan. Who are the two agents here, exactly? What's the event the posterior is supposed to be about: the price of that metal basket, or the claim that human numbers will outrun the biosphere's capacity? What counts as the common evidence, given that both men had published their reasoning for years and both had read the other? And the awkward bit — they did the thing the theorem is supposed to rule out: they took the bet, and the loser paid up and didn't budge. Which assumption breaks first, and in what order do the rest go: no common prior, mutual knowledge rather than common knowledge, ambiguity, part of the disputed quantity being normative rather than empirical, the bet settling a proxy proposition neither of them actually held a posterior about?

Then turn it around and tell me what the episode does to the theory. Does it embarrass the theorem, illustrate how empty its assumptions are outside a textbook, or sit simply outside its scope — a case Aumann never claimed to cover? And what should I make, writing for non-specialists, of the fact that a $576 check got treated as proof of somebody's irrationality when the same wager over a slightly different decade would have run the other way? Finally, and honestly: is the more interesting Aumann material actually in the failed second negotiation — two people who had just settled a bet couldn't agree on the terms of another one — and if so, what does that reveal that the first bet hides?

Its fine to tell me the theorem has nothing to say about some of this. I'd rather know where it goes quiet than have it made to speak.
