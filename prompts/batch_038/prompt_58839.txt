I run a Thursday-night reading group — 12 people, mostly ex-programmers plus one ER nurse and a guy who describes himself as a "reformed Bayesian" — in the back room of a tea place that keeps us around because we order a lot. Everyone has read enough rationalist blog material to be dangerous and not enough to be careful, which is to say the default failure mode of our meetups is that two people perform disagreement at each other for forty minutes while the rest of us eat biscuits. Next month's theme is disagreement itself, and I want to open the session with something physical around Aumann's agreement theorem, before anyone gets to say "steelman" or "double crux" out loud.

Here's the moment I'm trying to engineer. Not a lecture, and nobody hears the name of the theorem until the debrief. I want them trading numbers with each other for long enough that somebody looks up mid-round and says some version of "hold on, are we even allowed to still disagree?" The feeling I'm chasing is that persisting in disagreement after you've exchanged posteriors isn't a difference of perspective or a personality thing, it's closer to an arithmetic error — and then, second, that every way out of that is an escape hatch with a visible price tag.

Constraints, since they'll shape the design: no money and no real stakes beyond a chocolate bar and bragging rights. One whiteboard, index cards, pens, and a printer at home so handouts are fine. Roughly half the group will stall out if you ask them to compute 7/23 in their heads, so please build the information structure so that updating is basically counting dots or at worst halving and doubling — I want the arithmetic to be embarrassing easy and the logic to be the hard part. Nobody should be able to sit it out by claiming they're not a maths person.

What I actually want you to produce: a concrete state space and prior plus each player's information partition, small enough to print on cards; the round structure, i.e. exactly what gets announced each round (posteriors only, or evidence too?), how many rounds, and how you make the announcements genuinely common knowledge rather than merely mutual; and a scoring scheme with no money that makes honest reporting of your own posterior the dominant move and makes converging feel like winning rather than like surrendering. Then a second phase where I break exactly one hypothesis of the theorem, and I want you to pick which one. My instincts are (a) secretly hand two players different priors, (b) let people strategically misreport their posteriors, or (c) cut the announcement process off before it becomes common knowledge — tell me which produces the best gasp, or if you think all three are weaker than breaking something else entirely, break that instead and say why.

Then the debrief: map specific moments from the run onto specific hypotheses, and land the practical upshot I care about most, which is that if you and I share a prior and you tell me your posterior, your number is now evidence about mine, so a good disagreement is a hunt for where our information structures differ rather than a debate to be won. Also give me the usual honest section — what predictably goes wrong the first time this is run (I've done enough of these to know someone will announce a number they never actually computed, and someone else will refuse to reveal because it leaks their card), how I handle each failure live, and which feature of the theorem the activity flatly cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply by talking.

Please be committed about this: one design, not a menu of three options with tradeoffs for me to choose between. If you think my framing of what they should come away seeing is wrong, say so and build the thing you'd build instead. And show your actual numbers — the states, the prior, one player's partition, and the posteriors after each round, worked through — so I can sanity-check it before I print sixty cards and discover at minute 40 that the game doesn't converge at all.
