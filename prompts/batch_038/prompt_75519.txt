I have spent roughly fifteen years reading and posting on rationalist blogs and their offshoots, and the main habit I have taken from that milieu is an impatience with questions that cannot be lost. The debate over whether famines are natural disasters or political crimes seems to me badly posed in exactly that way, and I have drafted an essay proposing a replacement criterion. Below is the draft as it stands. I would like substantive comments on it, not encouragement and not a rewrite, before I post it.

**Draft: The Last Cheap Veto**

*§1. Three questions hiding inside one.* The familiar framing asks whether a famine is a natural disaster or a political crime. That framing fails because "what caused it" is not one question. There are at least three, and they come apart.

(i) Sufficiency: would the harvest failure, absent any human action, have produced mass starvation? For Ireland after 1845, plausibly yes, on some scale. For Bengal in 1943, unclear, since there was no single dramatic collapse of aggregate supply. For China between 1958 and 1962, no: drought and flood damaged harvests in places, but the mortality was not what the weather would have produced on its own.

(ii) Agency: was some human decision a necessary part of the causal chain? Almost always yes, and therefore almost never informative. Nearly every famine in the record passes this test, which is precisely why the test cannot carry the weight people put on it when they say a famine was "man-made."

(iii) The question I think is actually being asked: was there a point at which an identifiable decision-maker, in possession of — or cheaply able to acquire — the information that mass death was coming, could have prevented it at a cost they would have been willing to bear to save members of their own group?

I will call such a point the last cheap veto. "Last" because after it, prevention is no longer a matter of declining to adopt a policy but of reversing one already adopted. "Cheap" because the cost is measured against the decision-maker's own evaluative standards at the time: a veto is cheap if what must be given up is something the decision-maker would have given up to prevent comparable deaths among people they counted as their own.

*§2. Three cases, three veto structures.*

Ireland, 1845–52. The blight was exogenous; the veto structure was dense. Relief was wound back after the change of government in 1846, and a subsequent amendment to the poor law made the possession of more than a quarter acre of land a disqualification for relief, forcing tenants to choose between their holding and their ration. London declined to prohibit the export of food from Ireland, though it had prohibited such exports during earlier episodes of distress, so the option was live and known rather than novel. What I want to draw out is not the moral quality of any single one of these decisions but the fact that the veto was available over and over, at many administrative levels, and was declined at nearly all of them. Laissez-faire, on this picture, is not the absence of decisions; it is the name of a pattern of decisions.

Bengal, 1943. Amartya Sen's account is usually summarized as the claim that there was no shortage of food, which overstates it: his point is that the aggregate shortfall was modest and could not explain either the scale or the social distribution of the mortality. What did the work was the collapse of particular groups' entitlements — landless laborers, transport workers, fishermen — under wartime inflation, a boom economy in Calcutta, preferential supply schemes for designated priority classes, the removal of boats and rice stocks from coastal districts under the denial policies adopted against a Japanese advance, and provincial price controls that produced withholding, a black market, and speculative hoarding. The province never formally declared a famine, and relief became substantial only once the army took over its funding in late 1943. Bengal is the interesting case for my criterion because the epistemic condition is contested: for months the authorities were not merely failing to act, they were failing to agree that there was anything to act about. I do not want to require actual knowledge. I want to say that a decision-maker who declines to acquire information they could cheaply acquire has taken the veto all the same. Call this the epistemic veto.

China, 1958–62. This is the hard case, and it is hard because the standard story is a story about information. Procurement was levied on the basis of inflated yield reports; labor was diverted into steel and waterworks; agronomic fashions of Soviet origin damaged yields; the communal dining halls appear to have consumed stocks early in at least some regions, though how much weight that deserves is disputed; and grain continued to leave the country. On my criterion one might be tempted to say there was no veto, because there was no knowledge, and hence to classify the deadliest famine in history as something closer to a catastrophe of incompetence. I want to resist that, and the resistance is what the essay turns on. The information failure was produced. Criticism of the Leap was suppressed at a leadership conference in 1959 and converted into a campaign against those who had voiced it; accurate reporting had been made career-ending and exaggeration had been made safe. The right description is therefore not that the state did not know, but that the state had chosen the kind of apparatus that could not know. The epistemic veto, applied at the level of the choice of an information system rather than the level of an individual quota decision, is where the responsibility sits.

*§3. What this does to the democracy thesis.* Sen's widely repeated claim is that famines do not occur in functioning democracies with a free press. It is normally read as a claim about incentives: leaders who face elections and newspapers have reasons to prevent starvation that leaders who face neither do not. On my framework the better reading is about the price of information. A free press makes knowing cheap and makes refusing to know visible, which removes the epistemic veto from the menu; elections then supply the incentive to use what has become cheap to acquire. The two variables are separable, and separating them yields a prediction the usual formulation does not make: an authoritarian regime that maintains a protected, inexpensive internal reporting channel should do materially better at famine avoidance than a democracy whose reporting channel is expensive, captured, or dangerous to use. Conversely, the failure mode to watch in contemporary food crises is not the absence of elections but a rise in the price of telling the truth about hunger.

*§4. An objection I cannot yet answer.* A referee will say that the last cheap veto is negligence analysis with extra steps: a capacity to rescue at modest cost, a counterfactual, a duty. My answer is that the criterion is not about whether a duty existed but about where in time the duty was cheaply dischargable, and that the temporal index does real classificatory work — it separates regimes that never had the information from regimes that dismantled their own capacity to have it, which ordinary rescue analysis treats identically. I am not confident the answer holds.

**What I would like from you**

1. Try to break the criterion. Give me at least three cases, historical or constructed, where the last cheap veto delivers a verdict I should not want: something classified as a political crime that should not be, or something let off that should not be. I am particularly interested in famines where the party blocking relief is an armed non-state actor rather than a government, in sieges, and in cases where the "cost they would have borne for their own" benchmark is itself contaminated by the regime's values. For each, say whether the criterion can be repaired by tightening "cheap" or by tightening "in a position to know," and whether the repair is ad hoc.

2. Is the epistemic veto a genuine addition or a device for reaching my preferred verdicts? "Cheaply able to acquire" looks unbounded to me: for almost any government, almost any information is cheaply acquirable in some description or other. Tell me what would bound it, or say plainly that it cannot be bounded.

3. The cheapest deflationary reading of this draft is that the last cheap veto is Sen's entitlement approach plus Sen's democracy thesis with the labels changed. Argue that reading as strongly as you can, then tell me whether anything in the draft survives it. If nothing does, I would rather cut the essay than post it.

4. Accuracy. Flag, with a rough confidence level, any claim above that you think is wrong, overstated, or sits in genuinely divided historiography — especially the volume and causal significance of Irish food exports, whether Bengal's aggregate shortfall really was modest, how much weight the dining halls should bear relative to procurement in China, and whether my compressed account of the 1959 conference and the campaign that followed it is fair. Please do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; I will do the checking myself. What I want is a list of suspected problems and a direction to look in. Where you are unsure whether you are recalling a fact correctly, mark it as unsure rather than asserting it.

5. Fairness to the other side. Does §1(i) do enough justice to the view that weather and crop disease did the causal work? Is there a substantive version of "natural disaster" that is not merely the negation of "political crime" — something about the shape of the risk, or the absence of any margin in pre-industrial agriculture — that I am simply ignoring?

6. The normative payload in "cheap." I have only half unpacked it: cheap to whom, denominated in what, and why index the standard to the decision-maker's own valuation of their own group's lives rather than to any impartial one? My instinct is that the index is a feature, because it makes the criterion usable by people who are not consequentialists and because it isolates the specific form of bad faith I care about. I expect a referee to say it makes the criterion relativistic and therefore incapable of condemning anything. Which of us is right?

7. Structure. Which of the three cases is carrying the argument, and should the other two be compressed to a paragraph each? Is §3 the real contribution, in which case should it open the piece? Is this a long-form blog post of about three thousand words, or does the framework need a six-thousand-word treatment with one case study done properly?

8. Testability. Is §3's prediction falsifiable as I have stated it? Tell me what a minimally convincing comparison would look like and what would count as the prediction failing, because at present I suspect it is close to unfalsifiable and that bothers me.

Please write your comments in American English.
