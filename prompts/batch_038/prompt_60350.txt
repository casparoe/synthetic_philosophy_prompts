I'm TA-ing a master's seminar in practical ethics — 16 students, and I've been handed the 80-minute slot next Thursday to run something myself. About half of them came from policy degrees and are openly skeptical of philosophy games, so whatever I do needs actual numbers in it and a payoff they can point to at the end. We've done utilitarianism and Kant, and for the last two weeks we've been on the idea that people who disagree about what's good might still have reasons to cut deals instead of fighting — funding each other's projects, dropping campaigns in exchange for concessions, generally tolerating views you think are false because there's something to gain from it. Everyone in the room nods at this in the abstract and then nobody has ever had to actually sit down and do it. I want them to run into the problems with the idea on their own rather than listen to me list them.

So: design me a table-top game where small groups play members of a funding council dividing up a shared pot, and each group secretly holds a different card telling it what it actually cares about — one that only counts total wellbeing, one that only counts how the worst-off group does, one that refuses on principle to buy or sell a particular thing no matter what's offered, one that isn't sure which of two views is right and has to trade on some kind of average across them, and one whose view is genuinely weird and intense about something the others barely care about. Give me a printed menu of projects with a conversion table so each group can work out its own score from any allocation, but can't see anyone else's. I want at least three rounds: one where they get a fixed slice and can't trade at all and half the room ends up miserable, one where trading is open, and one where you introduce something that makes the boom turn sour. The thing I most want to emerge, and this is the part I'd build the whole game around if I had the time: the cards are private, so there's a real incentive to claim you hold an uncompromisable view you don't actually hold, or to overstate how much something costs you — and the groups that play that hardest should end up best off. If that dynamic doesn't come out of the rules by itself, tell me and change the rules until it does. I need the card texts, the project menu with real usable numbers, a score sheet, a minute-by-minute timeline, the debrief that maps specific moments in play onto the general problems, and a straight answer on what predictably goes wrong the first time someone runs it (I'm assuming the arithmetic and the group with the no-trading card getting bored, but you tell me). Also give me a shrunk-down version for 8 people in case of no-shows.

Constraints: no real money, just paper tokens; nothing gets named as moral trade or anything else until the debrief; and the point should come out of what happens at the table, not out of a speech I give afterwards. In the debrief I want you to be blunt about which features of the real thing the game can't reproduce — my own suspicion is that handing them a conversion table quietly gives them a common metric that doesn't exist in real disagreements, and that role-playing a value you don't hold makes selling it out feel free in a way it never is, but I want your list, not mine. And if you think the whole thing is going to collapse because the bookkeeping is too fiddly for people who've had one coffee, say so and simplify it rather than handing me something unusable.
