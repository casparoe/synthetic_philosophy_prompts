I'm drafting the second half of a seminar paper on metametaphysics, and the argument I built to motivate my deflationary conclusion has begun to frighten me. Each step looked like something a reasonable person would sign off on, and yet the argument appears to generalize into a thesis I don't believe and that my advisor, on reading the draft, described as self-undermining. I don't want a survey of the literature on this debate; I want a diagnosis of my own reasoning. Here is the argument as it currently stands.

(1) Call a dispute between two speakers merely verbal if there is an interpretation of each speaker's words that is admissible — one that respects the way each of them actually uses those words — and on which both speakers' claims come out true.

(2) Consider the dispute between a universalist, who holds that any two things have a fusion, and a nihilist, who holds that there are no composite objects at all, only simples standing in various relations.

(3) They agree about every concrete fact: which simples there are, how they are distributed, how they are arranged at each moment, and how those arrangements change. Neither thinks any further fact about the world could decide between them, and each is happy to grant the other's description of the concrete situation.

(4) So there is an obvious pair of interpretations available. Read the universalist's "there are" unrestrictedly; read the nihilist's "there are" as ranging over simples only. On those readings, the universalist's claim that there are tables and the nihilist's claim that there are only simples arranged tablewise are both true of one and the same world.

(5) Each reading is admissible in the sense required by (1). Neither speaker uses "there are" differently from the other outside the seminar room: both count the cups on the table, both report the same furniture moving in and out of the building, and each will cheerfully accept any ordinary claim the other makes once it is restated in his own idiom. What they diverge on is only what to say when the question is posed philosophically.

(6) Therefore, by (1), the dispute about composition is merely verbal: the two are not disagreeing about the world but about which way of using a quantifier to adopt.

(7) Nothing in the route from (1) to (6) exploited anything special about composition. The template is this: if the parties to a dispute agree on all the concrete facts, and no concrete fact could decide between them, then an interpretation exists that makes both of their claims true without making them contradict one another, and so the dispute is verbal.

(8) That template applies equally to personal identity over time, to whether there are numbers, to whether the vessel in the museum is the ship of Theseus, to whether races exist, and to whether anyone acts freely. In each case the parties agree on all the concrete facts and quarrel only over what to say about them.

(9) And in fact every philosophical dispute is like that. If two people disagreed about some concrete fact, they would not be having a philosophical dispute at all; they would simply be mistaken about the evidence. So by (7), every philosophical dispute is merely verbal.

(10) But then (9) is itself a claim in a philosophical dispute, and the realist I am arguing against may accept it without altering a word of what he says. If our disagreement is only about words, his rejection of my thesis is not the rejection of anything, and my conclusion cannot be stated as a disagreement with anyone.

(11) Worse still: if any interpretation that makes both speakers' claims come out true counts as admissible, then admissibility is doing no work in (1); but if admissibility is doing work, it must be fixed by something other than which claims come out true, and (1) offers no account of what that something could be. Either way, the argument gives no determinate content to the very notion it deploys.

Which step should I reject, and why did it look perfectly fine to me when I wrote it down? I want the diagnosis to be specific rather than a general expression of concern: identify the inference that fails, say what reading of a term makes it valid and what different reading makes the relevant premise true, and explain why both readings cannot be in play at once. I'm also prepared to be told that the argument is valid and that one of my premises is simply false — but then I want a price list. My own instinct is to keep (3) and (5) and give up (1), because the most striking datum of this debate seems to me to be that the universalist and the nihilist really do agree about every concrete fact, and I don't want to be told that this agreement is an illusion. If holding onto (3) and (5) obliges me to say what makes one interpretation admissible and another inadmissible, tell me exactly what resources that commitment draws on — a distinguished, joint-carving way of using the quantifier; a constraint imposed by compositionality together with each speaker's acceptance of obvious truths about the sentences he utters; a requirement that the interpretation leave the speakers genuinely contradicting each other rather than merely talking past one another; something else — and what each of these costs the person who adopts it. Finally: suppose I delete (7) through (9) and stop at (6), advancing the deflationary conclusion about composition alone and saying nothing about philosophy in general. Is the shorter argument harder to resist, or does whatever you say about the long version also dispose of the short one?
