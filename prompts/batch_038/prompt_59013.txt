I'm the registrar at a small regional heritage trust — four of us, one part-time conservator, and we look after about 1,200 accessioned objects plus a handful of listed buildings. The catalogue has a field nobody thought hard about when it was designed, "same object as accessioned: Y/N," and it turns out to drive everything: insurance schedules, whether a piece can go out on loan, whether a listed structure keeps its listing after a fire, and awkwardly whether a donor can claim they gave us the original thing. Since we've had two fire-damaged buildings, a flood, a lot of emergency conservation, and now people wanting to "restore" objects by scanning them and printing replacements, I end up being the one who has to fill that field in — roughly a couple of times a month — and Ive been doing it by vibes. My committee wants something written down by the spring meeting, and they want it short enough that a non-specialist can follow it.

So: could you, acting as a metaphysician who's actually spent time in conservation practice — someone who knows the material constitution literature cold (statue and clay, coincident objects, sortals, four-dimensionalism, the way artifacts seem to depend on somebody's intentions, the whole gradual-replacement mess) but who also accepts that a registrar has maybe twenty minutes per object and no philosophy training — write me a decision procedure I can actually run? I want the questions in order, what counts as a satisfactory answer to each, where it terminates in N, where it terminates in Y, and where it has to hand the decision back to the committee because what's left is a question of institutional policy rather than fact. Be explicit about which distinctions from the literature you're keeping and which you're quietly dropping, and why, because I can imagine an actual philosopher reading this and saying I've mangled the underlying ideas. Also flag honestly where you think the procedure will misfire. Then work through at least three of the following as real applications, showing the answers at each step in the order you set them: (a) a nineteenth-century fishing boat, re-planked and re-framed over forty years until nothing original is left except the keel and the painted name — the family who gave it to us will be furious if we call it a replica, but the insurers need an answer; (b) a bronze statuette that broke, was badly mended in the 1930s, and where we're now proposing to strip the mend and re-make the missing hand from a scan; (c) a listed building that burned down and is being rebuilt to the original drawings, new stone from the same quarry, same masons' firm — is the rebuilt thing the listed thing; (d) a carved figure destroyed in the flood and re-carved by the same carver, same tools, same species of timber, working from photographs; (e) an object we've scanned and printed, so there are now two candidates in the store and the file itself is what everyone wants to display. For at least one of these I want the procedure to produce an answer I know the committee will not like — don't soften it, and tell me what I should actually say to them.

Two more constraints. First, we have a house rule that the field can't be flipped back: once we've recorded N for an object, later stages of the same restoration programme can't get us back to Y. I realise thats going to interact badly with gradual replacement and with the transitivity of identity, so I'd like you to say straight out whether the rule is even coherent, and if it isn't, what to replace it with. Second, no citations, no reading list, no namedropping of papers — nobody here is going to look anything up, and I want the thing to stand on its own reasoning. Start by brainstorming out loud about what actually has to be decided and where the hard choices are (including whether "is it the same object" is even one question or three wearing a trenchcoat), then give me the procedure, then the worked examples, then a short section on what the procedure cannot do and what the failure modes look like in practice. The test I care about: could I hand this to a new assistant registrar in their first week and have them get it right most of the time, and defend the wrong ones?
