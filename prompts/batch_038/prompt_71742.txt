I am the graduate teaching assistant for a third-year undergraduate module, "Republic and Spectacle: Political Thought from Rousseau to the July Monarchy," and I am marking the unseen essay for one of two seminar groups. Marks are submitted out of 100 and are moderated against a second marker's independent assessment; my comments are read alongside the scripts by the module convenor, so they have to be tied to particular claims rather than impressionistic, and the feedback sheet allows roughly 250 words of written comment per script. The module's marking descriptors are the standard ones for this level: 70 and above for work that is accurate about the text, advances a case of its own, and meets objections; 60–69 for accurate, well-organized work that stays close to the line taken in lectures; 50–59 for broadly competent work with gaps or unexamined assumptions; below 50 where there are substantive misreadings. Students were told that footnotes were not required and that they might paraphrase rather than quote, so neither the absence of references nor the absence of quotation should count against the candidate. What follows is the essay question exactly as it appeared on the paper, and then one student's response in full.

Essay question (1,500 words maximum): Rousseau is routinely described as a philosopher who wished to ban the theatre. Explain, with reference to his reply to d'Alembert, what precisely he finds objectionable about the theatre; why he takes that objection to bear on a small republic such as Geneva rather than on every political community; and what he proposes in the theatre's place. In your answer, say whether Rousseau's reasons coincide with the reasons given for expelling the poets in Plato's Republic.

Student's script: Rousseau's quarrel with the theatre arises out of a particular occasion rather than out of general reflection. In the article on Geneva that he contributed to the Encyclopédie in 1757, d'Alembert admired the moderation of the Genevan pastors, who were prepared to tolerate doctrines a stricter Calvinism would have condemned, and suggested that the same liberality might reasonably extend to the stage: a well-regulated theatre, he thought, would polish Genevan manners without corrupting them. Rousseau, a Genevan citizen who had by then broken with the philosophes, replied at length the following year. The resulting letter is not chiefly a moral denunciation of playwrights; it is a political argument about which institutions a republic can afford, in the broadest sense of that word.

Rousseau's first and most consequential claim is that the theatre has no power to reform mores, because it is always downstream of them. A stage takes its tone from its public: dramatists flatter the tastes, prejudices and passions their audiences already possess, and succeed only in so far as they do. It follows that the theatre can serve as a diagnostic instrument — one learns a good deal about Paris from the plays Paris applauds — but never as an instrument of correction. He also rejects the consolations offered by the defenders of the stage, though here his objection is narrower than is often supposed. He accepts the general Aristotelian account of tragedy as a purgation of pity and fear; what he denies is that Geneva's citizens, being neither vicious nor idle, have passions standing in need of purgation. For such a people the theatre would be not a medicine but a poison administered to the healthy. More damagingly, the pity a spectator feels for a virtuous sufferer is a cheap and self-congratifying emotion: having shed a few tears in the dark, he goes home persuaded that he has already discharged whatever he owed to the unfortunate.

Rousseau's treatment of comedy is the most searching part of the letter. Comedy, he argues, attacks not vice but virtue: it makes goodness ridiculous. The extended discussion of Molière's Misanthrope is the illustration. Alceste is upright, plain-spoken and severe, and Molière converts precisely these qualities into comic defects, while Philinte, the accommodating man of the world who counsels tact and self-command, is made agreeable. The audience leaves having learned to distrust rigor and to admire flexibility. Ridicule, Rousseau insists, is the one sanction against which there is no appeal, since it cannot be argued with; a society that has learned to laugh at virtue has found a way of punishing it that no law could match.

There is also a political economy to the argument. A theatre requires leisure and money; it is an institution of large, wealthy, idle cities. In Paris the cost falls on people who have time to waste. In Geneva it would fall on artisans and labourers, who would spend at the door money their households needed and would forgo hours of work that a small city cannot spare, while the sums collected passed to a class of performers and to the apparatus of luxury gathered around them. Rousseau objects to the actor's condition as such: it is a trade in which a person sells his own public person, and, in the case of actresses, it exposes women on whose modesty the mores of the republic in part depend. He is careful to note that the Genevan law against comedians is not an anathema laid on the profession but a prudential measure appropriate to a city too small to police the disorder, indebtedness and dissipation that a playhouse attracts.

What Rousseau sets against the theatre is not austerity but a different kind of spectacle. The letter closes by proposing public festivals held in the open air and in daylight: races, gymnastic exercises, dances, communal meals, weddings celebrated in the presence of the whole city. His striking formula is that one should plant a post crowned with flowers in the middle of a square and gather the people around it, and there one has a festival, because in a republic the people are themselves the spectacle; the onlookers see one another and become performers in their own entertainment. This, however, sits badly with what precedes it. If Rousseau has established that no spectacle can alter mores, and if the fatal feature of the theatre is that it renders its audience passive, then the festival can only be a nostalgic afterthought or a piece of special pleading. It is hard to see how he can maintain both that aesthetic experience is politically inert and that a change in the form of public entertainment could sustain a republic.

The comparison with Plato largely settles the matter. Rousseau's reasons are in substance the reasons of Republic X: the stage trades in imitation, at several removes from the truth, and it strengthens the inferior part of the soul by encouraging citizens to indulge emotions they ought to govern. Rousseau knew this passage well and invokes Plato's expulsion of Homer with evident approval; his contribution is to have applied that critique to the concrete circumstances of an eighteenth-century city-state. The one genuine difference is that Plato's concern is with truth and the soul where Rousseau's is with the solvency and self-sufficiency of a small republic — a difference of emphasis rather than of principle.
