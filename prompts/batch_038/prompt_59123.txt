I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for something like fifteen years, and one thing I've learned is to be suspicious of arguments where the persuasive work is being done by a picture rather than by a claim. Last month our local meetup's group chat got into a fight about European migration policy and climate-linked displacement, and it collapsed into a rerun of Garrett Hardin's 1974 lifeboat essay: rich nations as lifeboats, the people of poor nations as swimmers in the ocean around them, a boat with a few places free but not a hundred, and the conclusion that admitting everyone sinks everyone. The version being defended here is not the undergraduate strawman, which is the problem. It's being run by a friend who works as a structural engineer and who insists that the standard rebuttals — the world already produces enough calories, commons can in fact be governed, the demographic transition has already happened in most of the places at issue — all answer a question Hardin wasn't asking. On his reading, the load-bearing elements are two: the safety factor, meaning the deliberate margin between rated capacity and actual load that every engineered system carries and that amateurs always want to spend; and a failure mode that is a cliff rather than a graceful degradation. He says that once you take both seriously, refusing the swimmers stops looking like cruelty and starts looking like ordinary load management. I think he's wrong, or at least that the analogy is carrying most of the weight his conclusion needs, but I can't get past a general suspicion, and I've noticed that my side reliably reaches for aggregate calorie arithmetic that the lifeboat framing simply absorbs without strain.

So I'd like the analogy audited as an argument rather than as rhetoric. Give me the correspondence table first — what plays the role of the hull and its rated capacity, the ocean, the swimmers, the current occupants, the safety margin, the absence of any authority entitled to decide who boards, and the one-shot character of the rescue — and then sort the disanalogies into those that genuinely threaten his conclusion and those that are merely unsatisfying. I want the engineering question taken seriously rather than waved away: is the safety factor being used the way engineers actually use it, given that a margin exists to absorb variance in loads and material strengths you can estimate statistically, not to justify refusing every increment of additional load forever; and does the cliff-shaped failure mode correspond to anything we know about how states, welfare systems, housing markets, or food distribution actually fail, as opposed to degrading, absorbing, and being repaired? Then please separate the three arguments Hardin's essay is usually credited with, because I suspect people fuse them: the lifeboat itself, the ratchet claim that relieving famine removes the check and thereby increases the number of future swimmers, and the commons claim he brings in when the topic shifts to immigration. Which of these is an analogy, which is a freestanding empirical prediction, and which one is actually carrying the conclusion my friend wants — a hard cap on admissions, rather than a case for triage and sequencing?

Finally, run the repair test. Rebuild the lifeboat so that it has the features the real target obviously has — production inside the boat, storage, trade with other boats, capacity that can be enlarged or that fails in stages, occupants who age and are replaced, swimmers who arrive as a flow spread over decades rather than all at once, and some body with legitimate authority over admissions — and tell me whether the refusal conclusion survives the rebuild or degrades into something weaker and more interesting, such as an argument about rates and ordering. If the repaired lifeboat no longer does the work, propose a better source domain for the underlying problem — hospital surge capacity, load shedding on a grid, backpressure in a queueing system, whatever you think is genuinely apt — and say what conclusion that one licenses and what it conspicuously does not. I would rather have a verdict that costs me something: if the analogy turns out to be sounder than I assume, and there is a real class of cases in which admitting the swimmers does sink the boat, tell me what that class looks like and how I would recognize a member of it in the wild. No bibliography, please; I'll chase down the sources myself.
