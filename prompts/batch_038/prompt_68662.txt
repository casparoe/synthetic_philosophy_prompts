I sit on a university animal care and use committee as its unaffiliated member — a retired physiologist who has only lately taken up philosophy, and the two habits now argue in my head. Next month's docket includes a proposal to add a terminal surgical laboratory to a graduate course in operative technique: pigs are anaesthetised, trainees practise anastomoses and haemostasis, and the animals are euthanised before consciousness returns. No pain is inflicted. Our terms of reference require replacement of animals wherever practicable, and the investigators have offered abattoir cadavers and two simulators as partial substitutes while insisting that living vascular tissue simply does not behave the same way.

One member, a veterinarian, objects on Kantian lines: we owe animals no direct duties, and cruelty is condemned because it uproots the shared feeling for suffering on which our duties to human beings depend — yet nothing here suffers, so the objection must rest wholly on what the practice does to the trainees' characters. Kant likewise permits the instrumental use of animals while recoiling from painful experiments undertaken for mere speculation when the end could be reached otherwise, and whether operative training is such an end is precisely what divides us.

Please draft the memorandum I shall circulate: a definite recommendation, with conditions and a timeline; an honest assessment of whether an indirect duty can bite at all when no suffering occurs; and the strongest case for approval, put so that a member who accepts it can still vote yes.
