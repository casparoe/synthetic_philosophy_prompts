I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy or neuroscience, and for the past several months I have been building a small active-inference-style agent as a side project: a discrete grid world, a hand-coded generative model over a handful of hidden states, policy selection by expected free energy with a five-step lookahead, and a softmax over policies at each step. In one corner of the environment there is a state with nearly perfectly predictable observations, and my agent reliably drifts there and then stops moving — which is exactly the behavior the so-called dark room objection says any free-energy minimizer ought to exhibit. Before I rewrite the whole thing, I would like a clear-eyed assessment of the standard replies to that objection, judged not by how they read in prose but by whether they would actually change what I put in the code.

Here are the six replies as I currently understand them; please tell me explicitly if I have mischaracterized any of them. (A) The agent's prior is not neutral: it encodes a preference for survival-typical states, so a dark room is itself surprising. (B) The objection assumes a static environment, whereas over a long enough horizon sedentary behavior accumulates surprise, because dying is about the most improbable state an organism's own model can assign probability to. (C) The objection targets variational free energy over a single state, but action selection minimizes expected free energy, whose epistemic term forces sampling of uncertain regions, so the objection is a category error. (D) Real agents are allostatic: their setpoints are themselves revised as self-evidence accumulates, so treating the prior as fixed is the actual mistake. (E) The free energy principle is a claim about model evidence rather than a behavioral prediction, so the dark room case does not refute it — it only shows the principle needs supplementary empirical assumptions about priors, at the price of being hard to falsify. (F) An agent prefers a dark room only if it has no reason to sample the world at all; once actions are folded into the generative model, the states worth occupying are action-dependent ones.

For each reply, give me three scores from 0 to 10: implementation leverage (does accepting it change the algorithm I write, and by how much), robustness (does it survive an adversarial reading, or does it collapse under one obvious counterexample), and non-question-begging (does it do any work beyond asserting that organisms happen to have precisely the priors that dissolve the objection). Then produce a single composite score per reply using weights of 0.5, 0.3, and 0.2 on those three dimensions, and a tier list running from S down to C, with one or two sentences per reply explaining where it lands and, where relevant, naming its specific failure mode. Finally, give me one number between 0 and 1 — your estimate of the probability that an agent of exactly the kind I described (fixed prior, five-step expected free energy, softmax policy selection) will exhibit sustained exploration rather than settling into a low-stimulus corner — and identify the single change to that setup which would move the number the most.
