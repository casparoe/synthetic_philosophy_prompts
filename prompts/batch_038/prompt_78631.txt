Thirty years at a bench, and only now that I'm retired am I reading this stuff properly. I've just come across the information-cascade and rational-herding literature -- Bikhchandani, Hirshleifer and Welch, and Banerjee's simple model of herd behavior -- and I want to run it on a case I half know from the history of science: the South Sea Bubble of 1720, and Newton's part in it.

Facts as I have them, stop me if I've got them wrong: the company offered to take on big chunks of the national debt in exchange for stock; the share price went from around a hundred pounds in the winter to somewhere near a thousand by midsummer and then fell back to roughly where it started; the company lent people money against its own shares so they could buy more of them; directors were trading on what they knew about the debt conversions; and in June Parliament passed an act, lobbied for by South Sea itself, banning new joint-stock companies without a charter, which killed off the rival schemes -- and shortly after that the price turned. Newton bought in early, sold at a good profit, watched it keep climbing, bought back near the top, and lost reportedly on the order of twenty thousand pounds. There's a story that he said afterwards he could calculate the motions of the heavenly bodies but not the madness of men.

So map the model onto this: who are the agents, what counts as each one's private signal, what observable action are they conditioning on, and roughly when in that spring and summer would a cascade have started? Then tell me where the model goes quiet or gets the facts wrong.

My sticking point is that the cascade story has people herding because they read others' buying as evidence. But plenty of the late buyers must have known the thing was overpriced and bought anyway, planning to hand it to someone else. That isn't a cascade, it's a different animal. Can you tell the two apart from the inside, and does it change what Newton should have done? Second: Newton re-entering was itself an enormously loud signal. Does the theory have anything to say about a high-status agent whose action everybody sees and everybody knows everybody sees? It strikes me that once the sharpest people in the room herd, the price stops carrying information, so herding ought to be self-undermining -- and I can't tell if the models capture that or assume it away. Third, does the Bubble Act fit the idea that public information can shatter a cascade, or am I just telling a tidy story afterwards?

And lastly: was Newton actually irrational? Given what he believed at the time, I mean, not by hindsight.
