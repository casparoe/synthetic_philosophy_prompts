I'm teaching a 75-minute session in a cross-listed ethics/econ seminar, 24 students, mostly third-years who've had one term of utilitarianism and zero measurement theory. I want an in-class game that makes them feel whats actually at issue in comparing one person's well-being to another's — not the cheap takeaway where they conclude "you can't compare people's happiness so summing it is meaningless, debate over," but the finer thing: how much comparability a given decision actually requires, and which decisions survive on much weaker assumptions than others.

Here's the skeleton I've got — tell me if it's rotten and fix it rather than just dressing it up. Everyone receives a secret conversion card turning tokens into their own private well-being points, and the cards really are different: diminishing returns for some, a flat rate for one, someone whose points come mostly from a good the group has no way of distributing. But the group never sees points. They only ever see self-reports on a shared 0–10 scale, and each player secretly anchors that scale differently — some use the whole range, one never reports outside 5 to 7, one is a consistent over-reporter, one reports in whole numbers only. Then they have to split a fixed pot and defend the split.

I want phase one to be a choice that only needs ordinal information (one allocation is better on every player's own reckoning, they just can't prove it to each other), phase two a choice that needs a genuine sum, and phase three to hand them the survey-research calibration trick where everyone rates the same written description of a stranger's life and you use those ratings to rescale the reports — and then let them discover for themselves that this repairs levels but not marginal rates.

Give me: the actual card contents and the vignette text, print-ready; a payoff scheme with no real money; roles and who runs what; a minute-by-minute timeline; a debrief that maps each phase onto the comparability assumption that phase quietly needed, and shows them which of their own arguments in the room would collapse if you weakened it; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm braced for the over-reporter getting accused of lying and it turning personal — how do I cut that off without killing the lesson); and a frank note on what the activity structurally cannot reproduce. I already suspect one thing: I'm the oracle holding the true points, and the real debate has nobody in that position, so the students get a fact about the world that no participant in the actual argument ever gets.

One constraint I feel strongly about: nobody should be cast as "the disabled one" or "the person with expensive tastes" as a costume. Build the asymmetries into card mechanics and reporting rules so no student's identity is the lesson and nobody has to perform being a problem case. And don't name the debate until the debrief — they should arrive at the distinction by tripping over it.
