**Lies that build the world they describe**

**1. The standard read.** Everyone who's read Lying in Politics comes away with the same two-part distinction. The old-fashioned political lie conceals or denies a fact that already exists; the modern one fabricates an image of something that doesn't exist yet — intentions, future policy, the shape of a war that hasn't been fought. The old lie is falsifiable against the record; the new one isn't, because the record hasn't been written. Then there's her point about the problem solvers: the systems analysts McNamara pulled into the Pentagon were unusually prone to self-deception because they worked in scenarios and cost-effectiveness metrics rather than in facts, and they deceived themselves well before they deceived anyone else. Her earlier piece Truth and Politics gives the supporting claim that factual truth is the most political kind of truth, and also the most fragile, because it's the one that says things have to be a certain way.

**2. The twist I want to defend.** I think the real difference between the two kinds of lie isn't falsifiability. It's which way the arrow points between map and territory. The traditional lie is a map that fails to match a territory that's already fixed; it stays false, and the damage is epistemic — people end up holding wrong beliefs. The modern lie is a map of a territory nobody has built yet, held by people who also hold the bulldozers. So the map can be made true after the fact: policy gets bent until it matches the projection, the press release gets backfilled, the lie converts itself into a fact by administrative action. Defactualization on this reading isn't only the erosion of a common fact-world. It's the substitution of a pre-scripted future for a future that could still have been begun anew. Which is why I think the deep harm in her account isn't epistemic at all — it's the loss of the capacity to start something that wasn't already in the plan.

**3. Why this fits the Papers.** It explains a puzzle. When the Papers came out in 1971 almost nothing in them was news: everyone already suspected the war was going worse than advertised and that decisions had preceded the consultations. On a purely epistemic story they shouldn't have landed hard, since they mostly confirmed beliefs people already held. On my story they landed exactly as hard as they seemed to, because what they exposed wasn't a set of false statements but a whole decision procedure running off an image — the same scenarios recycled, the same indicators optimized, the actual country treated as a residue to be explained away. The documents were a picture of a government with no remaining contact with the facts.

**4. The step I'm least sure about.** Extend it once more. A forecasting system that hands a government fluent, well-calibrated forward projections would be a defactualization machine in her sense even if every output were literally accurate. Accuracy about the model is not accuracy about the world, and the availability of the model substitutes for the friction of going to look. The problem solvers weren't wrong because their arithmetic was bad; they were wrong because the arithmetic was the only thing anyone consulted.
