I'm the plots coordinator for a small allotment society — about eighty plots, and a waiting list roughly as long again, so this stuff matters to people. Our constitution says a plot can be taken back and reallocated if the holder has let it become "significantly uncultivated". That's the whole of it. No definition anywhere, and its been that way since the 1970s.

The trouble is the cases don't arrive in neat packages. The one blowing up now: a member has had his plot for thirty-odd years, he's in his late seventies, and over about four seasons he's let more and more of it go — a strip of nettles along the fence one year, the bean frame collapsed the next, and now I'd say half the plot is bramble and couch grass and you can't see the paths. At no single point did anyone think "right, that's over the line", and if you'd asked the committee in any one of those years they'd have said he was fine. It's only looking back across the four that you can see something's changed. Meanwhile we reallocated another member's plot last year for what honestly looked like less — one bad season, mostly grass, no bramble at all.

The handbook also says each season's decisions should be consistent with previous seasons unless there's a good reason to depart from them. So I'm stuck: consistency pushes me to treat this case like last year's, but last year's feels wrong, and when I try to explain the difference I end up with something that sounds suspiciously like a rule I've invented on the spot.

There's a further wrinkle: the same plot looks completely different in March than it does in July, so anything like a photo assessment ends up penalising people for the season they happened to be inspected in.

Three things I'd like your help with, and please write in British English.

First, I have to draft guidance for next season and the committee is split between (a) putting a number in — say, reallocation once more than a third of the plot is weed or bramble — and (b) leaving it as a judgement call, on the grounds that any number is plucked out of the air and produces silly outcomes at the edges (a plot that's 34% weed but otherwise beautifully cropped, versus one that's 30% where the 30% happens to be the only bed anything grows in). Which should we do? Is there any genuine difference between the two options, or is putting a number in just the same difficulty dressed up as a solution?

Second, and this is the bit I keep going round in circles on. A retired member who used to be a magistrate has suggested we draw lots for the in-between cases. Her reasoning is roughly that where the rules don't settle whether someone has crossed the line, there's nothing there to get right or wrong, so it's fairer to randomise than to pretend one of us can see it. Another member says that's cowardly nonsense — either the plot is significantly uncultivated or it isn't, and theres definately an answer even in the awkward cases, we're just not in a position to work out what it is, and a lottery would be a way of ducking our job. I can feel the force of both and I can't tell whether they're actually disagreeing about anything. Does it change what we should do — including what I say to the man who's appealing, and whether we can honestly tell him we got his case right rather than just that we're standing by it?

Third: our appeals clause says a committee decision stands unless it's "clearly unreasonable", which I've noticed doesn't help me one bit, because now I have the same mess one level up and no idea whether the second level is any better off than the first.

No references or reading lists please — I'm not going to chase anything up and I'd rather you didn't guess. I just want the reasoning, worked through on our actual situation.
