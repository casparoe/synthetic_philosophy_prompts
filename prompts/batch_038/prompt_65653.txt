ok so I've been reading philosophy of perception on my own for a couple years now (no degree, just me and a library card and too many tabs) and I keep hitting the same wall: disjunctivists on one side, and on the other the people who think a hallucination and a matching genuine perception are the same kind of mental state underneath — call them the common-kind crowd, whether they say sense-data or representational content. Every summary I read just has each side repeating itself louder, and I'm pretty sure a chunk of it is the two camps using the same words for different things.

So: write the joint statement that a committed disjunctivist and a committed common-kind theorist could both actually sign after a real adversarial collaboration. Not a dialogue, not a verdict on who wins — the document itself, in a voice each of them could put their name to.

To stop them from drifting into generic slogans, anchor it on one stipulated case, and make both sides commit to predictions about it in advance:

A lab can produce a subject's hallucinations on demand. When she actually sees a yellow lemon on the table, and when the lab stimulates her visual cortex so that she seems to see a yellow lemon with nothing there, stipulate that the two episodes produce the same neural activity pattern, the same verbal report, the same confidence rating, and the same moment-to-moment dispositions to act and to reason from what she seems to see. Variant B: after a year of sessions, this same subject starts reliably flagging, before she reaches, which episodes were lab-made. She says she can tell; she can't say how, and there's no external cue she's using.

What I want in the document:

1. A numbered list of claims both sides genuinely accept — about the case, about what indistinguishability does and doesn't license, about what any theory of perception has to explain. If only one side would sign it, it doesn't belong here.

2. A disagreements table. Columns: the crux; the disagreement worded the way the disjunctivist would word her own view; the same disagreement worded the way the common-kind theorist would word his; the bit of the case (or some other argument or piece of evidence) both agree bears on it; and each side's prediction, recorded before the fact. Two of the rows have to be these: whether the disjunctivist owes a positive account of what the hallucination consists in, or whether saying it's a state introspectively indiscriminable from a real perception is already a complete answer; and whether, if the shared element explains the report and the behavior, the object-involving part is left doing any work at all. Also give Variant B its own row — I think that's where something has to give and I want to see which side it is.

3. A short section saying which parts of the whole fight were verbal, especially phenomenal character and same kind of state, and what each side should have been saying instead so the arguement was about something.

No bibliography, no names of books or articles — I couldn't check them anyway and I'd rather the words went into the argument. Around 1200 words max. And start with the document itself, no preface and no explaining what you're about to do.
