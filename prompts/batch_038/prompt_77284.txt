Been chewing on the debate proposal for a while — two superhuman agents argue in front of a human judge who is way too weak to evaluate the underlying question, and the bet is that pointing out a flaw is much easier than constructing one, so under optimal play the honest side wins. The cute illustration is the Go one: a novice can't tell you whether a given move is the best move, but if you let the engine play out a sequence of counters and counters-to-counters to the end of the game, the novice can just read off the score.

The case I actually care about is the obfuscated argument — a long chain where every individual step looks fine to the judge, each one small enough to check on its own, but the conclusion is false, and the judge only ever finds out because somebody isolated the bad step.

So: what epistemology does that design presuppose? It seems to take for granted that any truth a weak judge can be brought to accept is a truth that decomposes into locally checkable steps, and that checkability survives reassembly into a whole. Reconstruct that as an actual theory of justification rather than a vibe. Is it just verification-is-easier-than-generation, generalized from single claims up to entire arguments? Does it need the extra assumption that understanding is compositional — that if you can follow each inference you can follow the proof?

Then tell me which rival pictures of knowledge it quietly rules out. Im thinking of the view that some insights are irreducibly holistic and get destroyed when you parcel them into steps; the view that expertise is largely tacit, so a good reviewer recognizes a broken proof the way you recognize a face without being able to say how; and the flat complexity-theoretic possibility that for some true claims every supporting argument is way longer than some rival's refutation, so the asymmetry the whole scheme rests on just isnt there in general.

Also flag where the design is philosophically underdetermined — places where several incompatible theories of justification would license exactly the same protocol. My suspicion is that treating the judge as a reliable-but-uncomprehending instrument is doing sneaky work, but I cant articulate what.

And if you think there's an internal inconsistency — like, the judge has to be too weak to assess the claim yet strong enough to assess whether one debater's rebuttal of the other lands — say how much of the scheme survives it.

Finally, suppose we swapped in the opposite assumption: some truths are only reachable by a judge whose competence has actually been upgraded, not by better scrutiny of articulated steps. Concretely which rules of the protocol would you drop, what replaces them, and is the result still recognizably a debate?
