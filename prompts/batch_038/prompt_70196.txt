I write about science, medicine and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the past several months I have been reporting a piece on the boundary work that goes into calling something a disease: the arguments over grief that refuses to fade, over compulsive gaming, over whether ageing itself is a legitimate target for treatment, and over the various kinds of human difference that clinics are willing to correct for a fee. My editors keep asking, reasonably enough, whether every time I use that word I mean something medical or something moral, and I have found that I cannot answer them without knowing what I actually think. So I have been keeping a list of my own verdicts, case by case, and I should like to know whether they add up to a principle or only to a mood. Here they are. Type 1 diabetes: a disease, no argument. One copy of the sickle-cell gene in a man who has never had a symptom: not a disease — though I concede that the verdict seems to change if he lives where malaria is endemic, and I dislike the implication that a person's disease status alters when he gets off an aeroplane. Raised blood pressure in a country where most adults over fifty have it: still a disease, notwithstanding that it is statistically typical for his reference class. A woman carrying a rare variant that keeps her cholesterol extremely low, with no symptoms and better prospects than her peers: not a disease, though she is far outside the normal range and her internal machinery is plainly not doing what it was selected to do. Very short stature in an adult: not a disease, even though she is a statistical outlier and the world is built against her. A man whose sperm count is far below the population average and who wants children: a disease. A woman past the menopause with no ova remaining, who wants children, who is seen in the same clinic and suffers in the same way: not a disease. A person tested at thirty who carries a variant that makes Huntington's disease all but certain in middle age: not ill yet — and yet I want there to be something true of her now that amounts to more than a forecast. Ageing: a healthy ninety-two-year-old is not diseased, I think, even though everything that will kill her is already happening; but when a company sells an intervention against ageing I find myself willing to call the target a condition and the marketing honest. And the case I keep going back and forth on: a widow who, thirteen months after her husband's death, still cannot work and still cannot bring herself to enter his study. I would call that a disorder. The same presentation at eight months I would not — and I cannot identify anything that changed except the calendar, which is part of why it bothers me that the two official manuals set different minimum durations before grief qualifies. If I insist the calendar is irrelevant, then a person grieving at full intensity three weeks after a death is disordered too, which I refuse to accept.

For want of anything better I have been drafting a test of my own, which in my notebook I call the 'repair test': a bodily or mental condition is a disease just in case there is something that could be done to the person — as opposed to something that could be done to her surroundings, her employer or her culture — that would make her life go appreciably better without costing her anything she would miss. I know it is crude. It appears to make disease status depend on the current state of medical technology, and it makes an untreatable condition less of a disease than a treatable one, which strikes me as backwards; and I cannot see how it handles the infertility pair above, where the repair in question is available to one of the two and not to the other. What I should like from you is four things. First, run the repair test against the whole list and tell me which verdicts it reproduces, which it contradicts, and whether two or three added clauses could save it or whether I should throw it out. Second, state the account you would actually defend, in your own terms and clause by clause, saying for each clause which of my cases it exists to handle; engage seriously with the standard families — statistical normality relative to species-typical function, evolutionary dysfunction combined with a judgement of harm, health as the ability to reach one's vital goals, and the Rawlsian story that ties species-normal functioning to fair equality of opportunity — and with the objection that the supposedly value-neutral members of that list are value-laden after all, because the choice of reference class and the relativisation to environment are doing normative work; but do not catalogue them, tell me which you keep, which you discard and what you add, including whether your account needs disease, illness and sickness held apart, since I have been using the three interchangeably and suspect that this is a bad habit. Third, tell me which of my verdicts your account cannot accommodate and which single one I ought to give up, and then give me its verdicts on three cases of your own choosing that genuinely stress it — at least one involving a condition created almost entirely by social arrangement, and at least one involving an enhancement that has gone slightly wrong. Fourth, briefly, say what the account implies for the questions my piece turns on: whether a classification body is the right institution to settle them, and what a journalist ought to mean when she writes that word in a news story.

Please be committed rather than even-handed: I want one defended position with its reasons, and a clear statement of where you think I am wrong. If you believe the whole project of defining disease is confused, argue for that instead — but do not hedge across three views and leave me to choose. Write in American English; I am writing to you in British English, but the piece will run in New York and I expect to lift your sentences almost verbatim into a sidebar. End your reply with a single line beginning Verdict: followed by no more than twelve words naming the account you are defending.
