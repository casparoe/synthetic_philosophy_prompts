I convene our department's annual workshop on moral responsibility, and this year's closing speaker defends a particular account of what goes wrong with hypocritical blame. Her position, as I take it, is that hypocrisy leaves the truth of the blamer's judgement wholly intact — the hypocrite may be quite correct that her colleague's conduct was dishonest, and may know it on excellent evidence — but strips her of the standing to hold that conduct against him. Holding-against is on her telling a different thing from judging-blameworthy: it is the alteration of a relationship by the registration of a wrong within it, and it is this, and only this, that hypocrisy defeats. She is a formidable defender of the view, and her habitual manoeuvre when confronted with a case in which the hypocrite's blame looks plainly appropriate is to observe that we have run the two attitudes together, and that once they are separated the counterexample simply evaporates. I want questions that make that manoeuvre expensive rather than questions that merely invite her to repeat it.

I am chairing the closing session, which runs to some forty minutes before an audience composed largely of graduate students, several of whom are writing dissertations on moral address. Please draft eight to ten questions for me, arranged in three ascending rounds — the first establishing where the view actually bites, the second pressing its hardest internal problems, the third testing how far it generalises — and for each question supply a follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give. Across the set I want at least one question on each of the following, though you need not keep to my order. First: what it would look like from the outside for someone to have lost the standing to hold a wrong against another while retaining the standing to judge him blameworthy, and whether a distinction that cannot be witnessed is doing work or merely insulating the theory. Second: the self-blaming hypocrite, who seems both apt and indeed required to hold his own dishonesty against himself, and why this does not show that the deficit lies in address to another rather than in holding-against as such — with the follow-up to her expected reply that self-blame is not address at all. Third: whether the loss of standing is indexed to the type of wrong, and if so what happens at the near-miss, where the blamer has been dishonest in an adjacent but not identical register. Fourth: restoration, and specifically whether an apology restores standing or whether only a demonstrated reform of disposition does — and, if the latter, whether standing has collapsed into a measure of vice and is no longer an independent notion. Fifth: whether the person blamed can waive the deficit, since he may well say that he knows the blamer did the same and that she is nonetheless right. Sixth: the opacity problem, given that standing turns on facts about the blamer's own history that the addressee may never be in a position to know, and what becomes of the idea of moral address if its propriety is in principle hidden from the one addressed. Seventh: the case of purely private, unvoiced holding-against, which imposes no cost on anyone and so seems to leave nothing for the unfairness to consist in. Eighth: extension, to blame offered on a victim's behalf and to collective agents — the citizen who condemns her own government's dishonesty, the institution that reproaches another institution. Ninth: the neighbouring deficits of self-righteousness, meddlesomeness and coldness of heart, and whether her account has one phenomenon here or several that it is convenient to conflate. And finally a question about the practical upshot: if hypocritical blame can be true, well-evidenced and morally urgent, why should the person blamed care that the blamer lacks standing, and does the view not license wrongdoers in discounting accurate criticism?

Two further things. Mark clearly the single question you would hold back for the last five minutes and say in a sentence why it is best deployed late, when she has committed herself to her earlier answers. And please give me questions rather than scholarship: I know the shape of this literature well enough, and I do not want references, a survey, or any attempt to attribute positions to particular authors — I want the interrogative sequence itself, phrased as I might actually put it aloud from the chair.
