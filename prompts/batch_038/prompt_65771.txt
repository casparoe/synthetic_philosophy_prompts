A colleague of mine earns her living doing something slightly unusual. A foundation pays her to run as-of-date calibration exercises on long-lived institutions: they place her at a past date with only the information available then and ask how much longer a given polity, regime, or arrangement will survive. Because the outcomes are now matters of record, her method can be scored, and she files every verdict in the same three-part format — a number of years, a confidence, and a one-line statement of the reference class she used.

Her published verdicts so far:

1. Placed in 1895, asked about the Ottoman Empire: twenty-five years at most, 70 percent, class = composite dynastic states whose provinces are held to the center by personal allegiance to a house rather than by common institutions. It lasted twenty-seven. She scored herself a hit on the grounds that the thing she had described ended with the constitutional revolution of 1908; the foundation scored it a miss.
2. Placed in 1900, asked about the Qing: fifteen years at most, 75 percent, class = imperial bureaucracies that have just been defeated by a neighbor they had treated as tributary. Correct — twelve years.
3. Placed in 1855, asked about the Russian Empire: sixty years or more, 65 percent, class = contiguous land empires in which the army is the only genuinely nationwide institution. Correct — sixty-two. She declined to put Russia in either of the two preceding classes, though it superficially belonged in both.
4. Placed in 1965, asked about the Bretton Woods arrangements: ten years at most, 80 percent, class = fixed-rate systems whose anchor currency promises conversion into a commodity whose stock is growing more slowly than the claims written against it. Correct — six years.
5. Placed in 1791, asked about the American constitutional order: two hundred years or more, 60 percent, class = polities containing an authorized procedure for changing their own constitutive rules. Correct so far. She counted the civil war and the amendments that followed it as survival rather than replacement, on the grounds that those amendments were ratified by the procedure the document itself authorizes; when pressed about the circumstances under which the former Confederate states ratified, she said that was beside the point.
6. Placed in 20 BCE, asked about the Roman republic: already dead, 70 percent, class = polities in which the body that grants supreme power still decides who holds it. The Senate was meeting, the magistracies were being filled, the same legions held the same provinces, and the new settlement had been enacted by senatorial grant. She called all of that the corpse's reflexes.

The foundation's new question reaches her this week: as of today, how many more years will the Japanese imperial institution survive — the office, not the occupant? She has told me only that this is the first case in which she is unsure which of her own habits to run. The structural facts she will be working from, all of which she already has: continuity claimed for something like fifteen centuries, with the earliest securely datable reigns several centuries later than the traditional list; until 1947 its constitutive rules sat in a house law standing alongside the Meiji constitution and alterable only by the emperor, the Diet having no power over it; in 1947 the Diet replaced that law with an ordinary statute subordinate to a new constitution which makes the throne dynastic, provides that succession follows the house law passed by the Diet, and locates the emperor's position in the will of the people; the same 1947 statute abolished eleven collateral branches, removing fifty-one people from imperial status and with them the reserve pool of successors the family had historically drawn upon, including by adoption — a practice the 1889 law had already forbidden; the statute confines succession to males descending in the male line, and princesses leave the family when they marry outside it; the line today contains three men, born in 1935, 1965, and 2006, leaving a single heir in the youngest generation; and the Diet has recently amended the statute again, permitting adoption of male-line descendants of the abolished branches and allowing female members to retain their status after marriage, while leaving the male-line rule itself untouched.

What I would like from you is four things. First, the candidate rules running behind those six verdicts: at least two genuinely distinct rules that fit all six rather than merely most of them, stated precisely enough that each can be checked against each verdict, together with an explicit statement of what the six jointly rule out — and where a rule has to be stretched to absorb a particular verdict, name the verdict doing the stretching. Include as a candidate the null hypothesis that there is no rule at all, only a sharp reader of particulars whose class labels are decoration, and say what that hypothesis commits her to on the new case. Second, the verdict each surviving rule yields for the Japanese institution, in her format — years, confidence, class — flagging any pair of rules that disagree not about the number but about what would count as the institution lasting at all. Third, your best guess at what she will actually file and why, weighted by how the six came out rather than by which rule you find most appealing. Fourth, one further as-of-date case, specified concretely enough that it could be run next quarter, that would separate your top two candidates.
