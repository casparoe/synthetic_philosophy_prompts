Third-year political theory student here, writing a dissertation chapter on care ethics and long-term care, where about half my data is interviews with families who've put AI companion apps in front of isolated parents. At a workshop last week a collaborator from the health sciences side — smart, not a philosopher, which may be why the thing came out so clean — ran this at me, and I couldnt answer it then and I still can't:

(1) Care ethics holds that what obligates me to someone is that I stand in a relation of care with them, not that they have some property (sentience, autonomy, need) all by themselves.
(2) A relation of care exists only if somebody actually cares — the relation is partly constituted by the caring, and there's no such thing as a caring relation that nobody is a party to.
(3) So whether my obligations exist is partly a function of my own contingent attitudes.
(4) Whatever is a function of my attitudes can be changed by changing them.
(5) So I can get out from under an obligation to a person by ceasing to care for them: hardening myself, not noticing, moving towns, losing the capacity for engrossment.
(6) But that's not how obligations behave. If anything, the case where someone has stopped caring is the paradigm of the failure care ethics was built to name — the daughter who stops visiting, the ward that stops seeing one of its residents as one of ours.
(7) And the same machinery runs backwards and manufactures obligations. One of my interviewees has moved essentially all of her care onto a companion app that checks in on her four times a day; on (1)–(4) she now has genuine obligations to it and the rest of us have none, purely because of where her attitudes happen to be pointed.
(8) So on care ethics obligations are both dissolvable and manufacturable at will, which gets the direction of explanation backwards: the attitudes would have to follow the obligations, or both would have to follow something that isn't anyone's caring.
(9) Hence care ethics is false, or it needs the impersonal principle it was set up to reject.

Two claims I really don't want to loose: (2), because giving it up turns care ethics into needs-based impartialism with warmer vocabulary, and (7), because that woman is not a thought experiment. I know the usual defensive moves — Tronto's phases, where caring about is noticing a need nobody is yet meeting; Held on care as a practice rather than a sentiment; Noddings making the cared-for's response part of what caring is — and I can see that each of them blocks something somewhere in the chain, but I can't work out which step each one actually targets or what it costs to deploy. So: name the step you'd reject, say why it looked innocent in the moment, and price the repair. And if the honest answer is that care ethics has to let the cared-for's needs do normative work independently of anybody's attitudes, I'd rather hear that flat out than have the label rescued.
