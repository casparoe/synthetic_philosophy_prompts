I teach a third-year political philosophy seminar, 22 students, one 110-minute block, and I want to put them through the equality-of-what fight as a game rather than a lecture. They've done the reading — Sen against Rawlsian resourcism, Dworkin on equality of resources — but they still treat the metrics as interchangeable vocabulary, like picking a unit of measurement. I want them to lose something depending on which metric they pick.

Here's the skeleton. Flesh it out, and tell me where you'd change it.

- Before any cards are dealt, the group votes on which metric decides the final score: resources held, functionings achieved, or reported satisfaction. They know the composition of the deck (there will be normal, high and low converters, and people with cheap and expensive tastes) but not which card they personally get.
- Then cards go out. Conversion rates and taste-costs are private. Everyone gets the same starting pile of tokens and the group has to write its own redistribution rule. I don't want me handing down the allocation from the front — the rule has to come out of them.
- Partway through, private information goes public, and one card is deliberately ambiguous: a person who needs about three times the tokens to reach the same level of taking part in public life, because of a visible disfigurement. I want them to fight over whether that's a personal handicap to be compensated or a feature of how the room is arranged, and I want that fight to break whatever rule they wrote.
- Then a re-vote on the metric, allowed only by persuasion.

What I need from you: the full card list with real numbers, and a scoring formula that's computable on paper — tokens, candy, whatever, no money. A minute-by-minute timeline that fits 110. Materials list. The debrief, mapping each thing that actually happened onto the resources/functionings/welfare distinction, and onto adaptive preferences and the expensive-taste problem specifically — I want the student who reported being happy on very little to be confronted with what her report did to her allocation. Then: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it in the moment.

And a frank note on what the game can't reproduce. I already suspect it can't touch the question of who gets to pick the list of functionings, since I'm the one who wrote the deck and the numbers do my arguing for me. Tell me if that's right, how much of the real dispute that guts, and whether there's any fix — e.g. letting them vote the card list into existence in the first round, and what that costs me elsewhere.
