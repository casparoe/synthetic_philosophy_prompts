I am a junior at a public high school and I co-chair the allocation committee of our Student Senate. Every spring the Senate distributes a charity budget — this year roughly nine thousand dollars, raised through a combination of a dance, a restaurant percentage night, and a donation-matching drive — among about a dozen cause-oriented clubs. The process has been miserable for as long as anyone can remember. The animal welfare club, the climate club, the mental health awareness club, and the international aid club each deliver a speech arguing that their cause matters most, the vote then splits almost perfectly along club lines, and one or two groups walk away with a token amount and a grievance. I have been reading about moral trade and about satisficing on my own since last summer, mostly through essays a friend who graduated two years ago and now studies economics sent me, and I want to propose replacing the speech-and-vote ritual with an explicit trading mechanism. My proposal has three parts. First, thirty percent of the budget is set aside as a floor and divided by formula so that every recognized club receives a guaranteed minimum before any bargaining happens. Second, the remaining seventy percent is allocated by weighted vote, where each club's weight is proportional to its verified membership. Third, and this is the part I added myself, clubs may trade weight before the vote, but a trade counts as valid only if it is logged in writing, binding for the entire school year, conducted without any cash side payments, and — crucially — judged an improvement by each trading party according to that party's own ranking of the causes, rather than by any shared scale of value. My thesis is that with the floor plus that last condition, the familiar objections to moral trade lose their force: no club can be forced to abandon a core commitment, a large coalition cannot buy out a small cause, and because trades must improve things by each side's own lights they cannot generate the cycles and later regret that critics predict.

The room I will be presenting to is specific, and I think the specificity matters. There are twenty-two voting student senators, nearly all of whom are single-cause advocates and none of whom, so far as I know, has studied ethics or decision theory in any formal way; they are smart and they are invested, but they respond to concrete scenarios about their own club rather than to general principles. Two faculty advisors attend. One teaches AP Microeconomics, is enthusiastic about mechanism design, and will press me on incentives — what happens when clubs misreport their membership, whether the floor creates a holdout problem, whether anyone trades honestly in the last period. The other coaches our ethics bowl team, volunteers his time, and reasons in a broadly Kantian way; he is the one I am most worried about, because I suspect he thinks there is something categorically wrong about treating a moral claim as a transferable item at all, and I do not think my design answers him. The assistant principal also attends as a non-voting observer and cares almost exclusively about how the final allocation will read to donors and parents in the school newsletter, especially whether it will look as though the school ranked one form of suffering above another.

Please take the role of a professional philosopher who works on moral uncertainty, bargaining, and collective decision-making, and who has also spent time coaching secondary-school debaters, so that you are used to translating technical material for a young audience. Think carefully before you write, and begin with a short brainstorming paragraph — no more than two hundred words — in which you consider which of my three restrictions actually do philosophical work and which are cosmetic, so that the objections you generate target the proposal as I will really be defending it rather than a weaker version of it. Then give me a preparation sheet, not a defense of my view. I want the eight or so objections this particular audience will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the proposal if it landed rather than by how likely it is, and each phrased in the vocabulary the person raising it would genuinely use: the economics teacher's objection should not read like the Kantian's, and neither should read like a senator's. For each objection, give me a reply I could deliver in about ninety seconds while standing at a podium with no notes in front of me, and then a blunt sentence identifying where that reply is softest. Flag clearly which objections my floor-and-self-ranking design genuinely answers, which it merely softens, and which it does not touch at all. Tell me what I should concede in my opening two minutes so that I am not dragged into conceding it later under pressure, and tell me the one question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that, if it comes, I should have a holding answer for rather than a real one. Please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; I will add references myself later and I do not have access to a library database. Keep the language accessible to high school students, and if you need a technical term, define it in a single clause where it first appears.
