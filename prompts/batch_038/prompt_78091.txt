I run a reading group on epistemic game theory, and we have now spent two terms going in circles over the common prior assumption that sits inside Aumann's agreement theorem. I want a diagnosis of the shape of that argument, not another exposition of the theorem or a survey of the responses to it.

Here are the two camps as the people in the room actually argue.

The no-content camp says the assumption cannot fail, and so is not an assumption. Given any configuration of agents' beliefs you like, you can always enlarge the state space — add a type component encoding each agent's disposition to believe, in the Harsanyi manner — so that everyone's beliefs are conditional probabilities of one single prior over the bigger space. Difference of opinion that is not difference of information is always representable, once the space is rich enough, as difference of information about types. On this view the only real work in the agreement theorem is done by the common knowledge premise, and describing the prior as common either restricts nothing or is a restatement of the modeling framework.

The substantive camp says that move is a cheat. When you add the type coordinate you are no longer describing the same situation; the resulting measure is a bookkeeping device with no purchase on anything. Fix the state space exogenously as the worldly states the agents care about — the recession, the card, the outcome of the trial — and then requiring all agents' beliefs to be conditionals of one prior is a genuine constraint that some belief structures violate. In finite settings, they add, it has testable content: elicited posteriors together with elicited information partitions can be checked for consistency with a common prior, and certain configurations are ruled out. They also point to the stakes: if the assumption is empty, then the slogan that rational agents cannot knowingly disagree is a theorem about nothing, and the Bayesian program in game theory is left without a descriptive reading.

Concretely, our running example is two analysts who disagree about a firm's value while agreeing on every datum and every model. The substantive camp says that configuration cannot be described consistently at all, and that an analyst who reports it has misreported something. The no-content camp says you simply add a coordinate for analyst temperament, and the disagreement becomes an ordinary posterior difference.

Separately, one of us keeps raising the epsilon question: whether a small violation of the common prior assumption buys a small violation of agreement, or whether the theorem is discontinuous in the assumption, so that approximate common priors are compatible with near-total disagreement unless you also weaken common knowledge to high mutual belief. The room has not converged on whether this helps either camp.

My working hypothesis, offered as something to be attacked rather than assumed, is that there is no single disagreement here at all but three stacked ones, and that each camp's theorems are true of a different object than the one its rhetoric addresses. First, a question about when it is even meaningful to compare measures on two different measurable spaces, so that the enlargement argument and the fixed-space argument never contradict each other. Second, a question about which elicited quantities count as priors, which as posteriors, and which as descriptions of information — that is, about what would count as data — and this one looks genuinely formal and settleable. Third, a question about whether the common prior is a descriptive conjecture about real agents or a constitutive norm of rationality, and this one looks like a standoff no theorem can end. If that decomposition is right, the stalemate dissolves into one confusion, one open technical problem, and one irreducible normative disagreement.

Tell me whether that decomposition is correct, too tidy, or a way of dodging. Classify the disagreement on its own terms: verbal, substantive, a difference in standards of evidence, a difference about which cases count as data, two questions answered under one name, or an ill-posed question. Say what, if anything, would count as progress on each piece you identify — a representation result, a theorem about testability in finite spaces, a reformulation of the assumption, a change of question, an elicitation experiment — or argue that no progress should be expected and why. Engage with the epsilon issue explicitly, since it is where I suspect the disagreement is really live. Do not reprove the theorem and do not give me a bibliography; argue from the positions as I have stated them.

Please be explicit about how confident you are in your central claims, and single out the point in your diagnosis where you are least sure of yourself. Also flag any place where you suspect you are reasoning from my reconstruction of the two camps rather than from the arguments as their proponents would actually press them.
