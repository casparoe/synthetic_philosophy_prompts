'Weakness of will used to be a philosopher's puzzle. Socrates argued it was literally impossible: if you know the better course you cannot fail to take it, so what we call giving in must really be a miscalculation. The modern orthodoxy, following Donald Davidson, keeps the conclusion in a subtler form — if you eat the second slice of cake you cannot have judged, all things considered, that abstaining was better, because you cannot intentionally do what you judge worse. Then the economists arrived. It turns out people don't discount the future at a constant rate: the near future is discounted much more steeply than the far future. That has a consequence nobody in the philosophical tradition anticipated — our preferences are intransitive across time. In January I prefer being thin in June to eating cake now; in June I prefer the cake. Dynamic inconsistency, they call it, and it means the agent is really two agents, a far-sighted planner and a myopic doer. Only the planner's preferences are stable and considered, so they are the person's real preferences, and the doer's choices are less actions than failures of the brain's valuation machinery. Which dissolves the puzzle and, incidentally, the blame. Self-control turns out not to be a virtue but a commitment device: someone who leaves their card at home or pre-pays for the gym isn't weak, they're rational in the only way a creature with a hyperbolic discount function can be. Weakness of will is arithmetic, not character.'
