At the workshop I helped organize this spring, two of the invited speakers turned out to hold the same headline view — that dreaming involves no experience — for reasons that are not merely different but mutually hostile, and the two days were consumed by their objections to each other rather than by anyone's objections to the experientialists in the room. I have been asked to write up the joint statement they should have produced, and I would like it drafted properly.

The two parties, as they stated their own positions:

- **M (Malcolmian conceptualist).** Following Malcolm's 1959 monograph, M holds that the question whether dreamers really had the images and events while they slept is idle. To be asleep is to be unconscious, and an unconscious subject is not available to undergo anything; the truth of a report of the form *I dreamt that p* is constituted by the waking impression and the disposition to narrate, not by any earlier episode. M insists this is a point about the concept of dreaming, not a conjecture about the brain, and holds that no empirical result could establish that a sleeper was undergoing experience.
- **D (Dennettian revisionist).** D holds that the claim that dream content was experienced as it unfolded is an empirical hypothesis, and a poor one; the better hypothesis is that content is assembled and written into memory at or near waking, so that nothing was lived through. D agrees with M's conclusion but rejects M's grounds, and maintains that the hypothesis is falsifiable in principle.

Please draft the statement both could sign. I want:

1. A numbered section of claims both parties accept — about dreamless deep sleep, about what a dream report is and how it is produced, about the fallibility and suggestibility of recall, and about what makes a dream report true. Each claim must be one that neither would want to qualify into emptiness.
2. A disagreements table with columns: the disagreement; M's formulation, worded so M would accept it; D's formulation, worded so D would accept it; whether it is a crux; the test or case both accept as bearing on it; M's prediction; D's prediction.
3. An explicit ruling on how much of the dispute is verbal. I suspect the disagreement about whether the question is conceptual or empirical is doing most of the work, and that a further chunk concerns what *experience* is being required to amount to. Say which parts are genuinely substantive and which are disputes about words, and why.

Three case materials should be worked through under (2), since the two of them predict differently about each:

- **Decoding.** Intra-sleep imaging activity predicts the content category of the report given immediately upon awakening at above-chance rates; matched neural data from periods with no subsequent report serve as controls.
- **Dilation.** A subject awakened forty-five seconds into a REM period reports a dream whose narrated events would require at least ten minutes, with the report stable across repeated awakenings; set against the older finding that reported dream length tracks the duration of bursts of REM eye movements.
- **Signalling.** Lucid dreamers execute pre-arranged eye movements, time-locked to a designated event in the dream and verified by an outside observer.

Finally, the statement should answer one objection that both parties regard as the real threat: that the malleability of dream recall under leading questions shows nothing, because memory for genuinely experienced waking events is malleable too. Decide which of M and D has the resources to answer it, whether the joint statement should concede it in part, and what that concession costs each of them.

End with a single line of the form `Verdict: CRUX=<short phrase>; STATUS=<conceptual|empirical|mixed>`, where the phrase names the one sub-question whose settlement would most move these two parties toward or away from each other, and the status classifies the residual dispute that survives your analysis.
