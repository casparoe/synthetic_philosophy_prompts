I have just begun a secondment on the technology review panel at our regional transit authority — the standing body that signs off on our automated fare collection rollout, which replaces cash fares and paper transfers with stored-value cards and open-payment gates — and my first deliverable is a written statement of the assumptions I bring to the job. I would like them audited before I circulate them.

1. I accept Winner's thesis that artifacts have politics: the technical design of a system settles in advance who can use it and on what terms, and that settling is not merely a downstream by-product of how people happen to use it.
2. I am not a determinist. Designs stay open to reinterpretation and repurposing by the groups around them, long after installation.
3. Lock-in is real. Once hardware is fixed and contracts are signed, the practical option set contracts sharply and rarely reopens.
4. Our mandate is ex ante, and that is what makes it worth having: the panel reviews designs before procurement, and that is where the leverage lies.
5. The political character of a system is usually legible only after years of wide use, and the features that end up mattering are rarely the ones anyone debated at design review.
6. Responsibility for those features lies with human agents — designers, procurement officers, elected officials — never with artifacts, which are not the kind of thing that can be answerable for anything.
7. Most politically consequential features of a system of this kind are chosen by no one: they arrive through vendor templates, standards bodies, accessibility codes, and budget lines.

Tell me which of these genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to, rank the real conflicts by severity, and for each say what would have to go and what I would lose by giving it up. I have no favorite among them and would prefer not to discover that I do. Close with the single revision that would leave the set most defensible.
