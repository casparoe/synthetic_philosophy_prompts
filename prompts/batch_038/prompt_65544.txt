I sit on a small working group drafting a returns and restitution policy for a mid-sized English museum — university-affiliated, some forty thousand objects, a few hundred sets of human remains, and a handful of contested West African and Pacific material acquired in the late nineteenth century. Our instruction is to align ourselves with what the national institutions do, and one of the trustees has asked me to write a short statement of the principles behind the policy, on the grounds that a document which merely lists procedures will not survive its first legal challenge. Before I attempt that, I want to understand what the existing framework actually commits us to, because the more closely I look at it, the less it resembles a single set of principles.

As far as I can reconstruct current British practice, it runs in tiers. Human remains are the easiest case: the named national museums hold a statutory power to transfer remains out of their collections, but that power is confined to the remains of people who died within roughly the last thousand years; older material stays and is studied. Where remains are recent enough and a community can be identified, the strong presumption is return, usually with reburial, and there is a growing expectation that research on and display of such material requires the consent of descendant communities. Ritual and ceremonial objects connected to communities that demonstrably still practise something like the original use are returnable too, but the claimant is expected to show continuity — an unbroken line of affiliation, or a surviving rite the object belongs to. Objects for which no such community exists, or where affiliation cannot be established — Cycladic figurines, most Egyptian funerary material, pre-Columbian pieces, Palaeolithic material — are treated as permanently retained, and what is offered instead is loans, casts, digital surrogates, shared stewardship, co-curation. Alongside this, the largest museums are barred by their own governing statutes from disposing of all but a narrow class of objects, and their habitual answer to a claimant state is a long-term loan, which the claimant then refuses on the grounds that accepting it would concede the lender's ownership; yet the same lender will send one of the very sculptures in dispute to another national museum abroad for an anniversary exhibition, and nobody suggests that this compromises anything. Meanwhile the conservation department two floors down works to a philosophy of minimal intervention, reversibility and stabilisation rather than restoration, and has no objection whatever to displaying a good cast beside a fragmentary original — which appears to assume that what an object means is separable from the particular matter it happens to be made of.

So: what theory of meaning, value and identity does each of these tiers presuppose, and do they belong to one theory? I want to know where each rule locates the significance of a thing — in the material substrate, in the body it came from, in the practice it is caught up in, in relations between living people, in the conditions under which it is seen — and which rival accounts the tiers thereby rule out. The thousand-year limit especially puzzles me: what view of kinship, or of moral standing across time, makes it reasonable for the claims of the dead to expire on a schedule, and why that schedule rather than some other? There is also a structural oddity I cannot resolve. If significance resides in continuing relations and practices, as the treatment of remains and of ritual objects implies, then objects whose practices were destroyed — often by the same episode of violence that carried the objects away — ought to present the strongest case for return; instead the framework gives them the weakest. The tiering seems to invert its own rationale. Can any single account license the whole structure, or does the practice require incompatible commitments at different points? Please also separate what the framework strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with: a colleague of mine insists that all of it is just claimant identifiability, cataloguing convention and political cost, with no philosophy in it at all, and I should like to know whether he is right about some tiers and wrong about others. Then tell me what the loan deadlock shows — what conception of ownership makes title worth fighting over when possession, display conditions and conservation standards could be made identical, and whether title is doing recognitive rather than instrumental work in that exchange. Finally, mark where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, with several theories licensing the same rule, and set out what would have to change institutionally if we replaced the implicit theory with one on which objects mean nothing apart from the relations and practices they belong to, and separately with one on which the material substrate is the only locus of value. Which tiers would survive each replacement, and which would collapse? I am not after a survey of the controversy or a list of sources, only the reconstruction and the argument for it.
