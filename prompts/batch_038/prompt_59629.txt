Working on an essay about the treatment/enhancement line and I've decided Daniel Keyes's Flowers for Algernon is my main literary exhibit, which I know sounds like a strange choice, since basically nobody reads it as a text about enhancement ethics — they read it as a sad story about a mouse. Here's my actual claim; tell me whether the book supports it or whether I'm forcing it.

The standard way to draw the line (Daniels and the whole normal-species-functioning crowd) is baseline-relative: an intervention is therapy if it brings you back to normal functioning, enhancement if it carries you past it. My claim is that the book quietly wrecks this, because wellbeing turns out to be history-dependent in a way the baseline view is structurally blind to. Post-regression Charlie is, on any functional measure you like, right back where pre-op Charlie was — same IQ band, same prospects, Alice Kinnian still spelling things out for him — but he is much worse off, because he now knows what the guys at work were actually doing when they laughed, knows that Algernon beat him and then didn't, and has to feel himself going in real time and write it down as it happens. Two people can have identical functional profiles and very different welfare, because one of them was enhanced and came back down. So enhancement is not reversible in the welfare sense even when it's reversible in the functional sense, and a distinction indexed only to where you stand relative to a reference class can't see that at all.

Read the book through that lens — short story or novel, whichever you know better, but say which one you're using, since the details differ (I think he's a janitor at a box factory in one and works at a bakery in the other). What I want out of you:

- where the history-dependence actually shows up in the text — I'm thinking the late progress reports, and how he handles Alice and Nemur and Strauss after he understands the Algernon-Gordon effect — versus where the book just looks like a routine hubris story that happens to have this shape;
- whether Keyes is doing any of this on purpose, or whether I'm reading a structure into it that the book doesn't own. Be blunt about this. I'd rather hear it from you then from a reviewer.
- how the disability-studies reading cuts against me. Plenty of people take the book's moral to be that Charlie's worth was never a function of his IQ and that the real monsters are the scientists and the coworkers, not his brain — and if that's the moral, maybe the conclusion is that the operation should never have been offered, not that the baseline view is inadequate. Does that reading eat mine, or can they coexist?
- and what the book gives the enhancement debate that a journal-article version of my argument can't say. The progress-report form seems to matter: the reader gets the regression inside Charlie's own collapsing prose, which is a device an argument just doesn't have. Does the form do philosophical work here, or is it only pathos?

Don't quote the book at me, I don't need passages, I need the reading. And please don't hedge with a paragraph about how the therapy/enhancement distinction is contested and maybe not worth keeping — I know, that's sort of the essay.
