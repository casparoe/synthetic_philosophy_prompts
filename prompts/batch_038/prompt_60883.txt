**The Seam: Why America's History Wars Are Not Japan's and Korea's**

When American commentators describe the fight over how slavery and the founding are taught in schools, they often reach for East Asia as the cautionary comparison. Japan and South Korea, we are told, show what happens when a state lets national pride set the terms of the past. The comparison is not false, but I think it obscures a structural difference that changes what the fight is actually about, and therefore changes what could possibly end it.

The difference concerns the object of the dispute. Japan authorizes privately written textbooks through a ministry review process and then leaves the choice among authorized titles to local boards of education. South Korea went further in the other direction: in 2015 its government announced it would replace the certified private textbooks with a single state-issued history text, and after a year of protest and a change of administration it abandoned the plan in 2017. In systems like these, the unit of dispute is a sentence, or even a word. Controversies turn on whether a passage describes coercion, on whether a massacre is named, on whether a colonial institution appears at all. Japan's authorization criteria acquired a provision in 1982, after protests from China and South Korea, directing reviewers to attend to international understanding and the sensibilities of neighboring countries — a rare case in which the intended audience of a national canon is formally acknowledged to be foreign.

The United States has no national textbook and no national authorization process. What it has instead is a portfolio: state adoption lists, district purchasing decisions, supplemental packets, and, increasingly, statutes defining what a teacher may say. I want to call these two arrangements single-canon systems and portfolio systems, and to argue that the distinction matters more than the usual distinction between open and closed societies.

In a single-canon system, distortion is auditable. There is a text, it is revised on a schedule, and editions can be compared against one another. The long-running lawsuits brought in Japan by the historian Ienaga Saburō against the authorization system worked precisely because there was a decision to point at. The 2001 textbook produced by the Japanese Society for History Textbook Reform was a public object that schools overwhelmingly declined to adopt, and its adoption rate was itself a fact about Japanese civil society.

In a portfolio system, distortion migrates from the sentence to the frame. It shows up in what is treated as a live question rather than in how any question is answered. Consider the two texts Americans argue about most. The 1619 Project, which appeared in 2019, was journalism, not a curriculum, though it entered classrooms through district partnerships and teacher-made packets; a group of prominent historians of early America wrote to its editors asking for corrections, and the sharpest dispute concerned a claim about the motives of the colonists who declared independence. The 1776 Commission's report was issued in January 2021 and its commission was terminated within days by the incoming administration. Neither document was a textbook. Neither had to survive an adoption process. The war was fought anyway, and it was fought over the permissible range of things a teacher may say out loud — which is what the various state statutes of 2021 onward, restricting instruction about certain concepts while in at least one case also prescribing a list of founding documents that must be covered, actually regulate.

This is why I think the comparison to East Asia is used too casually. Portfolio systems do not produce euphemism. They produce parallel canons that never touch. A student in one district and a student ten miles away are not reading different versions of the same sentence; they are reading different questions, and there is no shared text against which to notice the difference.

Yet the two systems are convertible, and the conversion can happen locally. When California's history-social science framework was adopted in 2016, it included guidance for teaching about the women coerced into Japanese military brothels, and a Japanese diplomatic post in San Francisco objected. When a memorial to those women was installed in a San Francisco square in 2017, the city of Osaka ended its sister-city relationship with San Francisco the following year. A portfolio system, in one jurisdiction, produced a single mandated gloss on a contested event and immediate foreign-policy consequences. Institutional form, not national culture, does the work.

My proposal is deliberately modest and deliberately procedural. Rather than asking a school board to settle what the past means, a district could adopt a seam-exposure requirement: every textbook unit must include at least one document that pulls against the unit's own summary sentences, and the assessment must reward a student for identifying the seam instead of reproducing the summary. The reform is robust to whoever holds the board majority, because it does not require agreeing on a verdict. It moves the contested object from what happened to what the summary sentence is doing.

I can already hear the objection that this is a dodge — that a confident reader of euphemism can still be a nationalist, and that seam-exposure can be gamed by pairing a text with a fringe source and instructing students to dismiss it. I think that objection is partly right, and I want the essay to concede it without surrendering the proposal.
