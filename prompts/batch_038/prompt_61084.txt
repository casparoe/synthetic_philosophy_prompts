I sit in on a Saturday-morning history group at our town library — mostly retirees, no academics except an emeritus geologist who keeps the minutes. We'd spent six weeks on July 1914, and last Saturday two of the regulars, Walt (ex-army logistics, 79) and Ed (retired insurance claims adjuster, 82), got into it over who's to blame for the First World War and it ran long past coffee. I drove Ed home afterwards and he was still going. Neither of them will let me off without a verdict and I honestly don't have one, so I'm hoping you can referee. Here's the exchange as best I remember it, roughly in order:

1. Walt: Fischer basically settled this in the sixties. On July 5–6 Germany told Vienna it would back an attack on Serbia come what may, then spent the next three weeks pushing Austria to actually declare, so the "everyone was sleepwalking, everyone shares the guilt" line isn't a finding, it's a defense — one that started with the German White Book of 1914, which, as Kautsky's complete edition after the war showed, had documents quietly left out of it.
2. Ed: Fischer's premeditation case rests on two documents and neither will bear the weight. The December 1912 meeting the Kaiser called — the one everyone calls the war council — the naval cabinet chief wrote in his diary afterwards that the result amounted to almost nothing, the chancellor wasn't even in the room, and no decision came out of it. And the September Program of war aims is dated September 9, 1914, drafted by Bethmann's secreatary once the war was already on and every power was improvising annexations. You can't convict a July decision with a September document.
3. Walt: I don't need premeditation. Responsibility isn't having a plan, it's being able to stop and not stopping. Germany had two clean chances: July 5–6, and again at the very end of July, when Bethmann pressed Vienna to accept the halt-in-Belgrade formula after Germany had spent three weeks urging Vienna not to accept anything. Yes, Russia mobilized first, but that mobilization was aimed at Austria, and Russia only moved because Germany had told Vienna to go ahead — so the chain still runs back to Berlin.
4. Ed: Two problems. Russian general mobilization on July 30 covered Germany as well, not just Austria, and Sazonov knew exactly what it would set off. And on your own standard the chances to stop weren't Germany's alone: Poincaré and Viviani handed Russia their own assurance in St Petersburg on July 20–23 and were then at sea for most of the crisis; Austria wrote an ultimatum meant to be refused; Britain wouldn't say plainly that it would fight until the last days of July. If everybody had a chance to stop and nobody took it, "the one who didn't take it" doesn't pick anybody out.
5. Walt: The chances weren't equivalent, and that's what the shared-guilt story hides. Germany's deployment plan meant that answering Russian mobilization automatically scheduled France and Belgium first, so Germany's decision is the one that turned a Balkan crisis into a general European war in a way no one else's decision could — and Germany was the only power that marched into two neutral countries. Britain came in over Belgium, not over Serbia.
6. Ed: Which is exactly why I think the concept is broken. If what singles Germany out is the rigidity of a deployment timetable, then the thing you're blaming is a railway schedule, not a nation. And "Germany is responsible" attributes guilt to a collective almost none of whom had any say: the Reichstag was in recess all through July and only met on August 4, after everything had been arranged and packaged in a White Book. I'll happily blame the dozen men around the Kaiser. What I object to is the treaty clause that slides from that to a whole people paying for decades, and to historians who go on treating war guilt as though it were an empirical question about a country.
7. Walt: You're splitting one thing and then congratulating yourself for having found two. A state acts through its organs, and what its organs commit stays with the state rather than with the men who signed — otherwise no state could ever make a treaty, inherit an obligation, or owe anybody anything. Blaming the Kaiserreich's government just is blaming Germany.

Could you go through it move by move instead of just telling me who won? Rate each of the seven turns from clearly wrong to clearly right with a line or two on why, and where one of them has got a date, a document, or a sequence wrong, say so plainly rather than being polite about it. Then do the thing I couldn't: tell me which of the seven turns are really disagreements about the historical record of July 1914 and which are disagreements about what holding a nation responsible even means, because my strong suspicion is that they kept answering each other's historical point with a conceptual one and vice versa, and neither of them noticed. Finish with whatever both of them are missing. Lead with your bottom line — who is more right overall, and on what — in the very first sentence, then go straight into the move-by-move; no preamble, don't restate the argument back at me, and no reading list or citations, I'll track down the books myself.
