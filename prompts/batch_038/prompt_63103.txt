I'm a junior in my school's economics society. Two of our seniors were workshopping a presentation on Sen's result that individual rights and the Pareto principle cannot both be satisfied, and their rehearsal turned into an argument that neither could settle. Our advisor told me to write it up and get an outside opinion, so I'd like an adjudication before they present it.

First, the example they had on the whiteboard, which I believe they stated correctly. Two men and one copy of a scandalous novel; three outcomes: no one reads it, the prudish man reads it, or the lewd man reads it. The prudish man ranks them: no one reads, then he reads, then the other reads. The lewd man ranks them: the prudish man reads, then he himself reads, then no one reads. Each is given a protected pair concerning whether he himself reads. The prudish man is decisive over the pair (he reads / no one reads) and prefers that no one reads; the lewd man is decisive over the pair (he reads / no one reads) and prefers that he reads. So the social ranking has no one reads above prude reads, lewd reads above no one reads, and Pareto has prude reads above lewd reads, since both men prefer it. Cycle.

Their exchange went as follows.

1. Nadia: this is a real impossibility theorem, not rhetoric. It needs only transitivity, unanimity, and the two decisive pairs, so no appeal to moral intuition can rebut it — its force is precisely that it is arithmetic.

2. Owen: the impossibility is manufactured by how Sen writes a right down. He makes a right a constraint on the social ordering: if I prefer x to y inside my protected pair, society must rank x above y. But a right is a power to choose — a rule about who decides — not a requirement that some social ranking mirror my preference. Reformulate liberalism as a choice procedure and no cycle arises, so the theorem is about Sen's formalism rather than about liberty.

3. Nadia: that relocation isn't free. The two protected pairs share the state in which no one reads, so a procedure still has to say what happens when both men are simultaneously choosing over pairs containing that state, and any answer either reproduces the contradiction or lets one man settle whether the other reads. (She added that she wasn't sure the contradiction survives at all if the two protected pairs have no state in common; Owen said that would be a different theorem from the one on the board.) And even granting his reformulation, the lesson stands: ranking social states only by preferences over them is too thin an informational basis to represent rights, because a right is not a preference. The paradox is the symptom of that.

4. Owen: then the title misleads. If the result is about the poverty of preference-based evaluation, presenting it as proof that you cannot have both rights and Pareto is wrong. He then offered what he called the boring resolution: restrict the domain by excluding preferences about another person's private choices. The Pareto step that closes the cycle compares prude reads with lewd reads, and each man's ranking of that pair is exactly a ranking of the other's reading. Bar intrusive preferences, the unanimity disappears, and both conditions can be met together.

5. Nadia: that restriction is question-begging and self-defeating. Question-begging, because a private choice just is one over which the individual is sovereign, so the restriction assumes liberalism in order to rescue it. Self-defeating, because if the Pareto step no longer applies you have not preserved unanimity, you have discarded it — and unanimity is about the least controversial condition anyone could impose. It is also ad hoc: no independent reason is given for barring a preference over another's reading while permitting, say, a preference over another's noise.

6. Owen: there is an independent reason — a preference is inadmissible when satisfying it requires overriding something the other person is entitled to settle for himself. And unrestricted domain is a modeling convenience regardless; Arrow needs it too, and every actual mechanism handles only some profiles. If Sen's contradiction depends on admitting profiles containing combinations of preferences that cannot all be satisfied, it is a theorem about an idealization. He added that Sen's framework leaves no room for waiving or trading rights, which is a further sign of crudeness.

7. Nadia: waiver cuts against him. If the prudish man can sell his right, the outcome is produced by transfer rather than by protected choice, and calling that arrangement liberal stretches the word past recognition. And invoking Arrow is double-edged: if unrestricted domain is an idealization that reduces a theorem to a hypothetical, then Arrow's theorem is a hypothetical too, which Owen surely will not say.

Please go through moves 1 to 7 one at a time, rating each as clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification per rating. Then say who is better right overall about the status of Sen's result, and what each of them is failing to see that the other also missed. Finally, please flag where your own adjudication is most likely to break down: which of your ratings you would withdraw if you were shown a particular objection, and what that objection would be.
