TA'ing a seminar on the quest for the historical Jesus, second half is a take-home on method, and I've been reading these for six hours straight which is not a state I trust myself to grade from. I want an outside check on one of them.

The question I set (the criterion in it is my own invention, not any real scholar's — I told them that explicitly so nobody would go hunting for a source):

> Suppose a scholar proposes to discard the criterion of dissimilarity altogether and replace it with a two-clause test. A tradition is to be accepted as going back to Jesus only if (i) it is attested in at least two sources that can be shown to be independent of one another, and (ii) it can be shown to have served no discernible purpose in the communities that preserved and transmitted it. Explain what this proposal gains and loses relative to dissimilarity, and say whether you think either approach can settle what Jesus actually said and did.

Here's the answer, more or less verbatim (I fixed the spelling of one name and nothing else):

> The criterion of dissimilarity says we should accept a tradition only where it can be explained neither as a natural development inside first-century Judaism nor as something the early church would have had reason to invent. Perrin treated it as the fundamental criterion, the one every reconstruction has to be built on, and its virtue is supposed to be that it isn't circular. The criterion of coherence only counts as evidence once you already have a picture of Jesus for the new material to cohere with, so it can't get the reconstruction started. Dissimilarity doesn't work that way: it asks a question about the world outside our reconstruction rather than a question about the reconstruction itself.
>
> Its vice is that whatever it selects is guaranteed to be uncharacteristic. If we keep only what neither Jesus's Jewish contemporaries nor his followers would have come up with, we end up with a heap of sayings that fit nowhere, and a heap of anomalies is not a person. I think that objection is basically right, at least as an objection to building a portrait out of dissimilarity alone.
>
> The proposed replacement's two clauses fail in different ways. Clause (i) relocates the problem of multiple attestation rather than solving it, because independence is exactly what's in dispute: whether Thomas knew the Synoptics, whether John knew Mark, whether Q is one document or several are all live questions, and (i) can only be applied by somebody who has already settled them in some particular way. Clause (ii) looks more interesting but I think it's just the criterion of embarrassment generalized — instead of asking whether a tradition was awkward for the community that kept it, it asks whether the tradition was merely useless to them, and embarrassment is the limiting case where usefulness goes negative. Since embarrassment is itself a special case of dissimilarity, the proposal doesn't replace the criterion it was meant to replace, it collapses back into it.
>
> On the question the examiner actually poses, I'd say a modest yes. The two clauses are independent of each other, so a tradition that clears both has been confirmed twice over, and the same logic lets us rank traditions by how many tests they clear: dissimilarity, embarrassment, multiple attestation, the Aramaic traces and rhythmic patterns Jeremias tried to recover. Something at the top of that ranking is about as secure as any claim about the ancient world gets. So the criteria can't give us a biography, but they can give us a small, high-confidence core, and a scholar who says they can't even do that is holding Jesus to a standard nobody would dream of applying to any other figure from antiquity.
