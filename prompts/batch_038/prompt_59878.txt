I serve as the volunteer coordinator for a mutual-aid fund attached to a regional nurses' union: roughly 310 contributing households, an annual budget of about $40,000, and a standing allocation rule that I drafted myself nine years ago and that says, in so many words, that the fund should aim at the greatest total benefit to the membership. This year that rule has produced an argument I cannot resolve, and I have to write the board's recommendation and break a tie.

The case is this. One member needs about $36,000 for a surgery her insurer has denied; without it she will very likely lose the use of her right hand and, with it, her job. The alternative use of the same money is a $120 utility-bill grant to each of the 310 households, which is broadly what our rule has produced in most past years. Two board members, Owen and Marta, argued about it for the better part of an hour. Here is the exchange as nearly as I can reconstruct it.

1. Owen: Apply the rule we all signed up for. Three hundred and ten households spared a bad winter month beats one member's hand, and if you fund the surgery you are taking $120 out of each of 310 pockets and putting $36,000 into one. Don't tell me that's heartless — notice where your objection gets its force, Marta. It comes from a single life. Inside one life we all accept precisely this trade: I give something up now so that I have something later, and we call that prudence rather than exploitation. A fund like ours is the same thing writ larger. If the trade is permissible across the parts of one life, the burden is on you to say why it turns impermissible when the parts are households.

2. Marta: Because inside one life the person who pays is the person who gets paid. Skip the vacation and it is you who ends up with the money. Here 310 people pay and one person gains, and not one of the 310 receives anything in return. There is no such thing as the fund that has been made better off. You say the total went up. Whose total? A total belongs to nobody. What actually exists after your decision is 310 people each slightly worse off and one person much better off, and that is the whole of the fact.

3. Owen: If there's no subject of the total, then there's no subject of the individual either — not in the sense your objection needs one. The member with the hand is a stream of experiences and dispositions, not a substance, so all you've done is prefer a narrower bundle to a wider one. That's the reductionist line, and Parfit, as I read him, is the aggregator's best friend here: once identity is a matter of degree, the deep separateness you're appealing to goes with it. You can't use the dissolving of the self to block summing. The dissolving is what licenses summing.

4. Marta: Degrees don't help you. Whatever makes it the case that I am connected to myself in ten years — memory, character, the fact that I anticipate and dread — is present at close to full strength within a life and at close to zero between me and a stranger I will never meet. I don't need an immortal soul to say that compensation is real where the connection is real and empty where it isn't. Reductionism hands me a graded notion of connection, and grading is exactly what my objection requires.

5. Owen: Then you've conceded the shape of the thing. If connection comes in degrees, it isn't zero among us either: these are neighbours, they staff the same units, they signed up for mutual aid, they'd recognise one another in a grocery store. So your principle cannot say "never weigh the many against the few." It can only say "weigh them at a discount," and now you owe me a discount rate. I at least have a procedure that terminates.

6. Marta: Determinacy isn't truth, and there's a difference between asking whether a trade is permitted and asking how much it costs. Also, you keep describing the $120 as a benefit and it isn't one. Not one household's month is meaningfully different for $120. You are not distributing 310 small goods; you are distributing 310 gestures and calling the arithmetic a good. Give me 310 households facing eviction and one member facing a nicer kitchen and I'll take the 310 — but notice that in that case I am not responding to a sum. I am responding to the fact that there are 310 people each of whom is separately saved.

7. Owen: Which is the concession I wanted. "Each separately saved," plus a preference for the many over the few whenever the stakes per person are equal — that is just aggregation with a threshold on how large each benefit has to be. Operationally you and I will vote the same way in most cases that actually come before this board. You haven't defeated my rule. You've demanded better units of measurement.

8. Marta: I've demanded a subject. If your rule can only be stated over totals that are no one's, then it is a rule about bookkeeping, and bookkeeping is not why we have a fund.

Your task: assume the role of a moral philosopher who has worked seriously on aggregation, on the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism, and on the analogy between intrapersonal and interpersonal trade-offs. Think carefully rather than reaching for the standard summary of the debate, and please do all of the following.

(a) Before rating anything, spend a short paragraph on what Owen and Marta actually agree about, and state what you take the real crux to be — the one question on which the rest of the argument turns. If you think my reconstruction makes either side sound better or worse than it deserves, say so and repair it.

(b) Rate each of the eight moves on a scale running from −2 (clearly mistaken, and the mistake can be identified) through 0 (a wash: as much force against it as for it) to +2 (clearly right). Justify each rating in two to four sentences, and where a move partly succeeds, say which part succeeds and which part fails.

(c) Say who is more right overall about the decision in front of us, and separately who is more right about the general principle. These may come apart. If they do, tell me which of the two should govern the recommendation I have to write, and why.

(d) Identify what both of them are missing: an argument, a distinction, or some feature of this particular fund that neither raises and that would change the shape of the dispute rather than the score.

(e) Flag any place where either of them has garbled a well-known position, including — if in your judgment this is so — Owen's use of Parfit in move 3.

Two constraints on how you answer. I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or a literature review, so argue from the reasoning itself and refer to views by description wherever that is clearer than by name; do not invent sources. And do not split the difference for the sake of civility: if one of them has made a straightforward error, say so plainly.

Finally, and this matters to me more than the verdict: tell me what would change your mind. For your overall judgment in (c), name the argument or consideration that would move you to the other side, and identify the step in your own reasoning that you think is most likely to be wrong. If you believe the disagreement is one that competent people have simply failed to settle, say that too, and say what a board like mine should do in the meantime rather than manufacturing a winner for my benefit.
