Can you write me a short story for me, somewhere around 1,300–1,600 words? Small English market town, 1790s. Here's the setup.

A young clerk to the deputy magistrate has become a convert to the new arithmetic of happiness. He's been corresponding with a philosopher in London — keep that man completely offstage, he exists only as letters and loose manuscript pages, and never name him — who has sent him the argument that punishment is only ever justified by the mischief it prevents; that locking a man up because his neighbours find him revolting isn't a reason at all, just a prejudice wearing a wig; and that there are four situations where punishment is simply misapplied — where there's no mischief to prevent, where the penalty couldn't have changed anybody's mind anyway, where the penalty costs more misery than the offence did, and where the same end could be got some cheaper way.

The case in front of him: two men from the parish, a wool-comber and the assistant schoolmaster, brought up under an old statute that still carries the gallows. No force, no children, no broken promise, nobody robbed or cheated or deceived. The only thing anyone in town can actualy articulate against them is disgust.

The device I want the story built out of: the clerk keeps his reckoning in a ledger, and every pleasure and every pain he scores gets seven columns — intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent. Let's see real figures, invented but concrete, and let's see him totting them up on the page.

Then two turns I'd like:

First, partway through he should notice that the community has already punished these men without the court lifting a finger — work that dried up, a shopkeeper who won't serve them, a stone through a window, the name they get called in the street — and he has to work out whether the court's penalty laid on top of all that makes this the fourth case, the needless one.

Second, the seventh column should break his argument instead of saving it. Extent means counting persons, and there are a great many more disgusted townsfolk than there are accused men. I want that discovery to land as something genuinely awful, not as a clever gotcha.

Give the other side a real advocate — the town attorney, or the vicar — who argues honestly and well: alarm spreads, alarm is pain, and if the clerk refuses to count the town's revulsion as a harm then he has no business counting the town's relief either, its just picking which feelings get a column. Don't make him a bigot or a fool. Don't let either side win with a speech.

End on an actual decision and what it costs somebody specific. And please don't explain any of it to the reader in a narrator's aside — I want the argument to be reconstructable from what people do and say, not from a summary. Also don't quote from any real book or put words into any real person's mouth.
