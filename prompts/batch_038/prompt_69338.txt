Last night a friend and I spent four hours on van Fraassen's Reflection principle and ended up talking past each other, so I have decided to stop defending anything and simply lay out my position for an audit. She is a Bayesian of a stricter stripe than I am and left convinced that my package is incoherent; I left unsure whether she had located a real inconsistency or merely an uncomfortable one. I would like a third opinion that is not invested in either of us winning.

Here is what I actually hold, numbered so that you can refer to the items directly.

1. Reflection, in its conditionalized form: for any proposition H, any later time t, and any number r, my credence in H now, conditional on the hypothesis that my credence in H at t will be r, ought to be r. I take this to be a substantive requirement of rationality in its own right, not a harmless restatement of some other norm.

2. Strict conditionalization is the only rational rule of revision. Credences change when evidence arrives, by conditioning on the total evidence acquired; there is no other legitimate route. No independent work for inference to the best explanation, and no settling of a doxastic question by deciding it.

3. Accuracy-first about the norms: the force of a coherence requirement is exhausted by an expected-accuracy story, and a revision rule is rational just in case, from the standpoint of the agent who is about to adopt it, it maximizes expected accuracy by that agent's own lights. I accept this for conditionalization. I have never seen the parallel argument for Reflection and I doubt one is forthcoming, though I want that treated as a suspicion of mine rather than as a premise.

4. I do not think diachronic Dutch books establish irrationality. I grant that a bookie who could bind my later self would take money from me if I violated conditionalization, but I deny that this shows anything defective about my beliefs: the later wagers are accepted on a different evidence base, the losses fall on an agent who never endorsed the package, and I see no non-question-begging reason why my present self should treat my future self's commitments as her own.

5. A concrete plan of mine. This coming summer I am going to spend six weeks living with a community whose central empirical claim I currently put at about 0.2. I have read everything they have written. I do not expect to encounter a single new datum, and I do not expect to discover an error in my own handling of the data I already have. What I expect is that the life, the practices, and the company will make the claim seem considerably more plausible, and I expect my credence in it to be somewhere around 0.6 when I come home. I endorse the trip and would not cancel it.

6. Conciliationism about peer disagreement: if I learn that an epistemic peer — equally informed, equally competent, equally conscientious — assigns 0.8 to a proposition to which I assign 0.5, I ought to move substantially toward 0.8. I also hold that the person I will be at the end of that trip is exactly such a peer with respect to that proposition: same evidence, same competence, no discovery of any mistake on my part.

7. Finally, the point my friend pressed hardest and the one I could not answer at the time. She says item 1 is refuted by the ordinary case of waiting for evidence. I am told on Monday that I will learn the result of a test on Wednesday; I know that on Wednesday my credence in the hypothesis will be either very high or very low; so Reflection, she argues, requires me on Monday to hold some single definite credence that cannot be both of those, which is absurd, since I plainly should not already believe what the test is going to tell me. I suspect she is wrong here, but I could not say why, and I do not want to end up with a motivated answer.

What I want is an audit rather than an exposition of the principle, which I know well. Specifically:

- Rank the tensions among these items by severity. For each, name the smallest subset that cannot all be held by a rational agent, say what has to be given up, weakened, or reformulated to restore consistency, and say what that revision costs elsewhere in the package.
- Tell me which of the apparent conflicts dissolve on inspection, and show the dissolution rather than announcing it. If item 7 is not a real problem, state precisely what Reflection does and does not require in the waiting-for-evidence case, and draw out whatever lesson about the correct formulation of the principle the case is meant to teach.
- Say whether 1 and 2 are genuinely in conflict in case 5, or whether some reading of Reflection leaves them compatible. If the conflict is real, tell me whether the blame falls on Reflection, on my plan, or on my insistence that evidence is the only route to revision.
- Say whether 3 and 4 jointly leave 1 with any support at all. If they do not, tell me whether that makes 1 false, merely unmotivated, or in need of a different sort of motivation — something in the neighborhood of the continuity-of-the-agent thought my friend mocked, for instance, which I have not been able to articulate and would like to see whether it can be.
- Tell me whether 6 sharpens the problem in 5 or introduces a distinct one. I cannot work out whether conciliationism about my future self is doing the same work as Reflection, or whether it also generates a backward pull: if I move now out of deference to the peer I will become, that peer will later learn that her predecessor thought 0.2, and conciliationism would then seem to drag her back down.

Do not assume I am attached to any particular item, and do not soften the verdict for my benefit. I am deliberately not telling you which commitment I would sacrifice, because I do not know, and I would rather the ranking not bend toward my preferences. If the cheapest repair is a revision of the item I like best, say so plainly. I am not looking for citations or a reading list — I am looking for the reasoning.
