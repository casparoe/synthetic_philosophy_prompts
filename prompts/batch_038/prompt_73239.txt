I was reading a chapter in one of those pop-tech books on the train last week and it had a section on Langdon Winner's old essay about whether artifacts have politics. It went like this:

"There's a famous 1980 essay by the political scientist Langdon Winner, and its argument is really just one sentence: technology is never neutral, so the honest question about any tool is not whether it works but who it works for. Winner proves the point with two stories. Robert Moses had the overpasses on the Long Island parkways built too low for buses to pass under, which kept the poor and Black New Yorkers who depended on public transport away from Jones Beach — prejudice poured into concrete. Going the other way, farm equipment makers learned that automating a plant lets you deskill the work and break the union, and that is why they build their machines the clumsy way. The lesson is that once a design choice gets made the politics is settled and there's nothing left to argue about. You can't appeal to how efficient or convenient a technology is, because efficiency was never the criterion anyone was actually using. So the only defensible way to evaluate a technology is to ask which group it empowers, and any talk of neutral engineering judgement is just ideology in a hard hat."
