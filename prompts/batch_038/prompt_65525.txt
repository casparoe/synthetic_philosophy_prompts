For the past four years I have convened an unpaid study circle that meets on Thursday evenings in the back room of our town library. There are twelve regulars: two retired engineers, a district nurse, a school-bus driver, a married couple who keep the hardware shop, and a handful of others. None of us has a university education. I left school at sixteen and have read on my own ever since; I have never taken a course in philosophy or in mathematics. I set these closing assessments purely because writing forces precision on people who would otherwise talk past one another over coffee.

We have just finished a six-week unit on cooperation and superrationality, working from photocopied excerpts of Douglas Hofstadter's *Metamagical Themas*, including the Platonia dilemma and the Luring Lottery he ran through his *Scientific American* column in 1983. I now need the written assessment that closes the unit.

Please take the part of a philosopher of decision theory who has also spent many years setting and marking undergraduate examinations. Think carefully about where the genuine difficulty in this material lies before you draft anything, and open your reply with a short brainstorming section: list three or four candidate cruxes the assessment could be built around, and say in a sentence or two which you are choosing and why. Keep that section to a few lines — I do not want an essay about the essay.

The paper must be built on the two cases below, which are my own alterations of Hofstadter's twenty-telegram problem rather than the original. I have a strong hunch that they come apart in an instructive way, but I do not trust my own algebra enough to state the point precisely, and working that out properly is part of what I am asking you for.

*Case One.* An anonymous benefactor sends identical letters to twenty adults who have never met and who know nothing about one another beyond the fact that they have all received the same letter. Each must reply either YES or NO by noon on Friday. If exactly one of the twenty replies YES, that person receives ten million dollars and the other nineteen receive nothing. If two or more reply YES, or if all twenty reply NO, nobody receives anything. The twenty cannot communicate.

*Case Two.* Everything is exactly as in Case One, with one change to the payment rule: if exactly one of the twenty replies YES, then all twenty receive ten million dollars each. Two or more YES replies, or twenty NO replies, still leave everyone with nothing.

In both cases, assume that each of the twenty cares only about how much money they personally receive and not at all about what the others receive; that each is in precisely the same epistemic position as every other; and that each has read the same material and reasons at least as carefully as you do. Do not trouble yourself with whether a benefactor would really arrange matters this way — treat the rules as given.

What I want produced:

1. **The question as it would appear on the paper.** A header stating the time allowed (ninety minutes), that candidates may use their notes and a pocket calculator, and that the paper is worth twenty-five marks in total. Then three parts: Part A on Case One, worth nine marks; Part B on Case Two, worth ten marks; and Part C, worth six marks, comparing Case One with the Luring Lottery as it was actually run — a prize of one million dollars, divided by the total number of entries received and paid to whoever held the entry drawn at random, with readers free to submit as many entries as they liked by writing a single number on a postcard, and with the reasoning behind the recommended one-in-many dice roll explained in the very same column that announced the contest. State those rules inside Part C so that nobody needs to remember them. You may mention that a great many readers instead wrote numbers so vast that the prize, once divided, would have been worth a fraction of a cent.

2. **A model answer of roughly nine hundred words.** Where there is arithmetic, show the working rather than only the result, and explain each step in words as well as in symbols; approximate answers are acceptable where the exact figure is tedious. Part C should require no calculus — a clear verbal argument with at most simple algebra. I especially want Part B to make candidates determine for themselves whether ordinary self-interested calculation and superrational calculation give the same verdict there, and then to say what their answer implies about the work the symmetry assumption is doing in Part A.

3. **A marking scheme, part by part.** Name the specific moves a first-class answer must make and how the marks are distributed among them; then, for each part, a sentence or two on what separates a merely competent answer from an excellent one. Separately, list the mistakes that should cost marks, each with one line on why it is tempting — I need to be able to distinguish a confused answer from a well-argued one. Include guidance on how much credit to give a candidate who sets a calculation up correctly and then botches the arithmetic.

4. **A short closing note** on how I should treat a candidate who rejects the superrational framing altogether and argues coherently for doing something else. I have no wish to penalize dissent automatically, but neither will I hand out marks for confident nonsense; tell me where the line falls.

Two further constraints. The question must not be answerable by explaining what superrationality is, or by retelling the twenty-telegram story: a candidate who has memorized the labels should get no further than a bare pass. And please include no bibliography, no reading list, and no citations of any kind — we work from photocopies, none of us has library access, and anything you cite I could not check. For the same reason, do not put anyone's words in quotation marks; paraphrase instead. Write the paper in plain, careful English of the sort ordinary people use, not in the idiom of a journal article.
