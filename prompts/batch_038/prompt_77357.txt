So I cover politics and ideas for a magazine, and for the past year I've been sitting in on a constitutional commission that's rewriting the franchise rules — basically, who counts as part of the electorate. Their rulings so far: non-citizen permanent residents get the full franchise after eight years; seasonal workers on two-year visas get nothing; 16- and 17-year-olds vote in municipal elections but not national ones; adults under full guardianship don't vote at all; prisoners keep the vote, including lifers; emigrants keep it for fifteen years after leaving and then lose it; adult children of emigrants who were born abroad and have never actually lived here get nothing; tourists and business visitors nothing. Next month they take up cross-border commuters — people who live just over the line, work here fourty hours a week, pay income tax here, and are answerable to our courts and police whenever they're on our soil, but have never held a residence here and whose whole social life, family, kids' school, is on the other side.

Where do you think they land? Honestly I care less about the verdict than the machinery. I can already see at least two rules that fit every ruling above — one about being subject to the state's coercive power, one about having a life woven into the society — with the year-counts doing proxy work for either. So: lay out the candidate rules that survive thier track record, say what each implies for the commuters, tell me which one you'd bet on and why, and then name the next case this commission could decide that would force the two apart.
