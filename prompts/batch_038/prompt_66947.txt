Ok, work question that's turned into a philosophy question, or maybe it was always one.

I'm on the personalization side of a subscription app (meal kits, doesn't matter which). We have a rule on the team: the model only gets to use a behavioral signal if our data science lead signs off that it "counts as a preference." She's the arbiter. Econ background, way more careful about this than I am, and I keep guessing her calls wrong, so I've been writing them down. I have a new one and I want to predict it before I ask her.

Her calls so far:

1. Guy who picked 2-day shipping over free 7-day in about 60 seperate orders over a year, across completely different carts. Counts.
2. User who tells our survey that price is the only thing they care about, then buys the top tier every single time. Counts. Her note was something like "the survey is a report, the checkout is a decision."
3. Our app auto-reorders a consumable when stock runs low, nobody clicks anything, it just happens. Doesn't count.
4. A login flow where the continue button was the only thing rendered — 300 identical taps. Doesn't count. She wrote "no menu."
5. A user who went A over B, B over C, C over A, same screens, same prices, same time of day, and we could not find anything that predicted the sequence better than a coin flip. Doesn't count. She thought about this one the longest.
6. User who consistently pays about $3 more to buy from a vendor they have points with, even when an identical cheaper one is one click away and clearly labeled. Counts.
7. User who ended up enrolled in a paid add-on because the decline was a grey link in the footer, then contacted support, got a refund, and the following month declined the same add-on from a normal two-button screen. Doesn't count.
8. Takes the salad when it's salad $9 / burger $11, takes the burger when it's salad $9 / burger $8, and across a bunch of price points their pick tracks relative price cleanly. Counts — she said as clean as it gets.

New case. We ran an experiment flipping the default on the renewal screen for a random 5% of accounts: instead of "renew" being pre-ticked, nothing is pre-ticked and both buttons are identical. Renewal in that arm fell off a cliff. The user I have to make a call on is 4471, who's in the 95% arm. Forty months of renewals, never opened the screen more than once, never compared plans, and cancelling means four screens and then a phone call with a retention agent. I ran a consistency check on their history — no cycles, no violations, nothing. And on the last survey we sent they said twice that they'd rather not be paying.

So: does Dana say forty months of renewals reveals that this person prefers the subscription?

I care more about the machinery than the yes/no. What rule is she actually running? My own default going in was "if they clicked it, they wanted it," and I'm fairly sure 5 and 7 killed that, but I don't know what replaced it. Lay out the candidate rules that are consistent with all eight calls, say what each one says about 4471, and tell me which you'd bet on and why. I already know some of them are going to disagree here, and I suspect the ones that say "yes" don't mean the same thing by it — one of them would have me store "prefers renewal at this price" and another would have me store "prefers whatever is pre-ticked," which are completely different features and would push the model in different directions. Also tell me which of the eight is the closest analog to 4471 and where that analogy breaks down.

Then: what's the one extra case I could bring her that would show me which rule she's on? If no single case separates all of them, say so and give me the two you'd run.

And practically — since I do have to ship something — what should I encode if I want to be consistent with her precedents rather than with my gut, and if the precedents genuinely don't settle it, what principle am I committing the team to by picking either way? I'd rather know that I'm adopting a principle than discover it six months later in a retention report.

One more thing that's bugging me, and it's the part I actually need. Even if she says yes: does forty default-renewals tell me anything about how much? I have another user who also renewed forty times but re-opened the plan comparison every month and picked us anyway. My downstream model wants a number, not a rank, and I can't tell whether those two users are supposed to come out equal or miles apart. I know there's a whole literature here that I've never read, so if the answer is "that's not a question you can answer from choice data at all," I'd like to understand why not.
