I'm a staff software engineer at a mid-sized infrastructure company, and for the past year I have co-hosted a monthly, roughly forty-five-minute interview series that we publish internally and on a small public feed. The audience is working engineers; almost none of them have any philosophy training, including me. Next week's guest is a visiting scholar — I will call her Dr. H. for now — who works at the intersection of history of science and the sociology of technical organizations, and who has built a public argument out of the typology in Nietzsche's second *Untimely Meditation* (the essay usually titled *On the Uses and Disadvantages of History for Life*, 1874): monumental history, antiquarian history, and critical history. I have read only summaries of the essay. I know what the three labels mean at the level of a paragraph each, and I can repeat that, but I cannot defend an interpretation of the text and I do not want to appear to be doing so on air.

Her position, as she has stated it in talks and in writing, is roughly this, and the questions need to engage these claims rather than the essay:

1. She holds that the software industry runs almost entirely on monumental history — founder legends, canonical papers recited as scripture, keynote hagiography of pioneer figures, the cult of the singular brilliant engineer — and that this saturation is actively harmful because it produces in working engineers the sense of being latecomers, that everything worth building has already been built, so the only remaining move is imitation.
2. She defends architecture decision records, git archaeology, and preserved RFC archives as antiquarian history in its healthy form, and says the mode tips over into mummification when an artifact is kept out of piety to its author rather than out of use. But she has also said, separately, that no act of preservation in a technical organization is ever *merely* antiquarian.
3. She treats deprecation, sunsetting, and full rewrites of legacy systems as the engineering form of critical history — the past brought before a court, interrogated, and condemned — and her stock response to the objection that a rewrite destroys knowledge is that a rewrite is a verdict rather than a forgetting.
4. She says critical history can only be issued by people who suffer under a system, so the standing to judge a legacy service belongs to the engineer carrying the pager for it, not to the executive who benefits from it, and not to an outside reviewer.
5. She maintains that the sole standard for whether an organization's relation to its past is healthy or pathological is whether it serves the life of the teams doing the work, and she nevertheless ranks specific companies as historically sick or historically healthy in her talks.

Please write me eight to ten interview questions for this conversation, in the order I should ask them. For each question, give me three things: one sentence saying what a substantive answer would have to contain for me to consider it answered; the answer I should expect her most likely to give, in a sentence or two; and a follow-up keyed specifically to that expected answer, not to the question. Keep each primary question short enough to read aloud in a single breath — under about sixty words — and do not write any question that requires me to adjudicate a reading of Nietzsche or that she can answer by summarizing the essay back at me.

The set must include the following, distributed across the questions:

- A question on monumental history that forces her to say whether the mode has any legitimate use in engineering at all, given that the essay presents it as serving the person who is actively striving and needs a model of what was once possible; and a follow-up that turns on the reading list and canon she assigns her own students.
- A question on the measurement problem: if the only standard is whether history serves the life of the teams, who arbitrates when two teams in the same company need opposite relations to the same past — one that needs the founding myth and one that is crushed by it? I want her to give me something an engineering manager could actually apply, and I want the follow-up to press on whether that criterion is anything more than restating the standard it was supposed to operationalize.
- A question that puts her claims 2 in collision with itself: an artifact that is genuinely used, cited constantly, and load-bearing in decisions, but used in such a way that it always justifies not changing anything. Ask which mode that is, and let the follow-up pursue whether "use" can do the discriminating work she is asking it to do.
- A question on critical history and the objection that the critic is himself a product of the past he condemns — that the engineer writing the deprecation RFC was formed by the system he is deleting, and cannot step outside it. Her expected answer will appeal to process: a sunset policy, a deprecation committee, a checklist. The follow-up should ask whether a procedure can solve what looks like a problem about who the judge is, and should also raise the fact that the "new need" driving most rewrites arrives from outside the team — a new language, a new platform, a vendor roadmap — so the verdict may not be the team's own at all.
- A question on claim 4, the standing claim: does suffering under a system confer authority to judge it, or only motive? Press on the case where the person on the pager is also the person worst placed to see what the system does for teams they never hear from.
- A question on forgetting. The essay values the capacity to forget and the unhistorical, and she has implied in talks that institutional amnesia is always pathological. Ask whether her framework can accommodate a team that ships well precisely because it keeps no archive — all recent hires, no ADRs, no postmortem history — and what she would say about it if that team outperforms one that keeps everything.
- A question that makes her commit to a verdict on a case I will describe on air, so the audience can see the framework do work: a fifteen-year-old internal billing reconciliation service, no longer on any documented critical path and with no way to prove it is not; zero maintainers; unreadable code that has nonetheless never caused an incident; the architecture decision record that justified its original design is missing; it was written by an engineer who still works here, who trained most of the current staff, and who is now our most respected architecture reviewer; three teams have proposed deleting it and two have proposed freezing it in place. Ask what her framework tells us to do, which of the three modes she is herself operating in when she answers, and — if her answer is that it depends on what serves the life of the teams — exactly which fact about those teams would settle it.
- A final question on what would count against her diagnosis: what observable feature of an organization would show her that it is historically healthy rather than sick, and whether she can name a company she would call sick that is nonetheless doing very well, and how she would distinguish that from her framework being unfalsifiable.

One more item, in reserve and clearly marked as such: a short interrupting question I can deploy if she answers any of the above with a general lament about engineering culture instead of addressing the case in front of her, which is her habitual move in recorded talks.
