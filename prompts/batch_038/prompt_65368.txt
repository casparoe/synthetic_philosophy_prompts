I retired two years ago after thirty-four years in a low-temperature lab — mostly coaxing stubborn materials into telling me things they didn't want to, which is not so different from what my reading group does to arguments, except the materials never wrote back. Since then I've been reading philosophy at the public library on Tuesday nights, and last month we took up free will and the neuroscience of it, because half the group assumed the experiments had settled the question and the other half assumed they hadn't and nobody could say why. I offered to write the argument up cleanly for next week, and I got something that scares me a little, so I want a second opinion before I hand it around. I'm not after a literature review, just the reasoning.

Here's what I have:

1. An action is mine, free in the sense worth caring about, only if a conscious intention of mine is among the causes of the movement.
2. A cause cannot come after the process it causes; and if a process that reliably produces movement M is already under way before some event C occurs, then C is not among the causes of M.
3. In Libet's setup the slow build-up of electrical potential over motor cortex — the readiness potential — begins about 550 ms before the wrist flexion, while subjects place the moment they became aware of the urge to move at about 200 ms before it.
4. So the process that produces the movement is roughly 350 ms under way before the subject is aware of any urge to move.
5. Libet's own escape hatch — that consciousness still gets the last hundred or two hundred ms to veto — fails, because the veto would itself be a conscious event, and by (2) a conscious event that late cannot cause anything either.
6. Nor does it help that subjects have decided in advance to flex sometime in the next few seconds: a standing decision that is never revisited cannot inject causation at the moment of the movement.
7. So in the paradigm the urge is a byproduct that accompanies the movement rather than a cause producing it.
8. But nothing so far mentions wrists, or electrodes, or those particular numbers. For any action M whatsoever, whatever neural process produces M must begin before the subject could become aware of an urge to do M — and awareness of an urge is later still than the urge it is awareness of — so by (2) the urge is never a cause of M.
9. Therefore (8) is not an empirical finding at all but a consequence of (1) and (2): no experiment could have come out otherwise, and Libet's data illustrate the conclusion without evidencing it.
10. So no human action is free, and we could have known it without the EEG.

The thing that makes me want to hand it back is that it seems to prove far more than I asked it to. Run the numbers the other way — suppose the readiness potential started 5 ms before the movement instead of 550, or suppose some future scanner dated it after the urge — and (8) still goes through on the second half of its own reasoning, the part about awareness trailing the urge. In my old life we had a rule about results like that: if the answer doesn't move when the measurement moves, you haven't measured anything, you've written a selection rule and dressed it up as data. Worse, (10) tells me I didn't cause the two-page memo I spent a month thinking my way into last spring, and it tells me the subjects in Libet's chair didn't cause their own clock readings either, which would make the W judgments — the entire evidential base of the experiment — just more noise drifting along with the movement. An argument that saws off the branch it's sitting on and calls the sound a discovery is an argument I've gotten wrong somewhere. I should say I'd most like to keep (1) and (2), because those aren't claims about brains, they're just claims about what causation and free action are, and giving them up feels like giving up the game rather than losing a hand — but I'm not going to pretend that settles it.
