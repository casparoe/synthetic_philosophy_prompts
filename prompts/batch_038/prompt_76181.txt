I am in the second year of a philosophy degree, and our seminar on the semantics of general terms has spent the last two sessions on identity and predication. In the first, a classmate pressed the Warring States Chinese thesis 'a white horse is not a horse' — the one attributed to Gongsun Long and to the disputers around him — into service as a counterexample to the view that "is" in ordinary language ever expresses identity. His reconstruction went roughly like this: what picks out shape is not what picks out colour, so the compound name "white horse" and the simple name "horse" cannot have the same semantic value; and since someone who asks for a horse may be handed a yellow or a black one while someone who asks for a white horse may not, the two requests are not interchangeable and neither are the expressions that figure in them. On that basis he claimed the disputers had anticipated the Fregean point that an identity claim is informative only because the names flanking it differ in sense, and he waved off the objection that the thesis is a mere triviality about a proper subset and its superset as a failure to engage with the argument. Our tutor pushed back from the opposite direction in the second session: she suggested the thing was court entertainment, a deliberate piece of sophistry aimed at people who took naming too solemnly, and that reading it as a contribution to semantics imports a modern agenda into it.

Before I open my mouth in the next session I would like the sentence pulled apart. Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that a thesis of that form could be making — varying what the copula is taken to do (identity between objects, membership in a kind, inclusion of one kind in another, sameness of intension, sameness of extension, sameness of what satisfies a request, or sameness of name); varying what the two terms are taken to stand for (individual animals, kinds, universals, feature-complexes, or nothing beyond marks in a naming practice); and varying whether the claim is about language, about the world, or about the pragmatics of asking for and handing over things. For each reading, tell me whether it comes out trivially true, substantive but defensible, straightforwardly false, or too indeterminate to have a truth value at all. I would also like to know which readings survive the objector's reply in the dialogue — that if there is a white horse one cannot say there is no horse, so having a white horse is having a horse — and whether the argument about asking for a horse versus asking for a white horse establishes anything about semantics, or only about what different requests license a hearer to supply.

Then give me verdicts on the two uses it was put to. Which of these readings, if any, would actually earn my classmate his conclusion that "is" cannot be an identity sign and that there is a Fregean moral about sense to be drawn here — and does any reading make the thesis both non-trivial and true without simply exploiting the very conflation of sense and reference that the Fregean distinction exists to resolve? Which readings would support my tutor's deflationary verdict that it is verbal play rather than theory? Finally, I have been told that the classical Chinese does not mark grammatical number, so the sentence can be heard generically, as about some particular animal, or as a claim about the compound name rather than about the beasts; if that bears on which readings are even available, please say so. What I want is a list I can bring to seminar, with a one-line verdict attached to each reading, rather than one favoured interpretation argued at length.
