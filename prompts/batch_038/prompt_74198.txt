I am a third-year philosophy student, and this term I am taking a course on nineteenth-century European social thought in which we have just spent two weeks on Kierkegaard's 1846 review of Thomasine Gyllembourg's novel Two Ages — in particular his contrast between the age of revolution and the present age, his account of reflection and levelling, and the two categories I care most about, the public and the press. Separately from the course, I help convene a Thursday-evening reading group attached to my college: about a dozen students, almost none of them reading philosophy (two are journalism students, two read politics, one is an engineer, one a historian), and all of them will have read the excerpt on the present age beforehand. The regular convenor is away next week and has handed the session to me. I have forty-five minutes, a whiteboard, and no slides. What I want from you is not an explanation of Kierkegaard's argument — I have my own reading of it and a summary will not change it — but a plan for how I should explain it to this particular room. I am not asking for readings or a bibliography; the group has the text.

The part I most need help with is the dependency structure. My instinct is that the press has to be introduced before the public, since Kierkegaard appears to treat the press as what makes the public possible at all, and that reflection has to precede levelling, since levelling is presented as the outcome of a reflective age rather than of anyone's intentions. But I can see the case for the opposite order in both instances, and I worry that leading with the press invites exactly the instrumental reading — a paper that manipulates a passive readership — that the text seems designed to block. Please tell me which order to use, what each element depends on, and precisely what goes wrong if I introduce them in the other sequence. Second, I want one example to carry the whole thing, and I have deliberately ruled out anything digital: the moment I mention platforms the session collapses into a debate about social media and nothing about the argument survives. My candidates are the first-person plural voice of a newspaper editorial; an unsigned letter to the editor signed only A Reader; a subscription list of signatories to a petition who never meet one another; and the Corsair affair itself, where a satirical paper turned Kierkegaard into a man all of Copenhagen recognized and no one personally knew. Choose one, and tell me what the carrier must have and must lack — I suspect it must be a case in which no one is co-present and no one can be held answerable, and in which the sheer number of people is not what does the work, but I would like that stated sharply, along with what is lost by each of the alternatives I am giving up.

Third, the misconceptions. Here are the ones I expect, and I want each placed at the point in the sequence where it can still be headed off rather than argued with afterwards: that the public just means the crowd, or the majority, or a lot of people in a room; that levelling is a complaint about egalitarian politics, so that Kierkegaard is simply a reactionary snob; that talkativeness means speaking too much, rather than speaking without ever having to answer for what one says; that the press is dangerous because it spreads falsehoods, so that the whole analysis is really about accuracy; that the upshot is withdrawal into a private, authentic self; and, from at least one person in the room, that the text is nothing more than the sour reaction of a man who had just been ridiculed in the papers. Two regulars will also push in specific directions: a journalism student who will hear the session as an attack on her future profession, and a politics student who will insist this is an early version of the claim that media manufacture consent. Tell me how the sequence should absorb each of those rather than how to win an exchange with them. Finally, I want one closing question — ideally a discrimination between two briefly described situations — that would show whether they have grasped that the public is an abstraction produced by the press rather than a large collection of individuals, together with the competent-sounding wrong answer I should listen out for, so that I can tell a real understanding from a persuasive restatement. And tell me what to leave out at this level and how to say so honestly: the religious register and the single individual as an ultimately theological category, the plot of Gyllembourg's novel, the Danish Hegelian disputes, and the strategy of the pseudonymous authorship. I would rather announce those omissions than let the group leave believing they have read a piece of secular media sociology.
