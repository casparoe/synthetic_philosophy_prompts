So I'm helping run a small workshop on conditionals and we've decided to make the two camps in the room actually do an adversarial collaboration instead of another round of talking past each other. Camp A holds that indicatives and counterfactuals have the same basic semantic shape — both state truth-apt contents, both get their content from something like a selection among possibilities — and that the felt difference between them is mostly that the indicative mood carries a presupposition that the antecedent is still open as far as the context is concerned, so all the probability stuff is pragmatics layered on an ordinary truth-conditional core. Camp B thinks that's backwards in the part that matters: indicatives aren't in the business of stating truths at all, to assert one is to suppose the antecedent and, on that supposition, endorse the consequent, and their assertibility tracks the corresponding conditional probability strictly — whereas the counterfactual is a genuinely different animal that is *not* governed by that thesis. So one side says the gap between a present-tense if-clause and its past-subjunctive twin is shallow and pragmatic, the other says it's a difference in kind, at the level of what a conditional even is. Both think the other is confused by the same data: the cheating-card-player case where two people with different evidence each seem entitled to assert conditionals that can't both be true, and the Oswald pair, where the indicative version (someone else did it, if he didn't) sounds like a triviality anyone who believes the president was shot should accept, while the counterfactual version sounds like a substantive and probably false historical claim.

What I want you to draft is the document this collaboration should end up producing — the joint statement both sides could actually put their names to. Start with a numbered section of agreed claims, and be strict about it: nothing goes in there that a real partisan of either camp would refuse to sign, which means no smuggling in "conditionals express propositions" as a neutral premise. I'd like agreement sorted by topic: what the linguistic data are that any account has to handle, what each camp grants about the relationship between assertibility and conditional probability, what they agree the triviality-style results do and don't rule out, what they agree counts as giving a semantics of conditionals at all, and what they agree about how conditionals behave when they get embedded under negation, disjunction, probability operators, and indirect report. Then a disagreements table with these columns: the disagreement stated neutrally; how camp A would state it in words A accepts; how camp B would state it in words B accepts; whether the difference is substantive or verbal; and what would settle it. Then the cruxes — I'd say three to five — where a crux is a sub-question such that settling it would actually move at least one side, not just rephrase the standoff.

For each crux I want a concrete test or case that both sides accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction written down *in advance*, so please include a pre-registration block. Use these three vignettes, which I made up for the occasion and which I don't think either camp has an answer ready for. (1) A card is drawn and sealed in an envelope; you're told it's either the ace of spades or a red card; you say that if it's red, it's a heart; a week later, after the envelope has been opened and everyone knows it was the ace of spades, you say the very same sentence about the very same draw. (2) A defunct charity's charter still contains a clause to the effect that if an applicant has lived in the district for five years, the application is approved; the charity is being wound up and everyone knows no such applicant ever existed and none ever will — was that clause true, vacuously satisfied, or never the kind of thing that could be either? (3) An interpreter renders a witness's remark into English as an indicative conditional about the office light being on and someone being inside; you later learn the witness's own evidence differed from yours, in that she already knew the light was off — say what you may report, and whether what you report is true. For vignettes (1) and (3) I want numbers: predicted mean acceptability rating on a 1–7 scale for naive participants, and the predicted size of the shift between the before-the-reveal and after-the-reveal utterances, with each camp committing to a figure and saying what result would count against them.

Two more things. There's a suspicion in the room that part of this dispute is verbal — specifically that if camp A is allowed to say the content of an indicative conditional is something like a diagonal or context-dependent proposition, and camp B is allowed to insist that whatever survives that move isn't what they were ever denying, then a chunk of the disagreement evaporates. I want the joint statement to say explicitly which parts dissolve that way and which don't, and whether the diagonal maneuver is a genuine dissolution or a dodge; treat the words "proposition", "truth-apt", and "semantics" as suspect terms and say where each camp is using them differently. And include a short section where each camp names the finding or argument that would make them give up their view, plus a candid note on whether the other camp would accept that finding as decisive — if the honest answer is no, say no and explain why that's not just stubbornness. Please don't pad this with a bibliography or a reading list; refer to arguments and cases by description rather than by citation, and don't guess at page numbers or paper titles. Names are fine where everyone knows who you mean. Aim for maybe 900 to 1400 words, in a register that sounds like two people who disagree but have stopped scoring points off each other.
