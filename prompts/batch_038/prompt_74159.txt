I'm a fourth-year PhD student in economic history, and I've drafted an argument for a workshop paper on path dependence. My advisor's verdict was that the conclusion cannot possibly be right, but she couldn't tell me where the reasoning breaks, and three weeks of staring at it have left me worse off than when I started. The argument is supposed to establish that lock-in is universal — that every stable institutional outcome is path dependent — and hence that the convergence question ("would the same outcomes have arrived anyway?") always receives the answer "no."

(1) Call an outcome locked in if and only if it would continue to persist even were it known, at each moment of its persistence, to be inferior to the best alternative then available.

(2) For any institution that has persisted for some time, the alternatives now available are not the alternatives that were available at adoption: complements have grown up around it, agents have adapted their skills, capital, and expectations to it, and the coalition required to displace it has been reconfigured.

(3) Therefore the comparison that governs whether a persisting institution is retained — this institution now against that alternative now — is not the comparison that governed whether it was adopted.

(4) Where the comparison class has shifted in this way, persistence carries no information about ex ante optimality.

(5) So persistence never evidences that an institution was, or remains, the best of the alternatives open to it.

(6) But if persistence never evidences superiority, then any institution we observe persisting must be regarded as surviving without warrant that it is the best alternative now.

(7) And an institution surviving without such warrant is one that would go on surviving even if it were known to be inferior — which is precisely (1).

(8) Hence every persisting institution is locked in, and where the same institution arises independently in disconnected societies — double-entry bookkeeping, the grid plan, the joint-stock company — we must say not that they converged on it but that unrelated paths happened to deposit the same locked-in outcome.

I'd most like to keep (2) and (3), since they are the only premises doing economic work; they are, as far as I can see, just the increasing-returns claim that the QWERTY literature is built on. Which step should I reject, why did it look innocent when I wrote it down, and what do I lose on each of the available exits?
