In my philosophy of mind seminar we were assigned two papers that deploy the same picture in opposite directions, and I cannot tell whether that fact should worry me. The first holds that consciousness is a user illusion in the way a desktop is: the unified, qualia-rich display the brain presents is a simplified interface laid over massively parallel neural activity, so that once the functional and computational story is told there is nothing further to explain. The second uses the very same desktop metaphor for perception itself — spacetime is the desktop, physical objects are the icons, and an interface is shaped by selection to guide adaptive behaviour rather than to resemble what it stands for — but draws the opposite kind of lesson, namely that physics describes the interface rather than a mind-independent world, with consciousness as the deeper reality. In the first paper the interface sits inside the skull and hides the brain; in the second it is the world and hides reality.

Could you audit the metaphor as an argument? I want the correspondence spelled out for each deployment — who the user is, what the display is, what the underlying machine is, and what to make of the drag-and-drop that really does delete the file even though the icon's colour and position belong to no file — and then the disanalogies, sorted into those that genuinely threaten the conclusion each paper draws and those that do not. Two difficulties I cannot resolve on my own: whether an interface requires a user external to it, and what each theory is entitled to say the user is; and whether interface property can carry the weight illusion is being asked to bear, given that icons are genuinely coloured, genuinely movable, and genuinely causally efficacious. Finally, does a picture that supports both a deflation of experience and a deflation of the physical world thereby carry no content at all, or can one of the two uses survive the disanalogies when the other cannot — and is there a better analogy for what each is trying to say?
