I have no training in philosophy or psychology — I work in logistics — but for roughly twelve years I have written down my dreams every morning, and out of that habit I arrived at a view I cannot shake. I would like it pressed as hard as you can press it.

The view is this: for a person to have dreamed such-and-such is not for anything to have happened to them during the night. It is only for them to be so constituted that, once awake, they would sincerely narrate that content if asked. The narration need never actually take place; what matters is the standing condition that makes it available. Nothing need have been undergone while it was unavailable. I got the core of this from reading around two ideas I only know at second hand — Malcolm's claim that a dream is the impression left behind rather than an experience lived through, and Dennett's suggestion that a dream is less like a show watched during sleep than like a recording that gets played into memory at the moment of waking — but the version I hold goes further, because I think its virtue is that it dissolves a puzzle rather than that it describes sleep well. On my view the question of what dreamless sleep is like is a malformed question: there is nothing it is like, and the only reason that sounds intolerable is that we mistake a capacity to narrate for an event that once filled the interval. I also think the standard objection — that I seem to remember watching the dream unfold, so something must have unfolded — begs the question, because seeming to remember is just another capacity of the same kind, and a capacity requires no past event standing behind it.

Give me a map of this view's exposure:

1. Which observations or experimental results would genuinely count against it, restricted to results that are actually available rather than merely conceivable: real-time signalling from inside REM sleep by lucid sleepers following a code agreed before they fell asleep; dream content recovered from brain activity in sleepers who have not yet said anything; wake-ups at which the sleeper denies having dreamed and then narrates a dream minutes later, and the converse case of confident narration with nothing behind it. For each, say whether my view can absorb the result and at what cost.

2. Which conceptual cases bite hardest: a dream never told to anyone, never written down, and entirely gone by noon; a sleeper whose condition survives but who is drugged or paralysed so that narration is blocked; a sleeper who narrates in rich, confident detail a dream that did not occur. Say specifically whether my view makes error about the content of one's own dream impossible, and whether that is a cost I should reckon with or a bullet I should bite.

3. What my view predicts that the rival — dreams are experiences occurring during sleep — does not predict, and the reverse. Is there any asymmetry between them that could be checked, or do they agree on every observable?

4. Whether my view collapses into something else. If the standing condition is stored content that gets taken up at waking, is that not an item with a time of occurrence after all, so that my view differs from the rival only about when the content is deployed? Or does it collapse into the flat denial that anything mental happens in sleep, and if so, is that the honest label for the position I am actually holding?

5. What a committed defender of this view would concede as counting against it, as distinct from what critics merely assert. I want the defender's own concessions — the results and cases they would agree are damaging — not only the complaints of opponents.

6. Whether the disposition clause has been patched past the point of risk: would narrate, if awake, if not too groggy, if asked, if the condition has not decayed. If the view has been qualified into immunity, tell me plainly, and then tell me whether that is a defect for a claim that is partly conceptual rather than empirical. I would rather have an unflattering answer than a diplomatic one.

Format: rank the defeaters in a numbered list, most damaging first, with a sentence or two on each explaining why it sits where it does. Then, after the list and on its own final line, write Verdict: followed by exactly one of EXPOSED (the view risks something and there is a defeater it cannot absorb), PATCHED (the view survives only by qualification, and that is a genuine problem for it), or IMMUNE-AND-FINE (the view survives only by qualification, and for a claim of this kind that is not a problem).
