My friend and I argued for two hours last night about whether behavioural public policy has quietly abandoned revealed preference economics, and I would like the question settled by reconstruction rather than by assertion. Consider two things the same government does out of the same budget. In regulatory cost-benefit analysis it monetizes welfare from observed behaviour: the extra pay workers accept for riskier jobs helps set the value of a statistical life, and what people actually drive, buy, smoke, and rent fixes what cleaner air or an hour of saved travel time is worth. Meanwhile it funds a behavioural insights unit that re-arranges choice architecture — automatic enrolment in workplace pensions, opt-out organ donor registers, ordering of options on a form — on the explicit ground that people's on-the-spot choices are systematically distorted, while insisting that the resulting nudge still serves the person's own ends as that person would judge them.

What account of welfare, and of what a preference is, does the first practice strictly presuppose, and what does the second presuppose that the first rules out? Reconstruct both implicit theories, name the rival accounts each quietly excludes, and tell me whether the combination is outright incoherent or merely strained: my friend says the appeal to what people would choose on reflection shows that choice was never doing any evidential work, since you can always posit whatever preference you need to explain the surviving behaviour, and I am not certain that is fair to the economists. Please also separate what each practice is forced into from what it merely sits comfortably alongside, and say what would have to change in the arithmetic, and in the defaults, if welfare were identified instead with informed desire, with experienced well-being, or with what people are actually able to do and be.
