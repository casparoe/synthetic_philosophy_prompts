Okay, I've been chewing on hard choices for a while and I want to squeeze a principle out of my own gut verdicts rather than read more theory at me. Play the role of a philosopher who works on value theory and rational choice, but talk like a person, not like a lecture. Think carefully: before you commit to a principle, spend a short paragraph brainstorming two or three candidate diagnoses of what's going on in my data. Don't give me citations, references, or a reading list — I can't look anything up and I don't want you guessing at titles. I want the principle itself, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I haven't thought of yet.

Here are my verdicts.

1. Two careers: stay in a secure civil service job I'm reasonably good at, or go try to be a novelist. Neither is better. They aren't equally good either — I'd push back on anyone claiming they're exactly as good. And if you sweetened the novelist option a little, say a modest grant that buys me one free year, I still don't think it's settled. I'd still say that one's up to me.

2. Two candidates for one job post. A is better on the stuff that matters, but B has one extra publication that's honestly filler. I don't think the extra publication makes B better. But keep piling publications onto B and at some point B does become better. There's no sharp hair's-breadth boundary, yet there's still a fact of the matter once the gap gets big enough. Here I say: comparible, just vague.

3. Two apartments, identical in every respect except one has a slightly bigger closet. Bigger closet wins, full stop. The small improvement settles it. I know this looks like it contradicts 1 and I have no good story yet.

4. Money versus a friend's confidence. Someone offers me a large sum to betray a close friend's secret: not on, no number gets me there. But I couldn't state an exchange rate — it isn't that friendship costs X. Now offer me a trivially small betrayal, repeating a mildly embarrassing story everyone's already heard, for a lot of money: I take the money and I don't think I've done anything wrong. So the trade isn't banned outright, but I can't see where the line is, and I'm confident the line isn't a number.

5. Choosing between two people to date. I think this is a real hard choice in the sense of 1, and I also think that whichever I pick I'll feel a kind of pull toward the other that isn't evidence I chose badly. Contrast the candidates in 2: if I hire A and later learn B had the extra publication, I feel nothing but curiosity. No pull at all.

6. A case a friend threw at me: save one person you love, or five strangers. I think saving your person is permitted, saving the five is permitted, and neither is better. But this one feels different from 1 — here I think a small addition to the five-strangers side, a sixth stranger, genuinely shifts things, whereas a sixth year of grant money on the novelist side does nothing.

My tentative principal is this: hard choices come in two kinds. In one, the options are on a par, meaning no small improvement to either side tips the balance, and the choice is mine to make rather than mine to discover. In the other, the better-than relation is just vague: small improvements don't tip it either, but big ones do, and there's still a fact about who's better once the gap is wide enough. The tell is whether a small improvement ever starts to matter.

Now test it against my six, and be blunt.

- Does it get all of them? I suspect not.
- Which verdict would you throw out, and which would you keep by making the principle messier? I want the price of each option spelled out, not just a pick.
- 1 and 3 look structurally identical — same option plus a small bonus — and I give opposite verdicts. Give me the most charitable account you can of the difference. If you can't build one, say so and tell me which of the two goes.
- Does the leftover pull in 5 have any diagnostic value, or is it just noise I should stop trusting?
- Predict a case I haven't listed: is it a hard choice between becoming a slightly kinder person and becoming a slightly happier one, and would a small improvement — a bit more kindness on offer — settle it?

I care about the reasoning more than the label. If you end up needing a third category beyond parity and vagueness, argue me into it rather than just naming it and moving on.
