Bit of an odd one. I've got into an arguement with my flatmate about whether dreams are experiences at all, and I've realised I don't actually know what I think, so here's what I say yes and no to when I go through it case by case. Tell me what principle, if any, fits all of it.

1. Phone alarm goes at 6:40. In the dream the fire alarm has been ringing for ages and I've already got up and walked to the kitchen. I wake to the real alarm. Real dream, obviously, and the sound got into it — something was happening while I was asleep that the noise caused.
2. A friend of mine with an EEG headband thing wakes me at what he says is deep REM, and I say 'nothing, no dream'. Ninety seconds later a whole one arrives, in detail, and it obviously belongs to the moment I was woken. Verdict: I did dream, and I was wrong when I said I hadn't. So it can't be that what makes it a dream is how things seem to me on waking.
3. I tell my partner a long vivid dream over breakfast. By lunchtime I can't recall a single image, only that I told them. Still a real dream.
4. The lucid dreamers who agree beforehand to signal with left-right-left eye movements, and the machine picks it up in real time, so they're answering questions from inside the dream. Dreams definitely happen during sleep there — and I don't want to say lucid dreamers are a different species from me in (1).
5. My own tentative rule, which I know is broken: a dream is a real experience if there is a moment during sleep at which it's like something to be me having it, where 'it's like something' means I'd have said so if woken at that moment. (2) kills it outright and (3) sits badly with it.
6. I can tell roughly how long a dream was, and the long ones come after long sleeps. I take that as evidence it unfolded in real time — you can't report a duration you never sat through.
7. But: someone woken five seconds into REM who reports an enormous twenty-minute plot, and does this repeatedly. I still want to say they dreamt. And now I want to say the twenty minutes was a property of the story rather than of the experiencing — which contradicts (6), and I can't see how to keep both.
8. Nearly everyone has several REM periods a night, and plenty of people swear they never dream. I say they do and just don't keep them. So I'm committed to experiences that leave no trace whatever.
9. Yet someone in dreamless deep sleep, or under anaesthetic: I'd say they didn't dream. If a night is tracelessly dreamless, what's the difference between dreamt-and-instantly-lost and never-dreamt? I want to say there is one. I can't cash it out.
10. The pair I can't handle. Suppose a machine could print the dream. In version A the transcript starts appearing forty minutes before the sleeper wakes and finishes as they wake. In version B the whole thing appears at once in the last quarter-second, complete, including the feel of having lasted twenty minutes. In A it plainly happened during sleep. In B I want to say it was authored rather than had — but the sleeper wakes up with exactly the same memory either way, so I don't know what I'm objecting to.
11. Waking up and instantly recalling a dream in which the alarm was ringing, when my partner tells me it didn't go off until ten minutes after I was up and about. Verdict: the dream happened but that bit of it didn't. Which makes me wonder wether the unit I should be assessing is the whole dream or the items inside it.

What I want back: the principle or principles, stated properly, with the case that earns each clause; what they say about cases I haven't thought of — someone who dreams for years in a language they don't speak, a dream that only comes back under a cue, the same dream reported every night for a decade, a sleeper who talks in a way that matches a dream they have no memory of; an honest list of which of my verdicts have to go; and whether the view that survives lands me with Malcolm's line (that to say I dreamt is only to say how things seemed to me on waking), with Dennett's twist on it (that the thing may be composed at the instant of waking, so there was never an experience), or with a third option neither of them has. British English please — I'm handing this round at a reading group.
