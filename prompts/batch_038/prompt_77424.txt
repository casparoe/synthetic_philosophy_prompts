You are a philosopher who works on the ethics of consent and on the reconstruction of informal arguments. Think carefully and reason at length before answering; I want diagnosis, not summary, and no citations, reading lists, or name-dropping — argue entirely from the concepts as I have laid them out.

**The situation.** In a bioethics seminar we are discussing whether healthy volunteers who are paid to enter Phase I drug trials can give valid consent. A classmate argued that they cannot. Her intended conclusion is narrow and, I think, respectable: that payment aimed at people with no other way to make rent does not produce consent worth honoring. But when I formalized what she said, the reasoning ran well past that, and she was surprised by her own argument. I cannot tell whether the over-generation is a defect in my reconstruction or a feature of the view, and I cannot find the joint that bears the weight.

**Two stipulations, used consistently below.** Call an agent's option set *closed* at a time when, holding fixed everything about her situation that she did not choose — her resources, her beliefs, her desires, her history, and the proposals currently on the table — there is exactly one option she will take. Call a transaction *extractive* when the party making the proposal stands to gain more from a constrained counterparty than she would stand to gain from the same counterparty unconstrained.

**The argument.**

(1) Consent is valid only if given from an open option set; consent given from a closed set is not the agent's own, and what is not the agent's own cannot authorize anything.

(2) Whether a set is closed is a question about the agent's situation alone, not about who arranged it: a set closed by illness, by poverty, by luck, or by another person's proposal is closed in exactly the same way and to exactly the same degree.

(3) Every agent's option set at every moment is closed, in the stipulated sense: her circumstances and her psychology jointly determine which option she takes.

(4) Therefore no consent is given from an open set — so, by (1), no consent is valid: not the trial volunteer's, not the surgical patient's, not the employee's, not the spouse's.

(5) And since almost every real transaction is extractive, (4) has a second consequence: employment contracts, ordinary purchases, and medical interventions are impermissible, because an act that requires authorization cannot be authorized by consent that fails to be consent.

(6) It follows that the concept of consent does no normative work whatsoever: it never alters what anyone may do, because nothing it is meant to authorize can be authorized by it.

(7) But a concept that is never satisfied is not a concept with a demanding standard; it is a concept we should stop using. So informed consent should be retired and replaced by whatever other device we use to mark permissible treatment.

**Where I am stuck.** My classmate denies (7) emphatically and wants to keep informed consent as the thing that makes treatment permissible; she also insists her conclusion was only about badly-off volunteers. I share both reactions. I also feel, though I cannot defend it, that (3) is just true and that (1) states the intuition I came to this topic for — the thought that a choice wholly fixed by forces external to the agent is not hers. So I would rather not give up (1) or (3), and I would rather give up almost anything else. Tell me what that preference costs.

**What I want from you.**
