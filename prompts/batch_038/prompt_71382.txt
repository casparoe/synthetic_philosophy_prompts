I am in the third year of a philosophy PhD, and for fourteen months I have been building the vignette set for my supervisor's project on whether lying and misleading differ morally and not only descriptively. Every vignette receives two codes from her: a classification (LIE, MISLEADING, or NEITHER) and a culpability score from 0 to 10, the latter assigned independently of the former. Before she codes the newest vignette, I have to commit in writing to a prediction of what she will say, so that we can tell whether the codebook is actually constraining her. I would like help reasoning about what her rule is.

Her seven codes so far:

1. A landlord asks his tenant whether anyone else has been living in the flat; the lease permits single occupancy only. She says no one has, though her partner has stayed four nights a week for two months, and she intends the landlord to believe the lease is being honoured. LIE, 8.
2. A company spokesperson asked whether a completed trial showed a cardiac signal replies that the trial was neither designed nor powered to detect cardiac events. This is true, and she intends reporters to conclude that no signal appeared, though one did. MISLEADING, 7.
3. A patient completing an intake form marks "no" for self-harm ideation, believing the truthful answer is "yes." She expects the intake psychiatrist to find the flagged record within the hour and wants only to avoid being questioned in front of the waiting room; she has no view about what the clinician ends up believing. LIE, 2.
4. A nurse tells a dying patient who has read his own chart that he is going to get through this, believing it false and intending only to comfort him rather than to alter his beliefs; he half-believes her anyway. LIE, 0.
5. Matched pair. A buyer asks a seller whether a car has been in an accident. Variant A: the seller says it has not, believing that false. LIE, 8. Variant B: the seller says he has never filed a claim, believing that true and knowing there was an unreported accident. MISLEADING, 7. Same buyer, same question, same intent, same harm.
6. An estate-sale buyer realises the seller thinks a vase is a worthless reproduction, says nothing, and pays the sticker price. NEITHER, 6.

Cases 3 and 4 have persuaded me she is no friend of intent-based definitions, though I grant that a broadened account, on which intending to keep someone from the truth counts as intending to deceive, might try to absorb case 3. Beyond that I am stuck. I can see a bare-content rule, on which asserting what you believe false is lying whatever the setting, and I can see some species of assurance or warrant rule, on which what matters is whether the speaker's word was actually on offer, or whether this hearer was in a position to take it up; I suspect those come apart, and I suspect the culpability scores are tracking something else again, perhaps whether the hearer's own agency was usurped rather than whether a guarantee was broken. The one-point gap in the matched pair is, so far, her entire answer to the question the project exists to answer.

Here is the new vignette, which I drafted and she has not seen. A departmental planning meeting is governed by standing terms of reference, distributed in writing at the start of the academic year but not read aloud, stating that all figures and projections in these meetings are provisional and non-binding. The dean tells a first-year postdoc, who has never seen the terms and whom she knows has never seen them, that there is funding for his line for at least three more years. She believes this is false: the money has been redirected and she has signed off on the reallocation, which nonetheless still requires committee ratification. She intends him to plan around her statement, and he does, withdrawing from two other searches. Eleven months later the ratification passes and the line disappears. She could have produced the same belief with a literally true statement — that the reallocation is not final until the committee ratifies it — delivered in the same tone.

What I need from you:

- Your prediction of her classification and culpability score for the dean's statement, and of the score she would assign to the true alternative she could have used instead.
- An explicit reconstruction of the candidate rules her seven codes are jointly consistent with. At least two must survive all seven and diverge on the eighth; state what each predicts for both the classification and the two scores.
- Your best guess as to which she is running, and which of the seven codes you take to be carrying the evidential weight.
- One further vignette, statable in two or three sentences, that would discriminate the survivors even after we see her code on this one, together with the code each rule commits her to on it.
- Finally: does her pattern treat the difference between lying and misleading as one of kind or one of degree, and does the eighth vignette put that answer at risk?
