I am putting together a session for a law-and-humanities seminar on the paradox of self-amendment, and I want to anchor it in Shirley Jackson's "The Lottery" — not in the usual register of scapegoating, conformity, or mob psychology, but as a story about a legal order that has lost the capacity to alter its own constitutive rule. I would like a critical reading along those lines, argued rather than merely suggestive.

Here is the scaffolding I want the reading to be measured against. Alf Ross contended that a constitutional amendment clause cannot be validly used to amend itself, because the new clause would have to draw its validity from a premise affirming the old one while its conclusion denies that premise — a species of legal self-reference he took to be structurally akin to the liar, and one that leaves a supreme rule of change either immutable or paradoxical. Hart's answer, in essence, is that this misconstrues legal validity as though it were a truth predicate and amendment as though it were a deduction: at the foundation of a legal system there is no further rule, only a rule of recognition that officials and community actually accept, and a successor clause becomes valid when it comes to be accepted as such. Between those two positions I want to press a third possibility, for which I will coin a term. Call a legal order change-blind when its constitutive rule is unalterable neither because amendment is forbidden nor because the amending procedure is self-defeating, but because the order contains no procedure, no office, and no vocabulary in which a proposal to alter the rule could even be framed. Change-blindness, on this account, is not a logical puzzle at all; it is the disappearance of the question.

Now the materials in the story that I think matter: the original paraphernalia lost long ago, and the black box now in use older than Old Man Warner; the story that the present box was assembled from pieces of the box that preceded it, which was built when the first people settled down to make a village; Mr. Summers raising a new box every year and the subject being allowed to fade without anything being done; the successful substitution of slips of paper for the chips of wood used for generations, justified by the growth of the population; the recital and the salute that have lapsed while the drawing survives; Warner on his seventy-seventh lottery and his contempt for the north village where they talk of giving the lottery up; and Tessie Hutchinson's protests, first that her husband was not given enough time to choose and finally that the whole business is unfair.

With that in hand, please address four things:

1. Does the village have a rule of change, and if so, what is it? Account for the difference between the substitution of paper for wood, which happens, and the replacement of the box, which is unthinkable. Is that difference about the identity of the rule — Jackson gives us a box literally pieced together from its predecessor — or about who is permitted to propose alterations, or about something else?
2. Test my coinage against the story. Is this order change-blind, or does the paper-slip amendment prove it is not? If the village is not change-blind, specify what the rule of change is and why the box is exempt from it; if it is, explain why Mr. Summers can nonetheless keep proposing and keep losing.
3. Do Warner's and Tessie's appeals map onto anything in the Ross–Hart exchange? My hunch is that Warner speaks from outside the rule while Tessie appeals to its correct application, but I am not confident, and I would rather you argue the point than adopt it. Note in particular that Tessie objects to the conduct of the drawing and only at the end to the fairness of the institution, and say whether that sequence carries jurisprudential weight.
4. Where does the story resist this reading? Be candid about the passages doing other work — the ordinary summer morning, the domestic bickering, the children gathering stones, the flatness of the narration — and say what a nomic reading flattens or loses. Then, if you think it survives, say what Jackson shows about rule-change that Ross and Hart do not: whether the horror lies in a rule that cannot be amended, or in a community that has lost the concept of its rule as the sort of thing that could be.

Two constraints. Please do not quote Jackson verbatim; describe scenes and paraphrase, since neither of us has the text in front of us, and I would rather have accurate paraphrase than confident misquotation. And write for readers who know the story well but have never read jurisprudence, which means Ross and Hart need to be explained in the course of the argument rather than name-dropped. Something on the order of fifteen hundred words would be right.
