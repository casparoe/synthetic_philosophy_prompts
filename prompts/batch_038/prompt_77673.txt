I've been running a small well-being reading group for about two years now and we're completely stuck on one question, to the point where I'm starting to suspect the question itself is malformed. The question: what do all the pleasures actually have in common?

Here's the shape of the standoff.

Camp one: hedonic tone. Pleasure is one simple, repeatable quality that gets spread across wildly different sensations, the way redness shows up in both a firetruck and a rose. So the taste of a cold plum, the good-kind-of-ache from stretching after sitting too long, and the glow of finishing a hard piece of work all share an ingredient even though the sensations carrying it look nothing alike.

Camp two: introspection says otherwise. Go looking and you find a bunch of feelings of very different character and no shared ingredient at all — basically Sidgwick's complaint in the Methods of Ethics, and part of why he slides into talking about desirable states of consciousness instead of a single felt quality. From there some people make Feldman's move: pleasure isn't a feeling at all but an attitude, being pleased that p, with intrinsic attitudinal pleasure doing the value work. Others go further and deny pleasure is any kind of episode (Ryle), or cash it out causal-functionally: pleasure is whatever state tends to make you want the experience to keep going, or whatever the reward machinery produces.

And now we have a third person who thinks all of this is armchair navel-gazing that affective neuroscience has already answered. Wanting and liking come apart — dopamine versus opioid systems, hedonic hotspots, addicts who keep craving after the pleasure is gone, anhedonia cases where what's missing is the wanting rather than the capacity to like — so either pleasure is a mechanistic core or it's nothing, and the felt-quality people are just describing what that core feels like from the inside.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis I'd like you to attack: the camps aren't disagreeing about the world at all. They're answering two different questions under one word — what unifies our ordinary concept of pleasure, where introspection is decent evidence, and what unifies the natural phenomenon, where introspection is close to worthless. Plus a hidden third disagreement about the paradigm case: if your exemplar is a momentary sensory episode like tasting a peach you think in felt qualities, and if it's being absorbed in a long conversation you think in attitudes, and nobody noticed they picked their exemplar first. Related thought: maybe pleasure is a jade-kind — jadeite and nephrite, two mineralogically distinct things answering to one trade name — rather than an essence-having natural kind or a Wittgensteinian grab bag. If so the heterogeneity problem dissolves instead of getting solved.

What I want is a diagnosis, not a survey. I know the positions already; I want to know what kind of disagreement this actually is. Verbal? Substantive but badly described? Two questions sharing a name? A hidden empirical question (is there a common mechanism — and would a yes or a no settle anything for the ethics side)? A dispute about which cases count as data, or about whether introspection is admissible evidence at all? Or bedrock intuitions nobody can argue for? Work through the candidate diagnoses explicitly and rule them in or out with reasons drawn from what each side actually argues, not from labels. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, an experiment, a formal or functional result, a change of question, a new paradigm case — or, if nothing would, make that case and explain why the impasse is stable. And give me the strongest version of the diagnosis you reject, plus why you reject it.

Think carefully, and brainstorm the menu of possible diagnoses before you commit to one. Answer as a philosopher of mind and value theory who has actually taught this literature, not as a study guide. Also please be explicit about separating what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this — if something I sketched above is a minority position or an open question, say so rather than letting it pass as consensus, and flag anywhere you're extrapolating past what the literature really supports.
