Okay, weird request but bear with me. I'm a backend engineer, zero philosophy or lit background, and my wife's book club let me tag along — we're doing The Great Gatsby next month. I actually read it for the first time since high school and the thing that wouldn't leave my head was how much of it is just... signaling. Not in the fuzzy "rich people showing off" sense, in the actual animal-behavior sense. I went down a rabbit hole on the handicap principle afterwards and I want help building a real reading of the book with it, not a SparkNotes one.

Here's what I think I understand, please correct me where I'm mangling it:

- Zahavi's handicap idea is that the peacock's tail is honest *because* it's wasteful — only a genuinely fit bird can afford to carry it.
- Grafen did the math on it in the 90s and the key condition is that the cost has to bite harder on a low-quality signaler than a high-quality one, otherwise everybody signals and it means nothing.
- Maynard Smith and Harper split signals into handicaps, which are reliable because they're expensive, and indices, which are reliable because they physically can't be faked — a big toad croaks deep because it's big, end of story.
- And Dawkins and Krebs, in the 70s I think, were skeptical that any of this is information transfer at all and preferred to treat it as manipulation, the signaler just pressing buttons in the reciever's head.

Gatsby's parties, the mansion directly across the bay, the shirts, the yellow car — that all reads to me as textbook handicapping. Enormous public waste, and the waste is the point, because a bootlegger with cash can buy the house but can't buy whatever it is he's actually trying to buy.

So what I want is the disciplined mapping, and then the places it falls apart:

1. Which of Gatsby's spending is a handicap and which is an index, and does that distinction do any work in the novel? My hunch is East Egg is all indices and West Egg is all handicaps, and Tom beats Gatsby not by signaling better but by not having to signal at all — his position is unfakeable in a way Gatsby's can never be. Is that right or am I forcing it?

2. Run the cost condition on Gatsby and tell me whether it's even satisfied. This is where I got stuck. The whole theory assumes the cost is what makes the signal honest, but Gatsby's money comes from Wolfsheim and the bootlegging and the bond thing he floats past Nick, which means the resources paying for the signal were acquired by the exact kind of behavior the signal is supposed to certify against. Can you apply a marginal-cost condition to a signaler whose quality is fabricated? Does that make it a dishonest signal in the technical sense, or something the model just doesn't cover?

3. The shirt scene. Daisy cries. She's from the money class herself and married into more of it, so she already knows he's rich — the shirts tell her nothing she didn't have. If Dawkins and Krebs are right that this is manipulation rather than information, does that scene support them? Does it matter that the receiver is *moved* instead of *updated*?

4. The one that actually broke my brain: the green light and the five years. Those are the most expensive things Gatsby ever pays — he invents a whole new person and burns five years of his life on one woman — and Daisy never observes any of it. Every signaling model I read assumes a receiver who perceives the cost. What is a cost nobody sees? Is it a signal at all, or is the novel sitting on something the theory has no slot for?
