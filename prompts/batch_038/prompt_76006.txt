I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine, and I am currently reporting a long feature on how appeals to natural hierarchy have migrated from political theory into contemporary debates about who should decide things — algorithmic management, technocratic expertise, the rhetoric of competence. Late in the reporting process one of my sources, an economist who is not a classicist, told me on the record that Aristotle had simply asserted that some people are born to be owned, and that the whole of Book I of the *Politics* is therefore of no philosophical interest, only historical. My editor wants that claim either substantiated or corrected before the piece runs, and I would rather not have a scholar write to us afterward saying that I got the argument wrong in the other direction. So I want to work through the key passage myself, carefully, with your help. I have no Greek, and I am working from Benjamin Jowett's nineteenth-century translation, in the second chapter of Book I, where Aristotle is building up the household out of its elementary pairings:

> In the first place there must be a union of those who cannot exist without each other; namely, of male and female, that the race may continue (and this is a union which is formed, not of deliberate purpose, but because, in common with other animals and with plants, mankind have a natural desire to leave behind them an image of themselves), and of natural ruler and subject, that both may be preserved. For that which can foresee by the exercise of mind is by nature intended to be lord and master, and that which can with its body give effect to such foresight is a subject, and by nature a slave; hence master and slave have the same interest. Now nature has distinguished between the female and the slave. For she is not niggardly, like the smith who fashions the Delphian knife for many uses; she makes each thing for a single use, and every instrument is best made when intended for one and not for many uses. But among barbarians no distinction is made between women and slaves, because there is no natural ruler among them: they are a community of slaves, male and female. Wherefore the poets say, "It is meet that Hellenes should rule over barbarians;" as if they thought that the barbarian and the slave were by nature one.

What puzzles me is not the offensiveness of the conclusion, which needs no explanation, but the mechanics of the argument, and specifically whether my source is right that nothing is being argued at all. Several things strike me as doing more work than they look like they should. The foresight-versus-body distinction is announced rather than defended, yet everything downstream seems to hang on it, including the inference that master and slave therefore have the same interest — and I cannot tell whether that "hence" is supposed to follow from the division of capacities or from the prior claim that the two are components of a single household that must be preserved together, nor whether the shared interest is a claim about what benefits them or about how they actually feel. The Delphian knife comparison seems aimed at separating the female from the slave rather than at establishing that slaves exist by nature, which leaves me unsure what it is contributing. The final sentence is stranger still: Aristotle appears to be criticizing barbarians and their poets for running the two categories together, which reads almost like a rebuke to the very people who would enslave foreigners, and yet the passage is routinely read as licensing exactly that. I would also like to know whether anything here gives a slaveholder a way of being mistaken — whether the passage contains any test for identifying a natural slave that could come out negative — or whether the definition is drawn so that whoever is in fact enslaved counts as naturally so. And please tell me where Jowett's English is doing interpretive work that I should not lean on; words like niggardly and foresight may be standing in for something narrower or broader in the original, and I would rather flag that uncertainty than assert a reading I cannot support. One constraint: do not send me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to secondary literature, since I cannot check them and they will not help me. Reason from the passage and from Aristotle's own framework as you understand it, and say plainly when you are unsure rather than when a scholarly consensus would settle the point.

Please organize your answer under the following headings, in this order and at roughly these lengths: **What the passage is doing** (about 200 words, situating the excerpt inside Aristotle's account of how the household is assembled and what role the ruler–subject pairing plays in it); **The argument, step by step** (about 300 words, a numbered reconstruction that marks explicitly which steps are argued, which are merely asserted, and which are assumed from elsewhere in his framework); **The load-bearing moves** (about 350 words, taking in turn the same-interest inference, the Delphian knife comparison, and the closing remark about barbarians and the poets, and saying for each what it is meant to establish and whether it manages to establish it); **Could a slaveholder be wrong?** (about 250 words, on whether the passage supplies any criterion capable of failing, and on the difference between natural slavery as a theoretical category and the practice Aristotle's contemporaries actually engaged in); **Translation hazards** (about 150 words, on where Jowett's wording may mislead a reader who relies on it); and **Bottom line for my editor** (about 100 words, a single paragraph stating whether my source's dismissal is fair, unfair, or partly fair, phrased so that I could paraphrase it in print without misrepresenting either side).
