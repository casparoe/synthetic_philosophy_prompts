I am a novelist working on a book whose central device is a variation on the experience machine, and as far as I can tell the variation is one the philosophy of well-being has not been asked to handle in quite this shape. In my book the machine is a municipal service rented by the hour from a booth, used principally by shift workers and the bereaved, and sessions are capped, so that nobody ever emigrates. What happens instead is that a regular accumulates a single continuous inner life across hundreds of ninety-minute visits — a marriage, a vocation, a bereavement of her own — lived in fragments, with the intervals spent in an outer life that is decent, undramatic, and fully remembered. My characters thus never choose between a real life and a simulated one; they choose between two lives, one of which arrives in pieces.

I have roughly four months before I must lock the manuscript's structure, and the reading capacity of an intelligent non-specialist rather than a seminar. Which open questions in the theory of well-being are genuinely load-bearing for a book built on that premise, and which should I deliberately leave alone? Please rank three to five and defend the ordering rather than survey the field: why each question bears specifically on a life assembled discontinuously rather than on permanent escape, what my premise does to the standard arguments for hedonism, desire-satisfaction, and objective-list accounts, and what in the novel would come apart if the question were resolved the other way.

I also want the negative half. Which of the stock moves in this debate would be decorative or dishonest in my hands — treating the intuition against plugging in as though it refuted hedonism outright, invoking authenticity and contact with reality as though they settled anything on their own, or leaning on the experimental work suggesting that reluctance to plug in reflects a bias toward the status quo rather than a preference for the real?

Throughout, please separate what is settled among people who work on well-being from what remains genuinely contested, and do not hand me a minority view dressed as the consensus. Where my premise quietly depends on a contested position, identify it and tell me how much weight it can carry before the book's moral architecture begins to sag.
