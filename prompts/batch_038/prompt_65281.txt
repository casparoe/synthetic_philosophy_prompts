Okay, here's a thing in the restitution fights that I can't get a clean read on.

The big encyclopedic museums have basically converged on the same workaround for contested objects: no permanent handover, but a long-term renewable loan — the thing goes to the country that's asking for it, stays for years at a time, gets renewed. And the precondition that reportedly kills the negotiations isn't conservation capacity, isn't money, isn't insurance or transport. It's that the borrower has to acknowledge in writing that the lending museum holds legal title. Greece has said more or less consistently that it will not sign anything recognising British ownership of the Parthenon sculptures, and that's where things jam.

Berlin ran it the other way round in 2022: the Ethnologisches Museum signed ownership of its Benin objects over to Nigeria, and something like a third of them stayed put in Berlin, now on loan from Nigeria, on show in the Humboldt Forum for an initial ten years. Same category of object, same era of removal, opposite order of operations.

So what does the loan-plus-title-clause package actually presuppose? Reconstruct the theory of property sitting underneath it. Is ownership being treated as a bundle that can be split indefinitely — possession over there, title over here — with the ledger doing all the normative work while the object's location does none? And what does it presuppose about the *wrong*? If the harm of 1897 were about displacement, desecration, sovereignty, or the loss of a context that made the object intelligible, then better custody doesn't seem to touch it at all. The clause looks like it assumes the wrong is a property wrong, curable only by a transfer of title, which makes every arrangement short of transfer a favour rather than a remedy — and turns the claimant country into a tenant in its own heritage, on terms that are renewable, i.e. revocable.

Which rival accounts does it quietly rule out — shared or plural title, stewardship without ownership, the view that what matters is where the object's meaning is legible to the people whose ancestors made it? Test it for internal consistency against the other things the same institutions say and do: the collection belongs to the world, they borrow from each other constantly without anyone making a fuss about title, and provenance research on new acquisitions typically runs off a 1970 cutoff. Do those hang together with the title clause, or is there tension?

Then separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with. Lots of theories could license the loan workaround; can any of them license the clause? If not, what is the clause for — precedent management, a way of converting a moral claim into a contractual concession? Tell me what that would imply about whether the museum thinks these claims are governed by a principle at all. And finish with what would concretely have to change if you replaced the presupposed view with a stewardship model where nobody holds exclusive title: cataloguing and deaccession rules, statutory bars on disposal, insurance, who gets to veto a loan, what a wall label would have to say. One of the arguements I keep hearing is that dropping the clause would open the floodgates — work out whether that's a property claim or something else wearing a property costume.
