I have no academic background in philosophy. I left school at sixteen and have spent the last several years reading Popper on my own, slowly and out of order, together with whatever I can find about the disputes he got into. One thread has gripped me completely: his change of heart about Darwinism. I want to work out what he would have made of an argument that is being made now, in his name, by people defending evolutionary theory. I am not after references or a reading list; I want the reasoning.

Here is the record I have assembled, in my own words, of how he ruled on particular theories.

1. Einstein's theory of gravity: science, and the best kind. It forbade something — if starlight grazing the sun had not bent by the amount calculated, the theory would have been finished. It exposed itself.
2. Freud's psychoanalysis and Adler's individual psychology: not science, whatever their human interest. Any conduct whatever could be redescribed as confirming them; no possible behaviour by a child counted against them.
3. Marxism as a theory of history: scientific at the start, because it made predictions that might have come out false. But when one of them failed, the theory was not abandoned; it was fitted out with auxiliary adjustments until the failure could be recounted as a success. From then on he treated it as a myth that had immunised itself against experience.
4. Newtonian mechanics: science, and its overthrow by Einstein was a victory for the method rather than a mark against it. A bold theory that gets refuted outranks a cautious one that survives.
5. Darwinism by natural selection, as he judged it in the mid-seventies: not an empirical theory. His complaint, roughly, was that fitness is always read off from whoever in fact survived and reproduced, so the central claim reduces to saying that the survivors survived. He was willing to call it a metaphysical research programme — fertile, respectable, worth more than the positivists allowed — while insisting its standing differed from Einstein's.
6. A few years later he withdrew that charge openly. He said he had changed his mind about the testability and the status of the theory, that the tautology objection had been a mistake, and that he had underrated its predictive content. But he held on to a residue: his remaining difficulty was how a purely selective process could generate genuinely new possibilities — new organs, new levels of organisation, new problems to be solved — when selection can only sift among variations already present, and he did not see the theory explaining where the novelty comes from.
7. In physics he championed an interpretation of probability as a real tendency or disposition belonging to a single situation, a propensity, rather than as a tally of how often things turn out. Propensities are never observed, yet he counted the theory containing them as science, apparently because they sit inside a mathematical framework that is severely tested.

Now the new case. Biologists and some philosophers of biology now answer the circularity charge of entry 5 with an argument that looks, to me, like a direct borrowing of entry 7. Fitness, they say, is a propensity: a real disposition of an organism in a given environment, comparable to the fragility of a glass or the decay tendency of an unstable atom, and not a summary of who happened to leave offspring. The practice that goes with it is this. A researcher measures a variant's traits and its surroundings and assigns it a selection coefficient before the breeding season begins; the population is then censused and the change in frequency compared with what the coefficient predicted; agreement is reported as confirmation of the estimate. Pressed on circularity, the researcher replies that the tendency was specified independently of the outcome, from morphology and ecology, so the outcome cannot be what makes the claim true. When the numbers disagree, the coefficient is re-estimated in the light of new facts about the environment, predation, or density.

Please do four things. First, lay out the rules that are jointly consistent with all seven entries above — I suspect at least three fit everything, including a rule about what counts as an admissible rescue, a rule about whether a theory's terms must be specifiable before the facts they explain, and a rule about unobservable posits embedded in tested mathematics — and say where each is under strain. Second, predict his verdict on the propensity reading of fitness, naming which entry is the closest analogue and whether the residue he kept in entry 6 changes anything about that verdict. Third, tell me what his verdict commits him to: if he accepts the propensity reading, which of his own earlier refusals does he have to soften, and if he rejects it, why entry 7 is not equally vulnerable. Fourth, propose one or two further cases that would separate the candidate rules, and say whether either of these would do it — a coefficient estimated from controlled laboratory measurements and then used to predict a frequency shift in the wild that nobody had yet observed, versus a coefficient fitted after the census and revised each time it misses.

Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restating of my question.
