I am a third-year DPhil student in Philosophy, and this term I am marking the unseen examination script for a final-year undergraduate module on the first part of Parfit's *Reasons and Persons*. There are forty scripts; this is the first one I have opened, and I want to calibrate my standard against an independent reading before I work through the rest. The examination is a two-hour, closed-book paper in which candidates answer two questions from a list of six, and no texts are permitted, so the complete absence of quotations and page references below is entirely expected and must not count against the candidate.

The question the candidate answered was:

> **Question 3.** Explain Parfit's case of the hitchhiker, and explain what Parfit takes that case to show about the Self-interest Theory. In what sense, if any, does Parfit think the hitchhiker's predicament has a solution? Explain what Parfit means by the claim that it can be rational to be irrational. (100 marks)

The script runs to roughly 820 words. I have numbered the paragraphs so that comments can be keyed to them.

1. Parfit's Self-interest Theory, which he abbreviates to S, holds that what each of us has most reason to do is whatever will best achieve our own long-term self-interest, and that it is irrational to act against this. S is not merely one theory among others for Parfit: in the first part of the book he asks which of the competing theories of rationality is the best, and one way of settling that question is to show that a theory is self-defeating, meaning that if we accepted it and tried to follow it, things would go worse by the theory's own standard. This is an internal form of criticism, since it does not appeal to values that the theory's defender rejects.

2. The hitchhiker is meant to be such a case. Suppose that I am stranded in a desert, and that I shall die unless someone drives me to town. A driver stops, but he is willing to take me only if I first promise to send him one hundred dollars once I arrive. Parfit stipulates that this is a one-off encounter: I shall never see the driver again, and nobody will ever learn whether I kept my word. Making the promise is obviously in my interest, and the driver duly takes me to town. But once I am in town the situation has changed. The ride is in the past, and nothing I do now can affect it; sending the money will leave me simply worse off, so S tells me not to send it. Moreover I could have foreseen all of this while I was still in the desert. Parfit adds that I am transparent, in the sense that the driver can see through me and tell whether my promise is sincere. Since he knows that an S-rational agent in my position would not pay, he concludes that I shall not pay, and drives off. The agent who tries to follow S is left in the desert, and does worse than an agent who follows some other theory.

3. What the case shows, on Parfit's account, is that S is directly collectively self-defeating. This is the same diagnosis that he offers for the Prisoner's Dilemma and for the many-person dilemmas elsewhere in this part of the book: in each, if everyone does what S tells them to do, everyone ends up worse off than they would have been had everyone done something else. That is what makes the objection decisive rather than merely practical. A theory of rationality is supposed to guide us towards what we have most reason to do, and a theory which, when generally followed, systematically frustrates the very aim it sets us cannot be the best theory on offer. Nor can S's defender reply that this is somebody else's fault, since the failure arises precisely from everyone's doing their best to comply.

4. Parfit's own response is to change the level at which we assess. What is rational is not to be settled act by act alone: we should also ask what it is rational for me to want to be like. It is better for me to be someone who is disposed to keep such promises, someone who would send the money even when sending it does me no good, than to be someone who never makes such promises at all, since only the former sort of person is picked up in the desert. So I have most reason to acquire and retain a disposition that leads me to perform acts which S, assessing them one at a time, calls irrational. This is what Parfit means by saying that it can be rational to be irrational.

5. It is worth being clear, however, that none of this requires S to be abandoned. Once the case is described in full, the S-rational agent does have a reason to pay: he has an interest in being known as someone who keeps his word, and that interest is worth far more to him over a lifetime than a hundred dollars. Parfit's stipulation that nobody will ever learn whether he pays is what generates the apparent paradox, and it is a stipulation that no real agent can rely upon, since we are transparent and our dispositions are visible to everyone we deal with. So a properly informed S-rational agent pays, and S survives without amendment; what Parfit has really shown is that the crude act-by-act presentation of S, on which each act is assessed in isolation from the character that produces it, is not the version of the theory worth defending.

6. The same line of thought dissolves what looks at first like a parallel difficulty in Kavka's toxin puzzle, in which an eccentric offers me a large sum if I intend, at midnight tonight, to drink a mildly unpleasant toxin tomorrow afternoon. It is said that I cannot form that intention, since once the money is in my account I shall have no reason to drink the toxin, and one cannot intend to do what one has no reason to do. But if the proper objects of rational assessment are dispositions rather than acts, there is nothing here to explain: the intention just is the disposition, and the disposition is worth having because of what it gets me. Parfit's moral is therefore a general one about the structure of rationality, and it applies wherever a reward is attached to a state of mind rather than to an act.

7. In sum, the hitchhiker case shows that S is self-defeating, and that the way out of the desert is to become the sort of person whom a driver would pick up, a conclusion Parfit reaches without giving up the idea that rationality is a matter of pursuing one's own interests well.
