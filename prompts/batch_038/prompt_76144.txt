I'm a backend engineer — roughly nine years building data infrastructure for hospitals and insurers — with no formal philosophy training; what I know I've picked up by reading on my own. I'd like an essay-length audit of an analogy that surfaced in a data-governance meeting and that I suspect is doing far more work than its users realize.

The setup: one of the products my team maintains still exposes a race column — a small fixed enum, populated from patient intake forms, referenced by a few dozen dependent tables, reporting jobs, disparity dashboards, and a reimbursement ruleset. A staff engineer defended keeping it with what I'll call the legacy-column analogy: nobody alive chose these values, they don't carve the world at any joint, and the definitions differ from system to system and era to era — but the field is load-bearing, and you cannot drop the column without breaking everything downstream. On that basis he said the field is real enough for any purpose worth arguing about, and that constructionist talk (race isn't a biological kind; it's produced and reproduced by social practice) is either false or beside the point. A second colleague ran the same analogy to the opposite conclusion: precisely because the values are arbitrary historical accidents whose dependencies grew up around them after the fact, this is exactly the sort of thing you plan a migration away from, and the only reason we haven't is that the cost of the migration lands on people who aren't in the room.

I want the analogy audited rather than the policy. Specifically:

1. Lay out the mapping explicitly; a table is fine. For each element of the source — the column, its declared values, the rows, the dependents and foreign keys, the engine that enforces the type, whoever holds write access to the schema, the migration script, the documentation — say what corresponds to it in the social kind, or say that nothing does.

2. Sort the disanalogies into those that matter for the argument being made and those that are surface noise. At minimum I'd like a ruling on each of these:
   - Writing a value into the column leaves the rest of the row untouched; classifying a person seems to change the person classified — how they are treated, and eventually how they describe themselves. Can the enum picture absorb that by adding a trigger, or is reflexivity a difference in kind rather than degree?
   - Enum values change only when someone with schema-write access commits a change: one dated event, one script, one rollback plan. Racial boundaries have shifted across jurisdictions and decades with no such event and no identifiable authority to sign off. Is that just a great many unlogged schema changes?
   - A row cannot set its own value, and where self-report and stored value disagree we say one of them is simply wrong. Intake forms let people self-identify; ascription by others does not track it. Does the analogy assume a single field where there may be two different things going on?
   - Plenty of legacy columns in our systems are causally load-bearing and are also conceded by everyone to be proxy garbage. Does "load-bearing" get my colleague to the sense of real that he wants, or does it slide from has-effects to correctly-describes-something? Is the sense of real that this analogy can deliver the one a constructionist denies, or the one she asserts?
   - A migration presupposes a specified target schema and that the data survives the move. Is there anything here that plays the role of dropping the column, and what would have to be true of the social world for that operation to be well-defined rather than a rewrite in which the rows do not survive?

3. Tell me what the analogy imports that the target lacks — a designer, a specification, an enforcing engine, a clean separation between schema and rows — and also where it performs better than its users claim. Path dependence and lock-in genuinely do seem to be the right shape for this phenomenon, and I don't want a one-sided audit.

4. Close with a verdict on each of the two conclusions: which, if either, survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and whether either one rests on the analogy or on independent argument. If the analogy cannot be repaired, propose one or two better ones and say what each costs.

Please don't give me a reading list, bibliography, or citations — I won't be able to look anything up, so the essay has to stand on its own. If you attach a name or a label to a position, flag how confident you are in the attribution; I'd much rather you describe a view than misattribute it. Define terms of art on first use, but don't simplify the reasoning: I read design documents for a living.
