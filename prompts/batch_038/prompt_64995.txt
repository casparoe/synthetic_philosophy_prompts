I am three-quarters through a novel whose third section is a city council debate about a bronze equestrian statue in a river-town square. The book is set in an invented country whose civil war was fought, on one side, to preserve a system of bonded labor; the general on the plinth, the square, and the eleven years of protests against it are all my inventions, so nothing below depends on the historical record. What it does depend on is the argument my councillor makes, which has to be good enough that a reader cannot dismiss her as a zealot or a fraud. I tried to write it out rigorously, the way she would if she were being honest with herself, and the result runs considerably past the conclusion she wants. I would like to know where to cut.

(1) A public monument honors its subject; that is what separates a monument from a record. To set a person on a plinth in a square, in bronze, at civic expense, is for the city to say that this person merits esteem.

(2) Civic esteem is not divisible. A city cannot honor a man's conduct of a campaign while withholding honor from the man. What is honored is a whole person, and the public receives it as a whole person.

(3) A man who took up arms to preserve a system of bonded labor merits condemnation rather than esteem.

(4) Therefore the statue must come down.

(5) The same reasoning reaches names. A street, a school, a bridge, or a library wing named for a person is a monument with the bronze left out: it asserts the same thing, and the city reasserts it every time it prints a map, mails a bill, or dispatches an ambulance.

(6) What a monument asserts is fixed when it is made. Its content is the message it was erected to carry, and later audiences cannot re-authorize it by reading it otherwise, nor can the city launder it by adding text. A plaque is a second statement, not a correction of the first.

(7) My opponents will say the square's meaning has drifted, because the statue has now been argued with for eleven years. But an object that means whatever its current audience takes it to mean is not a monument; it is weather. If content floated with the crowd, the city could never be said to have honored anyone, and no removal campaign could ever have a grievance to bring.

(8) By the standards (3) applies, nearly everyone commemorated by a public name in a city old enough to have names worth arguing about is disqualified: they owned people, or ordered people killed, or wrote about women, or about Jews, or about the Irish, things we now call by their proper names.

(9) So by (5), (6), and (8), nearly every honorific name in the city must go; and the replacements do not settle it, because the next honorees will be drawn from the same history and judged by standards still being written, so the generation after ours will run this argument against them. There is no terminus.

(10) The consistent conclusion is that a city should stop naming public things after persons at all: numbers, coordinates, species of tree, or the names of the private dead.

(11) But that is absurd. A city that honors no one is not a better city, only a poorer one; it would have no public means of marking that anything had mattered, and it would have bought that silence by refusing to look at its own past rather than by judging it.

(12) Therefore something above is false.

I cannot tell you which, and I do not want you to take my word for (11) either — if you think the absurdity is only rhetorical, say so and tell me what a city loses or fails to lose by adopting (10). What I need is this: identify the first step that will not bear the weight placed on it, and explain why it looked unimpeachable when I set it down, since I set it down believing it. Then price the repairs. Cutting (2) seems to cost me (4), and I want to know exactly what has to be substituted if honor is to be conferred under a description — what criterion decides which aspects of a person are honor-conferring, and why that criterion does not simply hand my councillor's opponents the statue. Cutting (5) seems cheapest, and I want to know what it costs elsewhere, including whether it also disarms the city from naming anything new. Cutting (6) or (7) is the one I fear most, because the section turns on a plaque unveiling that fails in public; if I lose (6), I have to rewrite that scene so the failure is somebody's failure rather than the theory's. Tell me whether any repair preserves both (4) and a scene in which an explanatory plaque provably does not do what its authors hoped — and tell me, separately, whether (3) can be restricted without becoming arbitrary, since as it stands it seems to make one grave wrong forfeit every claim a person ever earned.
