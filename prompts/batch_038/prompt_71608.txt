I am assembling a set of positions on accuracy-first epistemology and want a credence, not a survey.

Consider this proposition, which I will call P: *For an agent with degrees of belief over a finite algebra of propositions and finitely many possible worlds, if those degrees of belief do not constitute a probability function on that algebra, then the agent is irrational, and that verdict follows from purely accuracy-theoretic considerations alone — that is, from the fact that some probabilistic alternative is more accurate at every world under a proper scoring rule, with no appeal anywhere to the agent's preferences, choices, or betting dispositions.*

Take the mathematical half as settled: I accept that the dominance result goes through in the standard finite setting. What I want a number on is the normative half, together with the claim that the derivation is genuinely non-pragmatic.

Here is the wrinkle that should shape your answer. Suppose an agent — call her Nadia — holds degrees of belief over a three-way partition, knows perfectly well that they are incoherent, and knows that probabilistic alternatives exist which beat them at every world on the Brier score. But every such dominating alternative assigns less than 0.05 to one of the three hypotheses, and Nadia holds, as a matter of substantive epistemic principle rather than squeamishness, that a floor of 0.05 on each live hypothesis is required of her, since she takes herself not to be in a position to discriminate among them so sharply. On her own view, the alternatives that dominate her are inadmissible. Does the accuracy argument establish that Nadia is irrational?

Please give me your credence in P, and decompose it. At minimum I want a probability, with one or two lines of justification each, for: (i) a non-arbitrary and uniquely correct measure of inaccuracy is available, rather than the choice among proper scoring rules and among ways of aggregating them over a partition being a free parameter that quietly does the normative work; (ii) dominance reasoning of this shape is genuinely non-pragmatic rather than decision theory with the labels repainted; (iii) the argument survives Nadia's problem about inadmissible dominating alternatives; (iv) accuracy is the sole fundamental epistemic value, rather than one value among several — resilience, safety against future evidence, explanatory coherence — any of which could defeat it in particular cases; and (v) "irrational" in P has determinate enough content for the proposition to have a truth value rather than merely expressing a theoretical preference. Add any further terms you think belong, including ones I have missed, and then show me how the terms combine into your overall figure — and if you think they do not combine multiplicatively, say how they do combine and why.

Then tell me which term your final number is most sensitive to: which single one, if I could convince you to move it by twenty points, would move your overall credence the furthest. And what argument or consideration would most change your mind about P as a whole?

For calibration of a different kind: my supervisor puts her credence in P at 0.8 and I put mine at 0.3. Given your decomposition, on which term is the disagreement between us most likely to live, and why?

One last thing, which I think is unavoidable here rather than cute. You are being asked to report degrees of belief, and on the accuracy-first picture those reports are themselves subject to exactly the norms under evaluation. Say briefly whether your own numbers, taken together, are coherent; whether you would count as irrational by the lights of P if they are not; and whether reflecting on that should adjust any of your estimates.

Finally, please be explicit about your confidence rather than letting the numbers imply more precision than they have: how stable is your overall figure — would it survive a day of reflection, or is it essentially a first-pass placement — and which single claim in your entire answer are you least sure of?
