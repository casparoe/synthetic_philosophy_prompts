I'm running the last session of a little reading group I help with — four second-years who've just finished the distributive justice material and are, frankly, a bit smug about it — and instead of handing them another paper I want to hand them a piece of satire they have to decode. Could you write me a fake Help Center article plus an attached support-ticket thread for a made-up SaaS company that licenses a priority-weighting engine to charities, hospital trusts and city councils? You feed it a list of beneficiaries and a cardinal number for how well off each of them is, and it scores proposed allocations with a concave, strictly increasing weight function, so the same benefit counts for more the worse off the person who gets it happens to be. It's prioritarianism sold as software. Give the company a grand, faintly misleading name, since the running gag in the ticket queue should be that egalitarians keep signing up expecting a product that cares about *gaps between people* and are then told, patiently, that no, the engine is not concerned with relations between persons at all and never was. Around 900–1100 words: the article first, then maybe five or six tickets with agent replies underneath.

The register should be deadpan support-documentation prose — courteous, slightly weary, completely straight-faced — and the humour has to come from the theory being right rather than from anyone being dumb. The article itself should be about the fact that the engine will never, under any setting, recommend a change that makes some people worse off and nobody better off, and it should say this with real corporate pride, because the rival vendor's product does exactly that and advertises it. Make sure the leveling-down objection gets stated accurately somewhere — including the awkward bit where you have to concede what the rival's own users have to concede about whether the leveled-down world is better all-things-considered, in any respect or only in some one way — and let the smugness about being immune to it curdle on its own, without anyone pointing at it. Then seed the pressure points into the tickets: a hospital wanting a "maximin mode" in which only the single worst-off patient counts, and the agent having to explain what the score does at ties; a council whose ranking flips when they re-report well-being on a different but equally defensible cardinal scale, and an evasive non-answer about which transformations the engine is invariant under; a customer requesting a "sufficiency mode" with a hard threshold and not understanding that it isn't what they already have; someone who pushes the same lottery through twice, weighting outcomes once and weighting expected prospects the other time, and gets two different verdicts; and one very smug ticket from a utilitarian insisting the whole product is just their own view with a monotone transform applied, asking what has actually been added. The agents should answer some of these badly — that's where the satire lands — but nothing in the piece may break the view's own commitments: Pareto holds throughout, nobody gets credit for relativity, weights track absolute level, and if the company ever slips into saying a distribution is just plain better, it should be the kind of slip a careful reader can catch and enjoy.

Keep it legible to people who've only had one term of this: if you strip the philosophy out it should still read like a plausible tech doc. No real philosophers, no real companies, no citations or bibliography or anything that needs looking up — invent everything. And no winking, please: nobody turns to camera, nobody says "obviously this is absurd," the company believes in itself entirely. The target is the practice of quantifying welfare well enough to hand it to a vendor, and the way an argument about equality gets conducted once it's been turned into product language — not any person, living or dead. Also I typed this on my phone between sessions so sorry for the run-ons, and if you think one of the tickets would be funnier as a feature-request thread than a complaint, go for it.
