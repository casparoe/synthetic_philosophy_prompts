My partner's Danish and I'm American, and every spring our taxes turn into the same argument. In March she logs into the tax authority's site and there's already a return sitting there: salary from her employer, interest from her bank, mortgage interest, pension contributions, all filled in from data those third parties report. She checks it, maybe adds a deduction, done. If she ignored it completely, the pre-filled version just becomes her assessment anyway — and from what she tells me, if the numbers were wrong and she didn't fix them, that's still on her, even though she never actually said anything. Norway does the same thing: miss the April deadline and the goverment's figures count as your filed return.

Meanwhile I sit down with W-2s and 1099s that my employers and my banks have *already sent to the IRS*, retype all of it onto a form, sign a declaration that it's true under penalty of perjury, and pay a private company for the privilege of doing the retyping, becuase there's a whole industry built inside that gap and it has fought like hell to keep the gap open.

So what does each setup presuppose? Not the folk story — I know the cheap version is "Scandinavians trust the state, Americans don't" — but what view of the relationship between a person and their government you have to hold for silence to count as agreement with the state's account of your financial life, while the state still holds you answerable for the accuracy of that account. Which rival views does it quietly rule out? Is this about trust at all, or about something else: who knows what about whom, who absorbs the cost of error, whether a tax return is even the kind of thing a person asserts?

Please be sharp about the difference between what each practice strictly requires and what it merely happens to sit comfortably with, and flag where the practice is underdetermined — spots where several incompatible theories would license exactly the same arrangement.

There's also something I can't make coherent: Europe wrote the strictest data-protection regime on earth and is permanently anxious about private firms profiling people, yet it's happy to let the tax office hold a complete dossier and let that dossier become your own legal statement. The US has no general privacy law, lets data brokers sell whatever they want, but treats the government knowing your salary as worse than that. Are those two postures compatible inside a single country, or does one have to give?

And then: if the American sign-it-under-perjury model is the defensible one, what would Denmark have to change? If the Nordic model is the defensible one, what would have to change in the US beyond the software — the legal machinery, I mean.
