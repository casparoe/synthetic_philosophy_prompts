I spent thirty-four years measuring things for a living — mostly diffusion and reaction kinetics in condensed matter, with a long detour into instrument calibration — and since retiring I have been reading philosophy with the seriousness of someone who has finally been given permission to. For the past two years I have facilitated a Saturday reading group at our public library. This month's session is two hours long, including a fifteen-minute break, with sixteen attendees of quite mixed preparation: three retired engineers, a hospice nurse, two secondary-school teachers, a pair of undergraduates, and the remainder general readers who are intelligent but have no tolerance for jargon. We sit at four tables of four. Paper, pencils and basic calculators only; no laptops, and the room has no display.

The session's question is whether the world is becoming less violent. I do not want a debate about that question, and I emphatically do not want anyone to hear a proper name — no author, no book, no dataset — until the final five minutes. I want the group to run into the difficulty themselves, in their hands, and to run into it early enough that they are still annoyed about it at the break.

To that end I have sketched a fictional archive, and I should like you to turn it into a working exercise. Suppose a river valley — call it the Vale — has continuous administrative records across three periods, roughly a century and a half apart. In the earliest period the population is small and the registers record violent death only for a narrow class of decedent, while also containing a category of killing that is legally sanctioned and therefore not counted as violence at the time. In the middle period the population has roughly doubled, the registers have widened, and a new category appears — grievous injury where the victim survives at least a season — that simply does not exist in the earlier record. In the late period the population has grown again several-fold, the sanctioned-killing category has been abolished and folded into ordinary killing, and two further categories have become visible that were previously invisible because nobody thought to record them: coercive confinement, and deprivation deliberately imposed by authorities. Recording coverage also improves steadily across the three periods, and this improvement is real but is never mentioned in the registers themselves.

The four tables are to be given four different mandates, fixed before they see any figures: total violent deaths regardless of population; violent deaths per ten thousand inhabitants; all harm events coded according to the late period's category definitions applied retroactively to the earlier records; and a harm-weighted index whose weights are set once, from the earliest period's own valuation of the categories, and never revised. Each table computes, reports a single verdict, and is then asked to state which of the other three verdicts is wrong and why. Nothing you write should let me adjudicate; I want to be able to say only that each table has followed its instructions correctly.

Please deliver the following. First, the actual figures: a printable one-page data sheet per table with the numbers you choose, plus a facilitator's key showing each table's arithmetic and its resulting ordering. Second, a check that the numbers do what I want — at least three of the four mandates must yield genuinely different answers about whether the Vale became more or less violent — and a candid statement if my sketch cannot in fact be made to produce that. Third, four mandate cards in verbatim language a layperson can follow without explanation. Fourth, a timed facilitator's script, with the questions I should ask and the questions I should refuse to answer. Fifth, a reveal to be held until after the break: the true recording-coverage fraction in each period, which the registers never state, chosen so that the verdict the group found most robust turns out to be the most fragile. Sixth, a closing device in which every participant privately writes whether they now think there is a determinate answer to the question about the Vale, with one sentence of justification, to be read aloud anonymously. Seventh, a debrief that maps particular moments of the exercise onto the general methodological choices it dramatizes, and connects those choices to how the same decisions are actually made with real historical and contemporary data — described in general terms only. Eighth, what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, including at least one failure that involves the more numerate attendees, and how to handle each. Ninth, an honest account of which feature of the real problem this exercise cannot reproduce, and where it quietly loads the dice toward the conclusion that the question has no answer, when a realist about measured quantities might reasonably resist that.

I would rather not have references or a reading list; the group cannot consult anything during the session and I find that suggested titles mostly go unread. Finally, please tell me how confident you are in the principal claims you make — above all in the arithmetic and in whether the numbers produce the intended disagreement rather than a coincidence — and single out the one point in the whole design about which you are least sure.
