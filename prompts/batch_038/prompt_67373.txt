For something like fifteen years I have been a reader of the rationalist blogs and their comment threads, and I have absorbed the house style thoroughly enough that it now runs on its own: steelman the opposing view, tabulate the cruxes, refuse to hold any position that cannot say what would change its mind. That habit is about to become a liability, because I have volunteered to run a session in which I must explain something rather than debate it.

The setting is a fortnightly evening seminar I have kept going for two years at the analytics practice where I work. Twelve regulars, almost all practising machine-learning engineers, plus two classically trained statisticians and one colleague with a philosophy master's who will be the most dangerous person in the room. Seventy-five minutes, a whiteboard, no assessment, and no obligation to cover anything beyond the single topic. The topic is the problem of old evidence: how a theory can be confirmed by a consequence it was constructed to fit, given that conditionalising on something to which you already assign probability one cannot move your credence in the theory at all.

They will arrive believing they settled this in practice long ago, and in a sense they have, because their entire discipline is built on withheld data. My own reading — the hypothesis I want the plan to test — is that what they possess is an institutional answer to a personal question. A holdout set, a preregistered protocol, a leaderboard, a code review: these are machinery for making a group's credences track evidence that individual members have already seen. They do not, without further argument, tell the single agent who already knows E how far E's being entailed by T ought to raise her credence in T. If that is right, the session's job is to prise the two apart and show where the seam runs. If it is wrong, I should learn so now rather than in front of the room.

What I want from you is the plan for the explanation, not the explanation. Specifically:

First, the sequence. Set out the order in which the components have to arrive and justify that order by stating what each one makes possible that was impossible before it. I am genuinely uncertain about three candidates for the opening slot: that credences are bound by the probability calculus in the first place; that conditionalisation is the only legitimate passage from one credence function to the next; and the difference between knowing E and knowing that T entails E. Tell me which is load-bearing first, which can be assumed from this audience, and which must be deferred until it can be earned.

Second, the vehicle. I am proposing to build everything on a model that has been tuned until it fits the data sitting in front of the person doing the tuning, and then to ask how much that fit confirms the model. Tell me what this case has to have and what it must conspicuously lack. I can already see one threat to it: the sharpest person in the room will say that a withheld test set is not old evidence at all, since the modeller never laid eyes on those points, and that their practice therefore faces prediction rather than this problem. If that objection holds, does the case survive by moving to the second iteration, where the same withheld set has been consulted a dozen times and has quietly become training data? Or should I carry something else entirely? On that question, tell me whether the historical anchor belongs in the room at all — Einstein's theory recovering the residual advance of Mercury's perihelion, roughly forty-three arcseconds a century, an anomaly that had been sitting in celestial mechanics since Le Verrier raised it in 1859 — and if it does, at which moment, given that my audience will find an astronomy story far less compelling than a story about their own quarterly model review.

Third, the misconceptions as a map with positions on it, not as a summary list at the end. The ones I anticipate: that this is merely overfitting and cross-validation is the cure; that one should simply never assign exactly one, so a credence of 0.999 dissolves the difficulty; that preregistration settles it; that Bayesianism is therefore refuted, which several of them have long suspected; and the one I fear most, that the historical form of the problem, where E was known before T was proposed, and the synchronic form, where I know E and only now come to see that T entails it, get collapsed into one problem under two labels when they are not the same problem. For each, I want the point in the sequence at which it must be met, and whether it is met by an argument, by a worked case, or by refusing to let a particular word be used loosely.

Fourth, the check. One question I can put in the final five minutes whose answers will separate those who have got it from those who can produce "P(E) = 1" on demand. It should be answerable at the whiteboard, and it should not be a question about Einstein.

Fifth, the omissions. I propose to leave out, and to announce that I am leaving out, the formal apparatus of Jeffrey conditionalisation; the route through logical omniscience, which I suspect would swallow the last twenty minutes if admitted at all; the Popperian dispute over the value of novel predictions; and Goodman. Tell me whether that list is right, and name anything I would be wrong to exclude — including anything I have not thought of that this audience will raise from the floor and that I would be unable to field.

Be committed rather than even-handed: choose a vehicle and an order and defend the choices. But mark your confidence as you go, and close by naming the single element of the design you are least sure about, together with what would settle it. Two housekeeping requests: no reading list, since nobody will read one and I have no hours to spare for chasing references, so the plan must stand on its own argumentative feet; and please write the whole thing in British English.
