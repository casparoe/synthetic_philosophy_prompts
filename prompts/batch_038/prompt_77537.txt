I'm a fourth-year PhD student leading an eighty-minute discussion section of about eighteen students in a second-year course on time, modality, and free will. They have read the sea-battle passage in *De Interpretatione* 9 in translation and essentially nothing else, and they arrive expecting to dispatch fatalism in a sentence. I want an activity that instead makes the argument grip them, and then makes them identify exactly where the grip comes from.

The structure I have in mind: divide the section into groups facing a costly choice that bears on a proposition about the group's own behaviour, so that the proposition is settled by their decisions before the period ends. Every group receives identical evidence about the likelihood of the outcome; only the metaphysical framing differs — one is told that the relevant future-tense claim already has a determinate truth value, another that it is not yet either true or false. The rules should make effort expensive enough in points that resignation shows up in the numbers, so the reveal can demonstrate that the behavioural difference was produced by a label rather than by anything anyone came to know.

Please supply: the rules and a points economy requiring no money; a method for keeping each group's framing invisible to the others until the debrief; and a debrief that maps what actually happened onto the fatalist inference, onto the distinction between a fact's being settled now and its being settled *by* the past, and onto the response that denies the proposition a truth value at all. Then tell me at least three things that will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I assume demand characteristics will be the largest — and how the design can be adjusted against each. Finally, be frank about which feature of the argument the activity cannot reproduce and what I will have to supply myself, and flag anything in the design that amounts to misleading my students, together with whether a version free of that problem is still worth running.
