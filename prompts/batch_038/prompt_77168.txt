Our reading group just finished What Is Art? and, being the kind of people who do this, we built a spreadsheet of every verdict Tolstoy actually hands down in it. Paraphrasing hard, since half the group has a different translation:

- Beethoven's late quartets: not art for anyone but a tiny circle of musicians, and even they mostly feel what they've been told to feel.
- Wagner's Ring: counterfeit. He went to a rehearsal and watched everyone involved grind through it exhausted, feeling nothing.
- Shakespeare's King Lear: bad, and its reputation is proof of how a taste imposed by one class can hypnotize everybody for centuries.
- The French poets everyone was raving about — Baudelaire, Verlaine, Mallarmé: obscure on purpose, no infection, written for each other.
- Dante: same complaint.
- Maupassant: the best technician of the age, but his novels are about the wrong feelings; in the late stories you can watch his conscience start fighting his subject matter.
- The story of Joseph, Schiller's The Robbers, Les Misérables, Uncle Tom's Cabin, Adam Bede, Dostoevsky's House of the Dead, Dickens (with heavy qualifiers): this is what real art looks like.
- Millet, Breton, Lhermitte, Defregger's pictures of working people, and Ge's Judgement: good.
- A Bach violin aria, Chopin's E-flat nocturne, selected bits of Mozart, Haydn, Schubert: good — and notice he almost only ever picks passages, not whole works.
- Church services, jokes, decorating your house: these count as art, if they carry feeling.
- His own War and Peace and Anna Karenina: art of the idle upper class; asked what of his own he'd keep, basically nothing.
- Plus his four counterfeit markers: borrowing, imitation, effectfulness, diversion.

Now we want to run his standard on things he never saw and we can't agree at all. Here's the four cases, described as neutrally as I can:

1. A three-minute pop song, written by a team of five for a singer who has said in interviews that she finds it embarrassing, about wanting someone across a dance floor. A couple billion streams. My teenage niece cried to it, twice.
2. A vertical phone video, shaky, no music, no narration, shot by a man in a refugee camp at his neighbor's funeral. Forty million views. Nobody composed anything, he just held the phone up.
3. A big canvas of grey fields with one orange stripe. The artist says she wasn't thinking about anything when she made it and was mostly worried about the paint drying evenly. A friend of mine, who teaches art history, stood in front of it for twenty minutes and then had to sit down.
4. A video game, about eight hours long, in which you play a daughter making decisions for her father as his dementia advances. Mechanically simple, the writing is flat and plain, and the designer says it's his own mother. People who finish it report calling their parents. You need a console and eight spare hours to experience it at all.

What I want: predict his verdict on each of the four and say how confident you are. But the real work is the rules. Spell out the candidate rules that reproduce the whole spreadsheet — I can see at least did-it-infect-anyone, is-the-feeling-the-right-one, can-everyone-receive-it, and was-it-felt-rather-than-manufactured, and I suspect there's a fifth hiding in there somwhere. Say which of those the spreadsheet actually pins down and which it leaves wide open, then show where they come apart on the four cases, and tell me which single one of the four is the hardest call and why.

Two more things. Half my group thinks the first two rules collapse into one for him — that anything which moves ordinary people is automatically good art, and that he never really held a separate content standard at all. Show me where the spreadsheet refutes that, or where it genuinely doesn't. And then name one further case, described as concretely as mine, that would discriminate between the rules better than any of these four does.
