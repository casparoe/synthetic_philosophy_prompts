I am a doctoral candidate co-teaching an undergraduate seminar on democratic erosion that deliberately pairs the political-science literature with a literary text each week, and our closing session is Shakespeare's *Julius Caesar*. My co-instructor in the government department thinks the pairing is category-mongering: backsliding is defined relative to a democratic baseline and is supposed to be the work of elected incumbents operating through legal means, whereas Caesar is an unelected general in a pre-modern mixed constitution whose offence, so far as the play shows, is being loved too much and possibly wanting a crown. I want you to take that objection seriously and then either defeat it or concede it. Begin by asking whether the concept travels at all — what has to be stretched, and what gets lost in the stretching — and use the play's own institutional furniture: the tribunes Marullus and Flavius, whom Casca reports were "put to silence" for pulling the ceremonial scarfs off Caesar's statues, which looks like the single most modern-seeming datum in the play; the absence of any court, legislature, or electoral machinery of the kind backsliding indices actually score; and the Roman allergy to the bare name of king, which is not quite the same thing as an allergy to authoritarianism.

Then I want the epistemics of the diagnosis, which is where I think the play is genuinely ahead of the literature. Brutus's soliloquy in Act 2 Scene 1 is a piece of reasoning about a harm that has not yet occurred, and Shakespeare is unusually candid about its evidential poverty: Brutus concedes he knows "no personal cause to spurn at him, / But for the general," that Caesar "would be crown'd," and that "How that might change his nature, there's the question." He then admits that Caesar's "affections" have never swayed "more than his reason" — that is, he has no behavioural evidence at all, only a generic "common proof" about ambition's ladder — and he draws the conclusion anyway: "So Caesar may. / Then, lest he may, prevent." Most strikingly, he acknowledges that the charge "Will bear no colour for the thing he is," and instructs himself to "Fashion it thus; that what he is, augmented, / Would run to these and these extremities," finishing with the image of the serpent's egg killed in the shell. Read that speech against the two rival methodologies in the field: the behaviouralist one, which insists on observable indicators such as toleration of violence, willingness to curtail rivals' civil liberties, and endorsement of extralegal means, and the constitutional-retrogression account associated with Ginsburg and Huq, which tracks the deliberate weakening of the institutional conditions of democracy. Brutus has the structure of the second without a shred of the first. Is his inference a legitimate disposition-and-incentive argument about what coronation would do to a man, or is it motivated reasoning wearing the clothes of political science — and note the awkward fact that Cassius supplies both the motive and the forged letters that manufacture apparent public demand. Does the play thereby vindicate the cynic who says that "backsliding" is a label partisans fabricate in advance of any act, or does it instead show what honest diagnosis must sound like when the harm is still in the future and the only available evidence is character?

Third, work through the interpretive problem the play keeps staging, and tell me where you land. Casca's report of the Lupercal — Antony offering a coronet three times, Caesar refusing it each time, swooning before the crowd, and the whole thing retold with sour commentary — is a single event that simultaneously supports the reading "he refused absolute power" and the reading "he staged the refusal to demonstrate demand." The play gives us no way to adjudicate between them, and no character claims to have one; Antony's funeral oration does the same thing from the other side, observing every rule Brutus imposed on him while converting those rules into instruments of mob violence, and Brutus's own forbearance — no oath, spare Antony, let him speak — looks like exactly the self-restraint that democracies are supposed to prize and that here proves fatal. Say whether Levitsky and Ziblatt's mutual toleration and institutional forbearance illuminate that exchange or misdescribe it, and whether the play's ending, with the triumvirs marking proscription lists over Lepidus's head and a worse autocracy installed than the one feared, confirms Brutus's diagnosis or refutes it. Be honest about where the reading breaks: Caesar's stage presence is vain, superstitious, deaf in one ear, and given to the falling sickness, which is not the profile of a competent would-be autocrat, and the Elizabethan succession anxieties surrounding a long reign with no heir may make the play about monarchy's future rather than democracy's past. I don't need you to quote the text and I don't want a plot summary; what I want is criticism that is faithful to both the play and the theory, a recommendation of the two or three scenes worth assigning with the questions to put to the students, and a final account of what the play contributes to the debate that argument alone cannot — in particular, whether tragedy supplies the variable the indices leave out, namely the cost of being wrong.
