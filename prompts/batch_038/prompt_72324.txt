My friend and I spent four hours last night on Aquinas and tyrannicide and got nowhere, and I would rather settle it with a constructed case than with another round of exegesis.

The disagreement, briefly. He holds that Thomas's framework can never license a private person to kill a ruler: the article of the Secunda Secundae on whether a private individual may kill reserves the putting to death of a dangerous member of the community to whoever is entrusted with the care of that community; the article on sedition makes the disturbance of public order a mortal sin by its very genus; and the tyranny chapters of *On Kingship*, on his reading, counsel patience under an endurable tyranny, direct any remedy to a superior with authority over the community, and treat the scriptural slayers of tyrants as acting under a divine commission that nobody today can claim. I hold that there is a third route, running through the origin of authority rather than through revelation: because the authority to direct action to the common good belongs to the multitude and is held by others only by delegation, a body that genuinely carries the community's charge can constitute a public office *ad hoc*, so that a man who was a private citizen an hour earlier acts, in doing the deed, not as a private person but as an instrument of public authority. He says this collapses Thomas's distinction altogether, since on my account whatever a frightened crowd authorizes becomes public authority.

Build me the case that decides it. Construct one scenario, three or four paragraphs at most, in which Aquinas's own framework — his, not a modernized natural-law reconstruction — yields the verdict that a private individual may licitly kill a reigning tyrant, and in which the killing is not seditious. Constraints:

- Contemporary and realistic: a weak or partly collapsed state, ordinary people, no medieval trappings, no science fiction.
- The ruler's title is not in question. He is a tyrant by exercise — a legitimate ruler governing for his own private good and destroying the common good — so the answer cannot lean on anything special about usurpers.
- No divine commission, no private revelation, no appeal to God's hidden judgment. That is precisely the route I am trying to do without, so a case that needs it begs the question against my friend.
- The actor begins with no standing public charge of any kind: not a soldier, not a police officer, not a judge, not a minister of a recognized government-in-exile, not a bodyguard with delegated protective duties.
- No superior authority is available, and the story must make clear why: no external power willing and able to act, no functioning tribunal, no constitutional organ that can convene, no ecclesiastical or international superior with any purchase.
- The killer gains nothing — no office, no property, no factional advantage — and the succession passes, on the story's own terms, to a body whose claim is independent of him.
- The killing is his own single deliberate act, directed at the ruler alone, with no bystanders and no collateral deaths, so the analysis turns on authority and not on double effect or on harms to third parties.
- Do not stipulate the verdicts. Supply the material — who knows what, how securely, what plausibly follows — so that the greater-harm qualification in the sedition article and the prudence clauses in *On Kingship* can actually be run on the case rather than assumed to come out my way.

Then demonstrate that the case meets specification:

1. Give the verdict each part of Thomas's framework delivers, in order — the article on whether a private person may kill, the article on sedition including its qualification about harm greater than the tyranny itself, and the treatment of tyranny in *On Kingship* — with the reason in each case, naming the feature of the scenario that satisfies or defeats the relevant requirement.
2. Identify the single feature doing the work: the element that converts a private person into a public person for Aquinas. State it exactly, not gesturally.
3. Remove it and report what happens. Which of the three verdicts flips first, and does the case then degrade into straightforward homicide, into sedition, or into an act that is neither sinful nor licit but merely inexpedient?
4. Press the instability in my route rather than papering over it. A body strong enough to confer a public charge looks strong enough to act as a body and depose the tyrant itself; a body too weak to act openly looks too weak to confer anything real. Tell me what sort of body can sit in the middle — actually existing, actually legitimate, actually unable to proceed publicly — or tell me plainly that no such body can exist and that my third route is therefore empty.
5. Give my friend his best attack, in the form he would put it, including his point that in a collapsed state there is no multitude in the sense Thomas needs, since the sedition article takes a people to be not any crowd but an assembly united by a fellowship recognized in law and directed to the common good — so there is nothing left capable of holding or conferring authority at all.

I want a committed answer, not a survey. Tell me whether the case you have built genuinely licenses the killing on Aquinas's own principles, whether my third route is real or is only the divine-commission route with the theology deleted, and where you would put your weight if the argument resumed tonight. If you conclude that no such case can be built without cheating, say so and identify which constraint is the unsatisfiable one. And wherever your verdict depends on a contested point about the texts rather than on the structure of the case — including whether the tyranny chapters of *On Kingship* are Thomas's own or the completion by Ptolemy of Lucca — flag that dependency explicitly and say whether it changes the outcome. No bibliography and no citations; I know the texts and want the reasoning.
