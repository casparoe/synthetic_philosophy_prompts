So, slightly odd ask. I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy or math degree, but for the past year I've been the unofficial "problem set guy" for an evening foundations class at a community college near me — mostly people doing a bridge program before a part-time masters. The instructor is a retired math prof who lets me draft questions and then edits them. I've got the last set to write and I want the big final question to be about the axiom of choice.

My problem: everything online about AC collapses into the same three items — Zorn's lemma, Banach-Tarski, and Russell's infinite pairs of socks versus shoes. I've tried getting models to write me a question and they all hand back some permutation of those. My students have also all read the same blog posts, so anything built on that trio is just a memory test.

So I want to anchor it in a bug from my day job instead. Roughly: we keep records sharded across machines, and a downstream job needs one "canonical" copy per logical record. We define an equivalence relation on records (same logical key means equivalent) and we want a function that takes the whole set and returns exactly one representative per class. The spec said "pick any one," and then the job assumed the pick was *stable* from run to run. It wasn't, because nothing pinned down which representative you get, and the resulting corruption was miserable to debug.

That's the seed. I want a question that uses it to get at things I only half understand, so please be rigorous and correct me where I'm wrong, I wont be offended.

The question should force students to:

- say precisely, in set-theoretic terms, what "there is such a canonical-representative function" is asserting, and why that's trivial when the set of records is finite but not in general;
- sort out which choice principle each of three versions of the spec actually needs — finitely many classes, countably many classes each of which is finite, and arbitrarily many classes — and get across that these are not equally hard, since I'm told the middle one is weirder than it looks;
- explain why the stability bug is its own problem, separate from the existence one, and what extra structure would fix it. My guesses are a well-ordering, or a definable tie-breaker like "lowest hash," but I don't know what's provable where, and I suspect the fact that our records all have unique IDs changes the answer in a way I haven't worked out;
- judge whether the Banach-Tarski line is even the right *kind* of objection — i.e. does "AC gives you a crazy decomposition of a sphere" actually bear on whether our representative function exists;
- and handle the student who says "but you never told me how to *build* it." There's a real philosophical point in there and I want the question to make them say something better than "mathematicians disagree about this."

Level: smart adults who've done an intro proofs course and have seen relations, equivalence classes, functions, countability, and Cantor's diagonal argument. They have not met Zorn's lemma or ordinals seriously, so the question can name-drop them but cant require them. Worth 25 marks, 75 minutes, done at home, allowed to talk to each other but not to the internet.

Give me three things: the question as I'd hand it out, with the sharding story told in plain language since not all of them are programmers; a model answer around 900–1100 words; and a marking scheme that splits the 25 marks into named chunks and says what has to appear in each. For the marking scheme I care most about the "costs marks" side — give me six or seven specific wrong moves I should expect, like students who think the whole issue is that the function isn't computable, or who think "the classes are nonempty so obviously you can pick" is a complete answer, or who blur "there's no unique choice" into "there's no choice function." Each one with what it costs. No reading list at the end, the answer should stand on its own.

One more thing: if any of the claims in my bullet list are wrong or oversimplified, don't quietly write around them — add a short note at the bottom saying what you changed and why. I have to defend this question to the prof.
