I'm a convinced expressivist of the plan-based variety and I think I've got a line on the Frege-Geach problem. Roughly: a moral sentence expresses the same kind of planning state whether it's asserted or embedded, and the logical relations between those states are fixed by whether holding them together would be incoherent as planning — not by truth conditions. So "murder is wrong" is being for blaming for murdering, "murder isn't wrong" is being for not blaming for murdering, and an occurrence embedded under "either...or" expresses being for a plan that settles the question one way or the other. Validity comes out as: you can't coherently accept the premises and deny the conclusion, where "can't" is a planning-level constraint rather than a truth-preservation fact.

What would count against this? I want a map of what the view actually risks, not a general assault on expressivism. Specifically: which cases or bits of linguistic data would be genuinely damaging — I'm thinking negation, whether moral connectives really behave like Boolean ones, moral modus ponens, and whether someone can grasp "wrong" without having the relevant attitude — and which are merely awkward. Where does my view stick its neck out in a way a truth-conditional rival doesn't, such that the rival could turn out right and I wrong? For what its worth, the thing that would move me most is a case where my account of the connectives predicts different inferential behaviour from the classical one and comes out on the losing end.

And please be blunt about the collapse worry: once I hand over a systematic rule assigning planning states to complex sentences, haven't I just written a compositional semantics with unusual values and called it non-representational? Is there a principled line there, or has the view been qualified until it can't fail? Sort the defeaters from fatal to cosmetic, and say which ones a committed defender of this view would already concede as counting against them rather than waving off.
