Retired three years ago from a synchrotron beamline — I spent my working life on diagnostics and error budgets, and now I read philosophy and economics for fun, which turns out to be considerably less disciplined than I expected. I've just run into the centipede game and the backward induction paradox, and I'm about to start on all the fixes people have proposed, but I don't want to be fobbed of with a list of named positions. First I want the spec: what does a solution concept have to deliver to count as actually solving this, rather than relabelling it?

Fix the purpose like this, since I know adequacy depends on it. Good enough means: a theory that lets me say, as a player, what to do at node 40 of the hundred-node centipede after my opponent has passed four times in a row, and lets me defend that to someone who thinks the answer is obvious in the opposite direction. That's the bar.

So write the desiderata, and for each one give me the case or consideration that forces it — not the label, the situation. Here are some I've come up with that I suspect belong on the list, plus at least one I suspect is junk and want struck:

- it has to say what a player is supposed to believe at a node his own theory has already declared unreachable, since that seems to be where the whole thing comes apart;
- it has to be explicit about how much mutual knowledge of rationality it's leaning on. Does a hundred-node game need common knowledge, or only a hundred levels of the mutual sort, and should the verdict change if the levels run out halfway down?
- it has to explain why an early pass isn't simply evidence that my model of the other player was wrong, and what I'm then licensed to infer about their later play. This smells like the predatory-pricing / chain-store business and I can't tell whether that connection is a virtue or a dodge;
- correspondence: in games where nobody thinks there's a paradox at all, does it still land where backward induction lands, or is it a different theory in the same clothes;
- robustness: does it's verdict survive a small wiggle in the payoffs, or is it a knife-edge result? In my old life a result that vanished under perturbation wasn't a result;
- and length-consistency, the one I'm least sure about: should the answer for the hundred-node game be forced by the answer for the six-node one, or may a theory say the verdict genuinely changes with how long the game runs? I can see both, and I want to be told whether demanding this is legitimate or just a physicist's prejudice about scales.

Then the part I actually want most: which of these are load-bearing and which are one camp's taste. In particular, is "it must predict what real subjects do in the lab" a genuine requirement or a category error for a normative concept? The experimental data has people passing deep into the game, and I can't work out whether that refutes backward induction or only refutes the assumption that the subjects were the rational agents the theory posits. And if some subset of the list can't all be satisfied at once, identify the subset and show me the collision as tightly as you can — because if it happens, the list is the result, and every proposed fix is just a choice about which requirement to drop.

Coda only at the very end, three or four sentences each: how the trembling-hand style refinements, the common-knowledge-of-rationality line, and the forward-induction / belief-revision people do against the list.
