I've been chewing on Avicenna's Flying Man for a while now — the one where a fully formed adult is created all at once, suspended in calm temperate air, eyes veiled, limbs seperated so no part of him touches any other, no memory of ever having had a body, but with his intellect fully working — and Avicenna's claim is that this guy would affirm that his own self exists while affirming nothing at all about his limbs, his organs, or anything external, and that what he affirms therefore isn't the body.

I want to build a modified version and get a number out of you on the original. Call Avicenna's setup A. Call B the same setup except the floating man keeps full proprioception and interoception: he feels his posture, the position of every limb, the beat of his heart and the pull of his own breathing, a complete interior map of himself — he just has no vision, hearing, smell, taste, no sensation at the skin's surface, and still no memories of embodied life.

Target proposition (P): the Flying Man argument in form A, all by itself, establishes that the self the subject affirms is not identical with his body — where "establishes" means it does real probative work rather than just dramatizing a conclusion you need independent arguments for, and means a genuine non-identity claim, not merely that the subject is attending to something other than his body.

Give me your credence in P, but decomposed, roughly along these lines (add or cut terms if you think I've drawn them wrong):

1. the probability that a subject in A would actually make the judgment Avicenna predicts, rather than being unable to form any self-conception at all or forming some garbled one;
2. the probability that the judgment, if made, is explained by the self's being non-bodily rather than by A's having deleted every representation of the body — and here I want your read on B: what would B's subject say when asked whether he exists and whether he is this felt body-map? That's my probe for whether A's result is an artifact;
3. the probability that the inference from "he conceives of himself without conceiving of his body" to "he is not his body" actually goes through, given the standard worries about substitutivity in opaque contexts and about conceivability generally;
4. the probability that the conclusion is substantive rather than verbal — that the thing Avicenna's subject would point to as "I" and the thing a modern identity thesis is about (organism, person, subject of experience) are the same relatum;
5. whatever else you think belongs.

One line of justification per term, then show me how they combine into the final number. Then: which single term does your number lean on hardest, and what argument or piece of evidence would move it the most? My own credence in P is about 0.3, so also tell me which term you think I'm over- or under-weighting to land there. American English, please.
