One of the habits I've never quite lost from a decade of reading rationalist blogs is a deep distrust of people's stated reasons and a corresponding appetite for their voting records. That's the background to this request, and I'll try to keep the setup honest about its own limits.

For three years I've sat on the model release committee of a mid-size frontier lab. I can't name it, and I've shuffled enough surface detail below that the record shouldn't be identifiable, but the decision structure is intact. We are five members with a persistent swing vote I'll call M., who came over from an interpretability group and who keeps — uniquely, as far as I know — a written log of her own votes. She has let me read it. My working hypothesis, which she considers naive, is that her decision procedure is far more stable than the justifications she offers for any given instance; she considers her justifications load-bearing and my inference premature. So please treat her recorded reasons as data, but as weak data, and do the actual work off the votes.

Her record so far, in order:

1. Delayed a general-purpose assistant launch by five months to add a resistance-to-targeted-persuasion fine-tune, at a moment when a competitor was credibly rumored to be weeks from shipping. Recorded reason: the delay bought a margin the team could measure, and unmeasurable prudence was not a reason.
2. Voted to open-weight a small model that our bio-risk battery scored just above an uplift-relevant threshold, on the grounds that three public models already scored at least as well.
3. Voted against publishing our internal writeup of a novel jailbreak class even after a rival lab published an adjacent result, drawing the line at the fact that the rival's piece was vague about transfer conditions and ours would have been a recipe.
4. Voted against a six-month delay to add interpretability-based behavioral monitoring to a model shipping into exactly one enterprise integration, under contractual use limits, with an audit log we owned and revocation inside twenty-four hours.
5. Halted a release for two weeks and re-ran the entire battery after learning a post-training contractor had silently altered a data mixture, with every eval score still inside noise of the pre-change run. Recorded reason: she did not think the model had become more dangerous; she no longer knew which of our numbers described the model and which described the change.
6. Voted to fund an external red-team program no competitor was funding, explicitly on the grounds that it would repay itself in hiring and regulatory standing. It did.
7. Voted to ship a coding agent whose sandbox-escape results fell below our own published threshold, conditional on early external access plus a bounty, reasoning that a controlled window with adversarial users would teach us more than five further weeks in-house.
8. Voted against joining a bilateral slowdown she had publicly endorsed the previous year, because the verification terms turned out reciprocal but unenforceable and she expected quiet defection.

Thursday's proposal is this. We have a model that scores meaningfully above anything we've previously been willing to run in production on our internal battery for automated AI research and development; deploying it plausibly compresses our own timeline by two to four quarters. By our best intelligence three rival labs are within a quarter of the same result and none has it. The safety case rests on a monitoring stack — interpretability-based deception and goal-misgeneralization detection, plus human review of the model's research suggestions — which the team rates at roughly sixty percent likely to function as designed and which has never been validated at this capability level. Full hardening to our published standard takes six months; a partial pass takes three. Management proposes release to a closed cohort of about two hundred enterprise customers under audit, with automatic revocation if the monitors trip and a standing halt commitment. I have drafted an amendment to begin with twenty customers for three months during the partial hardening and revisit. And one fact keeps surfacing on both sides: deploy or not, the model exists, and we will be using it internally on our own research either way.

What I'd like from you: reconstruct at least three decision rules consistent with all eight votes, or say frankly which votes each rule strains against and how badly. My own shortlist of candidates is a marginal-contribution rule keyed to race dynamics, a verification-first rule with competitiveness as a side-constraint, a reversibility-and-containment rule, and a concreteness asymmetry that discounts speculative harms against near ones — but please include at least one rule I haven't listed, and tell me if one of my four can't actually survive the record. For each rule, give its prediction on Thursday: full release, my amendment, or block, together with whatever conditions it would attach. Then commit to a best guess, name the one or two past votes doing the most work in your inference, and say what would flip her. I'd also like you to address what I suspect is the hidden variable: whether her unit of analysis for "marginal effect" and for "reversibility" is the weights, the access grant, the capability advantage, or the timeline itself — the case reads very differently under each, and I want to know which the record supports. Finally, write the two-paragraph rationale she would publish whichever way she votes, since I think a rule that can't generate her voice probably isn't her rule, and name one further case, real or hypothetical, that would discriminate between your top two rules given that her after-the-fact explanations are worthless as evidence. Two friends who both read in this area have bet me a dinner, one backing a straight race-dynamics consequentialist reading and one backing something closer to epistemic integrity with a competitive side-constraint; resist the temptation to declare a single elegant winner, and be explicit about where eight votes genuinely underdetermine the ninth.
