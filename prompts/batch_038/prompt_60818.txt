I am a second-year PhD student in economics, and I am trying to decide whether to build my dissertation prospectus around the "ergodicity economics" program associated with Ole Peters and his collaborators. Before I commit, I want to be sure that I am stating the program's central achievement correctly and that I understand what it does and does not license. For calibration: I have completed a first-year micro sequence and one graduate course in decision theory, and I know the definitions of an ergodic process and of the Kelly criterion, though I have not worked through either in this setting.

Here is my understanding. Expected utility theory evaluates a gamble by averaging over an ensemble of parallel copies of that gamble — an average across outcomes at a fixed time — whereas a real agent lives a single life, sequentially, and what matters to her is the time-average growth rate of her own wealth along the one trajectory she actually inhabits. The two averages coincide only for a restricted class of processes, and wealth dynamics are not in that class. Once the time average is substituted for the ensemble average, the program holds, the St. Petersburg paradox and the equity premium puzzle dissolve with no need to postulate any concave utility function at all, and the Kelly criterion drops out as the uniquely rational staking policy. Samuelson's 1971 objection to maximizing the geometric mean is, on this reading, merely a symptom of having set the problem up in the wrong coordinates.

Two questions follow, and I suspect they are related.

First. The standard objection to the growth-rate criterion is that maximizing the expected logarithm of wealth just is maximizing expected utility with a logarithmic utility function, so the criterion smuggles in a preference assumption after all and cannot pretend to be preference-free. But the criterion is instead derived from the dynamics of the wealth process itself: one computes the growth rate of the quantity the agent actually accumulates, not of some psychological index of it. Does the program therefore escape the objection? I want to write that it does, yet I cannot articulate the escape, and I have begun to worry that the identification with log utility is not a happy coincidence but the entire content of the criterion. Is there a principled way to state the growth-rate criterion that does not amount to a preference assumption, or is the charge correct?

Second, and more concrete. Consider a repeated gamble: each period a fair coin is flipped; if it lands heads, the fraction f of my current wealth that I staked gains 60 percent, and if it lands tails, that staked fraction loses 40 percent. I may revise f each period, and the odds are known to me. I take the Kelly fraction to be f ≈ 0.42 and stake it; my advisor, who says she has diminishing marginal utility of wealth, stakes f = 0.15 every period. Over a hundred periods my policy maximizes the expected value of my terminal wealth as well as its typical size, and the probability that she finishes ahead of me is negligible; in the limit, her wealth is vanishingly small next to mine with probability one. I should like to claim in the prospectus that growth-rate optimality therefore renders her policy strictly irrational — not merely differently tempered, but wrong. Is that claim defensible as stated? If not, which pieces of my reasoning survive, and what is the correct formulation of the dominance result I am reaching for?
