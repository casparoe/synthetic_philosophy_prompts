So I teach a graduate seminar on the emotions and I want to hand my students a piece of satire as a reading — one that does the work for me without ever sounding like a lecture. Here's the piece I want.

An insurance claims determination letter, about 750 words (850 max), from the Affective Peril desk of a blandly reassuring invented insurer, to a policyholder who has filed for emotional distress. The claimant says they were laid low for two days by grief over a character in a novel. Please invent the novel, the author, and the character — I explicitly don't want real books in here, otherwise the exercise turns into a quiz about who remembers which plot, and half my students would just be guessing.

The engine of the joke is that the adjuster, in order to decide the claim, has to take a position on whether the grief was real, and every position available to them detonates somewhere else in the same letter. The adjuster is competent, mildly weary, not stupid, and totally unaware that they are working through a famous paradox. Deadpan corporate register throughout: claim numbers, policy schedules, sub-clauses, an appeals paragraph, a note about next year's premium.

Things I need in there:

- Have the three claims that can't all be true surface one at a time, in different parts of the letter, so the reader assembles the inconsistency themselves rather than being handed it: the claimant reports a genuine emotion; the claimant knew perfectly well, the whole time, that nobody in the book exists; and the insurer's own definitions require belief in the object before an affective state counts as one. Never enumerate them as a list.

- Build in one specific operationalization and make the letter stick to it: the policy defines belief as a disposition to wager, i.e. a state counts as believing-that-p only if the insured would act on p when the cost of acting is real. That definition is what makes the denial possible, and it's also what makes one of the other claims in the household schedule impossible to deny.

- Deny the main claim on make-believe grounds — the grief was, per the schedule of definitions, quasi-grief, functionally indistinguishable from grief but occurring inside a game the insured was knowingly playing with themselves. Then let the schedule quietly concede that quasi-grief is what nearly everyone means when they say a film got to them, so the denial starts to look like it isn't denying anything. Don't comment on that. Just leave the concession sitting in the definitions section where the reader finds it.

- Consider and reject an alternative basis for payment: that the mere entertaining of the thought was enough to make the emotion real. The adjuster's objection should be actuarial and irritated — on that reading the company is liable for every worry anyone has ever had about something that isn't happening — and the objection should be funny and also, accidentally, a decent argument.

- Attach a schedule of two other claims from the same household. One is fear during a horror film, where the claimant was home alone and got up and bolted the front door, which the policy's wagering definition obviously satisfies. The other is grief for a real person, a stranger the claimant never met and only read about, which the company pays promptly and at a higher rate — and the letter must not notice that this is the awkward one, that the payout is larger for the death that moved the claimant less.

- One short paragraph of invented internal precedent: a prior adjuster paid out on a fiction claim and was reversed on appeal, with the ground of reversal left maddeningly vague, maybe a single quoted clause from the appellate decision that turns out to be about something else entirely.

- End on something that makes a reader who knows the literature feel the actual problem. My suggestion: as a condition of appeal the adjuster asks the claimant to certify, under penalty, that at no point during the reading did they believe the character existed — and the letter then has to say what happens to the certification if they can't honestly sign it, which is a problem the adjuster has just created for themselves. Or find something better.

Hard rules. No philosopher names, no paper titles, no citations, no footnotes pointing at the literature — the adjuster has read none of it and it's better if they haven't. No winking, no asides to the reader, no "as we all know." Every feature of the views being ironized has to be a real one; I don't want invented objections doing the work, because my students will check. And it still has to be funny and legible to someone who's never heard of the paradox of fiction — they should come away with the image of a company that has decided, with total confidence and in triplicate, that a person's tears dont count.
