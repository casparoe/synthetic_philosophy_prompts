**Role and approach.** You are a philosopher of science who works comfortably with the technical rulebooks of the earth sciences, and whose specialty is reconstructing the implicit metaphysics of institutional procedures. Think carefully rather than quickly. Before you commit to a reconstruction, spend a short brainstorming section — a handful of bullets — listing candidate theories of periodization that the practice below might presuppose, including at least two you expect to reject and why. Then write the full answer under separate headings.

**The practice.** Since 2009 an interdisciplinary Anthropocene Working Group has operated inside the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy, which sits under the International Commission on Stratigraphy, which in turn reports to the International Union of Geological Sciences. Its mandate was to determine whether the Anthropocene should become a formal epoch of the geological time scale and, if so, where its lower boundary falls. In 2023 the group proposed a mid-twentieth-century start, keyed to the worldwide spike in fallout plutonium recorded in the annually laminated sediments of a lake in Ontario. That layer was to serve as the golden spike: the single physical point in a single physical record that defines the boundary for the whole planet. In 2024 the proposal was voted down, first by the working group's own parent subcommission and then confirmed further up the chain, on grounds centering on how thin and how recent the sedimentary record is. Afterwards the union said, in substance, that the term would continue to be used by earth scientists, social scientists, policymakers, and the public as a descriptor of human impact on the Earth system — real and valuable, but not a unit of time.

The rulebook the group was working under requires, among other things, that a boundary be fixed by a primary marker, typically the first appearance of a fossil species, supported by secondary markers; that the marker correlate regionally and globally; that it be independent of the kind of sediment it happens to occur in; and that sedimentation at the site be continuous and undisturbed by tectonics or metamorphism. Alongside these it also requires that the site be accessible to research and free to access — which the guidelines gloss as being reachable quickly from an international airport and roads, being kept in good condition, ideally within a protected area, being extensive enough to allow repeated sampling, and being open to researchers of all nationalities. (Strictly, spikes define the bases of stages; an epoch boundary is the base of the lowest stage of that epoch.)

Three further features of the surrounding practice belong on the table:

(a) The base of the Holocene — the epoch the Anthropocene was to replace — is not an outcrop. It is a point in a Greenland ice core, fixed by the end of a cold interval roughly 11,700 years before the year 2000. A climate signal in ice, rather than a body in rock, marks the beginning of the current epoch.

(b) For intervals so old that no suitable section survives, the same commission permits a boundary to be set by a fixed numerical date rather than by a place. The device exists in the rulebook. It is treated as unavailable for the recent past, where the record is best.

(c) The boundary at the base of the Cambrian is keyed to the first appearance of a particular burrowing trace fossil at a site in Newfoundland. That fossil has since been identified in strata several metres *below* the boundary it defines. The boundary has not been moved.

**What I want reconstructed.** For each of the following, distinguish what the practice *strictly* presupposes from what it merely sits well with, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several incompatible theories of natural periodization would license the identical rulebook.

1. What does it take for a stretch of Earth's history to be a *period* rather than an *event*? The rejection turned on thinness and recency. Is that a claim about the world — that a boundary requires some depth of settled aftermath before it can be a boundary at all — or a claim about the institution's need for a boundary that is closed, stable, and cheap to maintain? Note that the rejected interval is the only one whose boundary is still moving.

2. Let me stipulate a distinction, and I want you to work with it rather than dispute the labels. Call a periodization *onset-based* if a period begins when the responsible process begins, and *legibility-based* if it begins when that process first leaves a trace that can be read the same way everywhere. The spike procedure looks like it implements legibility. Which of the competing start dates proposed over the past two decades — Neolithic agriculture, the post-contact depopulation of the Americas and the resulting proposed dip in atmospheric carbon dioxide around 1610, the Industrial Revolution in the late eighteenth century, the postwar acceleration — are disputes about onset, and which are really disputes about legibility? Has this literature been conducting one argument or two, and if two, which one is answerable by evidence?

3. Address the apparent inconsistency between the Holocene's ice core and the Anthropocene's lake varves: physically these look like the same kind of move. What must the practice presuppose for one to be acceptable and the other not? Separate reasons that are genuinely about the world from reasons that are about resolution, durability, and the convenience of future correlators — and tell me whether the difference in verdict is defensible on the second kind of reason alone.

4. What does it say about the practice's ontology that the reality of a boundary in Earth's history depends in part on whether the site can be reached quickly from an international airport? Is this an embarrassment that the rulebook has never absorbed, or the consequence of a coherent view — for instance, that a boundary is real only insofar as it is publicly checkable by a community with bodies, visas, and travel budgets? Take a position and defend it.

5. On authority: a few dozen stratigraphers, voting up a committee chain, decided when — or whether — the age of humans began. Compare the parallel case of an expert astronomical body settling by vote what counts as a planet. What has to be true of the object being classified for a vote to be the right procedure? Are epochs and planets relevantly alike here — is there a fact of the matter the vote tracks, or is the vote partly constitutive of the fact? If constitutive, is the Anthropocene decision any less objective for that, given that the same is arguably true of every ratified boundary on the chart?

6. On the final concession: the union affirmed the term's descriptive value while denying it chronological standing. What view of the relation between *significance* and *periodhood* licenses that split? Is significance a legitimate criterion for a geological time unit, and if not, what work was the proposal actually doing when it asked for a place on the chart — was it a claim about rocks, or a claim about who counts as a cause?

7. Now a counterfactual I want you to reason through seriously rather than dismiss. Suppose the commission established a second, official, parallel chronology — call it the significance scale — whose boundaries are set by the onset of processes that matter rather than by markers in an archive, and administered jointly by stratigraphers and historians. It would place the start of the current interval centuries or millennia earlier than 1952. What would have to be true for the two scales to be describing one world rather than merely defining different artifacts? Where exactly would they disagree, and would each disagreement be a substantive discovery or a definitional artifact? Is the significance scale internally coherent, or does it collapse — either into the marker scale, or into ordinary history wearing a geological uniform? Say which collapse it faces and whether it can be avoided.
