I am convening a ninety-minute interdisciplinary workshop on population ethics. The participants come from philosophy, ecology, and public health; with one exception none of them has read the population axiology literature, and none will have met the repugnant conclusion before the session opens. My aim for the first twenty minutes is not to teach but to sort. I want each participant to discover, from their own answers about described outcomes, which substantive view they are already committed to, so that the remainder of the session is an argument between identified positions rather than a scramble in which people defend labels they were handed a minute earlier.

For this I need a self-administered paper instrument — a decision tree presented as a short case-based quiz — together with its scoring key. Please design it to the following specification.

**The instrument.** Eight to ten items. Each item presents two concretely described outcomes and asks which is better, or whether they are exactly equally good; at least two items must offer that third option, and one item must also permit the answer that the two outcomes are not comparable. No item may ask about a principle, an axiom, or a theory, and none may use the vocabulary of the literature ("repugnant," "sadistic," "mere addition," "person-affecting"). Every outcome description must run to under sixty words and be matched to its partner in length and register: no evaluative adjectives, no rhetorical framing, and nothing that hints at which aggregate feature is under test. A participant should be able to answer every item without any suspicion of what the items add up to.

**The discriminations the items must effect.** I want the following axes covered, each by at least one item:

1. A fixed population in which everyone's life improves, and separately a fixed total distributed equally rather than unequally.
2. Adding lives that are worth living to an existing population, at no cost to anyone and with no resources diverted.
3. Whether there is a reason to bring a happy person into existence of the same kind as the reason not to bring a miserable one into existence.
4. Whether a small population containing a modest amount of mild suffering can be better than a population containing no suffering and only faint pleasure.
5. The large-scale trade: a small population of very high-quality lives against a vastly larger population of lives that are worth living but barely. This must be described as plainly as any other item, with no signal that it is the famous case. One of my participants is a committed anti-natalist and this is the item on which I expect the room to fracture.
6. Subject neutrality: the same comparison as in 5, but with one population human and the other nonhuman sentient animals, matched for the quality of each life. I have a wildlife biologist attending who will resist treating the two as fungible, and I want that resistance recorded rather than argued away.
7. Lifetime aggregation: ten billion lives of twenty years each against one hundred million lives of two hundred years, matched for total welfare and for moment-to-moment quality.
8. The status of "worth living": at least one item on a life whose subject would be wholly indifferent to continuing it and which contains neither suffering nor any positive experience worth naming, and at least one on a life its subject desperately wants to continue but whose experiential quality is very low.

**Construct the tree from the logical space, not from a list of names.** First give me the discriminating questions and the answer combinations they permit; only then locate the actual positions in that space. Where a combination is unoccupied — where the pattern of answers corresponds to no view anybody defends — mark it and diagnose it in a single sentence as incoherent, coherent but unattractive, or merely unexplored.

**Leaves.** The tree must terminate in named positions, and at minimum must be able to deliver the following: the total view; the average view; critical-level views with a threshold above which a life adds value and below which it subtracts; variable-value or number-dampening views, on which the weight of each life falls as the population grows; negative utilitarianism; the narrow person-affecting view; asymmetry-based views; the egalitarian total view; maximin; a species-relativist view; and an explicit sceptical leaf for the participant who concludes that no adequate theory exists. If my list omits a leaf that your items can actually reach, add it and say why it belongs there.

**Scoring key.** Give me a compact rule, applied in a stated order, that assigns a leaf to any answer pattern, rather than an enumeration of thousands of strings. Then enumerate the patterns that are jointly inconsistent: for each, name the two answers that conflict, say which commitment has to give, and give the minimal single-item edit that repairs the pattern. Where two different repairs are equally minimal, give both and say which turn out to be different views. Also say what the "not comparable" answer is diagnostic of, and what it is not.

**Two further things.** Flag every pair of leaves my instrument cannot in fact separate, and for each say what one extra item would separate them. And state your reasoning about order: where item 5 must sit and why, which other items are contaminated by what precedes them, and the final sequence.

Keep the whole instrument finishable in twenty minutes. No citations, no reading list, and no author names attached to positions — standard names for the views only.
