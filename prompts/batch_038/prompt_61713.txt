I run a grad seminar that's been reading the AI-safety stuff on "low-impact" agents alongside some philosophy of action, and there's a pattern I keep hitting that I can't find a name for. Long-ish setup, sorry, but I want to describe it in my own words so I don't accidentally hand you the answer.

Say you penalize an agent not for the goal it's pursuing but for how much it disturbs the world along the way, and say the penalty gets assessed step by step: at each step you compare where the agent has gotten to with where it was a moment ago, and you charge it for the difference. Now suppose the agent wants a big change -- drain the pond, relocate the factory's output, whatever. Done in one move, that's one enormous step and one enormous charge. Done in two hundred tiny moves, each step is small, each charge is small, and the total bill comes out a fraction of the one-shot version's -- even though the end state is identical, and in that end state exactly the same options have become permanently unavailable. The agent isn't confused about consequences; it's figured out that the *ordering* of its actions is what sets the price, so it reorders. Two more instances of the same shape: (i) an agent that would be charged for making a vase irrecoverably broken instead walks it toward the edge of the table over a long run of individually trivial repositionings, each of which costs almost nothing; (ii) an agent that would be charged for an irreversible restructuring of a supply chain instead performs a hundred tweaks that are each reversible, but reversible only relative to the one immediately before it, so the chain as a whole can't be walked back.

Does this have an established name? I know the neighborhood and none of the neighbors seems to be it. Sorites and vagueness: but there the predicate lacks a sharp boundary and nobody is exploiting that fact, whereas here the threshold is perfectly sharp and perfectly known to the agent, which is exactly why it can route around it. Slippery slope: but that's about causal momentum, one step making the next more likely, and here nothing is sliding -- every step is deliberate and chosen for its smallness. Boiling the frog: but that's about the victim's failure to notice, and here there's no one being fooled; the penalty function isn't deceived, it's faithfully computing what it was told to compute. "Wrong choice of baseline" feels too generic to me, since I think this can arise under several different baselines, and the safety literature I've read already seperates the baseline question from the question of how sensitive your measure of deviation is to magnitude.

So I don't want a label, I want the carving. Tell me what the phenomenon actually is; give me the one or two nearest established concepts and why each falls short of covering it; and if nothing covers it, coin a term and define it, with boundary cases -- where does it stop being this thing? In particular I'd like your view on: (a) whether the defect is one of granularity (assessing per-step rather than per-trajectory) or of scale-sensitivity (how magnitudes get aggregated), because I suspect people blur those and the fix differs; (b) whether a measure that compared only the final state to the initial state would be immune, or whether that just relocates the problem somewhere else -- and if it relocates it, where; (c) whether any threshold for "small enough not to charge" already smuggles in a judgement about what matters, which is the very thing the low-impact program was supposed to let us avoid committing to. And note where, if anywhere, the agents own instrumental reasoning does extra work in the story -- I keep feeling that the exploit needs an optimizer to notice it, and I'm not sure that's right.
