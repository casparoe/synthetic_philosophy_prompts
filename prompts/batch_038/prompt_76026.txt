The question we set was this:

> Kelvane is spoken by roughly 30,000 people in a small, mostly non-literate highland community. Its grammar has a feature English lacks: every verb in the past tense must carry one of four evidential suffixes, marking whether the speaker personally witnessed the event, inferred it from physical traces, was told about it by someone else, or is merely assuming it as a default. There is no way to say "the pot broke" without also saying how you know.
>
> A research team tested 60 monolingual Kelvane speakers and 60 monolingual English speakers, matched on years of formal schooling. Each participant sat in a room for twenty minutes and picked up 40 unrelated trivia facts from four different routes: ten appeared silently on a screen, ten were spoken aloud by a recorded voice, ten were handed over on a card by an experimenter who left the room, and ten had to be worked out by the participant themselves from other facts given earlier. The routes were randomly interleaved and participants were never told to pay attention to them.
>
> Afterwards, without warning, came a source test. For each of the 40 facts, participants saw four line-drawn icons — a screen, a loudspeaker, a hand offering a card, and a lightbulb — and pointed to the one showing how they had learned it. No words were required at any point in this test.
>
> Results: Kelvane speakers 87% correct, English speakers 61% correct. Chance is 25%. The authors conclude that grammatical evidentiality causally improves non-linguistic source memory.
>
> In 400 words: does this study establish what the authors claim? Identify the single most serious problem with the inference, say what additional condition would actually address it, and explain what the study can and cannot tell us about the strong and weak versions of linguistic relativity.

And here is the student's answer:

> The authors claim that speaking Kelvane changes how you remember, and I think the evidence is weaker than they say in some places and stronger in others.
>
> Start with the strong version of the Sapir-Whorf hypothesis, the idea that your language fixes the limits of what you can think. That is clearly not supported here. English speakers got 61%, which is far above the 25% you'd get from guessing at four icons, so they can obviously track where a fact came from even though their grammar never forces them to. Worth noting that Whorf himself probably wasn't a determinist — the slogan about language determining thought comes mostly from later textbook summaries rather than from his own writing, which was more about habitual patterns of attention than about hard walls. So the study can only really speak to the weak version.
>
> Now the design. The obvious objection is that Kelvane speakers simply tagged each item silently with a source word as it came in and then remembered the tag, meaning the whole thing was verbal rehearsal rather than memory. But that can't be the explanation in this case, because the test was pictorial: people pointed at a drawing, so no language was involved at retrieval. What the gap must reflect is that Kelvane speakers encode source as part of the event itself, which is exactly what the weak Whorfian claim predicts. So on my reading the study does confirm the weak version, at least for source information.
>
> There is however an ecological fallacy problem. They compare two groups and then infer something about the grammar operating inside individual heads, and a group-level correlation doesn't license that. The real worry is culture. A close-knit community with an oral tradition and constant face-to-face dealing has endless practical reasons to keep track of who said what, entirely apart from any suffixes on a verb. Literacy could push the other way too: heavy reading trains you to retain content and discard source, which might explain why the English group did worse. They matched on years of schooling but not on reading habits or on how much of daily life is oral.
>
> Finally I'd want a bilingual group. If Kelvane-English bilinguals landed between the two monolingual groups, that would be a dose-response relationship and would basically settle that the grammar is doing the causal work, since the only thing varying is how much Kelvane you have.
>
> Overall, this shows Kelvane speakers have better memory than English speakers, which is a striking amount of cognitive difference to hang on one bit of morphology.
