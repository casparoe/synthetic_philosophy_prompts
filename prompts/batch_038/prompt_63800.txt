I retired four years ago from protein crystallography — forty-one years at one bench, mostly on a decarboxylase nobody else ever cared about — and my wife says I've swapped the lab for the tablet, which is roughly fair. What happened is that I fell into Plutarch's Parallel Lives, and then for reasons I can't fully account for into a stack of medieval saints' lives, and I've been reading them the way I used to read other people's methods sections: for what has been left out. I've also been reading a bit of philosophy of history at night, badly. So this request may be a strange one.

My old department is holding a memorial in the big lecture theatre on the 12th for my PhD supervisor, Gilbert Ferrand, who died in April at 94, and they've asked me for the principal address, ten minutes or so. The room is going to be a mess. Sixty-odd crystallographers and biochemists who worked with him, forty-odd current students who never met him, his two daughters in the front row, and — I can't get around this — Thomas Vail, who is now a very distinguished professor in California and whom in 1979 Gil cut off the paper that made both of their careers. Vail has told people he's coming. He is coming to hear exactly what I say. My problem is that Gil was not a good man and he was an unusually great one, and every memorial adress I've ever sat through solves that by lying upward or by lying downward, and I refuse both. I've told the elder daughter what I intend to do and she told me to go ahead.

So what I want is an address built the way I've come to think Plutarch builds a life, and I want the building to be visible in the structure rather than in a couple of name-drops up front. Three things should do the work. First, the idea he opens the Alexander with — that he is writing lives and not histories, and that a slight thing, a phrase or a joke, often reveals a man's character better than battles where thousands fall. I want the speech to run on small particulars, so here are the ones I have, all true, use what you need: he kept everyone's notebooks, including the technicians', in a cabinet by his desk in numerical order and read them at night, so he usually knew what you were doing before you told him; he answered all his correspondence by hand in green ink, and the crank letters got the longest answers; he once made a postdoc discard eight months of crystals because she had written "probably contamination" in the margin instead of naming the contaminant — probably is not a datum — and she runs a lab in Zurich now and still makes her students do it; he could not bring himself to say he didn't know, so he would invent an answer on the spot, and about half the time the invented answer turned out to be right, and nobody including him ever tracked which halves; and for eleven years after his wife died he came in at four in the morning and slept in the department and the whole staff conspired not to mention it. Oh, and he never let a junior person pipette for him, my hands my error, and he meant that about credit too, up to a point, and the point was 1979.

Second, the passage at the start of the Lucullus where he says a portrait that reveals character is finer than one that merely copies features, and then — I have this written out in my notebook from the old Loeb — that the transgressions and follies which sully a man's career "we must regard rather as shortcomings in some particular excellence than as the vile products of positive baseness," and must treat "as though we were tenderly defending human nature for producing no character which is absolutely good." Note he writes "our history" in the same breath as insisting he is no historian, which I found telling. My question, which I have gone round and round on: is that advice generosity, or is it hagiography with better manners? I want the speech to come down somewhere on it and to own that it is coming down, rather than to perform balance. Third, the saints' lives: in those, the template is holiness, and the details that actually make a life readable are the ones that don't fit the template. I want the address to notice that knowing in advance what a life is meant to demonstrate is precisely what flattens it — and then to be uneasy about whether the same is true of this speech, since I already know what I want it to show.

Then my own idea, which is probably a bad one but which I want tried: the phase problem. In crystallography you measure the intensities of the diffracted beams and lose the phases, so you have the magnitudes and never the angles, and you can't build the structure until you get the phases back. Evidence about a person is like that — we get the intensities, what was done, when, to whom, in front of whom, and never the phases, never what it was from the inside. Two quite different models fit the same data. And the standard old trick for recovering the phases is to soak a heavy atom into the crystal, something foreign and dense whose position you can fix exactly, and then to take the difference between the pattern with it and the pattern without. I'd like the 1979 business to be the heavy atom in this speech: the one thing about Gil whose position we can locate precisely, and from which you get phases for everything else. Don't make it a lecture; the students should get the shape of it without knowing what a phase is.

Hard constraints, please take them literally. Don't quote Plutarch anywhere in the speech — refer to the ideas and name the Lives and that's all, because I don't trust my memory of translations and I would get them wrong on my feet. State one bad thing plainly, exactly once, with no softener and no "but" welded to the end of it; it can be the 1979 paper or it can be the fact that in 1988 he quietly told another institute's PI that his own senior technician was unreliable in order to stop her transferring, and she never found out and stayed nineteen more years — but only one of them, and only once. State one good thing plainly, exactly once, without inflating it. Banned words: complicated, flawed, legacy, giant, "a man of his time," and nothing about difficult times or uncertain times. Vail is not named, and the 1979 business may be in the speech — it has to be, it's my heavy atom — but not in a way that lets the room catch me addressing him, apologising to him, or using him; and not one sentence may be false if he is checking each one as it goes past. The last two minutes go to the students in the room and should be about how to read a life, not about their careers. Roughly 950 to 1100 words, written to be said out loud — I have asthma, so no sentence I can't get through in a single breath.

After the draft, give me five or six lines telling me which way you came down on the Lucullus question and how much weight you let the heavy-atom business carry, so I can overrule you. My wife thinks I am overengineering a eulogy. She's not wrong.
