Our head of comms has this as her email signature and I finally found where it comes from — apparently it was a catch-phrase of Neurath's Vienna museum, the one that grew into Isotype: "To remember simplified pictures is better than to forget accurate figures". She uses it like a trump card, and this week it killed a chart I'd built.

The chart was childhood asthma admissions by neighbourhood. I'd done it the Isotype way: rows of identical little person-icons, each standing for a fixed number of admissions, all the same size, no perspective, key underneath. Her three edits: delete the key ("nobody reads keys"), recolour so the worst three neighbourhoods go red and the rest grey, and run a big photo of a kid with an inhaler across the top. When I said the whole point of repeating identical signs is that they can be counted and compared, and that a red/grey recode plus a hero photo turns a quantity chart into a mood chart, she pointed at her signature.

So what precise claims could that sentence actually be making? I want the menu, not a pick-one-and-defend-it: the readings from the near-tautological up to the radical, each with a verdict — trivially true, empirical and actually testable, false, or true only under a qualifier. Spell out the parameters each reading silently fixes: remembered by whom, over how long, accurate in what respect, simplified how far, and which two alternatives are being compared. Include whether the slogan even assumes pictures and figures are measurable in the same units, because I think thats a lot of the sleight of hand.

Then tell me which reading, if any, actually gets her those three edits. i suspect none do — deleting the key doesn't trade accuracy for memorability, it just throws the accuracy away while keeping the picture — but I can't put it crisply. And finally, which reading is Neurath himself committed to given his own rules (repeat a standard sign rather than enlarge one, keep perspective out, pictures as a helping language alongside words rather than instead of them), and which would he reject? If a single use of the line slides between two readings, name the slide — that's the part I want to say out loud at the review on Thursday.
