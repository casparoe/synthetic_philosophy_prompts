I have been arguing with myself about advance directives for the better part of a decade, and the standard moves in that debate now feel like they have gone around as many times as they can bear. The case that keeps snagging me: a woman in her late sixties, wholly competent, writes a directive refusing antibiotics, tube feeding, and resuscitation in the event of advanced dementia, explaining in her own hand that she would regard a life without memory or agency as no life of hers at all. Twelve years later she is in exactly that state — no continuous memory, no recognition of her daughters, but demonstrably content, humming along to records she can no longer name, and pulling at any tube anyone tries to place. Her daughters want the directive honored; the ward nurse thinks honoring it means killing a happy patient on the written instructions of a stranger.

Now put this in front of John Stuart Mill in 1870 and ask him to rule. I want a serious reconstruction rather than a gesture at his reputation as a liberal. Work through the resources actually available to him: the harm principle and its clause about the individual being sovereign over his own body and mind; the explicit proviso that the principle is meant to apply only to human beings in the maturity of their faculties, and not to children or to those who still require being taken care of by others; his argument in the applications chapter that a man cannot validly sell himself into slavery, since a doctrine of freedom cannot require that he be free to destroy his own freedom; and his reluctance to have the law specifically enforce engagements whose performance is the promisor's own future conduct. Does the directive look to him like sovereignty exercised over a life as a whole, or like an alienation of the later self's freedom on precisely the model he refuses in the slavery case? Does the proviso swallow the case outright — is the demented woman, for Mill, in the position of a child, such that her own good becomes a sufficient warrant again? And how much weight would he give to the personal-identity worry that dominates the modern literature, given that he is a utilitarian about welfare and not obviously a metaphysician about persons?

Tell me where you land. I would rather have a definite verdict — enforce, do not enforce, or enforce only under some specification of the case — with the reasoning that gets Mill there and an honest note about which of his commitments is doing the work, than a survey of how one might read him either way. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables: an essay of the kind one could send as a long letter.
