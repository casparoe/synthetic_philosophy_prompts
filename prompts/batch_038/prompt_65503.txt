I am preparing a session on the principle of indifference for a graduate seminar in the philosophy of probability, built around a passage from Chapter XI, "The Calculus of Probabilities," of Poincaré's *Science and Hypothesis* (*La Science et l'hypothèse*, 1902), in the 1905 English translation. This is the chapter in which Poincaré raises the problem that now goes by Bertrand's name, and although the extract is short enough to hand out, I am not confident that I have correctly identified what Poincaré is committed to in it. Here it is:

The definition, it will be said, is very simple. The probability of an event is the ratio of the number of cases favourable to the event to the total number of possible cases. A simple example will show how incomplete this definition is:—I throw two dice. What is the probability that one of the two at least turns up a 6? Each can turn up in six different ways; the number of possible cases is 6 x 6 = 36. The number of favourable cases is 11; the probability is 11/36. That is the correct solution. But why cannot we just as well proceed as follows?—The points which turn up on the two dice form 6 x 7/2 = 21 different combinations. Among these combinations, six are favourable; the probability is 6/21. Now why is the first method of calculating the number of possible cases more legitimate than the second? In any case it is not the definition that tells us. We are therefore bound to complete the definition by saying, "… to the total number of possible cases, provided the cases are equally probable." So we are compelled to define the probable by the probable. How can we know that two possible cases are equally probable? Will it be by a convention? If we insert at the beginning of every problem an explicit convention, well and good! We then have nothing to do but to apply the rules of arithmetic and algebra, and we complete our calculation, when our result cannot be called in question. But if we wish to make the slightest application of this result, we must prove that our convention is legitimate, and we shall find ourselves in the presence of the very difficulty we thought we had avoided. It may be said that common-sense is enough to show us the convention that should be adopted. Alas! M. Bertrand has amused himself by discussing the following simple problem:—"What is the probability that a chord of a circle may be greater than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle?" The illustrious geometer successively adopted two conventions which seemed to be equally imperative in the eyes of common-sense, and with one convention he finds 1/2, and with the other 1/3. The conclusion which seems to follow from this is that the calculus of probabilities is a useless science, that the obscure instinct which we call common-sense, and to which we appeal for the legitimisation of our conventions, must be distrusted. But to this conclusion we can no longer subscribe. We cannot do without that obscure instinct. Without it, science would be impossible, and without it we could neither discover nor apply a law. Have we any right, for instance, to enunciate Newton's law? No doubt numerous observations are in agreement with it, but is not that a simple fact of chance? and how do we know, besides, that this law which has been true for so many generations will not be untrue in the next? To this objection the only answer you can give is: It is very improbable. But grant the law. By means of it I can calculate the position of Jupiter in a year from now. Yet have I any right to say this? Who can tell if a gigantic mass of enormous velocity is not going to pass near the solar system and produce unforeseen perturbations? Here again the only answer is: It is very improbable. From this point of view all the sciences would only be unconscious applications of the calculus of probabilities. And if this calculus be condemned, then the whole of the sciences must also be condemned.

In my notes I have been distinguishing two claims that this material might be taken to support. On the first, call it the *partition* claim, the chord problem only shows that the question was ill posed: specify a sampling procedure and the ambiguity disappears, leaving a determinate answer. On the second, the *warrant* claim, specifying a procedure never discharges the harder question of why that procedure rather than another equally natural one is the right basis for assigning probabilities, so the difficulty survives every proposed solution to the paradox. My working hypothesis is that Poincaré starts with something like the partition claim, slides into the warrant claim, and then moves to a third issue — induction — that neither of them entails. Please argue me out of that hypothesis if it is wrong. Specifically:

1. Reconstruct the argument as a sequence of steps and mark which of them Poincaré asserts and which he merely voices for an interlocutor. The extract is studded with hedges — "it will be said," "It may be said that common-sense is enough," "Alas!," "But to this conclusion we can no longer subscribe" — and I want to know whether the sceptical conclusion he reports is attributed to Bertrand, floated as a temptation, or advanced as Poincaré's own.

2. What exactly is the charge in "we are compelled to define the probable by the probable"? Is the definition being accused of vicious circularity, or is the point that equiprobability is an undefined primitive whose application must be justified from outside the calculus? And does the dice example really support that charge? The 21-combination partition is not equiprobable, and it seems to me that once the clause "provided the cases are equally probable" is appended, the dice case is settled by the physical symmetry of the die with no appeal to common sense at all. If so, the two examples differ in a way that matters, and I want to know whether Poincaré's argument can survive that difference or whether he needs them to be alike.

3. Poincaré reports two answers, 1/2 and 1/3, where the standard modern treatment of the problem gives three. Does his argument need only two? Is the third way of choosing — treating the midpoint of the chord as uniform over the disc rather than uniform along a radius — different in kind from the two he cites, and would including it have strengthened or weakened his point about common sense?

4. What is the logical form of the turn at "we can no longer subscribe" and of the extension to Newton's law and Jupiter? Is this an indispensability argument (science is impossible without the instinct, therefore the instinct is to be trusted), a reductio (condemning the calculus commits you to condemning all science), or something weaker and pragmatic? Does the inference from indispensability to legitimacy go through, and does "no longer" imply that Poincaré once subscribed to the conclusion?

5. In "all the sciences would only be unconscious applications of the calculus of probabilities," is the operative notion of probability the same one at issue in the chord problem? The chord case concerns a choice among reference classes where there may be no fact of the matter; the Jupiter case concerns a future contingency assessed against background knowledge. If these come apart, tell me where the analogy genuinely holds and where it is rhetorical — I would rather be told it is rhetorical than have it assumed to be an argument.

6. Does the passage commit Poincaré to treating equiprobability assumptions as conventions in something like the sense in which he treats the choice of a geometry as a convention, or would he reject that assimilation? And among the options available now — symmetry or group-invariance constraints on priors, maximum-entropy reasoning, the insistence that the question is simply not well posed until a procedure is supplied, or a contextualist view on which the conversational context fixes the reference class — which, if any, would count as making his "obscure instinct" rigorous rather than explaining it away? Does his worry survive all of them?

I am not looking for bibliographical pointers or a survey of the secondary literature; I have those. What I want is a close reading of the extract and your own judgement on the questions above, including a candid verdict on whether my partition/warrant distinction misdescribes what Poincaré is doing.
