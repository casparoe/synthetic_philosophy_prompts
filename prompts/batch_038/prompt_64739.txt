I teach a philosophy of time seminar and I want to screen Arrival as a foil to Gödel's rotating universe rather than as the usual vehicle for foreknowledge-and-free-will puzzles. Two things have to be kept straight. On Gödel's side, the philosophical work isn't done by the prospect of meeting your younger self but by the fact that his 1949 solution admits closed timelike curves and hence no global ordering of events into past, present, and future — which is what he took to show that objective lapse isn't a feature of reality, even though that universe isn't ours (it rotates rather than expands). On the film's side, nothing about spacetime changes: Louise's transformation is cognitive, grounded in Chiang's source story in variational physics and in the film in acquiring the heptapods' non-linear writing, and what she gains is knowledge of events that, for everyone around her, haven't happened. Can an epistemic device do the work Gödel's structural one does, or does the film presuppose the very thing he denies — a single real ordering relative to which her knowledge is genuinely of the future? I'd also like the reading to say where the film resists Gödel instead of illustrating him: her choice to have Hannah, and the grief that choice already contains, seem to depend on the asymmetry between what she knows and what she hasn't yet lived, an asymmetry that carries weight only if becoming is real. Close with what the film adds that the argument can't supply — is there something it is like to hold a whole life at once, and is that a philosophical claim or a mood? I don't want to hand my students a reading that trades on confusing foreknowledge with block structure.
