I am convening a session of an interdisciplinary reading group on bounded rationality, and I want to build it around Borges's short story "Funes the Memorious." The group has worked through Herbert Simon on satisficing — aspiration levels, the contrast between economic man and administrative man, and Simon's image of rationality as a pair of scissors whose two blades are the cognitive limits of the actor and the structure of the task environment — as well as Gigerenzer's claim that fast and frugal heuristics can exploit environmental structure and sometimes outperform more demanding methods. The story is short and everyone will have read it, but I want the criticism written up in advance so that the discussion has something substantial to push against.

Here is the reading I want you to develop, and then to test for how much weight it will bear. Funes looks at first like the anti-Simon: after his fall from the horse he has no storage limit and no retrieval cost, so the standard diagnosis of why we satisfice — search is expensive, and our models must be coarse because we cannot compute better ones — appears not to apply to him at all. And yet he is precisely the agent who cannot decide, cannot plan, cannot generalize, and cannot sleep, because every crack and moulding of the houses around him is present to him at once. I want you to say where in the decision pipeline his failure actually sits, using the episodes the story gives us: the enumeration scheme in which every number receives its own arbitrary name, and the narrator's point that this is the exact opposite of a system of enumeration; the fact that reconstructing one past day in full detail occupies a further full day; the insomnia; the incapacity for general ideas; the narrator's judgement that these absurd projects nevertheless carry a kind of stammering grandeur; the death at nineteen from congestion of the lungs.

Four questions I would like answered inside the reading rather than appended to it. First: does Funes show that satisficing is not merely a concession to limited computation but constitutive of thought — that an agent able to entertain every distinction at once could never arrive at a choice? If so, does that collapse Simon's careful separation between a descriptive account of how people decide and a normative claim about how they should decide, given that Simon insisted his was a description and not an apology for second-best? Second: Blackwell's result says that for a Bayesian decision maker a more informative signal is never worse, provided processing is free; Funes drowns in information and cannot act. Does he refute the theorem, expose a suppressed hypothesis, or simply fail to satisfy its assumptions in a way that tells us nothing? Give a verdict, and say what work the word "free" is doing in that proviso. Third: is his trouble one of attention — he cannot ignore — or of conception — he cannot treat a token as an instance of a type? I suspect the second is the deeper one, since an aspiration level is a stopping rule and stopping requires seeing two situations as the same situation; test that hypothesis and tell me whether it survives contact with the text. Fourth: does Funes serve as a reductio of the less-is-more thesis, or does he show only what happens when there is no environment for a heuristic to be tuned to, given that he is paralyzed, confined to a dark patio, and without practical projects?

Then be honest about where the reading breaks. Funes is not a decision maker in any ordinary sense: he has no stakes, no future, no goals of the kind Simon's administrative man has, and Borges frames him as a failed Platonist rather than a failed optimizer. Tell me how much of an instrumentalist grid the story will support before it starts to distort, and where you would advise me to stop pushing it in the seminar.

Close by saying what the story contributes to the bounded rationality debate that argument alone cannot — whether it is the felt texture of a mind without abstractions, or the fact that it makes an intuitive rather than a deductive case for the necessity of forgetting, or something else you find there.

Aim for roughly fifteen hundred to eighteen hundred words of criticism, organised as continuous prose with the questions treated as movements rather than as a questionnaire. No bibliography, no citations, and no attempts to reproduce Borges's wording: my translation differs from yours in any case, so describe the episodes instead of quoting them.
