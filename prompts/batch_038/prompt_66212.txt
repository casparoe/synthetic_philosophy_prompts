I write documentation for a living at a mid-size software company, and once a quarter our internal engineering newsletter runs a piece of invented prose from whoever volunteers. I've put my name down for the next issue, and I'd like to contribute a parody of a help-center article — the kind of page that lives on a SaaS vendor's docs site — for a fictional product that upgrades ordinary mental states into conscious ones by pairing each of them with a suitable higher-order representation. The company, the product, the support engineers, and every quoted customer should be invented. I don't want any real philosopher named or thinly disguised: the target is a view and the discourse that surrounds it, not a person.

Here is what I understand of that view as an amateur reading around it, and please correct me where I've gone wrong, because the piece depends on the details being right rather than on my impressions: a mental state is conscious not in virtue of anything intrinsic to it but because there is a higher-order thought about it, formed without inference and without self-observation; that higher-order thought must itself remain unconscious, otherwise you would need a further one about it, and so on without end; the subject has to possess the relevant concept for the state to be promoted, so a red experience requires having red in one's repertoire at all; and there is a standing dispute between those who insist the higher-order thought must actually occur and those who say it is enough that the state be merely available to one. There is also the uncomfortable case in which the higher-order thought fires and there is no first-order state there for it to be about. My reading is that some people treat that as fatal and at least one defender treats it as a feature, on the grounds that it would explain cases where what someone seems to be experiencing and what they can actually put to use come apart.

The humor should come entirely from that machinery, not from stock remarks about consciousness being mysterious or hard. Structure it like real documentation: a title, a version line and an applies-to line, then sections for eligibility, a plan comparison, troubleshooting, known issues, and an escalation path, closing with the standard was-this-helpful footer. Second person, mildly apologetic, relentlessly reassuring, with a couple of cross-references to pages that obviously don't exist. The document should never step outside itself to explain the philosophy or signal that it's a joke; the objections should be legible only as support's cheerful concessions — a customer reports something broken, and the reply explains that it is working exactly as designed.

Entries I'd like to see, though you should improve, reorder, or cut them as your judgment dictates: a ticket about a promotion that arrived with nothing underneath it; a ticket asking why the upgrade team appears on no org chart and is never itself upgraded; a ticket from someone whose dog, or infant, seems to have states in abundance and none of the required concepts; a question about whether a household device that has states about its own states qualifies; a plan-comparison section that quietly fudges the one difference that matters between the tiers; an optional beta tier in which the state promotes itself, which nobody has ever successfully provisioned; and one place where a customer asks why any of this makes it feel like anything at all, answered in pure marketing copy.

Aim for 800 to 900 words. It should be readable to engineers who have never opened a philosophy of mind paper, and funny to someone who has.

After the piece, add a short note, clearly separated and not part of the document, telling me where it took liberties: which entries are fair renderings of objections the theory actually faces and which are comic exaggeration, which side of the actualist/dispositionalist dispute you wrote against and what would change if you'd picked the other, and any place where my summary above is simply wrong about the view.

One last thing, and I mean it rather than offering it as a formality: if you think this shouldn't be done as I've described it — if the help-center form can't really carry this material, or if you think a fair parody of this view has to strawman it to get any laughs, or if a fictional product can't convey the theory to readers who've never met it and the piece will just be inside jokes with no content — say so plainly and tell me what you would write instead. I would far rather have that objection than a polite, mediocre document.
