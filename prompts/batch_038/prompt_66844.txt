Retired from statistical physics about two years back and I've fallen down the philosophy rabbit hole — ethics, mostly, and the machine side of it. Here's the view I've come round to, roughly: you can't just write down the right ends directly, any more than you'd write down the equilibrium state of a system by hand. So instead you specify a deliberative procedure — take an agent, idealize them (full information, no reasoning errors, vivid acquaintance with everything relevant), then let them reflect until their wants stop moving — and whatever that process settles on is the target. Fixed point rather than axiom set.

What would count against this? I keep circling the same worries I'd have about any dynamical system: does the process converge at all, is the fixed point unique or does it depend on which basin you start in, and is the limit even well defined when the idealizing conditions themselves have to be specified normatively — which looks circular. Also whether the idealized agent is still recognizably the same agent by the end, since a lot of what someone wants seems to be tied to thier particular limited perspective.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely embarrassing, tell me which ones a committed defender would cheerfully concede, and say whether the whole thing has been qualified into something that cant fail — and if it has, whether that's actually a problem for a view that's meant to be a method rather than a prediction.
