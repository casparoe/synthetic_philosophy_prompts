I've been going down a rabbit hole on the algorithmic fairness impossibility results lately, mostly through secondary sources — I'm not a statistician, tho I did sit through enough of a probability sequence to not be totally lost, and I read enough rationalist-adjacent stuff that I have a reflex for "this sounds like a gotcha, check the hypotheses." A friend sent me a longread in a tech magazine with a section on it, and I typed up the relevant part so I wouldn't misremember it:

'There are two obvious things you might want from a risk score used in, say, bail or parole decisions. First, calibration: when the model says a set of defendants is 30% likely to reoffend, about 30% of them should actually reoffend — and this should hold inside each racial group seperately, not just on average. Second, equal error rates: the model should wrongly flag low-risk people as high-risk at the same rate, and miss genuinely high-risk people at the same rate, across groups. A landmark proof established that these two can never both be satisfied — not by any model, not with better data, not with more compute. It is a theorem about possibility, in the same league as Arrow's theorem or the impossibility of perfectly secure encryption, and it means every risk score is guaranteed to be biased against somebody: either the numbers mean something different for different groups, or the model makes more mistakes about one group than another. The only way out is a predictor that never errs, which statistics does not allow. Since you cannot have both, the choice is purely political, and the right choice is to give up calibration: a calibrated score just ratifies whatever base rates happen to obtain, and those base rates were produced by decades of over-policing, so calibrating to them is laundering the past through a formula.'

My gut says this is one of those cases where the shape is right and the details are load-bearing-wrong, but I can't tell which details, and I'd rather not just assume the magazine is sloppy becuase that's the cheap move. Can you go through it claim by claim and sort it into three buckets: what a fairness researcher would sign off on without qualification, what's a defensible popularization that's silently dropping a hypothesis, and what's actually false? Four things I specifically want pinned down. (1) Is the impossibility really unconditional the way the article says, or is there a condition being omitted — and if so, how likely is that condition to hold in the settings people actually fight about? (2) Is "the only way out is a predictor that never errs" the whole story? I have a vague memory that people do things with thresholds after the score comes out, and I can't tell whether that dodges the theorem or just relocates the unfairness to a different place where it's harder to see. (3) Is "either the numbers mean something different for different groups, or the model makes more mistakes about one group" a fair rendering of a genuine dichotomy? Those two options don't feel like contraries to me — I can imagine a score doing both at once, or a degenerate one doing neither, and I want to know if that's a real hole or my own confusion about what the quantities are measuring. (4) Is the last move — drop calibration because base rates are politically contaminated — a consequence of the theorem, or an independent normative argument wearing a lab coat? I suspect the latter, but I also suspect it might be a good argument, and I want those two questions answered seperately.

Two extras. Someone on a forum claimed the tradeoff basically evaporates if your model is accurate enough, that it's only severe for weak predictors and you should just go build a better model. Is that right, or is it cope, and does the answer change depending on whether you're asking about exact versus approximate satisfaction of the criteria? And a meta question I care about more than it probably sounds like: how much work is the rhetoric doing? Comparing this to Arrow and to encryption impossibility makes a bounded tradeoff result feel like a refutation of the whole enterprise, and I'd like to know what a reader who swallowed that framing would predict wrongly about, e.g., what happens when a jurisdiction switches which criterion it optimizes.
