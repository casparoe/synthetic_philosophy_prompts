Our team is finalizing a fairness position statement for a machine-learning screening tool, and I have been asked to set out the commitments the statement rests on before it circulates. What I want is not a defense of each commitment in isolation but an audit of the set as a whole: which of them pull against one another, which only appear to, and what the cheapest repair is in each case.

The setting. We build a score that ranks applicants for a large employer's open positions, predicting the performance rating a supervisor assigns at the end of the first year. Race is collected for audit purposes only. In our data, mean performance ratings — and therefore the base rate of applicants who count as successful — differ across racial groups. The statement asserts the following.

1. The impossibility result in this area is a mathematical theorem, not a normative argument. It establishes that certain formal criteria cannot be jointly satisfied by any non-trivial score when base rates differ across groups; it does not establish which criterion one ought to adopt. That choice is moral and political, and the theorem is silent on it.

2. Our target criterion is sufficiency: calibration within groups. Among applicants assigned any given score, the observed rate of successful performance should be the same regardless of race, and among applicants we advance, the rate of success should be the same regardless of race. We privilege this criterion because it preserves the score's interpretation as an estimate of probability, and because — unlike the alternatives — it does not require us to make the groups come out equal on anything.

3. We also hold that the burdens of error must fall equally. Both the false positive rate (applicants who go on to perform poorly but are ranked above the cut) and the false negative rate (applicants who would have performed well but are ranked below it) should be equal across racial groups. In an adverse-decision setting, unequal exposure to error is itself the harm, not merely a symptom of one.

4. Because 2 and 3 cannot both hold exactly when base rates differ, our proposed resolution is to retain a single calibrated score and to apply group-specific decision thresholds selected to equalize false positive rates. This leaves the score, and hence its calibration, untouched; only the point at which we act on it changes.

5. Individuals who are equally likely to perform well should receive the same decision, whatever their race. We regard this as the individual-level counterpart of 2 — both are concerned with how particular persons are treated rather than with how group averages come out — and we expect them to reinforce one another.

6. The base rates in our data are not a neutral benchmark. Group differences in supervisor-assigned ratings are, we hold, substantially artifacts of discriminatory evaluation practice and of unequal prior opportunity rather than of differences in the trait the score is meant to track. A fairness target that treats those differences as reflecting genuine risk will encode the injustice it claims only to measure.

7. Since the criteria are jointly unsatisfiable, the statement should say publicly that whatever thresholds we adopt, some group will bear a disproportionate share of the error, and that no technically fair configuration is available to us. We regard this candor as part of the point of publishing the statement at all.

One further line, which I mention for completeness: the statement commits us to publishing error rates disaggregated by race every quarter, while also insisting that the model is group-blind — no racial variable, and no deliberate proxy for one, enters the feature set or the decision rule.

What I would like from you:

- Identify the genuine tensions, ordered by severity rather than by the order in which they appear above. For each, state precisely which commitment has to be modified or dropped, what the modified version would say, and what the statement loses by the revision.
- Identify separately any pair that only looks inconsistent, and explain why it survives inspection. I would rather be told that a pair is fine than have it flagged along with everything else.
- Where a commitment is offered as the resolution of a conflict, check whether it actually resolves it, or whether it relocates the problem.
- Say whether the most severe tension you find is an artifact of this particular deployment — adverse decisions, a contested outcome label, an employer setting — or whether it would arise for any prediction task of this shape.
- Close by naming the single revision that would clear the most, and say what it commits us to that we may not have intended.

No references or further reading, and no restatement of the theorem itself; I want the tracing of what these commitments oblige us to, including where they oblige us to more than we have noticed.
