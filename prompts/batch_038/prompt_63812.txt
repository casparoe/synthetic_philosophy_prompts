I spent three hours last night arguing with a friend — I work in health policy, she is finishing a doctorate in moral philosophy — about whether people who disagree about what is good should strike deals with one another. She calls it moral trade and thinks I should too. Her position is that we should trade aggressively: none of us is confident about the right axiology, each of us can produce more of what the other values than we can produce ourselves, and a refusal to deal is just a fastidious way of producing less good. My position, or what I took to be my position, is that some trades are plainly good and others are a kind of corruption; but by the end of the evening I could not state the difference in a way she did not find question-begging, and she argued that every line I drew was either a covert restatement of my own values or an arbitrary stopping point. So I would like to find out what my verdicts actually add up to.

Here they are, in the order I gave them. (1) A blood service agrees not to schedule its annual drive on a particular religious holiday; a congregation agrees to publicize the drive among its members. Neither thinks much of the other's reasons, and both end up with more of what they value. Fine — better than either acting alone. (2) A prosecutor who believes the defendant is guilty agrees to drop the charge if the defendant's family funds a clinic she cares deeply about. Not fine, and not a close call. (3) Someone offers to pay me to keep quiet about something I believe to be true. Fine; people may buy my silence. (4) Someone offers to pay me to endorse in print something I believe to be false. Not fine, even where the coalition's overall output would be better with me aboard. (5) Two neighbours: one stops reporting the other's unpermitted construction work, the other keeps his dogs quiet. Both gain. Not fine — though I cannot say whether what offends me is the danger to the people living near the construction or the fact that a reporting duty was the thing being traded. (6) Two advocacy groups agree to specialize: each funds only the cause it values more, and neither criticizes the other publicly. The specialization is fine; the no-criticism clause is not, but I seem unable to say why without just repeating (4). (7) I keep flying to conferences but pay for the removal of the carbon those flights emit. Fine — I have settled a bill. (8) I keep eating factory-farmed chicken but give enough to animal charities to prevent, on my own estimate, more suffering than my diet causes. Not fine — this feels like buying a licence. She pressed me on the fact that (7) and (8) have the same structure, and I had no answer. (9) A hospital agrees to stop providing a procedure that a religious community regards as murder, in exchange for that community dropping its campaign against the hospital's other services; on my arithmetic the deal saves more lives than it costs. This is the one I keep returning to. I lean toward not fine, but I suspect I would fold if the numbers were extreme enough, and I do not know whether that makes me a consequentialist who flinches or simply inconsistent.

My own guess at a principle is that a trade is permissible when each party gives up only what is theirs to give up, and nobody outside the deal is made worse off. It handles (2), (5), and part of (9), and it says the right thing about (1). But it cannot be right as stated: my diet is mine to give up as comfortably as my silence is, and (7) and (8) violate the second clause equally, since the animals and the people downstream of the emissions are both outside the deal. Adding an offsetting clause to rescue (7) would rescue (8) too, and I do not want it to. So: state the principle, or the small set of principles, that best fits this pattern, precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I have not imagined, and tie each clause to the case that motivates it. Where the fit fails — and I expect (7)/(8) to be the breaking point — tell me plainly which verdict the account forces me to abandon, and whether the simpler principle that overturns a verdict is better than the baroque one that saves them all. Then run your account on two cases I have not listed: paying a healthy person to give a kidney to a stranger who will benefit from it more than she will, and a government agreeing to relax an environmental standard in exchange for a firm siting its plant in a poorer district rather than a richer one. I am aware that there is work on incommensurable values, on taboo trade-offs, and on the claim that pricing certain goods wrongs them; I am not asking for a survey or for references, only for whether any of it predicts these verdicts and where it fails to. Finally, tell me what would change your mind — which single one of my verdicts, if I gave it up, would let a much simpler account through, and at which step in your own reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong.
