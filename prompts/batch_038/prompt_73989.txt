I am a second-year philosophy undergraduate, and next week I have to lead twenty minutes of my early-modern tutorial on the essay Hume wrote on suicide — the piece he pulled from his volume of dissertations before it went to press, and which only circulated after his death. My tutor has told us to come prepared to state Hume's argument in premises and conclusion, and to identify one place where it looks vulnerable. The essay opens like this:

> "One considerable advantage that arises from Philosophy, consists in the sovereign antidote which it affords to superstition and false religion. All other remedies against that pestilent distemper are vain, or at least uncertain. Plain good sense and the practice of the world, which alone serve most purposes of life, are here found ineffectual: History as well as daily experience furnish instances of men endowed with the strongest capacity for business and affairs, who have all their lives crouched under slavery to the grossest superstition. Even gaiety and sweetness of temper, which infuse a balm into every other wound, afford no remedy to so virulent a poison; [...]"

The stretch that actually defeats me comes a few paragraphs later, and since my scan is poor I will render it as a close paraphrase rather than type out the wording: Hume asks us to consider the principle that disposing of human life belongs so peculiarly to the Almighty that for a man to dispose of it, or to determine concerning it, would be an encroachment on the Almighty's right; he then observes that if that principle held, it would equally be an encroachment for us to preserve or lengthen life when we find it troublesome, or to defend it against the injuries to which it is naturally exposed — the river diverted from its course is no more a violation of providence than the house built or the medicine administered; and finally he presses the comparison that the life of a man is of no greater importance to the universe than that of an oyster, so that a principle which made the disposal of the one impious would have to make the disposal of the other impious too.

Please help me understand what this machinery is doing. Specifically: is the move against the encroachment principle a pure reductio — the opponent's principle licenses too much, so the principle must go — or does Hume also need a positive thesis about how divine government works, namely that providence operates through general laws of which human prudence and agency are themselves instances? Those look like different claims with different burdens, and I cannot tell which one the passage is relying on at each step. Relatedly, the parity cases seem to be doing two distinct jobs: the diverted river and the built house concern human artifice altering the course of nature, whereas the medicine and the defence against injury concern lengthening a life, which is the mirror image of shortening one. Does the second group require a symmetry premise — that if shortening life is an encroachment, lengthening it must be one too — that a theist could simply refuse, on the grounds that God commands the preservation of life so that prolonging it is obedience rather than trespass? If so, does Hume have a reply available inside the passage, or is that a genuine hole? And what exactly is the oyster comparison contributing: an argument from cosmic insignificance, such that a principle resting on the weightiness of what is destroyed cannot single out human beings, or an argument from the uniformity of providence, such that nothing special can be inferred about men because the same government extends to oysters? I want to know which reading makes the sentence load-bearing, and whether the first reading sits uneasily with a sentimentalist moral theory that does not rank beings by their importance to the universe. My own tentative diagnosis, which I would like you to test rather than confirm, is that the argument turns on an equivocation between two senses of the course of nature — the whole ordained causal order, which includes human action, and nature as a domain contrasted with human artifice — and that Hume slides from the first sense, where his conclusion follows trivially, to the second, where the opponent's principle was actually aimed. Finally, tell me what the clearing of the charge of impiety establishes: Hume's essay works through the question as a possible offence against God, against one's neighbour, and against oneself, and the last section argues roughly that no one discards life while it is worth keeping, because the natural horror of death is such that whoever has recourse to this remedy must be under a misfortune or a temper that makes existence worse than non-existence. Does discharging all three charges amount to a positive permission, or only to the removal of a religious veto — and does that last section quietly convert the permissibility of suicide into a claim about the rationality of the agent's own assessment, so that the substantive work is being done by a premise about what a person in despair can be presumed to have judged? I am after reconstruction and diagnosis, not a survey of the secondary literature, and no readings or references please.
