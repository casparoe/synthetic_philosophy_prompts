We're doing a unit this term on cultures that punish success — tall poppy syndrome, Jantelagen — set against the American Dream, and on Tuesday a classmate offered what he took to be a decisive analogy. A Jante society, he said, is like a bucket of crabs: no lid is needed, because the moment one crab starts climbing out the others haul it back down; the American habit of celebrating its winners at least leaves the bucket open, however much the crabs still inside resent the one that gets out. He used the picture to argue that norms of self-effacement are simply pathological — a collective refusal to let anyone escape — and that a culture's cruelties toward its successful members are the price of an open bucket.

Could you audit that analogy for me? I'd like the correspondences spelled out — what is the bucket, what is the lid, who are the climbers and who are the pullers — and then the disanalogies that actually matter for the use he is putting it to separated from the ones that are surface noise. Two spots I'd especially like you to press on: whether the bucket's walls survive the transfer to human societies (who built them, and are they natural or made), and whether the relation between the escaping crab and the ones it leaves behind does. Then tell me whether his conclusion holds up once the disanalogies are on the table, and if it doesn't, propose a better analogy for the difference between a Jante culture and a tall-poppy culture and test that one too. One thing my professor added, which I don't know how to weigh: the poppy image goes back to Livy's story of Tarquin answering his son's messenger by knocking off the tallest poppies in his garden, a signal about which eminent men to eliminate rather than about envy. Say whether that genealogy does any real work here or is merely decorative.

I'd rather you commit than hedge — give me a verdict on whether the crab argument survives and your reasons for it, not a survey of what could be said on each side. And please write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter: no headings, bullet points, bold, or tables.
