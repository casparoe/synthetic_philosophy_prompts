Ok so I'm working through something about Mill and I keep going back and forth — I want a real argument, not a both-sides shrug.

The setup as I understand it: Mill spent essentially his whole working life inside the East India Company's correspondence office, ending up as examiner of correspondence until the Company was wound up in 1858, so he wasn't theorizing empire from an armchair, he was drafting the dispatches. And his defence of foreign rule over India is explicitly developmental and time-indexed: it's legitimate because and only for as long as it improves people's capacity to govern themselves, and a tutelage with no exit is just despotism with better paperwork. On top of that, in the Representative Government he argues that taking part in government has value over and above the outcomes it produces, because it forms an active rather than a passive kind of character.

Here's the case I want you to actually engage with. It's hypothetical but realistic. A consortium of foundations plus one bilateral aid agency takes over the running of schools and primary health clinics in a district of a low-income country, on a ten-year renewable performance contract. Payment is tied to independently verified outcomes — learning assessments, under-five mortality, vaccination coverage — and the interventions themselves are chosen on the basis of randomized trials run by outside researchers. The district's elected council retains formal sovereignty and can decline to renew. In practice it never does, because declining means losing the money and losing the measured gains. By every metric anyone is tracking, the consortium is much better than the state apparatus it replaced. Nobody is coerced in any old-fashioned sense: parents can send their children elsewhere, the schools teach the national curriculum, the local language is used in early grades.

Does Mill sign off on this? I want you to notice the structural problem I think is sitting in the middle of it, which is that his test for a legitimate tutelage and the contract's test for success are not the same test. His test is whether the pupil is becoming capable of self-government. The contract's test is whether outcomes are improving. Those come apart, and in a particular direction: a contractor paid on outcomes is rewarded for producing outcomes, and a population that has learned to defer to a competently run outside administrator can score beautifully forever without ever acquiring the capacity Mill cared about. The exit right is nominally present and practically inert, which I'd have thought is exactly the configuration he was worried about — though I can see the counter, that a renewable contract with a stated exit right is the most accountable arrangement anyone had ever invented for running a poor district, far more accountable than anything the Company had, and that Mill would say the council's refusal to exercise the exit right is itself the evidence that it's become capable and is choosing well.

So: give me your best guess at his verdict, and take both readings of his own view seriously instead of just noting that they exist. Where I'd push you hardest is that the temporary, self-terminating character of his argument is what makes a renewable contract with an unexercised exit right genuinely hard to classify, and I dont think the two standard reflexes — Mill as straightforward racist imperialist, Mill as subtle and self-critical liberal — do any work at all on that specific problem. They just restate the disagreement.

Separately, and this is the part I'm least sure about: does his answer change depending on whether the district sits in a country Mill would have counted as civilised, or in one of the societies he thought could legitimately be governed from outside for a period? Say whether the whole case is contaminated by that distinction or whether it can be run without it — because if the honest answer is that the arrangement is only legitimate in the second kind of place, then his view isn't buying me anything I can use.
