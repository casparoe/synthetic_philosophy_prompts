I've been going around in circles on whether stakes change what you know, so I wrote down my actual gut verdicts on a pile of cases and they clearly don't hang together. I don't want the standard tour of the debate — I want to know what principle, if any, delivers my verdicts, and which of them I'd have to give up.

The cases.

Bottle in my hand, label says 50mg. If its my own headache I'll say flat out that I know what I'm holding. If it's a dose for my niece, who's got a strict ceiling and I'd be calling an ambulance if I got it wrong — same bottle, same label, same pharmacy, same five seconds of looking at it — now I say I don't know it, I just believe it.

Lottery. My friend has a ticket, nothing whatever rides on it, she'd shrug if it lost. I still say she doesn't know it'll lose, even though those odds are better than the odds the label is right.

Grad student who worked through the proof of the theorem herself, line by line, and the stakes are her entire career. I say she knows it. Stakes didn't touch that one at all.

My uncle takes a pill every morning that his doctor prescribed. If he turns out to have the allergy nobody's tested him for, he dies; he has no idea that's on the table and no reason to suspect it. My instinct is that he knows it's the right pill — he knows, it's just that knowing isn't much use to him. But that looks like I'm letting stakes off the hook any time the subject is ignorant of them, which can't be right either.

And the mirror image: a colleague who's convinced that missing the 8:05 will cost her the job and then the visa, so she won't say she knows the 8:05 is on time — same timetable, same platform as the rest of us who say we know it perfectly well. Objectively almost nothing rides on it; her contract says so; she just doesn't believe the contract. My gut sides with her anxiety rather than with the actual consequences, and I don't like that about my gut.

Third parties. I know the flight lands at six, no problem. Then I remember my cousin is going to drive three hours to collect him on my say-so and can't easily turn back. Now I feel like I've stopped knowing it, or at least that I shouldn't have said it out loud — and I genuinely can't tell which of those two feelings I'm having.

Retroactive. Last year I formed a belief offhand about which folder a document was filed in; today I find out a whole dispute turns on it. My instinct is that my knowledge evaporated backwards, which sounds insane, but I can't shake it.

And the one that might be the real tell. Same stakes, no possible check: I'm on the plane, I can't ask anybody, there is nothing I could do to find out whether this is the Denver flight, and a lot rides on it. I say I still know where I am. Compare the pill for my niece — same stakes, but the pharmacy is open and I could ring them in two minutes. There I say I don't know. So maybe its not stakes at all; maybe its whether I'm sitting on a cheap check I'm not bothering to do.

So: is there a principle that gets all or most of this? I can already see that "higher stakes, more evidence required" doesn't, because of the lottery and the grad student. Tell me what the clauses would have to be and which case motivates each one; tell me what it says about a case I haven't listed — say, high stakes, a cheap check exists, but I've already decided I'm not going to do it: do I lose the knowledge the moment I decide, or only once the window closes? — and then be straight with me about which of my verdicts the best principle forces me to abandon. I also want to know whether my pattern is even the stakes-shifting one people usually have in mind, or whether something less exotic fits better: 'knows' being context-sensitive, my really applying a rule about what you're allowed to say, or the dull point that high-stakes people just gather more evidence so their evidence isn't the same after all. Rank those against my list and say where each one breaks.
