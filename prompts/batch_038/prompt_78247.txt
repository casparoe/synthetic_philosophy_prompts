I am assembling discussion material for an advanced seminar on responsibility, and I want to retire the two cases the topic of moral luck almost always gets taught with — the driver whose identical lapse of attention kills a child in one instance and nobody in the other, and the person who would have acquiesced in a wicked regime had he happened to be living inside it. My students have met both elsewhere, and both have a structure that hides something. So I have written a replacement case, and I would like a dialogue built around it rather than around the familiar ones.

The case is this. Ines Varela is the duty engineer for a regional flood authority, responsible for deciding when to bring a set of backup pumping stations online ahead of forecast storms. On the first occasion, she judges that the forecast does not warrant the cost and the downtime, keeps the pumps offline, and redirects the money to a deferred maintenance program. She is not reckless: she reads the same models her predecessors read, consults two colleagues, documents her reasoning, and the authority's later internal review finds no procedural fault. The storm veers out to sea. Nothing is flooded. She is commended for the saving; her reasoning is written up as a departmental heuristic; and the council, reassured by the authority's public account of how prudently the catchment is managed, approves a substantial residential development on the lower floodplain over the following decade. Eleven years later, Ines — still competent, still conscientious, and now with a decade of institutional confirmation behind her judgment — makes the same call on the same kind of evidence, keeping the same pumps offline. This time the storm arrives as forecast, the defences are overwhelmed, and seventeen people die. Note three features I want the discussion to keep in view. First, her epistemic position at the two moments is as close to identical as I can make it: no new warning existed on the second occasion that she ignored, and no old warning existed on the first that she dismissed. Second, the outcome of the first decision partly produced the conditions of the second, in two ways at once — it hardened her own confidence and the department's practice, and it put far more people in the path of the water. Third, if outcomes matter to desert, then it looks as though the second failure can change the moral status of the first decision itself, after the fact, so that what was a praiseworthy economy in the year it happened becomes a culpable gamble viewed from eleven years later, even though nothing about that earlier act has changed except the world's subsequent history.

Please write a dialogue of roughly eleven hundred to fourteen hundred words between two philosophers who are discussing this case — call them Nadia and Tomas — while reading the authority's inquiry report before a public hearing. Nadia holds that what actually happened makes a genuine difference to how blameworthy Ines is, and not merely to how we should feel about it or what compensation is owed; Tomas holds that blameworthiness is fixed entirely by what was within Ines's control, so that she deserves exactly the same on both occasions and the whole apparent difference belongs to regret, liability, and institutional response rather than to moral desert. Neither may be a soft touch: both should have real arguments, and both should be allowed to score points that the other cannot simply wave away. They must genuinely respond to what the other has just said rather than deliver alternating position statements, and at least twice one of them should be forced to precisify or narrow their own view in response to pressure rather than restate it. I want them to work through the second and third features of the case explicitly, including whether the feedback from the first outcome into the second shows that so-called resultant luck was never a distinct category — that it collapses into luck in one's circumstances and in the formation of one's own judgment — or whether there is a residue that survives that reduction, and if so what it consists in. Somewhere in the middle, have one of them raise the suspicion that they are no longer disagreeing about the world but only about how a word is being deployed, and let them try to settle that question, with partial success at best. Keep it argument rather than intellectual history: no naming of philosophers, no claims about who holds which view in the literature, no references, further reading, or footnotes; if a character wants to lean on a principle, they should state it and defend it. Let the stage business be light — a room, a report, an imminent hearing — so that the reasoning carries the weight, and do not end with either of them converting the other or with a tidy compromise. Instead, close with each saying as precisely as they can what the residual disagreement now turns on, and what further case or what further consideration would, on their own account of things, decide it.
