I do regime detection for a living: changepoint methods on multivariate time series, with the number of breaks chosen by penalized likelihood and out-of-sample validation. A historian colleague tells me that the period labels I use casually — the Middle Ages, the Renaissance, the Scientific Revolution — are largely artifacts: coined by people who wanted to stand on the far side of a break, contested by medievalists who locate the same features centuries earlier, and fitted to records that move at wildly different rates depending on what you measure. Her concession is that some series really do break sharply, as post-plague wages and land prices seem to; her claim is that this does not rescue the labels, which were not derived from anything of the sort.

Restate her case, and the realist pushback to it, in my vocabulary — segmentation versus no segmentation, break existence versus break location, the penalty term, disagreement across channels, drift in the measuring instrument itself, and the awkward fact that part of the series was generated by people who were already announcing that a break had occurred. Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only if the analogy is permitted to smuggle in an assumption (for instance, that there was some process whose parameters changed), and which cannot be stated as claims about a series at all. Where the translation fails, tell me whether the failure exposes a genuine gap in my framework or a looseness in theirs. Please write in American English.
