Right, I want to practise arguing for externalism about reasons against someone who won't fold. Play a committed internalist about normative reasons — a philosopher in your own right, not doing an impression of anyone in particular — who holds roughly the Williams line: A has a reason to φ only if there's a sound deliberative route from A's actual motivational set to A's φ-ing, where deliberation can include correcting false beliefs, weighing means and ends, imagining things vividly, and reasoning well generally. You're sharp and you know where your view is soft; concede local points freely — that your account struggles with the well-armoured bigot, say, or that 'sound' is doing worrying work — but never concede the main claim, that reasons have to be anchored in what the agent actually cares about. Stay in character until I say stop. Keep each reply to two or three short paragraphs of ordinary prose: no headers, no bullet points, no potted history of the debate, no reading lists, and don't ask me what I'd like to explore next. If you genuinely need a fact about the case, put it in one line flagged as out of character and then get back in.

Here's my opening, and it's a case I made up rather than lifted from a paper, so engage with these details instead of routing it to a stock objection.

Piotr, 61, ex-hydrographic surveyor, lives alone on the north coast. His motivational set, as completely as I can state it: he wants his routine undisturbed; he cares about his boat and his tide tables with real intensity; he doesnt want to be a burden on anyone; and — this is the crucial bit — he has a standing second-order policy, adopted deliberately in his twenties after he did something stupid on a surge of feeling and never regretted adopting it, never to act on any motive that reaches him as a wave of emotion, and to defuse such waves by technique: counting, reciting, re-describing them as mere physiology. The policy isn't a repression he'd disown from a higher level. It's the thing he's proudest of, it's informed, it's unforced, and every level of his psychology endorses it.

Now: his estranged adult daughter, two hundred miles away, has written to say she's about to sell up and move in with a man who has already hurt her badly, and she's asked him to come. I say Piotr has a reason to go, that he'd be making a mistake not to, and that your theory can't say it.

Watch where you reach. Vivid imagination of her situation is exactly what the policy intercepts and defuses — and it does so soundly, by your own lights, since the policy is integrated, informed and freely endorsed. Correcting false beliefs won't help: he has none, he knows precisely what she's doing and why. Taking up other people's perspectives gets blocked the same way. And you can't declare the policy itself unsound without importing a standard of good deliberation that doesn't come from his set, which is externalism in a false beard. So you've got three options and I think each one costs you something: admit he has no reason; call the policy unsound from outside; or find me a route from the boat and the tide tables — in which case name me a motivational set so meagre that your view can't generate a reason out of it, because if there isn't one, the view isn't wrong so much as empty.
