I'm auditing a history-of-economic-thought seminar as someone trained in political theory rather than macroeconomics, and I keep returning to Hume's economic essays in the Political Discourses — the ones on money, on the balance of trade, and on public credit. In rough paraphrase, and please correct me where I'm getting him wrong: money on his account is not itself one of the objects of trade but the instrument men have agreed upon to facilitate the exchange of one commodity for another, something like oil that makes the wheels of commerce turn more easily; a country that ends up with too much coin loses it again through higher prices and a worsening trade position, while a country drained of coin becomes cheap and draws money back, so that the distribution of specie across nations tends to right itself; and there is an interval between the arrival of new money and the full adjustment of prices in which industry is quickened rather than merely taxed, which is why he is no simple neutralist. Layered on top is his distrust of artificially multiplying paper credit, and his darker claim about sovereign borrowing — that a state which keeps running up debt is heading toward a reckoning in which either the nation destroys public credit or public credit destroys the nation, and that the reckoning will be political before it is financial. I have also come across the suggestion, in the secondary literature, that the tidy self-equilibrating mechanism we attach to his name is substantially a twentieth-century reconstruction by trade theorists, and that the essays themselves are looser, more qualified, and partly rhetorical in aim.

I want to test that apparatus against the eurozone crisis of 2010 to 2015, with Greece as the focal case but the periphery more broadly in view. Here are the facts I take to be settled, so that you need not hedge about them: the members surrendered their own currencies and hence their own exchange rates and national money stocks; the adjustment demanded of deficit members was therefore what policymakers called internal devaluation — cuts in nominal wages, prices, pensions and public employment — combined with fiscal austerity; through the 2000s German unit labour costs were held roughly flat by labour-market reform while those of Greece, Spain, Portugal and Ireland rose, opening a large real exchange-rate gap inside a fixed nominal one; the pre-2008 periphery boom was financed by cross-border bank lending rather than by any trade surplus; when private capital fled after 2010 the periphery's national central banks accumulated enormous liabilities, and the Bundesbank matching claims, inside the Eurosystem's payment system — the TARGET2 balances — which struck some observers as a monetary flow of the sort the union was supposed to have abolished; there was no fiscal transfer mechanism; the European Central Bank was constrained for most of the period and sovereign spreads in Italy and Spain collapsed only in mid-2012, after its president publicly committed to doing whatever was necessary, within the bank's mandate, to preserve the currency; private holders of Greek debt were restructured in 2012, after which the debt was overwhelmingly official; Cyprus imposed capital controls in 2013; and in 2015 a Greek government that had just won a referendum against its creditors' terms capitulated within days.

What I want is a disciplined mapping, not a list of resemblances. Begin element by element: what has to be substituted for Hume's variables inside a currency union — what plays the part of specie when there is neither specie nor a national money stock, what plays the part of the price level when the variable that actually moves is the nominal wage, what counts as the trade balance when the decisive flows were financial, and who the agent of adjustment is when there is no mint and no treasury that can inflate. Then tell me which features of the episode the Humean picture genuinely predicts or explains — including whether the German wage restraint of the 2000s is best read as a member deliberately breaking an automatic mechanism, and whether TARGET2 is a fair candidate for the specie flow he posited — and, with equal care, where the picture goes silent, misdescribes, or must be stretched past recognition: debt deflation, nominal-wage rigidity, a lender of last resort in a shared currency, bank runs inside a monetary union, the difference between a debt owed in a currency you can print and one you cannot. Then run the analysis in the other direction. Does the crisis confirm the underlying logic under changed institutions, in the sense that the adjustment he predicted duly arrived but through the most politically expensive channel available; is it an anomaly; or is it evidence that the mechanism was never a mechanism at all but only a manner of speaking? Take a position on the reconstruction worry as well, and say plainly whether the self-equilibrating reading is defensible on the essays' own terms, or whether the episode is being used to test a theory Hume never held. I want commitment rather than a survey: rank the components of his monetary thought by how well they survive contact with the episode, name one thing he gets right that the mainstream analysis of the crisis missed or learned late, and one thing he gets wrong that mattered concretely, and give your reasons for each. No bibliography and no list of further reading, please — I want the argument, and I would much rather you flag the points where you are extrapolating beyond what Hume actually says than paper over them with references I cannot check.
