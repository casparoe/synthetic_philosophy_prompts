I am composing a novel set in a provincial university, one of whose principals is a lecturer in the philosophy of biology whose career is quietly stalling; in the middle section of the book she sets and then marks the final examination for her third-year course, and I intend to quote both the paper itself and her marginalia on a weak script. I have been careful with the technical texture of the book, and readers who work in this field should have nothing to smirk at, which is why I am asking you to write the examination question as it would actually appear, together with a model answer and a marking scheme. The question must concern the dispute between etiological accounts of biological function — the selected-effects tradition associated with Wright and developed by Millikan and Neander, on which a trait has a function in virtue of having been selected for producing a certain effect — and causal-role or dispositional accounts of the kind Cummins defends, on which a function is a contribution a trait currently makes to some capacity of the containing system. It must not be answerable by a candidate who has memorised the two definitions and can recite them; I want the crux to be a case in which neither account emerges cleanly, so that the marks go to students who can identify what is really at stake rather than to those who can reproduce a survey.

To that end I have invented a scenario, which my adviser sketched and which appears in the novel, and I should like you to build the question around it. You may refine the details, but please do not soften the tension in them. A synthetic microorganism has been assembled in the laboratory. First, it carries an enzyme that has never occurred in any ancestor and has been present in the line for only a few dozen clonally propagated generations, during which no variant lacking it has ever arisen and been outcompeted; the enzyme does, however, presently detoxify a solvent in the culture medium and plainly keeps the cells alive. Second, the organism retains a motility apparatus inherited from its wild ancestor, where it was selected for chemotaxis towards nutrient gradients; in the perfectly uniform medium of the laboratory it still rotates and still propels the cell exactly as it always did, but it contributes nothing to survival and costs adenosine triphosphate to run. Third, one sub-lineage carries a point mutation in that apparatus which leaves it structurally intact but unable to rotate at all. The question should require the candidate to say which, if any, of these three items has a function, on what grounds, and — this is the part I most want pressed — whether the mutant counts as malfunctioning when the intact apparatus is itself of no use in the environment it now inhabits. I would like the candidate to be pushed towards noticing that the two accounts are not even fixing on the same relata: the causal-role verdict shifts with the choice of containing system and hence with the explainer's interests, while the etiological verdict shifts with the choice of ancestral environment, and a strong answer should say what follows from that rather than lament it.

Please give me, then, the question in authentic examination-paper register — twenty-five marks, sixty minutes, with the instruction about how the parts are to be weighted as the lecturer would phrase it; a model answer of roughly seven hundred words that a first-class candidate could have written in the time; and a marking scheme that distinguishes a first from a solid second by naming the specific moves each must contain, with the marks attached. The marking scheme should also list four or five identifiable mistakes that ought to cost marks — among them, I should think, declaring a comfortable pluralism in the final sentence after merely restating both positions; asserting that the causal-role account cannot accommodate malfunction at all without explaining what it would have to appeal to in order to try, and whether that appeal collapses into a statistical notion of the typical case or quietly concedes history; treating the bioengineers' design intention as a substitute for selection history without remarking that biology has reason to exclude designer intentions; and supposing that the interest-relativity of causal-role functions is simply a defect rather than something Cummins insists upon as a feature. My character's annotations should be quotable, so the scheme ought to be specific enough that a sentence of it could stand as marginalia. I do not want a bibliography or a list of further reading: the candidates would have none in the examination room, and a novelist cannot use a reference list.
