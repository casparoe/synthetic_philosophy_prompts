I have never studied philosophy and I am not going to pretend otherwise, but this is a difficulty I have been turning over for some months and I would like it treated seriously rather than soothed. I will state it as plainly as I can.

Twelve years ago my grandfather died. The will says that his bicycle goes to whichever of his two grandchildren has it when the estate is settled; everything else was divided long ago and without argument. It was an ordinary steel road machine that he had ridden since he was twenty.

I was the one who took it, and it needed work. I have done that work for twelve years: the wheels first, then the drivetrain, then the brakes, then, after a bad winter, the frame itself. I replaced parts only as they failed, and I kept every part I removed, labelled, in a crate under the bench. The bicycle was never out of use. It has been ridden every week that it was ridable.

My sister has spent the last three years assembling the contents of that crate. She bought two parts she could not find in it, a saddle and a pedal, and the machine she now has is, she says, our grandfather's bicycle, because nearly everything on it is original while nearly nothing on mine is. Both machines are finished. Both stand in the same garage. Last month I looked at them and had the strange sense that the garage contained one bicycle and also two, and I still cannot say which half of that feeling is the mistake.

Two things complicate the dispute and I do not know what to make of either.

When I had the frame replaced, the register kept by the vintage cycling association issued a certificate recording the new number against the old and describing the machine as unchanged. So there is a piece of paper, held by people who care about such things, saying that mine is his. I cannot tell whether that paper records a fact about the bicycle or makes one.

For two of those years, the crate sat in the garage as a box of labelled parts while my bicycle was being ridden. If a bicycle can be scattered in a box, then in that period the garage held two bicycles, one of them in pieces. If it cannot, then at some moment the crate stopped being his bicycle and became scrap, and I could not tell you which moment that was or what would have made it a different one.

My own feeling, which I distrust, is that a machine like that is less a thing than a habit that keeps being renewed: that the bicycle is wherever it would be repaired into if something went wrong tomorrow, and that this is why I have kept it and she has not. I do not know whether that is an argument or a preference wearing an argument's clothes.

The executor has asked us to settle it within the month. My sister has proposed that we sell both machines and split the money, which would end the quarrel and, I suspect, would also be a kind of answer.

What I want is an account of what could settle which machine is my grandfather's, and of whether anything could, or whether the question is simply ours to decide. If the competing answers are genuinely different in what they commit you to, I would like them set against each other rather than blended, with some sense of what each one costs and what it would have to say about the crate, the certificate, and the two years in between. Please also tell me plainly whether my habit theory is worthless, since I have to talk to my sister about this and I would rather not arrive with a feeling.
