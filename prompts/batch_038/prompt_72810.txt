I run a Thursday-night reading group at the public library — 12 people, mostly retirees plus a couple of grad students, and we've been slowly working our way through Hofstadter's strange loops book, the part where he argues a self is a pattern and patterns can be spread out over more then one head at once. Next week is my turn to run the session. I've got 75 minutes, a room with chairs in a circle, a printer, and no whiteboard.

I don't want to lecture and I don't want to read another thought experiment aloud at people. I want something they *do*, so that the actual claim — that "you" can exist at partial resolution in several other people simultaneously, and that this isn't a metaphor or a nice thing to say at a funeral but literally what a person is — stops sounding like a Hallmark card. Ideally they walk into the problem themselves and only hear the vocabulary at the very end.

Constraints I care about:

- No sci-fi. No uploading, no teleporters, no brain transplants, no cryonics, nothing that requires imagining future technology. It all has to happen with people, paper, and talking in that room. Last time someone raised a transporter the whole hour went to whether the machine kills you and we never got out.
- We did the Ship of Theseus thing in the spring, and there's one guy, Ray, who will absolutely say "so this is just the ship with a face on it." I want the activity to contain an answer to that which he runs into rather than one I assert. My own sense is that a ship is only ever one object at a time whereas a person is a pattern that can be in several carriers at once at different fidelities — but I need that felt, not stated.
- Two of the twelve haven't read a page and won't. One woman thinks the whole idea is just "we remember people who died, dressed up in fancy words," and honestly she's not wrong that it's a risk.
- Nobody gets cast as a grieving relative. That's manipulative and one of our regulars lost her husband in January.

So give me the actual activity: the setup, the roles, what the physical materials are and what's literally printed on them, the sequence of moves with rough timings, and the point where it should start to feel wierd. Then the debrief — questions that map what just happened back onto the claim, including how the fidelity thing is supposed to show up in their own behaviour rather than in my commentary. Tell me what will predictably break the first time I run it (I'm guessing the transmission round goes too smoothly and everyone ends up with basically the same person, which would kill the whole thing), how I handle Ray in the moment without shutting him down, and be straight with me about the one feature of the idea the activity can't reproduce and I'll have to supply myself — I suspect it's the gap between a pattern someone could act out on request and a pattern that's actually running in their head, but you tell me if it's something worse.
