Next month I'm chairing the closing session of a one-day workshop on naturalizing content that my department runs every couple of years, and I'm on the hook for moderating the Q&A with the keynote speaker — 40 minutes, mixed room, half philosophy grad students and half psych/neuro people who suspect were all just talking about nothing. I'd rather not name her here, not because its a secret but because I want the questions keyed to the view and not to the person.

Her view, in brief, because the questions need to bite on specifics: she's a teleosemanticist who thinks the selecting is done not by phylogeny but by the individual's own learning history. Reinforcement does for neural states roughly what differential survival does for traits — variation in dispositions, differential retention by reward — so a state can have a proper function it picked up last Tuesday. She argues this buys her two things she wouldn't otherwise get: a clean answer to the worry about how we ever come by concepts of things our ancestors never encountered, and a way to concede the swamp case instead of fighting it (her swampman starts out contentless and fills in fast once he starts getting rewarded). Content is then fixed by a combination of what a state has the function of indicating and what the proper functions of the downstream consumers require, and misrepresentation is what happens when a state gets tokened outside the range of conditions its reinforcement schedule was actually tuned for. Her stock move when somebody pushes the disjunction problem — fly versus dark moving speck versus passing shadow — is to say that the reward history tells you which misfires were cheap and which were costly, and that if you can't yet say which content that selects, you simply haven't done the psychology.

So: draft me eight to ten questions that don't let her just restate all that. I want at least one that goes after the circularity of individuating proper functions without already knowing what the state is about; one on what happens when a single state feeds several consumers whose functions conflict; and one asking whether reinforcement machinery does any work over and above a purely informational account — i.e. whether "proper function" is earning its keep or is just a redescription of "this got shaped by reward" in TD-error language (a student writing her dissertation on RL will be in the second row and would like that one to sting a bit). Also something on how she gets content for beliefs that were never themselves reinforced — testimony, arithmetic, the town you've heard about but never visited — and something on whether making content this individual breaks the publicity of disagreement, since two people raised differently might not share the concept even when they're pretty clearly contradicting each other. For each question, give me the reply she's most likely to make and the follow-up that reply invites, so I'm not caught flat-footed. Then, separately from the list, tell me which two of them are the real cruxes — where a bad answer costs her the theory rather than just an amendment — and rank the rest by how much I should be willing to drop them if the clock runs out.
