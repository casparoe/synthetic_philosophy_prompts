I'm co-running a small reading group that's been alternating between Lewis on convention and some of the newer stuff on how norms actually change, and last week one of our members — she's an economist — argued that the two literatures are describing the same thing, with the QWERTY keyboard as her exhibit. Her line went like this: nobody picked QWERTY for its merits, it beat out better layouts early on for contingent reasons, and now we're all stuck with it because it's individually irrational to retrain on Dvorak however much you'd prefer a world where everyone typed Dvorak. And that, she said, is just what a convention is on Lewis's account — arbitrary, self-enforcing, and only movable if something big and central moves everybody at once. She then turned it on the norm she actually cares about (women taking their husband's surname, which she reckons most people in the room privately find hard to defend but keep doing anyway), and the upshot she drew was that working on individual attitudes is a waste of time, that what reformers need is a focal institution — a church, a big employer, a celebrity, whatever plays the role Remington played — and that once it moves, everyone else moves with it and cheaply.

I half buy it and half don't, and I'd like a proper audit before I write anything up. Can you go down the clauses of Lewis's own definition of convention one at a time — the regularity in behaviour, the near-universal conformity, the expectation that others conform, the conditional preference to conform given that others do, the clause about everyone having roughly the same preferences over the possible combinations of actions, and the bit about there being some alternative regularity that would serve almost as well — and say for each whether the keyboard case actually satisfies it? My hunch is that it doesn't, or not in the interesting way: what keeps me on QWERTY isn't any expectation about what other people are going to do so much as the keyboards that already exist, the muscle memory in my hands, and the sheer cost of retraining, which sounds a lot less like a convention and more like standardisation propped up by capital goods. If that's right I want to know which of the disanalogies actually hurt her argument and which ones she gets to shrug off — I can already hear her saying fine, call it a hybrid, the strategic element is still doing the work. There's also a wrinkle I don't know how much weight to give: as I understand it the usual story about why the layout was designed that way (something to do with typebars jamming) is itself disputed, and someone at the seminar insisted the whole lock-in literature has been answered and the layout was never inferior in the first place. Does any of that change what the example can be made to carry, or is it beside the point?

Then the payoff question. If the surname norm turns out to be Lewisian rather than QWERTY-ish — and in particular if it fails that clause about everyone sharing the same preferences over outcomes, which would make it a conflict game rather than a clean coordination problem — does it still follow that changing attitudes is useless and that you should be out hunting for a coordinator? And does Sweden flipping to right-hand traffic in 1967, which is the case everyone reaches for when they want to show a convention being moved on purpose, support her or cut against her, given how much state machinery it took to pull off? Please end with a better image than the keyboard if there is one — something that keeps the arbitrariness and the stickiness without smuggling in factories and installed machinery. British English throughout, and no need for references or a reading list, I just want the reasoning laid out plainly.
