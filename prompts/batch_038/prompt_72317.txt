For fifteen years I have investigated serious accidents for a freight railway, which means that after each bad event I sit down with a pile of statements, a maintenance log, and a timetable, and produce a report that says what caused it. In my line of work there is a permanent, unresolvable fight about what such a report should look like: one camp holds that a cause is only a cause if it names something about the setting — the rostering, the incentive, the fact that everyone before him had done it the same way for years — and the other camp holds that you have not explained anything until you have said why the act made sense to the person who did it. We have run this argument to exhaustion, and nobody has moved, because each side can re-describe the other's best evidence as an instance of its own category.

Reading Christopher Browning's Ordinary Men (1992) and Daniel Goldhagen's Hitler's Willing Executioners (1996) back to back, I had the uncomfortable feeling that I was watching the same argument, thirty years earlier, with far higher stakes. Both authors study the same Hamburg order police battalion, the same middle-aged reservists in occupied Poland in 1942, the same episode in which the commander told his men they could step out of the shooting if they did not feel up to it and only a dozen or so of about five hundred did, the same fact — which both grant — that refusing brought no serious punishment and that men who asked to be reassigned were reassigned, one of them even promoted. Browning explains the killing through the pressures of the moment and of the group: authority, conformity, the gradual routinization of the work, the brutalization of wartime, drawing explicitly on laboratory studies of obedience. Goldhagen explains it through a specifically German, eliminationist antisemitism that made the killing intelligible and in some cases attractive to the men, treats a single cultural cause as sufficient, and points to the death marches at the end of the war, when nobody was compelling anyone, as his cleanest evidence. As I understand it, each also accuses the other of mishandling the sources, since so much of what we know comes from statements the men gave as defendants decades later: one says the other has been taken in by self-exculpation, the other says that testimony is the best there is and must simply be used with care. The professional reception was lopsided against Goldhagen, while the public reception was overwhelmingly in his favour.

My working hypothesis is that this is not a factual dispute in disguise, and that it stalled because the two men are answering different questions under one heading — one asking what produced the behaviour in that place on that day, the other asking what made such a programme thinkable to those who carried it out — with a further, genuinely empirical question buried underneath that neither of them addresses head-on: what comparable men, in comparable circumstances but without that cultural formation, actually did. I may well be wrong, and I expect part of the disagreement is verbal, turning on what willing and ordinary are being made to mean.

So: diagnose it. Which kinds of disagreement are actually tangled together here — verbal, evidential, a dispute over levels of explanation, a hidden counterfactual, incompatible starting intuitions about how much weight a person's own character can bear, or something about the moral and political consequences each author fears? Sort them, and say for each whether more documents, a comparison across other battalions or other nationalities, a sharper distinction, or a change of question could settle it, or whether it is the kind of thing that cannot be settled and should not be expected to be. Treat what each author actually argues as your evidence. Tell me too whether the gap between the historians and the readers is itself a clue about what kind of disagreement this is. I am not asking for a verdict on who was right, nor for a summary of the two books, nor for anywhere to go and read further.
