Question 3 (20 points). Gödel's ontological argument is set out in second-order S5, with positive property as an undefined primitive governed by five axioms: a property and its negation cannot both be positive; any property necessarily entailed by a positive property is itself positive; being God-like is positive; whatever is positive is necessarily positive; and necessary existence is positive. God-like is defined as having every positive property. A property is an essence of a thing if the thing has it and it entails, necessarily, every other property the thing has. A thing exists necessarily if each of its essences is necessarily exemplified. Using the first two axioms, any positive property is possibly exemplified; hence, since God-likeness is positive, possibly something is God-like; hence, with the remaining axioms and S5, necessarily something is God-like.

(a) [10] Explain what work the axioms about positive properties do in this proof, and say as precisely as you can where the argument's genuinely controversial content lies.
(b) [10] Gödel's argument, it is often said, escapes Kant's objection that existence is not a real predicate. Explain the objection and assess that claim.

Student's answer:

(a) The whole proof runs on the positivity axioms, since positive property is never defined. Everything it means comes from what the axioms say about it: it's exclusive, it's closed under necessary entailment, and it's stable from world to world. The first two together give the lemma that any positive property is possibly exemplified. If φ were positive but nothing could possibly be φ, then φ would be necessarily unexemplified, so ¬φ would follow from any positive property whatsoever, and by closure ¬φ would be positive too, contradicting exclusivity. So the possibility premise isn't assumed at the start, it's earned, and that's the main respect in which this is more rigorous than Anselm. It's also Leibnizian rather than Anselmian in spirit: Leibniz's problem was showing that all the perfections are compossible, and the positivity axioms do that job here, by stipulation instead of by argument.

Where the controversial content lies: in the last axiom alone. Everything before it is either a definition or a consequence of S5 together with axioms that only say how an undefined notion behaves, so they aren't premises anyone could reasonably dispute about the world. The interesting question is just whether the necessary-existence axiom can be accepted without begging the question, and there Gödel gives us nothing to work with: the axioms tell us how positivity behaves without ever telling us which properties are positive, so a critic can grant every step and still refuse the last one. Read that way the argument is a conditional, and a clean one.

There's also the modal collapse objection, which I think is worse than that, because it isn't about plausibility at all. From these axioms you can derive that whatever is true is necessarily true, so there are no contingent truths anywhere. An axiom set with that consequence is inconsistent, and that's a formal defect in the argument rather than a disputed premise, so it's not something a defender can argue with or repair by weakening one axiom.

(b) Kant's objection is that existence is not a real predicate. Adding exists to the concept of a thing doesn't enlarge the concept, it only posits the thing with all the predicates already in it. So an argument that reads existence off the concept of the most perfect being trades on a confusion: existence was never in the concept in the way the argument needs, and that's why it can't get from what is conceived to what is.

Gödel's version isn't built like that. Nothing about existence goes into the definition of God-like, which is spelled out purely in terms of positive properties; existence comes in through a separate defined notion, necessary existence, which a God-like being has because God-likeness is an essence of it. And the whole derivation has been formalized in higher-order logic and machine-checked. That's what settles it against Kant: a valid derivation from the definition of God-like to the conclusion that necessarily something God-like exists just is a case of existence coming out of a concept, and the check shows there's no gap left for the objection to get a grip in.

Two more things. The step from possibly God-like to necessarily God-like does depend on S5, specifically on the principle that whatever is possibly necessary is necessary; someone could push on that, though it's far less controversial than the positivity axioms. And the conclusion is only that something God-like exists necessarily. Because God-likeness is defined by positive properties, nothing in the proof shows that this being is a person, made the world, or answers prayer. That's a different problem from Kant's, and it doesn't go away just because the derivation is valid.
