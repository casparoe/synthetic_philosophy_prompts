I teach a graduate seminar on personal identity and have grown dissatisfied with the standard way of running it. We spend the term inside thought experiments that no institution has ever been required to administer, and then marvel that the students cannot see how the questions bear on anything. This year I want to invert the order and begin from a practice that has already settled by regulation what philosophers treat as open. What I need from you is a reconstruction of the metaphysics that practice presupposes.

The practice is the determination of death by neurological criteria in American hospitals. The governing legal formula holds that a human being is dead upon the irreversible cessation of all functions of the entire brain, including the brainstem, and that this determination is to be made in accordance with accepted medical standards. Operationally, that means a bedside examination conducted by a physician: a documented cause for coma, no evidence of sedatives or paralytics, no severe hypothermia, no profound metabolic derangement; absence of pupillary, corneal, oculocephalic, gag, and cough responses; and an apnea test in which the ventilator is disconnected and the patient observed for spontaneous respiratory effort while oxygenation is maintained. Once that examination is completed and repeated, the patient is legally a corpse. Organs may be recovered, the ventilator may be withdrawn, the estate may be opened, the marriage is dissolved, and the death certificate is issued — all without any consent that would be required if the patient were alive, and without anyone being guilty of homicide.

Several features of the practice puzzle me, and I want them treated as data rather than smoothed over.

First, the examination tests the brainstem and the capacity for respiration. It does not test the hypothalamus or the neuroendocrine functions, and a substantial number of patients who satisfy the criteria continue to secrete antidiuretic hormone and never develop diabetes insipidus. The standard is stated in terms of all functions of the entire brain; the test is of considerably less.

Second, irreversibility is fixed relative to what medicine will do. A cessation that is irreversible for a patient who refuses transfusion is not irreversible simpliciter; a function that could be replaced by a device we have not yet built is not yet irreversibly gone. The criterion seems to smuggle a policy judgment into a biological predicate.

Third, we treat the resulting body inconsistently. Procurement teams in many centers administer an anesthetic agent before the incision, justifying it by reference to tachycardia and hypertensive responses in a body that is legally dead. Families are usually asked not to be present, and the room is prepared with a decorum we reserve for patients. And yet the same body may be extubated without anyone's agreement, may be maintained for weeks to gestate a fetus to viability, and may be the subject of a court order compelling continued somatic support — as in one widely reported American case involving a pregnant patient, where a state statute requiring the continuation of life-sustaining treatment for a pregnant person was read to apply to a patient already declared dead. Meanwhile at least one state permits a family to refuse the neurological determination altogether on religious grounds, so that the identical body is a corpse across the state line and a living patient on the near side of it; in one case a family transported a declared-dead teenager to such a jurisdiction and cared for her there for years.

Here is what I want.

Reconstruct the theory of the person this practice presupposes. Not the theory its authors happened to hold — I want the theory the practice commits us to in order for each of its steps to make sense. Whose body is a corpse when the brain has ceased to function but the heart, the gut, the immune system, and the endocrine periphery continue? What has to be true of persons for that body to be nobody? Which rival views does the practice rule out — the view that the person is the whole organism, so long as it is metabolically integrated; the view that the person is constituted by a narrative or a set of relations that survive in the memories of others; the view that personhood is a graded status that admits of partial loss? And which views merely happen to fit the practice comfortably without being required by it?

I want you to be disciplined about the difference between strict presupposition and good fit. Where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several incompatible theories of the person would license exactly the same examination and exactly the same legal consequence — say so and say why.

Then turn to the direction of the commitment. Consider Dennett's essay "Where Am I?", in which a narrator's brain is removed and maintained in a vat while his emptied body is kept alive and running elsewhere, the two coupled so that the question of where he is becomes genuinely unanswerable by inspection. The neurological criterion appears to endorse half of that intuition: the person goes where the brain goes, and where the brain has stopped, the person has gone. But I want you to test whether it endorses the other half. Call it the one-way commitment: the practice licenses the inference from a destroyed brain to a dead person, yet it has never had to license the inference from a surviving brain to a surviving person, since no hospital has ever been presented with an isolated functioning brain and asked whose it is. Does a doctrine built only to authorize extubation and organ recovery actually commit us to the full brain-transplant intuition, or only to the weaker and possibly quite different claim that organized cortical and brainstem function is a necessary condition of personhood? These come apart, and I want the divergence exhibited rather than glossed.

Finally, tell me what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced. Suppose a hospital system adopted the higher-brain standard instead: irreversibly lost capacity for consciousness, with brainstem-only bodies classified as living patients who may be withdrawn from support only with the consent that living patients require. What follows for organ procurement, for the apnea test, for gestation cases, for the religious exemption, for the death certificate? Which of those consequences are forced by the substitution and which are contingent institutional choices?

On the epistemic side, I ask one thing explicitly. Distinguish throughout between the parts of your reconstruction you regard as settled and the parts you regard as your best guess among live alternatives, and then tell me what would change your mind: what argument, what fact about the practice, or what consideration about the criteria of death would move you from the verdict that the practice presupposes a particular view of the person to the verdict that it presupposes nothing beyond administrative convenience, or the reverse. Where is your reasoning most likely to fail, and if you find yourself relying on a contestable reading of what irreversibility or of what a function of the brain amounts to, flag it as contestable rather than presenting it as background.
