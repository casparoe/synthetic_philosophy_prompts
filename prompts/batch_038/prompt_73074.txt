So, kind of a weird request and a long one, sorry. In six weeks my town is laying stumbling stones in the pavement outside the house my family has owned since 1939, and I've been asked to speak at it. Five minutes, maybe eighty people in the street: the two school classes who did the research project, the mayor, three people from the family that used to live there (the daughter is 71 and flies in from Haifa, plus a nephew from Chicago), and about half my own relatives, including my father, who'll sit in the front row and has already told me I'm making criminals of all of us. My uncle isn't coming at all.

The facts, which took me two years to get out of the archives and which nobody in my family had ever discussed with me: the house and the haulage yard behind it belonged to a Jewish family, they were pushed out in 1938, my great-grandfather bought the premises for roughly a third of what they were worth, the family was deported in 1942 and one of the four came back. Everything the firm still is — including the building I grew up in and the money that put me through university — runs through that purchase. Nobody in my family was ever charged with anything, and by the standards of the time nobody did anything illegal. That's precisely the problem.

I want to build the speech around Jaspers's fourfold distinction from his book on the guilt question — criminal, political, moral, metaphysical — but not as a seminar. Nobody standing in that street should hear a taxonomy. The reason I want it is that it forces me to be exact about what I can truthfully say: I'm not criminally guilty; I can't be morally guilty of 1938, because moral guilt only attaches to what I actually willed; the political one is the kind you inherit as a citizen of a state that keeps on going, and it looks to me like liability rather than sin — you get the bill, not the stain. I want to lean hard on that.

Here's where I'm stuck, and where I need you to actually think rather than write something moving:

1. My fear about the metaphysical one. It's the most universal of the four — solidarity with everyone, co-responsibility for every injustice, especially the ones done in your presence or with your knowledge. It sounds like the humblest thing you can say, and I think it's where speeches like this go to hide, because if everyone is guilty in that sense then nobody has to answer in the other three. Can a five-minute speech name it and at the same time refuse to let it do any exculpatory work? Or is the honest move to leave it out entirely?

2. A rule I want to hold myself to: in the whole speech, wherever the word means moral guilt, its subject has to be I. Not we, not my family, not Germans. I get to accuse nobody but myself. Does that survive contact with my father in the front row — can I claim the political kind of him too without it landing as an accusation? He's convinced it must.

3. Restitution. The firm is going to announce at the ceremony that it's transferring the yard to the town's memorial foundation. My cousin says that's buying an indulgence; the daughter wrote to me that she doesn't want money, she wants to hear what we knew and when. So does the payment discharge the political guilt, or purchase silence? And should it be in the speech at all? My instinct is one sentence, no numbers, after the names have been read — but I can't tell whether announcing it in public contaminates it by turning it into a performance.

4. She asked for facts and I'm not getting a document-by-document account into five minutes. My plan is to give one date, one piece of paper, how long it took me to find, and then admit that the two years of not looking were themselves something I did — the only thing in the whole story that's actually mine in the moral sense. Is that enough of an answer to her, or a dodge dressed up as humility?

5. No shame language anywhere. I've decided shame sticks to who you are and can't be paid off, while guilt sticks to what you did and can be answered, and the shame register has been the default here for decades and has turned into a performance of its own. Tell me if you think that's wrong for this particular room, because she may well think so.

Write it in English — I'll do the German myself, its my mother tongue. One thing to keep in your head while writing: in German Schuld means both guilt and debt, which is arguably Jaspers's whole point about the political category, so use it, but don't wink at the audience about it, and I know Nietzsche made something of that etymology, please don't. Also dont quote Jaspers or anyone else verbatim — I can't check quotes and I don't want a reading list.

Then two or three sentences afterwards telling me where you had to bend the framework to make it survivable in front of real people. I'd rather know that than get something seamless.
