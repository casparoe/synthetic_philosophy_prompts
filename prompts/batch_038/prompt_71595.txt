Two members of our department's philosophy-of-biology reading group have spent three years arguing about the question Hofstadter's Ant Fugue dialogue poses: whether a colony, in which the individual ants are cognitively negligible but the shifting organisation of ant teams implements something like a persistent point of view, can literally have a mind. They have now agreed to attempt an adversarial collaboration, and I would like you to draft the joint statement they ought to produce.

The two positions are as follows.

**R — the colony-mind realist.** R holds that what makes a system a candidate for having a mind is the presence of a single self-maintaining functional organisation that takes in information, weighs alternatives, and issues coordinated action across the whole system over time. On this view the ants stand to the colony as neurons stand to a brain: the identity of the constituency is irrelevant to whether there is a subject, provided the right organisation is instantiated and persists. R accepts that no individual ant has anything resembling a rich psychology, and R does not claim that colonies enjoy phenomenal consciousness.

**S — the colony-mind sceptic.** S holds that although colonies demonstrably process information, remember routes and reach collective decisions, these are descriptions we impose for predictive convenience, and that the only genuine agents in the picture are the individual ants. On S's view the missing ingredient is not information-processing capacity but a single locus of integrated control, or a single subject for which things can go well or badly as a whole; absent that, ascribing beliefs and preferences to a colony is like ascribing them to a market. S accepts that intentional vocabulary may be indispensable for predicting colony behaviour, and that this indispensability is not merely a regrettable feature of our epistemic situation.

Both sides have agreed to treat the following three experiments as their shared test material. The results are not yet in, and each side is to record its predictions in advance.

*Case 1 — Fission.* A nine-year-old laboratory colony is divided by a permeable mesh into two demes, each receiving roughly half the workers and brood and, crucially, half of the colony's chemically inscribed foraging network: the trail architecture, midden placement and nest-matrix layout that carry what the colony has learned. During eleven weeks of separation each half independently solves the same two-choice discrimination (which of two nest boxes offers a preferred entrance width) at the rate the undivided colony achieved, and each retains the idiosyncratic preference profile the whole colony had acquired over the years, including a standing aversion to a food source at which the colony was never punished but which it abandoned in its third year. The halves are then re-joined. Within days the merged colony resumes behaving as before; where the two halves had acquired conflicting preferences during separation, the merged colony reverts to the pre-split preference.

*Case 2 — Substrate transfer.* Over eighteen months the colony's inscribed chemical architecture is progressively replaced by an array of programmable odour dispensers and airflow controllers that reproduce, signal for signal, the effect the trail network had on ant behaviour. By the end, none of the accumulated information is carried by ant-laid trail; the array carries it. (The founding ants have, in the ordinary course, died off several times over.) Foraging decisions are unchanged, including the acquired idiosyncratic preferences. The array is then switched off for a week while the ants are permitted to re-lay trails from scratch: the system reverts to naive behaviour and the acquired preferences are lost. When the array is restored, the acquired behaviour returns intact.

*Case 3 — Crossed transplant.* Using the Case 2 apparatus, colony X's inscribed architecture is played to a deme of colony Y's ants, and colony Y's architecture to a deme of X's ants, the ants in each deme having been reared from pupae in the other colony and so carrying the other colony's individual experience. The design is meant to separate the contribution of the ants' own history from the contribution of the inscribed architecture.

Please produce the document itself, with the following components.

1. A numbered section of claims both R and S can sign, including claims about the Ant Fugue case itself, about what the three experiments already presuppose, and about which notions — agency, subject, unified control, belief, memory — each party is willing to use in which sense. These must be substantive: "both accept that individual ants are small" is not an agreed claim.

2. A disagreements table, one row per live disagreement, with columns for: the disputed question; R's formulation of it, in wording R would endorse; S's formulation, in wording S would endorse; whether the dispute is substantive or verbal, and, if verbal, what must be disambiguated in order to see that it is; and what, if anything, would settle it. At least one row must concern whether the fission in Case 1 shows that the pre-split colony was two minds, that one mind became two, or that there was no mind there at all.

3. A cruxes section. For each of the three cases, state what R predicts and what S predicts; identify precisely where the predictions diverge and where they coincide, and for any case in which both sides predict the same result, say so explicitly and explain why that case therefore fails to discriminate between them. For each case also record which result would oblige each side to revise its position, and whether such a revision would count as a change of mind or as a reinterpretation of its own terms.

4. A closing section stating what, if anything, both parties agree could actually move the debate. If the honest answer is nothing, say so, and state what the collaboration establishes instead — for instance, that the headline question is malformed because "mind" is being made to do several distinct jobs, together with an enumeration of those jobs and an account of which job each side was really arguing about.

Write in the first-person plural wherever R and S speak jointly, and in the third person wherever their positions must be kept apart. Write in British English throughout. Do not include citations, a bibliography or a reading list; argue from the cases and the concepts themselves.
