I have been keeping a dream journal for roughly seven months, at the suggestion of a clinician, as part of a structured treatment for recurrent nightmares. The therapeutic rationale rests on a straightforward empirical assumption: that the entries I write down each morning are records of something that actually happened to me during the night, and that by rehearsing an altered version of the nightmare while awake I am doing something to the original. Recently that assumption has come unstuck for me. I have been reading around the debate between Norman Malcolm, who argued that a dream is not an event occurring during sleep at all but rather the impression one has on waking — the impression, that is, of having dreamed — and Daniel Dennett, who reaches a broadly similar destination by a different route, suggesting that the narrative may be assembled retrospectively at or near the moment of waking rather than having been "played out" in any inner theatre beforehand. (I may be compressting both positions; please correct me where I have, but do not fill in bibliographic detail you are not confident of, and do not supply a reference list.) If either of them is right, then my journal is not a record of experiences. It is a record of constructions, and the clinical premise I have been operating under looks confused.

I want an essay that adjudicates this dispute and commits to a verdict. Not a survey, and not a "both sides have a point" ending.

What has made the question vivid for me is not the published arguments but three features of my own practice, which I will call the ten-minute case, the alarm case, and the incorporation case.

The ten-minute case: on at least three occasions I have woken, written out a dream in detail, and then — perhaps ten or fifteen minutes later, with the notebook still open in front of me — found that I could not recall the dream at all. Reading my own handwriting felt like reading a stranger's account. If, on Malcolm's view, the whole of the dream consists in an impression available on waking, what has happened to that impression? An impression that decays in ten minutes is not obviously the sort of thing that could constitute the dream, yet there is nothing else on offer.

The alarm case: when I wake to a sudden noise — an alarm, a door — I almost never have anything to report. When I wake on my own and lie still for a few minutes, I almost always do, and the entry grows richer the longer I stay awake and unoccupied. Something about the manner of waking governs whether there is a dream to report at all.

The incorporation case: twice this year an external sound (a car horn, a phone alarm in another room) appeared in the narrative in a way that seemed, at the moment of writing, to have been there from the beginning — not as an interruption of the dream but as a component of it.

Your essay should do the following.

First, reconstruct the disagreement carefully enough that it is clear what is genuinely at stake, and separate the conceptual strands from the empirical ones. Malcolm's argument and Dennett's are often lumped together, but they do not stand or fall together; say which of them my three cases put under pressure and which they leave untouched.

Second, address the ten-minute case directly. I take it to be the strongest thing I have, and I suspect it is a genuine difficulty for the Malcolm–Dennett line rather than a merely psychological curiosity. If you disagree, show me why.

Third, consider whether a third position is available: the view that during sleep there is structured, dream-shaped content which is not experienced by anyone, and which acquires phenomenal character only in the act of recall — a dormant narrative, so to speak, rather than either a real showing or a pure invention. I want to know whether this is a coherent middle way or merely Malcolm's view wearing a different coat. If it is coherent, say whether you would adopt it.

Fourth, consider how far the argument generalizes. If the reasoning that dissolves dreams is sound, does it also threaten my confidence in fast-moving waking reports — my sense of what someone's face looked like in a half-second glimpse, or of the steps that led me to a conclusion I just reached? Tell me whether that generalization is a reductio of the view or a consequence its proponents should accept cheerfully.

Finally, give me a verdict on the practical question, stated as a conclusion you are willing to defend: are my journal entries evidence of anything, and should I keep writing them?

Please write between 1,800 and 2,500 words, in a formal register, with your own conclusion stated explicitly and argued for rather than announced. Do not include citations, a bibliography, page references, or a reading list; where you are relying on a remembered detail of a philosopher's argument, describe the argument rather than naming its source. End with a short paragraph identifying the single strongest objection to the position you have taken, and explaining why it does not overturn your verdict.
