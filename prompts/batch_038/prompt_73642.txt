I run pre-departure orientation for a mid-sized NGO — we post staff to a dozen-ish countries a year, mostly people on their first long posting, and the whole when-in-Rome part of my session is currently one slide. It isnt working. Half the people over-comply: they hand cash to a baggage handler or an office guard where its really a shakedown, or they fall in with a queue-jumping habit that screws whoever queued properly. The other half under-comply and then cant figure out why everyone went cold on them.

Give me a procedure a trainee can actually run on a convention they've just run into, in the twenty minutes we have: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and where it terminates in follow it, dont, or escalate to the country director. Ground it in whatever the philosophy of convention says thats usable — the difference between a convention thats just solving a coordination problem and one carrying a moral load because specific people are relying on you, and whether being a visitor changes any of that, since we dont benefit from the coordination, we're not reciprocating, and we might be judged on a different standard entirely. I've got a rough trichotomy in my head — load-bearing conventions, expressive ones where non-compliance gets read as a statement about all of us, and friction-only ones where nothing really turns on it. Sharpen it or throw it out, but tell me which.

Then run it on three cases: jaywalking in a city where the crowd crosses on red and standing at the curb makes you an obstacle; a porter who visibly depends on the tip when our finance policy bans cash gifts to anyone in an official capacity; and a norm where youre expected to defer to a senior local official in a way that marks women or juniors as lesser. On the third one I expect the procedure to land somewhere I wont like — dont soften it to spare me. Finish with what it cant settle, which steps are values calls wearing a procedural costume, and how it misfires when someone runs it carelessly.
