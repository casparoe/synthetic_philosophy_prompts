I spent thirty-odd years running a physical chemistry lab and only took up philosophy seriously after I retired, which turns out to be a bad idea, because now I corner people at dinner parties.

The thing I can't let go of is the heterogeneity problem for hedonism. Hedonism says pleasure is one thing, that every good-feeling experience has some property in common and that property is what makes it good. Then somebody asks: fine, name it. Cold water when you're properly thirsty, and the moment a proof you've been fighting with for a week suddenly falls into place, and a baby gripping your finger — what's the shared ingredient? The replies I've come across go in a few directions: there's a common hedonic tone threaded through all of them; or pleasure isn't a sensation at all but a kind of attitude you take toward some state of affairs; or pleasure is a determinable with wildly different determinates, the way colour is one property with red and blue under it; or you just concede there's no single property and hedonism was always about a loose family of things.

I want a short story that makes that argument into machinery. Here's the setup — it's my own invention, so please don't go looking for a source for it and don't pretend it exists:

In the near future there's a lab instrument, call it a hedonometer or something with a better ring to it, that isolates and extracts whatever property is common to all pleasant experience. Not the content of a pleasure, just the shared part. The extract is stable, storable, doseable. Somebody can be given the pure common factor of eating an apricot with no apricot attached.

The story should turn on what the extract turns out to be, and I want that to be a philosophical twist rather than a body-horror one. Candidates: the extract works perfectly, but people who take it insist over and over that it isn't a feeling at all, it's more like a not-minding, and nobody can say what they were expecting. Or every purification run leaves a specific residue behind — apricot-pleasure can't be fully de-apricoted, and the leftover keeps contaminating the next batch. Or the extract behaves like a determinable that refuses to be instantiated on its own, and the instrument only ever outputs it wearing a borrowed face. Pick whichever failure mode makes the best story and commit to it. I don't want all three hedged in together.

What I need from you:

- Roughly 1,200 to 2,000 words.
- At least four pleasures described concretely enough that a reader could argue about whether they really share anything. One bodily, one intellectual, one social or emotional, and one the character is a bit ashamed of.
- The lab should feel like an actual lab: calibration drift, consumables, a grant that's up for renewal, a technician who is profoundly bored. No sleek white corridors.
- Two characters, one who holds one of the hedonist replies above and one who holds the heterogeneity objection, and let them argue for real. Keep it in the mouths of people who would actually say it that way, not in essay voice.
- Ending: I'd rather it come down on one side than stay coy, but let the losing side get the better lines.
- No real philosophers named anywhere, no footnotes, no afterword explaining what it all meant. If the story needs the philosophy explained, the story is broken.

And please keep it out of the evil-corporation-monetises-your-joy register. I've read that one already and so have you.
