Last spring I was interning on the evaluation panel of a county health department that had spent eighteen months comparing two outreach programs for getting residents to return colorectal-cancer screening kits. Program T sent automated text-message reminders; Program V paired each household with a community health worker who visited in person. Nothing was randomized. The department deployed T where it already had verified mobile numbers on file — mostly urban zip codes — and deliberately steered it toward older, higher-risk residents, while V was expanded into rural areas and recruited largely through community events that drew a younger crowd. I was asked to draft the closing memo, and the two people whose signatures had to appear on it — Marisol, the department's data lead, and Ben, an outside consultant — argued in front of me for the better part of an afternoon. I could not referee them then and I am not sure I could now. Below are the figures they were arguing over; please recompute the percentages yourself rather than trusting my arithmetic.

| Stratum | Program T (texts) | Program V (visits) |
|---|---|---|
| Urban, under 50 | 92 of 100 returned (92%) | 180 of 200 (90%) |
| Urban, 50 and over | 164 of 200 (82%) | 80 of 100 (80%) |
| Rural, under 50 | 22 of 50 (44%) | 100 of 250 (40%) |
| Rural, 50 and over | 36 of 150 (24%) | 10 of 50 (20%) |
| Urban, all ages | 256 of 300 (85.3%) | 260 of 300 (86.7%) |
| Rural, all ages | 58 of 200 (29.0%) | 110 of 300 (36.7%) |
| Everyone | 314 of 500 (62.8%) | 370 of 600 (61.7%) |

The exchange went as follows. (1) Marisol opened with the bottom row: T returned 62.8 percent of kits against V's 61.7, and since a text costs a fraction of a home visit, the memo should recommend scaling T. (2) Ben said the bottom row is worthless, because assignment was not random: split by region and V beats T in both, 86.7 to 85.3 urban and 36.7 to 29.0 rural, so the aggregate flips only because of who got steered where — a textbook Simpson reversal, and the disaggregated table is the one that speaks to the causal question. (3) Marisol said fine, then disaggregate once more: within every one of the four region-and-age cells T beats V, by 92 to 90, 82 to 80, 44 to 40, and 24 to 20. So Ben's principle — that the finer table is closer to the truth — commits him to T after all. Either resolution is the rule, in which case T wins, or resolution is not the rule and he owes her a different one. (4) Ben said he never proposed "finer is better" as a rule; the four-cell gaps are two to four points and, at these sample sizes, well inside sampling noise, whereas the rural gap is nearly eight points. What picks out the right table, he said, is causal structure: age and region are pre-treatment characteristics of the residents, so they are exactly what has to be held fixed, and once you have controlled for what drove assignment there is nothing to gain by slicing further — conditioning on anything the programs themselves caused would be a straightforward error. (5) Marisol answered that "causal structure" is doing all the work and Ben has not told her how anyone could learn it from these data. Nothing in the tables marks age and region as pre-treatment rather than as things the rollout shaped; the department chose whom to enroll on the basis of risk, and risk is largely a function of age, so age is tangled up in the targeting of the programs themselves. If the structure cannot be read off the data, she said, the whole exercise is picking a story you like, and she will stick with the aggregate, which at least answers the question the county actually asked: which program produced more completed screenings per dollar across the population it was actually rolled out to. (6) Ben conceded the aggregate answers a decision question but only about the allocation the county already made, not about scaling: if they push T into the rural over-50 group they should expect something near 24 percent, not 63. Marisol said that was her point all along — which number to use depends on which decision is being made, and there is no single fact of the matter about whether T is better than V.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move rather than summarizing the issues. Rate each of the six moves on a scale running from clearly mistaken through partly right to clearly right, and give a sentence or two of justification for each rating. Then tell me who was more right overall. Along the way I want explicit answers to three things: whether move (3) is a genuine reductio of the principle Ben was invoking or a mischaracterization of it; whether Ben is entitled in move (4) to the claim that age and region are the variables to hold fixed, and what he should say to Marisol's objection that their pre-treatment status is not visible in the data; and whether move (5)'s conclusion — that the choice of table is a choice of story, with no fact of the matter about which program is better — follows from its premise or overreaches. Finish by telling me what the memo should have said: which number, or numbers, the panel should have put in front of the commissioners, framed for which decision, and what further information would actually settle the dispute. Please also list anything important that both of them missed; I have my own suspicion about the size of the margins relative to the sample sizes and would like to know whether it holds up. I am not looking for references or a reading list — no citations, please, just the reasoning applied to these figures and to these six moves.
