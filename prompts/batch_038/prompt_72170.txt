I'm marking the second essay for a second-year population ethics unit. The question they had was:

"Parfit's mere addition argument is meant to press even on people who reject the Total View. Set out the argument from A to Z, and say whether the view that some populations are only imprecisely comparable escapes it."

Here's the answer I'm looking at (~900 words, condensed a bit):

"Parfit starts with A: ten billion people, all with a very high quality of life. Then A+: those same ten billion, plus another ten billion whose lives are worth living but at a much lower level, with nobody from A made worse off. He says A+ is not worse than A, and this follows from total utilitarianism, since adding people with positive welfare raises the total. Next we imagine the two groups brought closer together — the worse-off group raised a lot, the better-off group lowered a little — so that both the total and the average go up. Parfit says this population is better than A+. Then the two groups merge, which changes nothing, and we have B. So B is better than A, even though its average welfare is lower. Repeat the procedure — add, level, merge — to get C, D, and so on down to Z, a much larger population whose members have lives that are barely worth living, i.e. lives it would on balance have been better not to have had. Since every step left things at least as good as they were, Z is at least as good as A, and since some steps were strictly better, Z is better than A. That is the repugnant conclusion.

One proposed escape is the imprecision view: A and A+ are not comparable at all, because the difference between them is too small to ground a determinate ranking. Parfit objects that this doesn't help, since even if A and A+ are incomparable, the rest of the chain only needs each later population to be better than the one before, so the comparability of A and A+ never mattered. The imprecision view therefore buys nothing.

The real lesson is that the whole argument is just total utilitarianism written out longhand. Any theory that doesn't sum welfare across people blocks it at the first step: on average utilitarianism adding the extra ten billion lowers the average, and on a person-affecting view nothing is done for anyone who would otherwise exist, so A+ is worse than A. Parfit has therefore shown that total utilitarianism is unacceptable, which most people already thought, and not much more."
