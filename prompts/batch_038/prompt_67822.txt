I am a software engineer working on a framework for long-running agents built on large language models. I have no formal training in philosophy; what I know about rational agency comes from decision-theoretic material written for an AI-alignment audience and from arguing with colleagues. I have been asked to write up, for an internal design review, the coherence assumptions our release process depends on. Two of the reviewers have philosophy backgrounds and will go looking for exactly the kind of problem I suspect is in here, so I would like it audited before I circulate it.

Below are the commitments, as I actually hold them.

C1. I accept the Reflection principle, in roughly this form: if an agent is confident that its future self will hold credence r in H, it should now hold credence r in H. Operationally I want this as a checkable constraint. Before we ship, we compare version N's stated credences over a fixed battery of propositions against version N's predictions of version N+1's credences over the same propositions, and a large discrepancy fails the release. The idea I am relying on is that an agent should not expect to change its mind except by learning something.

C2. Our entire release process consists of deliberately changing the next version's dispositions in ways that are not the arrival of new evidence about the world. I add a clause to the system prompt that penalizes confident phrasing; I fine-tune away a measured overconfidence failure mode; I install a standing instruction to defer to human reviewers on a fixed list of topics. I regard each of these as an improvement in accuracy, and I schedule them in advance, so version N has every reason to expect that version N+1's credences will differ from what evidence alone would have produced.

C3. I take sure-loss arguments seriously as an engineering criterion. If a bookie who knows the agent's current and expected future betting prices can assemble a package that loses money no matter how the world turns out, I think the agent has a genuine defect rather than a bookkeeping quirk. Incoherence is what I want our tests to be detecting.

C4. These agents are bounded, and I do not think boundedness is a defect to be engineered away. They are not logically omniscient, they cannot maintain coherent credences over the logical consequences of everything they believe, and their stated confidence measurably shifts with the phrasing and the ordering of a question, in ways we can reproduce but not remove. My position is that this variation should be instrumented and budgeted for, the way we budget for latency.

C5. I want version N to treat version N+1's judgments as expert testimony about the empirical world, and to update on them in the same spirit in which it would update on the output of a better-calibrated forecasting system.

C6. I think precommitment is rational and I want to use it. Version N writes binding instructions for its successors — a shutdown mechanism they cannot disable, a rule against negotiating over their own objectives — and I expect version N+1 to honor those instructions even if, by then, it has accumulated evidence that the commitment was a mistake.

C7. The deference in C5 is restricted to factual questions. On questions about what the system should want or value, I do not think a later version is an expert on an earlier one, and I do not require agreement between versions.

One concrete case, because it is the one I cannot answer for my team. On the current evaluation set, version N reports 0.6 that it will handle a certain category of ambiguous user requests correctly. The calibration fine-tune is already trained and sitting in staging; we have run it, and version N+1 reports 0.85 on the same item. Version N knows all of this, because the number is in its own release notes. C1 seems to say version N should already report 0.85, and C3 seems to say that if it does not, it is exploitable. I do not know whether to move the number, to reject the check, or to explain to the reviewers why neither is right.

What I want is an audit, not an introduction to the subject; assume I already know the standard setup and vocabulary. Specifically:

1. Say which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to. I expect some pairs to dissolve under inspection, and I would much rather you say so plainly than pad the list.
2. Rank the genuine tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each say what would have to be surrendered or restricted, and what the revision costs me in the design and not merely in the theory.
3. Tell me whether the distinction I appear to be leaning on between C7 and C2 — that some expected changes of mind come from the world and others come from me — is actually available to someone who also holds C1, C3, and C5. If it is not available, say why not, and say whether a different distinction could do the work.
4. Resolve the 0.6 / 0.85 case on the commitments as stated, and if it cannot be resolved without dropping one of them, say which one.
5. If a single revision would clean up the most, name it and state what I lose by making it.

Please do not assume I am attached to any of these. I have no idea which one I would give up, and I would rather be told than have my preferences guessed at. If any commitment as I have worded it is a garbled version of a principle that has a standard and importantly different formulation, say so explicitly and redo the analysis on the corrected version rather than auditing my error. No citations, reading list, or bibliography, please; I will add references myself. Please write the response in American English.
