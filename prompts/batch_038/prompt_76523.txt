I have no academic training — I work in freight logistics — but I have spent several years reading Lakatos on my own, and I have built a scorecard for grading the competing schools of thought in nutrition science, which I publish in a small newsletter. Before I put it in front of readers who know this literature better than I do, I would like a consistency check on the commitments behind it. Here they are:

1. I am a falsificationist in the sense that a theory earns its keep only by risking something, but I also hold that no single observation can refute a theory, since every prediction leans on a pile of background assumptions.

2. Every programme has a hard core that its practitioners treat as irrefutable by decision. A programme cannot be faulted for its core; the only legitimate target of criticism is the protective belt and the way it gets modified.

3. The sole test of whether a programme is progressive is whether its belt modifications produced novel predictions that subsequently came true. A programme that goes a decade without such a success I grade as degenerating.

4. A modification is ad hoc, and so earns no credit, whenever it was introduced for the express purpose of neutralizing the anomaly that prompted it.

5. Appraisal is comparative: a programme is degenerating only relative to a rival doing better, so in a field with a single live programme the label never applies.

6. It can be rational to keep working inside a degenerating programme for years — rivals collapse, and comebacks happen — so I do not treat the label as a verdict of irrationality on the people inside it.

7. My scorecard nonetheless has to yield a usable verdict within three or four years, because I use it to decide what to believe about, say, dietary fat. A framework that only appraises programmes after a generation is useless to me.

8. When I write up a degenerating programme, I name its hard core as the culprit in print, since the core is what forces the increasingly contrived belt adjustments.

Consider a case. Suppose a school predicts that cutting saturated fat reduces heart disease, the trial comes out null, the school adds an auxiliary — the benefit appears only when the fat is replaced by polyunsaturates rather than by refined carbohydrate — and that auxiliary later predicts a subgroup result that a subsequent trial confirms. My rules appear to hand out two different grades for that single move, and I cannot tell whether that is a genuine contradiction or a trade-off I have simply failed to price.

Assume I know the vocabulary; I want the audit, not a tutorial. Tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another and which only look as though they do, rank the real tensions by severity, and for each say which commitment has to give and what the cheapest repair costs. Finish with the single revision that would clean up the most. Open with a one-sentence verdict on whether the package hangs together and which item goes first. Please keep the whole response under 600 words.
