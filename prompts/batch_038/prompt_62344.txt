I've got a ten-week paid summer research post in a philosophy dept — one person, no real supervisor, and at the end they want a paper of about 6,000 words plus a twenty-minute talk to the research group. I want to write it on the problem of old evidence in Bayesian confirmation theory, but from an angle I haven't seen anyone take seriously, and I need help turning a half-formed thing into an actual proposal.

The angle: every standard fix I know of — letting the agent update on something other than the evidence itself, or letting them learn a fact about the logical relation between the old evidence and the hypothesis — quietly assumes that once the agent spots the relation, the confirmation that comes from spotting it is a fixed quantity. But spotting it isn't free, isn't instantaneous, and isn't unique. Sometimes you grind an entailment out through a horrible fifty-page calculation; sometimes you see it in one line because somebody reframed the problem. My hunch is that if how much confirmation old evidence can give you really depends on features of the derivation — its length, how much work it took, whether it generalises — then the problem of old evidence isn't the static-coherence puzzle everyone treats it as, but a puzzle about the temporal profile of an agent's credences and what they're entitled to update on at each moment. And if it doesn't depend on any of that, I want to know why, because I think that's an interesting result in its own right and it would tell us something about what confirmation is even supposed to be measuring.

Second thing I want folded in rather than bolted on: the canonical examples are all scientific (the Mercury perihelion business and so on), but the same structure shows up in mathematics — a conjecture you've had good reason to believe for years because of a pile of checked instances finally gets a proof, and everyone agrees the proof raised its credibility even though it entailed things you already knew. Does whatever solution I propose for the science case carry over, or does its not carrying over show the framework was only ever built for empirical theories?

So: write me the proposal. I want the research question stated sharply enough that it has a determinate answer and someone could tell whether I'd answered it; why the existing positions leave it open rather than merely disagreeing about it; the method — what I would actually be doing week by week across the ten weeks, flagging which parts are formal and which parts are just arguing, and why this route rather than the obvious alternatives; what a successful result looks like and, separately, what would make me say the project had failed and needed reframing; and an honest take on whether it fits in ten weeks for one person or whether some part of it has to go.
