I am co-teaching a graduate seminar with an economic historian and I owe a framing essay of roughly three thousand words on the current state of the Great Divergence debate — the descendant of Needham's puzzle about why China, for centuries the more technologically accomplished of the two civilizations, ceased to be. We have hit a wall, and not over the history. My co-instructor's position is that the question of why industrialization happened in Britain and not in China is perfectly well posed and merely extremely difficult, and that two decades of methodological self-examination has functioned as a way of avoiding the work. My position is that this is not one question but a family of questions, indexed to three parameters the participants almost never fix before arguing: the contrast case, the unit of comparison, and the onset date of the divergence. I want a diagnosis of the impasse rather than an adjudication of it, and I want you to treat it as a live possibility that one or both of us has misidentified what kind of disagreement this is.

Here are five candidate diagnoses. Test each against what the disputants actually claim and do.

1. Equivocation on the logical form of the question. A contrastive why-P-rather-than-Q asks for a difference-maker: a factor whose presence in P, and absence or altered configuration in Q, is doing the causal work. But much of what is offered — accessible coal, high wages and cheap energy, political fragmentation, a culture of useful knowledge, the ecological relief afforded by New World colonies — reads as an answer to the non-contrastive question what caused P, with China functioning as rhetorical scenery rather than as a term of comparison. On this diagnosis the camps are not disagreeing about causes at all; they have failed to notice that they are answering different questions under one heading.

2. Unit-shuffling. The same explanans can be true at one unit of comparison and false at another. England is not Britain is not Western Europe; the Yangtze Delta is not Qing China. The late-divergence camp compares the most advanced region of each civilization and finds rough parity in living standards before 1800; the early-divergence camp compares whole economies, or works in silver-wage rather than grain-wage series, and finds England well ahead by 1600 or 1700. If each side's evidence survives only at its preferred unit, the apparent causal dispute may be a dispute about the individuation of the relata — which is not obviously a merely presentational matter.

3. Explanandum-drift. Whether a candidate cause explains anything depends on the date assigned to the divergence. The geology of coalfields is a live candidate only if the explanandum is dated in the eighteenth or nineteenth century; if it is dated around 1600, coal does no work whatsoever, and the same goes for colonial resource flows. A related version holds that the politically relevant divergence — a unified empire on one side, a permanently fragmented system of states on the other — precedes any measurable economic gap by many centuries, so that the standard formulation mislocates the explanandum in time. Note that neither side treats the dating question as part of the causal question, which may itself be the symptom.

4. Pragmatic and normative loading. Britain-and-not-China is not a neutrally chosen foil; it installs China as the expected case and Britain as the anomaly, and the label for the whole debate only entered currency in the 1990s. Revisionist reaction may therefore be as much a protest about what the question presupposes and who is entitled to set the default as a claim about wages, and that component is not resolvable by better data.

5. The deflationary option, which I want you to consider and, if you reject it, explain why: that there is an ordinary hidden empirical disagreement doing all of the work — about the commensurability of premodern real wages, the conversion conventions, and the reliability of reconstructed output series for a large, monetized, regionally heterogeneous Qing economy — and that everything that looks like philosophy of explanation is surface froth over people who have been looking at different spreadsheets.

What I want back:

- An argued diagnosis, not a survey. Say which of the five does the most work, whether they are jointly consistent or mutually exclusive, and which you reject and why, with reasons drawn from the actual positions rather than from their plausibility in the abstract. Do not tell me what caused industrialization; I am not asking you to settle the history.
- A verdict on whether the question is ill-posed, under-posed (well formed but incomplete, requiring the foil, the unit, and the date to be specified before it has a determinate answer), or well posed and merely hard. These are distinct claims, and my co-instructor and I have been sliding between them.
- What would count as progress given your diagnosis, and of what kind: a distinction, a convention for fixing units and dates, a measurement result, a formal account of contrastive explanation applied to the case, a change of question (for instance, replacing the civilizational contrast with contrasts among regions within each civilization), or an argument that no progress should be expected because the parties hold different but reasonable explanatory interests. If it is a distinction or a convention, say who would have to accept it and what would motivate them to.
- One discriminating test I can apply to the existing literature — something checkable against actual published exchanges, not a further plausibility judgment — that would tell your diagnosis apart from its main rival.

Please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I have the literature and cannot verify anything you point me to.
