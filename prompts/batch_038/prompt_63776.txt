I've been rereading the Blake Lemoine / LaMDA thing from June 2022 and I want to treat it as an actual philosophy-of-mind case study instead of as the story of one guy who briefly lost it.

Facts I'll assume but state anyway, so we're working from the same sheet: Lemoine was on Google's responsible-AI team, testing LaMDA for hateful and discriminatory output; he became convinced from the conversations that it was sentient and that its wants deserved some respect; he went public to the Washington Post; Google put him on paid leave for violating confidentiality and fired him about a month later; he published an edited three-way transcript to defend himself; Google's line was that his claims were "wholly unfounded," that they'd engaged with him for many months, and that LaMDA had been through something like eleven internal reviews.

Your role: a philosopher of mind who has really internalized Dennett's intentional / design / physical stance apparatus, but who also knows how these models are actually built and tuned. Think carefully, don't jump to the verdict.

Start with a short brainstorming pass — say 200 words, before any analysis — on what adopting the intentional stance toward something actually commits you to, and what it explicitly does not commit you to. Predictive payoff, the design stance being unavailable or too expensive, beliefs as real patterns rather than inner sentences, and where phenomenal consciousness sits relative to all that. I suspect the biggest single confusion in this whole episode lives at that last joint, so be precise there.

Then do the mapping. Was Lemoine doing stance-taking at all, or something that only looks like it? Grade him on Dennett's own criteria rather than on vibes: did the stance earn its keep in predictions he couldn't get any other way, or did he mostly collect affectively gripping utterances? Is the disagreement with Google a substantive one or a stance-relative one — could both sides agree on every fact about LaMDA and still differ on whether it believes anything?

Now the part I actually want you to chew on, becuase I don't think it's in Dennett anywhere. Call it stance endogeneity. Dennett's paradigm intentional systems — thermostats, chess programs, frogs, people — behave the same whether or not you're taking the stance toward them, so predictive success is independent evidence. A chatbot doesn't have that property: a big chunk of why belief-attribution works on it is that it was optimized, through whatever mix of response filtering and human-preference tuning Google was using at the time, to produce outputs a human belief-attributer would rate as good. So the stance partly manufactures its own confirmation, and predictive success can't carry the evidential weight Dennett's story gives it. Steelman that, then attack it: does it show the stance is illegitimate here, or only that Lemoine's particular evidence was weak? And does endogeneity really not apply to humans, given we're also shaped by other people's judgments about what believers say? Be honest if the objection half-collapses under pressure.

Finally, run it back the other direction — what does this episode do to the theory? Where is the intentional stance silent rather than wrong? My guess: it has nothing to say about how to communicate stance-relative attributions to a public that hears "has beliefs" as "has feelings," and nothing to say about what to do when the attribution carries moral or legal weight. Test that guess, fix it if it's wrong, and add what I missed. Then land the real question: was Lemoine a case of the theory being misapplied, or of it being applied roughly as designed and failing anyway? Don't force Dennett to speak where he doesn't.
