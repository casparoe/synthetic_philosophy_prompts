I am applying for a two-year postdoctoral fellowship at an institute for legal and political theory, and I need a proposal of roughly 1,800 words built from an idea I have not yet been able to sharpen. The topic is the familiar question of how much citizens ought to be able to hide from the state — the question usually posed by lining up German data protection, American transparency law, and China's Social Credit System on a single axis running from most protective of the citizen to least. My suspicion is that the axis is the problem: that the debate treats visibility as a scalar magnitude, when the normatively significant variable is its architecture.

The three parameters I have in mind are these. First, direction: who is made visible to whom. The Chinese system is conventionally read as the state watching citizens, but its official framing includes the trustworthiness of government agencies and courts among its objects, and the American tradition contains a strong state-to-citizen direction — compulsory disclosure of government records — alongside an almost total absence of restriction on private aggregation. Second, the object of regulation: whether the law attaches to the moment of acquisition or to the structure of the resulting corpus. American Fourth Amendment doctrine has largely policed the search event and has been notably permissive about data lawfully handed to a third party, a permissiveness only partly walked back for historical cell-site location records; the German constitutional doctrine that emerged from the 1983 census decision is instead preoccupied with what happens when many innocuous items are combined and retained, with purpose limitation rather than consent doing much of the work. Third, revisability: whether a record can be corrected, expunged, or allowed to lapse.

If visibility has this structure, then the standard ranking is unreliable, and it should be possible to construct cases in which a regime with more state-visible information is less damaging to whatever interest privacy protects than a regime with less, because its architecture differs. Two test cases I would want worked through: the German obligation to register one's address with municipal authorities upon moving, which sits uneasily beside Germany's reputation and which no American analogue matches, since the United States maintains no general resident register; and the popular picture of the Chinese system as a single citizen score, which by most accounts misdescribes a fragmented apparatus of blacklists and redlists in which court-maintained lists of judgment debtors carry most of the practical weight.

Please turn this into a proposal that does the following. State the research question precisely enough that it has determinate possible answers, and say what those answers would be. Explain why the question is genuinely open — what the scalar framing forecloses, and why neither the legal self-understandings of the three regimes nor the existing comparative literature settles it. Set out the conceptual apparatus: define each architectural parameter explicitly rather than by borrowed label, justify why these three and not others, and name at least one further candidate parameter you considered and rejected, with reasons. Describe the method — how one constructs an architecture-based metric and tests it against real regimes — and defend it against the obvious alternatives: a doctrinal comparison of the texts, an empirical study of surveillance practice, and a first-principles normative theory of the value of privacy. Say what a successful result would look like (I would expect, at minimum, a case where the framework ranks two regimes differently from the amount-based metric and where that difference tracks a real normative intuition), and what would count as failure — in particular, how the proposal would show that the framework is not merely relabeling a scalar in architectural vocabulary. Give a quarter-by-quarter work plan for twenty-four months, and a candid paragraph on what would be cut if only twelve months were funded. Close with a short statement of why an institute for legal and political theory should fund conceptual work here rather than area studies or empirical legal research, and with an honest account of what the project deliberately does not attempt.

Two constraints. Do not include a bibliography, a literature review organized by citation, or references to specific scholarship — I will supply those myself, and I would much rather have invented nothing than have plausible-looking attributions. And do not attempt any comparative claim about the effectiveness of the Chinese system, about which I can gather no reliable evidence; treat its official framing and institutional structure as the object of analysis, not its outcomes.
