I'm teaching the last unit of an upper-level seminar on personal identity, and rather than assigning one more anthology paper I want to run a disciplined anachronism exercise with the students. The unit covers the narrative theory of the self from three angles: Dennett's deflationary picture, on which the self is a center of narrative gravity — an abstraction we posit the way Newtonian mechanics posits a center of gravity, not a thing in the world but a device for organizing and predicting the behavior of a body, so that we are all virtuoso novelists trying to make our material cohere into a single story; the much stronger constitutive claim, in the spirit of Schechtman's narrative self-constitution view, that we become persons only by spinning, inhabiting, and acting on a life-story; and Strawson's attack in "Against Narrativity," which splits people into Episodics, who do not experience their past as theirs or their future as a continuation of it, and Diachronics, who do, and argues that narrativity is neither universal nor a prerequisite for an ethically good life, and may even license a kind of bad faith by letting people tidy up what actually happened.

The exercise puts Montaigne in front of the digital-remains industry: chatbots and voice models trained on a dead person's emails, journals, texts, and posts so the bereaved can keep talking to them, alongside the quieter technology of the algorithmic profile, a "data double" assembled from behavioral traces by parties who have no interest in the person it describes. Montaigne is the natural subject because he is so often cast as the patron saint of narrative selfhood — a life spent making himself the matter of his book, with a prefatory address to readers that is frankly funerary in intent, offering the text to relatives and friends so they might recover some features of his character and humors after he is gone, and an insistence that the book is consubstantial with its author. But the case for calling him a narrativist in the current sense looks shaky on inspection: he foregrounds mutability and self-contradiction rather than coherence, describes his project as tracking passage and change rather than portraying being, and revised accretively, letting later additions sit in the margins beside the original text so that successive versions of himself coexist instead of one overwriting the other.

Here is what I want from you. Reconstruct, as rigorously as the evidence allows, what Montaigne would make of a bot built from his own papers and correspondence: would he have commissioned one, declined one, or wanted one made precisely so that he could argue with it? Take a definite position on where he falls in the Episodic/Diachronic taxonomy, and on whether his practice of self-portraiture supports Dennett's deflationism or Schechtman-style constitutivism — I suspect the interesting answer is that his fidelity to inconsistency does work neither position anticipates, but defend or reject that suspicion rather than assuming it. Address the asymmetry between self-authored narrative and third-party narrative explicitly: does a data double or a griefbot count as a center of narrative gravity in Dennett's sense, and if selves are posits whose job is to make behavior predictable, is a smoothed, consistent posthum simulacrum any worse — or is smoothing exactly the sin Montaigne thought he was avoiding? Please flag at each step whether you are resting on something Montaigne actually commits himself to or extrapolating from temperament and period, register at least one substantial pressure pointing the other way (his vanity, his appetite for posthumous fame, his pleasure in being read), and refuse the two cheap endings: Montaigne as our scold and Montaigne as our fan. Close by saying what the thought experiment contributes to the debate that argument alone would not — in particular, whether the existence of a working posthumous narrative tells us that authorship is essential to narrative selfhood or merely typical of it. I don't need a bibliography, page references, or scholarship; where you're uncertain, say so in the text.
