I am fifty-four, I ran a small regional freight dispatch business for twenty-two years, I sold it last spring, and my formal education stopped at a two-year technical certificate. I have roughly three years of unstructured time, enough saved that I do not need to earn during them, and one concrete aim: a public-facing body of work on societal collapse — a series of long essays and a run of talks for library and historical-society audiences, written for intelligent readers who absorbed this subject through bestsellers and documentaries and never looked further. I came at it in an unglamorous way. Over the past decade my county lost its weekly newspaper, the maternity ward at the regional hospital closed, four school districts consolidated into two, and the courthouse now manages its own records by contract with a firm three states away. None of that resembles the fall of a palace economy, and yet the shape of it — administrative reach thinning while population stays roughly flat — has made it impossible for me to stop asking what the word collapse is actually supposed to pick out. What I want from you is not an overview; I have read the overviews, and I can get them again any time. I want a defended agenda for those three years: which open questions in this field deserve a non-specialist's full attention, in what order, and which I should leave alone entirely.

To make that concrete, here is the framework I have been building privately, which I would like attacked rather than flattered. It seems to me that the word bundles at least four losses that come apart in time: demographic (population size, health, settlement density); administrative (who can tax, conscript, and keep records over what territory); material (long-distance exchange, craft specialization, monumental construction, and the literate machinery those require); and legitimating (the story that made the order feel natural — divine kingship, the ritual calendar, the palace as cosmic center). My suspicion is that most arguments about whether a given case was a collapse are really arguments about which of those four supplies the criterion, and that the interesting question is not whether the losses occurred but what fixes the order in which they occur. My guess at an answer is that the decisive variable is whether the people who carried the administrative and ritual knowledge physically survived and stayed put — scribes, accountants, priests, the households who knew how to run a redistributive economy. Where that carrier group persists, administration can be reconstituted quickly by whoever takes over, and what we are watching is a change of management; where it is destroyed or dispersed, the loss runs for generations even if the population barely dips. I have been using Rapa Nui and the Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean as my two test cases, partly because the popular stories of both strike me as wrong in opposite directions. I also hold a second, less comfortable hunch, and I want to know whether you think it fair or merely cynical: that the two camps in this debate are partly arguing about the moral use of the word rather than about evidence, one side wanting a concept strong enough to warn the present, the other wanting to stop implying that non-European societies destroyed themselves while the people who arrived afterwards were incidental. If that is right, the dispute may be unusually resistant to new data, and settling it might sit upstream of everything else.

So: rank the open questions you think matter most, and defend the ordering against criteria I actually care about — whether someone with library access, published datasets, translations and a spreadsheet can genuinely advance it without excavating anything or learning a dead language; whether settling it would change what a general reader believes or does, or would interest only specialists; how much attention it already receives relative to its importance; and how many other questions hang off its answer. Tell me which two of the questions the popular debate obsesses over I should refuse to write about, and why they are less live than they appear. Tell me what evidence over the next few years would move a question up or down this list, and what my first twelve months should concretely look like given that I work alone and slowly. Be frank about where my four-part decomposition is load-bearing and where it is decoration, and whether the carrier idea is a real proposal or a relabeling of something already argued to exhaustion. I am not asking for a reading list — argue with me instead.
