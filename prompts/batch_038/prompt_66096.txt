I build formal ontologies for a living — mostly knowledge graphs for a freight and logistics company — and I have run into a wall that I strongly suspect is a very old wall with a name I have never learned. Here is how I got to it. Our data is full of relations that are not honestly binary: a shipment is delayed at a particular port on a particular date for a particular reason, and the standard way to keep that in a graph is to turn the whole situation into a node of its own, with ordinary binary edges running out to the shipment, the port, the date, and the cause. The reason for doing this is practical: it gives you something to hang further information off. But the more I stare at it, the less it looks like an explanation of anything. What I wanted to understand was what makes that delayed-shipment-at-this-port-on-this-date one situation rather than a small constellation of nodes and edges that happen to be adjacent to one another, and what the reification actually delivers is a larger constellation. If I say that the new node and its edges are held together by a further edge, then I need a further node for that, and there is no evident place to stop. I had assumed this was an artifact of graphs until I noticed the same shape in two unrelated things I have been reading. In a discussion of sentence structure, the question was what makes a sentence more than the list of its words, and every answer on offer was some further item — a tree, a predication, an operator — which immediately faced the same question about itself. In a metaphysics reading group I audit, someone defended the view that an ordinary object is nothing over and above the properties it has, and when asked what bundles them he said co-instantiation, and when asked what makes those properties co-instantiated he said that co-instantiation is not a further relation on top of them; I could not tell whether that was an answer or a decision to stop asking. A cousin of my own graph case turns up in a semantics of action sentences that converts the verb into an extra participant in its own event, which then has to be tied back to the very participants it was introduced to bring together.

So I have four cases that look to me like one pattern, and I want to know whether it has an established name. I am not after the label alone, though; the label without the carving would be useless to me. I want to know whether these are four instances of a single thing, or two or three things that resemble each other from where I am standing, and what would settle that. In particular: is the case about objects and their properties really the same animal as the case about my graph nodes, or is one of them a merely pragmatic difficulty with a well-understood engineering answer and the other a genuine problem? If there is a name, tell me which of my cases is the canonical one and which are only relatives, and what criterion decides that. If the name covers only part of what I described, or if what I have lumped together is not one phenomenon at all, say so plainly and then give me a definition that draws the edges — what belongs to it, what merely resembles it, and what I would be wrong to fold into it. I would also like to understand, as far as anyone understands it, what would make the endlessness objectionable rather than merely endless: is there an agreed account of when this kind of never-stopping counts against a theory, or is that itself contested? And what are the moves available for stopping it — do any of them generalize across all four of my cases at once, or does each case need its own local fix? That last question is the one with practical stakes for me, because if some category of thing simply cannot be made one thing without the trouble recurring, then modeling situations as nodes is the wrong architecture and I would rather find that out now.

Please do not give me a bibliography or a list of things to read; I will track down whatever you point me at on my own, and I would much rather have your map than your references. Write the whole thing as continuous prose in paragraphs, the way you would write a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. And at the end, tell me how confident you are in the map you have drawn and single out the one claim in it that you are least sure of — above all if you suspect that I have misdescribed one of my own examples, or conflated two patterns that someone who works in this area would keep carefully apart.
