My friend Daniel is a consultant endocrinologist and I finished a philosophy master's last spring, and the two of us spent something like five hours on Saturday night, and most of Sunday morning, arguing about a question that turned out to be much harder to pin down than either of us expected. It began over a patient of his: a woman in her forties with a thyroid-stimulating hormone level modestly above the assay's upper limit, entirely asymptomatic, whom he has been treating for years as a case of subclinical hypothyroidism. I said that calling her ill looks like a category error, because nothing about her life has gone worse and she has no complaint; he said that my objection was not a philosophical discovery but a piece of administrative squeamishness, since her thyroid is measurably failing to do what thyroids do and the only reason I hesitate is that no insurer will pay for it. We kept generating cases and each of us kept claiming that the other's theory gave the wrong verdict, and by Sunday we had realized that we were not actually sure whether we disagreed about the world, about language, or about who is entitled to decide. I would like you to produce the document we should have written instead: a joint statement that a committed Boorse-style naturalist about disease — one who holds that pathology is a statistical deviation from species-typical functional organization, indexed to reference classes of age and sex, with no appeal to whether the deviation is bad for the person — and a committed normativist in the Canguilhemian tradition — one who holds that nothing counts as pathological except in relation to a lived norm the organism or the person is trying to maintain, so that harm, disability, or the frustration of a valued capacity is constitutive rather than merely correlated — could both put their names to after a serious, good-faith adversarial collaboration. I want it to read like an actual working document, not a summary of the literature: no citations, no reading lists, no name-dropping of positions beyond the two just described plus, where unavoidable, hybrid accounts such as Wakefield's harmful-dysfunction analysis, which should be mentioned only to explain why neither party is prepared to retreat into it.

Structurally, please give me a numbered section of agreed claims first, and for each numbered claim a parenthetical note recording what that claim costs the party who is conceding more by accepting it — I want the concessions to be visible, because a joint statement in which every clause is symmetric is worthless. Then a disagreements table with four columns: the disagreement as the naturalist would phrase it in his own preferred vocabulary; the same disagreement as the normativist would phrase it in hers; what would have to be the case for each formulation to come out false; and a verdict on whether the difference is substantive or verbal, with the reasoning given rather than asserted. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions such that a settled answer would move at least one of the two parties — and for each crux a concrete case, experiment, or piece of evidence that both would agree bears on it, together with each side's prediction recorded in advance, so that the disagreement is falsifiable in practice and not merely in principle.

Two further constraints, which are where I think the real work lies. First, Daniel floated a proposal of his own at about two in the morning, and I want the document to test it rather than ignore it: he suggested that every disease attribution silently fixes two parameters, a reference class against which functioning is measured and a goal relative to which functioning is assessed, that the naturalist fixes the goal in advance and then presents the reference classes as discovered rather than chosen, and that the normativist insists both are chosen — so that our entire dispute, on his account, is not about disease at all but about which of the two parameters a given speaker is holding fixed without saying so. Please have the joint statement say explicitly whether this reframing dissolves the disagreement, relocates it, or simply redescribes it, and whether it survives contact with the athlete whose resting heart rate sits far below population typicality while being an unambiguous good, with male pattern baldness, with the asymptomatic carrier of a high-penetrance cancer allele, with age-related sarcopenia in a seventy-eight-year-old, with myopia corrected by spectacles, and with prolonged grief disorder as classified in the DSM-5-TR since 2022. Second, I want at least one case on which the two theories are forced to deliver opposite verdicts, and at least one on which they deliver the same verdict for mutually incompatible reasons — because the latter is what Daniel kept using to claim the whole debate is idle, and I think he is wrong about that, but I cannot yet articulate why, and I suspect the answer has to do with what each theory licenses us to do once the verdict is in: who gets to set the cutoff for a national screening programme, who gets to say that a lowered fasting-glucose threshold has manufactured a disease, and whether a benefits assessor may conclude that a claimant is not ill because she does not feel ill. If you conclude that some portion of the naturalism–normativism dispute is genuinely verbal, say which portion and why, but do not let that become an escape hatch for the rest of it.
