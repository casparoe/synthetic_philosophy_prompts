I write literary fiction — third novel out in the spring — and it's gotten me a slot on a book-festival panel that's nominally about bioethics. Three of us on stage, 45 minutes, and 15 of those for audience Q&A. What I need from you is a prep sheet for that Q&A. Not a defence of my view, and definitely not a summary of the book.

Setup, so you know what I'm actually going to be arguing. The novel sits inside a public health system that covers embryo scoring for a basket of traits. Not designer-baby sci-fi — a clinic with a waiting list, a budget, and a form. The narrator is the counsellor whose job is to present the ranked options and talk nobody into anything. What turns out to be the book's real subject: the ranking the clinic hands parents comes out of a scoring model whose weights nobody can defend in plain language. Everyone in the story is furious at the clinic, but the clinic is doing exactly what the principle of procreative beneficence tells it to do — take the expected-best child — and which child is expected-best depends on how heavily you weighted, say, mild chronic pain against sociability. That's a decision wearing a measurment's clothes.

So my line on stage is going to be: the interesting problem with liberal eugenics isn't the state, and its not the expressivist point about screening sending a message about who's worth being born. It's that "best life" has to be collapsed into one number by somebody, and once you see that, the coercion debate is downstream of an aggregation debate nobody wants to have. I want to say this without sounding like I'm handing selection a free pass.

The room: a disability-studies scholar who's written about prenatal testing and will hear "it's only a measurement problem" as laundering a power problem into a technical one; a clinical geneticist who runs a PGT service and thinks philosophers have never met a decision they couldn't problematise; a legal academic working on wrongful-birth suits and reproductive liberty; a moderator who writes review essays and wants a clean fight; plus a couple hundred festival-goers who mostly haven't read the book.

Give me the objections I'll actually get from that room — not the generic conference ones — ordered by how much damage each would do. For each one: how that specific person would phrase it, because the geneticist and the disability scholar do not use the same words for the same worry; a reply I can land in about ninety seconds while standing up; and a note on where that reply is thin. Then tell me what I should concede in my opening minutes so I'm not conceding it under pressure, which single reply has to land for the whole thing not to collapse into "so you're against choosing?", and the question I should be praying nobody asks — I suspect it's some version of "fine, then who sets the weights, and if your answer is a democratic process you've just handed one person's traits to everybody else's vote." Also flag how the moderator is likely to mis-frame me before I've finished a sentence.
