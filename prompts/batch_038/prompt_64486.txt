Please answer as a philosopher of mathematics and metaphysician of time who has worked seriously on supertasks. Spend a few sentences brainstorming what could possibly distinguish my cases before you commit to any principle.

Background: I am writing next month's talk for the philosophy society I belong to. It began when my eleven-year-old asked why Achilles ever catches the tortoise if there is always another half of the distance left, and in trying to answer her honestly I discovered that my verdicts about completing infinitely many acts do not hang together. I would rather be told which one to abandon than watch them reconciled by hand-waving.

My verdicts:

1. The runner, who covers half the remaining distance each second and finishes in a minute: possible, plainly.
2. Thomson's lamp, switched on a half-minute before noon, off a quarter-minute before, on an eighth before, and so on: impossible. "Is it on at noon?" has no answer, and a device with no state at a moment is not a device.
3. The vase: a half-minute before noon, balls 1–10 go in and ball 1 comes out; a quarter-minute before, balls 11–20 go in and ball 2 comes out; and likewise onward. At noon the vase is empty. I am confident of that, and confident that "empty" is a genuine answer rather than the absence of one.
4. The same vase, except that at each step some ball is removed without my specifying which. Whether the vase ends empty, holding one ball, or holding infinitely many depends entirely on those choices. I want to call this still a possible task, merely under-described — not the failure I called the lamp.
5. A counter that has been ticking since infinitely long ago and has, by noon, written down every natural number one at a time: possible, I think, though it unsettles me in a way the runner does not, and I suspect that unease is prejudice rather than insight.
6. Infinitely many poisoners, numbered 1, 2, 3, ..., the nth instructed to act at a time 1/n of an hour before noon unless the victim is already dead. The victim is dead by noon, and yet no poisoner did it. I judge this impossible, though my reason feels like a cheat.

Two things I cannot reconcile. The runner and the lamp share exactly the same temporal shape, so whatever separates them is not the number of acts or their arrangement in time. And cases 3 and 4 are worse: in both, the description fails to fix the final state, yet in one I hear a determinate answer and in the other mere sloppy bookkeeping. If indeterminacy of that kind is fatal in 2, why is it survivable in 4?

What I want: one or two candidate principles, stated precisely enough to be applied mechanically; for each clause, the case above that motivates it; a plain statement of which of my verdicts the principle forces me to give up; and the principle's verdict on a case I have not listed — ten balls in at each step, with the ball removed always being the highest-numbered one present. Lead with your bottom line in the first two sentences, and keep the whole response under 600 words.
