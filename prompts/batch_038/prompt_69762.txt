You're playing the role of someone who has both taught social epistemology to grad students and sat through a genuinely large number of university committee meetings where a pilot program's evaluation came back messy and everybody wanted it to say something it didn't. Before you write anything, spend a short block just thinking out loud — brainstorm the three arms, the two rival readings of them, and what a memo that actually gets acted on has to look like. Then drop that and write the memo.

Here's the situation. I'm a third-year PhD student and I'm the grad-student rep on my university's Digital Citizenship Task Force, which the provost stood up two years ago to figure out what, if anything, the university owes first-years by way of "navigating information." Last fall we finally ran a pilot: a required one-credit module for the whole incoming class (~1,150 students), randomly assigned to three arms.

- Arm A ("Crossfeed"): six weeks, twenty minutes a week, students had to read a curated feed of articles from outlets on the opposite side of their own stated political leaning. We built the feeds.
- Arm B ("Provenance"): six weeks, students never saw opposing political content at all. They practiced tracing a claim back to its primary source, identifying authorship and funding, and telling a study apart from a press release about a study. We also handed them a curated list of "reference-grade" sources to practice on.
- Arm C: control, the standard one-session library orientation.

We measured three things at week 8 and again in April: an affective polarization index, a 30-item factual quiz built from claims that were actively disputed across the political spectrum (not from one side's fact-check list), and a source-trust survey rating twenty outlets and institutions. Results, roughly: Arm B beat control on the quiz by about 8 points and was flat on polarization. Arm A was flat on the quiz, worse than control on polarization by about 0.4 SD, had three times the non-completion rate — and, this is the part I keep thinking about, its trust ratings didn't spread out or redistribute. They went *down across the board*, including for outlets those students had started out trusting. Arm A's April numbers look like a general collapse in willingness to take any source's word for anything, not like a shift from one side to another.

There are three readings in the room. The chair, a comms professor, thinks Arm A confirms that exposure backfires, full stop — that you can't read your way out of an echo chamber and we should stop pretending the assignment was ever going to work. The dean of students, plus the trustee-appointed member, thinks Crossfeed was defective by design: we picked the content, the students knew it was assigned, so it was a compliance exercise wearing exposure's clothes, and if we kill it we're the university deciding which opinions first-years may encounter. My own read is that both are describing the same result from different floors of the building, and that what we actually failed to establish is which thing our first-years are in. There's a distinction everyone in my program invokes constantly and that I'd want the memo to use without ever lecturing about it: one failure mode is that people simply don't hear the other side, and the fix is more exposure; the other is that people have been actively taught to distrust the other side, and the fix is rebuilding trust, because handing them content from a discredited source doesn't just fail, it confirms the discrediting and spreads it. If that's right, Arm A's uniform trust collapse is the tell, and it means we may have accidentally run a distrust-confirmation experiment on 380 eighteen-year-olds.

The decision: the provost wants a recommendation by the end of the month on whether we (1) scale Provenance to the full first-year class next fall, (2) revise and re-run Crossfeed at smaller scale, (3) revert to control and spend the money elsewhere, or (4) fund some fourth thing. We have about $180k and one staff FTE, and the module can only occupy the one credit-hour slot — whatever we pick displaces the rest.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate to the task force before Thursday's meeting, in the voice of the committee's own working document, not a student paper. It has to recommend one option concretely — what happens this spring versus next fall, who does what, and what we do if it doesn't work — and the reasons have to be ones the chair and the dean could actually argue with rather than things they'd just nod past. Take the dean's objection at full strength, including the part I suspect is right, and don't let the free-speech worry get folded into a paragraph about "stakeholder concerns." Also flag the thing I'm most embarrassed about in my own arm: Provenance's students were working from a source list *we* curated, so scaling it may just be building a slower, better-funded version of the same structure, and the memo should say what design change that forces.

Please use exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **Bottom line** — under 120 words. Which option, what happens, by when.
2. **What the pilot actually shows** — around 300 words. Interpret all three arms, and be explicit about which results are ambiguous or over-determined rather than smoothing them.
3. **The mechanism we're betting on** — around 250 words. Why the exposure-versus-trust distinction changes the choice between scaling Provenance and re-running Crossfeed. Mechanism only, no survey of the field.
4. **The strongest case against this recommendation** — around 250 words. The chair's and the dean's best arguments, stated better than they'd state them, plus the curation problem in Arm B.
5. **Conditions and kill criteria** — around 200 words. What we'd have to see in year one to revise or stop, and separately: what evidence would make me give up my own reading of Arm A, since if the chair is right that this is a general truth about exposure then my recommendation is overfit to one campus and one semester.
6. **Confidence** — around 120 words. Say how confident you are in the recommendation overall, and name the single point you're least sure of and why that one and not some other.

Two ground rules: don't cite anything by name and don't invent additional studies, effect sizes, or literature — I'll bring my own references to the meeting and a made-up citation would be worse than none. Keep it to about 1,300 words total, plain prose, no bullet points outside section 5. And be careful with the polarization measure: a rise on an affective index after reading the other side is not automatically a bad outcome, and the memo should say whether we care about it and why, because if we don't answer that the chair will answer it for us.
