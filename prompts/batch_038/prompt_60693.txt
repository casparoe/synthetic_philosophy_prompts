DRAFT — "Who exactly collapsed? notes toward the idea that collapse is a property of records, not of people"

1. The popular version. Jared Diamond's Collapse (2005) basically invented the modern idea that societies fail because they make bad environmental choices: Easter Island cutting down the last tree, the Maya wrecking their soil. It's a moral story — they could have chosen differently.

2. The better version. Tainter's The Collapse of Complex Societies (1988) defines it without the morals: a rapid, significant drop in the level of socio-political complexity a society had reached. Nothing about good or evil, just about how many specialized roles there are, how much administration, how much tribute flowing to a center. On that definition a smaller village farming the same land and speaking the same language is a different society, but not obviously a worse one.

3. My argument. Neither definition says *whose* complexity got lost, and the layers come apart all the time. A society is a stack: how people farm and eat; where they live and how big the settlements are; who collects tribute and settles disputes; whether anything gets written down; whether monuments are still going up; what language is spoken. When we say something collapsed we're usually reporting that two or three layers fell and the rest kept going.

— Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, around 1200 BCE. Palaces burned (Hattusa, Ugarit, the Mycenaean centers), the Sea Peoples turn up in Egyptian records and are treated as the cause, Linear B stops being used and Greek doesn't get written again for centuries until the Greeks reinvented writing. But villages continue, pottery traditions continue. It's mostly a story about the top of the stack: administration and writing.

— Classic Maya, 8th–9th c. CE. Southern lowland cities stop raising dated monuments and get largely abandoned, but the north (Chichen Itza) rises at roughly the same time, and when the Spanish arrive in the 1500s they find Maya-speaking polities that have to be beaten one at a time. Millions of Maya people are alive today. What collapsed was a political geography, not a people.

— Rapa Nui. The old story is ecocide and starvation. Newer work says the palm forest went mostly because of the rats the settlers brought, which ate the nuts so nothing regenerated, and that the obsidian points everyone assumed were war weapons look more like everyday tools. So: environmental change, yes, self-destruction narrative, no, and the population was more or less holding steady until Europeans brought disease and slave raids.

4. The part I think is actually mine. Every one of these cases needs a written or monumental record before we can even know a high-complexity phase existed. A society with no writing and no stone temples can't collapse in Tainter's sense, because there's no baseline to measure a drop against — we'd just find the later village and call it normal. So collapse might be less a fact about the past than a fact about which pasts left archives. And the archive layer is also the layer that survives best in the ground, because elites build in stone and keep clay tablets while farmers build in wood and mud and leave potsherds. Which means the evidence for collapse and the thing that's said to have collapsed are the same layer twice over. That's a selection effect, and it suggests the concept is measuring how fragile record-keeping institutions are, not how fragile societies are.

5. Proposal. Stop asking whether X collapsed. Ask, per layer, whether it fell, when, and who lost what — a layer ledger. Then "the Maya collapsed" isn't false so much as incomplete to the point of misleading, and the interesting question becomes which layers can drop out without taking the others with them, since apparently writing and administration can go while food, language and population stay.
