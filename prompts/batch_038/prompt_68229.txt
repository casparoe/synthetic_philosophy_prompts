I'm setting the final for a senior seminar on individual responsibility for collective harm — climate, consumption, voting, that cluster. It's 2.5 hours with two questions, so this one gets about 70 minutes and is worth 25 marks. I've got the skeleton of a question built around a made-up case with actual numbers in it, and I want help turning it into something exam-ready plus a model answer and a marking scheme.

The case, as I have it in my head. Tighten the wording however you like but keep the numbers — the numbers are the whole point:

A coastal fishery sells a cheap white fish, $9 a portion, through supermarkets. Retailers place around 3 million orders a season, from a customer base of about 250,000 households, so each household orders maybe a dozen times over the season. The boats operate under a rule: they run the sustainable season only, unless cumulative retail orders pass 3 million, in which case they add a second bottom-trawling season that destroys the breeding stock for good. The damage from that second season is roughly $2 billion — a food source and the economy built on it, gone permanently, and borne largely by people who never bought the fish.

Two versions of the same season:
- Version A: expected total orders = 3.4 million, standard deviation about 20,000 (the aggregate is noisy — weather, a competitor's promotion, whatever).
- Version B: expected total orders = 3.0 million, same spread.

Here's what I want students to land on, and why the case has arithmetic in it at all. In Version B, the chance that any given order is the pivotal one — the one that moves the count from exactly 3 million to 3,000,001 — is about 1 in 50,000; you can just stipulate that figure for them, it's the density of the distribution at the threshold times one unit and I don't want anyone needing a probability course. Multiply by $2bn and a single $9 order carries roughly $40,000 of expected harm. In Version A the expected total sits twenty standard deviations past the threshold, so the pivotal probability is zero for any practical purpose and the expected harm of an order is zero. Same act, same harm function, same crowd — and an expected-harm account says the order is grotesquely wrong in B and completely fine in A.

The question should make them: (1) actually do that multiplication and the A/B comparison, not gesture at it; (2) say what it shows about the standard expected-utility reply to the inefficacy objection, including the corollary I think is the real killer — the act comes out *permissible* in exactly the version where the disaster is certain, so permissibility tracks the location of a crowd rather than how bad things go; (3) handle the epistemic wrinkle, which I think is underappreciated: no diner can know the mean or the spread, so on this account permissibility depends on facts nobody can access, and it looks like it gives agents a reason not to read the mid-season sales report — is a moral theory allowed to make your duty depend on news you'd rather not get?; and (4) say whether the non-causal alternatives do better or just relocate the problem. By (4) I mean complicity-style views and membership or joinability views, where wrongness comes from taking part in a practice rather than from contributing to the harm. I want them pressed hard on the discrimination worry: if participation is doing the work, why isn't buying electricity or a bus ticket in a modern economy exactly as wrong as the fish, and what would it take to answer that without collapsing back into consequences.

They'll have seen the usual material in class — Parfit's harmless torturers, the sorites-style each-drop arguments, the voting version of the same problem. The question must not be answerable by reciting any of that, and I don't want them citing anything: the case is self-contained and everything they need is inside it. There also shouldn't be one right verdict. A student who argues the purchase is permissible in both versions, or wrong in both, should be able to get top marks if the argument is good.

So, deliverables:

1. The question as it would appear on the paper, case stated cleanly and compactly, with the Version B pivotal probability given to them as a stipulated figure rather than something to derive.
2. A model answer of about 700 words.
3. A marking scheme out of 25, built as five or six must-hit moves with marks apportioned, and for each move an explicit line on what a B answer does where an A answer does something else — I want to be able to hand this to a TA and get consistent grades.
4. A short list of four or five wrong answers you'd realistically expect to see, e.g. treating Version A's certainty of collapse as making the act worse, or writing "small contributions add up" as if that were a reply to a threshold case, or concluding from the epistemic problem that morality must be subjective. For each, one line on where the mark ceiling is and what partial credit it can still pick up.
