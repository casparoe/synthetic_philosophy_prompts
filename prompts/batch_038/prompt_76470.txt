---

Braudel's *The Mediterranean and the Mediterranean World in the Age of Philip II* (published 1947, though he wrote much of it as a prisoner of war in Germany) is the classic statement of the Annales rejection of event-based history. Braudel, together with Marc Bloch, founded the Annales approach in 1929, and in this book he organises his material around three scales of historical time, which he sets out in the preface and then embodies in the structure of the book itself.

The slowest scale is the longue durée, the subject of Part One: the relationship between men and their environment — mountains and plains, coastlines, the seasonal winds, the distances between ports. Braudel is explicit that this level is almost motionless; the same patterns of transhumance, the same tension between mountain dwellers and plain dwellers, recur across the whole of Mediterranean history. The point of the longue durée is that it looks at a very long stretch of time, centuries rather than decades, so that the historian can see trends a narrative of a single reign would miss.

The middle scale is the conjuncture, which is what Part Two deals with: events. Wars, diplomacy, the decisions of Philip II and his ministers, battles like Lepanto. The conjuncture is where the historian narrates what actually happened, and it is the scale that most history before the Annales was written at.

Part Three then deals with the slow movement of economies, societies and civilizations — trade routes, price cycles, the great urban civilisations of the Mediterranean — which is the level Braudel thinks really matters, and the level at which he does most of his own original work.

On agency: because Braudel holds that individuals are carried along by currents they do not see and could not describe, events being for him mere surface agitation above much deeper movements, he cannot consistently hold that Philip II or Don John of Austria made free choices that changed anything. Part Three of the book is therefore a concession to the old Sorbonne historians he was arguing against rather than something he believed in. His geohistory is deterministic in the way Ratzel's was: the environment sets limits, and what happens inside those limits is more or less forced. So the charge that Braudel leaves no room for agency is broadly correct, though he would have denied it. The best that can be said for him is that he leaves room for collective agency: Mediterranean societies respond over generations to the constraints of the sea, and in that limited sense they make their own history, but not in conditions of their own choosing.
