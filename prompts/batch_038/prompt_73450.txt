I am drafting a chapter on the three canonical failures of the Athenian democracy — Mytilene, Sicily, and the trial of Socrates — and before circulating it I would like a consistency audit of the interpretive position I have settled into. The commitments are these:

1. The reversal of Cleon's decree at Mytilene, decided in a second assembly on the following day, evidences a genuine capacity for collective self-correction that I regard as distinctive to the Athenian constitution. That the repeal came too late to save the men Paches had already sent forward, and that the ringleaders were executed and the walls demolished anyway, I treat as regrettable consequences rather than as defeaters of the point.

2. The speech that secured the reversal argues entirely from expediency: it declines to plead the Mytilenaeans' innocence, warns against pity and against the deterrent value of extreme penalties, and contends only that massacre is bad policy. I accept Thucydides' presentation of this argumentative strategy as historically faithful in substance.

3. At Sicily, the demos heard Nicias's objections, reconvened, and voted not merely to proceed but to enlarge the armament on the strength of his own inflated estimate of what would be required. Within a dozen years, the mechanism that had functioned at Mytilene had ceased to function.

4. The catastrophe owed much to defective intelligence — the borrowed plate displayed at Segesta, general ignorance of the island's size and population — so I resist the diagnosis on which Athens was simply undone by its own excess of character.

5. Thucydides states that he composed the speeches to suit each occasion while keeping as close as he could to what was actually said. Accordingly, I treat those speeches as unusable evidence for the reasons any particular orator advanced.

6. In 399 Socrates was convicted by a jury of his fellow citizens, and when invited to name his own penalty proposed first public maintenance in the Prytaneum. I count the conviction as a failure of Athenian toleration, and the penalty phase as showing that the procedure offered him no realistic way out.

7. Methodologically, I want to hold that these are failures of three different kinds — normative at Mytilene, informational at Sicily, and a failure of toleration at Athens in 399 — rather than three illustrations of one generic democratic irrationality.

8. I treat Plato's Apology as a philosophical composition rather than a record of proceedings, and I do not build arguments on its details.

Please tell me which of these pull against one another and how severely, ordered from the most damaging downward. Some pairs will look inconsistent and turn out not to be; I want those distinguished explicitly from the ones that are genuinely untenable, rather than a list that treats every friction as equally serious. For each real tension, state the minimal revision that would resolve it and what the revision costs me — in particular, whether any fix would collapse commitment 7, since the taxonomic separation of the three episodes is what the chapter is built around. I have deliberately not indicated which claims I would be most willing to surrender, and I would ask that the audit not be shaped by a guess about my attachments. Finally: identify the single revision that would buy the most coherence overall, and assume familiarity with the events and the texts — I want the audit, not a summary of the history.
