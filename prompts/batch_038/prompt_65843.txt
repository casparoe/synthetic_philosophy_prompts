Last term I audited a reading group in our department that worked through contemporary debates about material objects. Two of the regulars, Ingrid and Marcus, ended up in a long argument about the special composition question, and I was the one taking notes. Their exchange ran as follows.

1. Ingrid opened by claiming that the question — when do some things compose a further thing? — admits only two non-arbitrary answers: always, or never. Her support was this: any restricted answer must place a boundary in what is really a continuous series. Imagine particles spread across a room, then nudged closer together one small adjustment at a time until they form a tight cluster. A sharp cutoff somewhere in that series looks unprincipled, and a fuzzy one is unavailable, because vague composition would entail that it is sometimes vague whether certain things exist, and vague existence turns into vague identity, which is ruled out.

2. Marcus replied that this argument proves too much. By the same standard, sharp boundaries everywhere become suspect — including ones we seem to need, such as between the living and the non-living. He added that never is not the cheap option Ingrid suggests. Nihilism implies there are no tables, so ordinary talk is either systematically false or owes a paraphrase; and the familiar paraphrase, that the simples are arranged tablewise, smuggles back everything nihilism claimed to do without, since the arrangement is now doing all the explanatory work the table was supposed to do.

3. Ingrid granted the paraphrase charge but said it rebounded on Marcus. If the paraphrase really preserves everything worth preserving, nihilism costs nothing in practice and gains a genuine advantage: it can explain why the composites are exactly the ones that strike us as salient, namely because salience comes from our descriptive practices rather than from the world. She added that the living/non-living case shows a restricted view can be principled, which is why she takes the view that only living things compose to be the one restricted answer worth taking seriously — though she rejects it, because it cannot mark when a life begins or ends without generating arbitrariness of its own.

4. Marcus then attacked a shared presupposition. Both of them had been assuming that an answer must be an informative general principle. He said the assumption should go: composition may simply be brute, obtaining in just the cases it obtains, with no unified criterion behind them. On his telling, the demand for a principle is an aesthetic preference dressed up as an argument.

5. Ingrid answered that brutalism is not an answer but a refusal to give one, and that it faces her salience objection in an aggravated form. On brute restricted composition there is no account whatever of the striking match between the composites that exist and the composites we find worth naming, whereas nihilism explains the match through our practices and universalism dissolves it, since on that view everything composes and there is no selection to explain.

6. Marcus closed by suggesting the whole dispute might be verbal: if the domain of the existential quantifier is fixed by a conceptual scheme, nihilists and realists may be saying one thing in two idioms. Ingrid said that reduces metaphysics to semantics, and that in any case her claim concerned the structure of the world, not merely what exists.

They agreed to disagree. I would like a careful adjudication rather than a survey of the terrain. Please do not list the standard positions or narrate the literature; work only from the moves as I have described them. For each of the six moves, assign a rating on a scale running from −2, clearly mistaken, through 0, indeterminate or double-edged, to +2, clearly correct, and justify the rating in two or three sentences that identify what specifically does the work — noting, where a move is only partly successful, which part survives. Then state in a short paragraph who is better placed when the exchange ends, and why that verdict does not simply follow from which of the two views you find more attractive. Finally, name one consideration that neither of them raised which bears directly on move 5, and say whether it raises or lowers the score you assigned there.
