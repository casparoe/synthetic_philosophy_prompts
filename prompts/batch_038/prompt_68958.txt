I'm working on a paper about the problem of old evidence and I've got a draft of the core section. Could you read it and tell me honestly whether its actually saying something, or whether it's a relabeled version of an existing solution?

DRAFT

The puzzle is familiar. In 1915 Einstein found that his field equations gave the leftover advance in Mercury's perihelion that Newtonian perturbation theory couldn't account for. Astronomers had known the number for decades, so Einstein learned no new fact about the world — yet everyone agrees this was a major confirmation of general relativity. On the Bayesian account it shouldn't have been: if P(E) was already 1 for Einstein, the ratio definition gives P(H|E) = P(H), and conditionalizing on E moves nothing.

The standard repairs: learn something about the entailment relation rather than about the world, and drop logical omniscience; or deny that any E ever gets probability exactly 1, so there's always slack; or say the boost comes from eliminating rivals rather than from E itself; or treat it as an artifact of modelling discovery as instantaneous, and cash it out in terms of prediction versus accommodation.

My claim is that these are all groping at the same thing at the wrong resolution. What changed for Einstein wasn't any entry in his credal state. It was the contents of a set: the derivations available to him at a cost he was willing to pay. So let A_t(H,E) be the proposition "there is a derivation of E from H accessible to me at time t for a cost no greater than c." Old evidence confirms H exactly when A flips from false to true, and the size of the boost is set by P(H | A) against P(H | not-A). A is contingent and empirical — a claim about my cognitive economy, not a claim about logic — so P(A) is neither 0 nor 1 for anyone, and no surgery on logical omniscience is needed. The entailment relation never enters the object language.

The view has teeth. It says old evidence confirms more when the derivation was hard, which matches how the Mercury episode is actually talked about compared with a theory that turns out to imply some datum it could have been shaped around in an afternoon. It says the confirmatory gain from a piece of old evidence decays as a field's tooling improves and A gets cheap for everyone. And it says prediction beats accommodation only derivatively: not because the data is fresh, but because in the prediction case the agent had already committed before A became true, so there was no opening for tuning.

The obvious worry is psychologism — confirmation shouldn't depend on who's doing the confirming. My reply is that it already does, since P(H) is a property of an agent, but I'm not sure the analogy holds: priors are at least answerable to coherence norms, and "what I can derive by Friday" isn't answerable to anything.

END DRAFT
