I lead a small working group at a consumer app company: four engineers and one product designer, with about three hours a week each for the next two quarters. Our charter is to produce an internal standard that says which engagement features we are permitted to ship. The technical part is easy; the conceptual part has stalled us completely, and I want your help deciding what to spend our remaining hours on.

Here is the problem we keep hitting. We cannot say what separates manipulation from legitimate persuasion in a way that survives contact with real features. Our first draft test was that a feature is manipulative if it changes behavior without routing through the user's reflective judgment, or if it exploits a known cognitive bias, or if it leaves users worse off by their own stated aims. Every clause misfires. A streak counter with loss framing satisfies the first two clauses, and so does a meditation app's daily reminder that the user explicitly asked for; a countdown timer on a discount satisfies all three and also describes a sale sign in a shop window; a "three of your friends are active now" banner looks benign on our criteria but works precisely by manufacturing a social fact the user cannot check; and a plain, informative email about a feature someone would like seems to fail none of the clauses even when the timing was chosen by a model optimizing for reply rate. Meanwhile our most contested internal case is not a dark pattern at all: it is a re-ranking of a feed that no one would call deceptive, whose only problem is that the objective it serves is not the user's.

Given that starting point, which open questions here are actually worth our time? Please give me an ordered agenda of five to seven questions, not a survey, and no bibliography or reading list — nobody on the team has philosophy training, and I would rather have your reasoning than your sources. For each question, tell me: what makes it upstream of the others on your list, whether a group like ours can make real progress on it at three hours a week without formal background, which specific shipping decision at a company like ours it would unblock, and what would count as progress as opposed to more disagreement. Where two of your criteria conflict — a question that is foundational but hopeless for us, or one that is tractable but changes nothing — say which you weighted and why.

Then tell me what you are deliberately leaving off. I expect there are questions that dominate discussion of this topic that you think are overrated for our purposes, or that only look like they bear on design decisions when they do not; name two or three and explain why they would waste our two quarters.

Finally, identify the single crux that any standard of the kind we are trying to write quietly depends on — the assumption that, if it is false, undermines the whole approach rather than one clause of it — and say what we would have to observe or be persuaded of over the next two quarters to move items up or down your ordering. Assume I will push back on anything that reads like a definition we could have written ourselves.
