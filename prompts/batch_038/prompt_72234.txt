I'm building a training module for people about two years into jobs at regulatory agencies — the level where you're signing off on enforcement discretion, not the level where you set policy — and before I feed it any more dirty-hands literature I want to know what I should be demanding of a theory in this area at all. So: write me the spec. What does an account of dirty hands have to deliver to count as adequate *for that audience*, and I want the consideration or case behind each requirement, not just the requirement. Mark which ones are non-negotiable and which are really the preferences of one camp wearing a neutral face. If some subset turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say so plainly and show the argument — that's the part I most want, because if the genre can't coherently demand what it demands, that's a finding, not a defect in my syllabus.

Here's the kind of case the module runs on, so calibrate to it. An enforcement attorney learns from a source she's promised to protect that a mid-sized firm will start routing contaminated process water into a municipal system in about eleven days. She can't get a warrant that fast and can't corroborate without burning the source, so she authorizes a pretext inspection on a fabricated paperwork deficiency. It turns up exactly what she expected, the dumping never happens, nobody is hurt by the pretext itself. A year later the firms lawyers work out how the inspection was triggered and the case against them is thrown out on the paperwork. She keeps her job, keeps her promise, and thinks about it most nights. A theory of dirty hands that handles the wartime head of state but has nothing to say about her is not the theory I need, so several of the requirements should be keyed to features this case has that the classic examples don't: the wrong is small, procedural, and invisible; it's discovered later by the people it was aimed at; the role she occupies arguably pre-authorizes a wide band of enforcement discretion, so it's genuinely unclear whether anything dirty is left over once role morality has done its work; and the residue has to be cashed out as something she actually owes somebody rather than as a mood.

Two more things I want you to do with the list rather than just append to it. First, treat at least three of these as candidate requirements and tell me whether each is a real desideratum or a prejudice: (a) that the theory explain why the agent is not simply off the hook; (b) that the theory be usable prospectively, in real time, by someone with twenty minutes and a phone — because most accounts only work as hindsight and trainees will use whatever I give them as a decision procedure; (c) that the theory be non-self-exculpating, meaning a motivated agent can't deploy it to license their own preferred option, which I think deserves to be a hard requirement rather than a worry in a footnote. Also tell me whether requiring the agent to feel distress is a substantive claim or a literary tic, and whether the demand that the theory keep blameworthiness and all-things-considered rightness apart is a genuine distinction or a stalemate between Kantians and consequentialists with a nice name on it. Second, name the single requirement that knocks out the most contenders and say why it does the work. Skip the full scorecard, I'll read Walzer and Nagel and the rest myself — but give me two or three sentences at the end on whether the standard wrong-yet-right-all-things-considered formulation survives the list as you've stated it.
