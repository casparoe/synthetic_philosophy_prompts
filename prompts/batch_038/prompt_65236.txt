For my senior ethics seminar I wrote an argument defending our county board's decision to reject a pilot release of gene-drive mosquitoes intended to suppress an invasive midge that is destroying the sedge meadows in the wetlands. Reading it back, the argument seems to prove far more than I intended, since it appears to condemn the board's own decision as well. My teacher had formalized the precautionary idea as what she calls the Reversibility Constraint (RC): an intervention is permissible only if some later, feasible course of action could return the affected system to the state it would have occupied had the intervention never been undertaken.

1. RC states the precautionary idea correctly: what makes a risk unacceptable is not how large it is but whether we could take it back.
2. The drive the board was offered was designed to be self-sustaining and to spread by inheritance, so once released, no feasible later action could return the midge population to the genetic condition it would have had without the release.
3. Therefore the release violates RC.
4. The board's alternative — leaving the meadows to the midge — is condemned by exactly the same reasoning, because the species losses and habitat conversion it permits are just as unrecoverable.
5. If RC condemns both intervening and refraining in one and the same choice, it condemns every option in every choice of this kind.
6. A principle that leaves an agent nothing permissible to do is not a principle of decision at all, but at most an expression of regret.
7. So RC does not state the precautionary idea correctly.

I showed this to my teacher and she said only that it was unsound, not where. Which step should I drop, and what made it look innocent to me? If more than one is a candidate, tell me what I would be committed to by dropping each. I would rather give up (6) than (1), since (1) is the claim I actually believe, but I have no idea what giving up (6) would be buying me.
