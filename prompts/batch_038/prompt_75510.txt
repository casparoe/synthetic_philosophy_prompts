Consider the following precisely described case. A patient has had the corpus callosum completely severed, but is tested tachistoscopically: for roughly 150 milliseconds different stimuli are flashed to the left and right visual half-fields, so that during that window each hemisphere holds information the other cannot reach, while arousal, attention, motor control, and autobiographical memory remain continuously shared across the whole interval. Call the claim at issue S: during such a window there are two numerically distinct subjects of experience, rather than one subject whose experience is internally disunified.

My credence in S is about 0.35, and I am less interested in the number itself than in where a reasonable person could land differently from me. Give me your own figure together with the decomposition that produces it — at minimum: the probability that phenomenal unity is an all-or-nothing matter rather than something that comes in degrees; the probability that, if it comes in degrees, the degree realized in this window is low enough to license counting two subjects rather than one; the probability that co-consciousness, rather than some functional notion such as integrated information or common access to a single workspace, is what constitutes unity; the probability that a 150-millisecond window is on a metaphysical par with permanent disconnection for purposes of counting subjects; and the probability that the question how many subjects there are is determinate at all, rather than one on which our counting conventions simply run out. Add any further term you think is doing real work. Give each a number and one sentence of justification, show how they combine into your total, and then say which term my 0.35 and yours most likely diverge on, and what argument or empirical finding would move that term most.
