Our department is running one of those half-day adverserial collaboration things in March — two people who've been sniping at each other in print for years get put in a room together and, instead of giving talks, they have to leave having co-signed a document. My advisor volunteered me to draft the skeleton, which is well above my pay grade, and I've been going in circles for a week. The two parties: side A holds the Block-style line that phenomenal and access consciousness come apart, so a global workspace / global broadcast theory is at best a theory of access and leaves the interesting part untouched; side B holds that there's no phenomenally conscious state that isn't globally available, so the overflow intuition is an artifact of how we measure — roughly the "consciousness can't overflow" crowd around Kouider and Dehaene, with Lamme's no-report people sitting uncomfortably between the two camps. What I need from you is the document itself, not a survey of the debate; I can get that out of any review article. So start with a numbered list of claims both sides can honestly sign, and make them non-vacuous — nothing side A would only agree to because it's too vague to disagree with. My own guesses at what belongs there: that a large amount of visual processing is unconscious by everyone's lights; that report is a fallible and interest-laden measure with well-understood confounds (criterion shifts, decay between stimulus and response, the fact that asking for a report itself redirects attention); that partial report beats whole report in the classic Sperling-style setups, with both sides accepting the data and disagreeing about what's still in memory at the moment of the cue; that whatever else broadcast does, it's supposed to make information available for reasoning, verbal report and flexible action control; and that neither side has a full account of attention. Add to that or cut it down as you see fit, but tell me which of my candidates is actually contested — I suspect one of them isn't nearly as safe as I think it is.

Then a disagreements table: one row per genuine disagreement, and for each row give the formulation of the issue that side A would sign for its own position, the formulation side B would sign for its own, and a column marking the row as verbal or substantive. If it's verbal, say what the underlying substantive question is, if there even is one; I have a feeling a chunk of the "is there such a thing as unconsciously represented phenomenality" business is two people using one term for different things, but I can't pin down which chunk. Then the cruxes — the sub-questions where settling the issue would actually move someone. I think there are at least three candidates: (i) whether sensory memory is rich, and if it is, whether richness buys phenomenality rather than just decaying, unusable information; (ii) whether "globally available" can be characterized without smuggling in phenomenal report, since if it can't, side B looks circular at the exact point it needs to be non-circular; (iii) whether a no-report paradigm can ever show consciousness in the absence of access, and what each side would commit now to saying about such a result. For each crux, name the experiment or case both parties would agree bears on it, and write each side's prediction down in advance, in wording they'd accept as theirs.

One of those I want to hang on a specific design we've been kicking around, which we've taken to calling the lagged-report thing: twelve-item array, very brief presentation, a cue at a delay varied from about 100 ms out to about 1.5 s, and then both a cued-item identification response and a separate visibility/confidence rating on a forced scale. My guess at the predictions is that side A says identification and visibility stay coupled out to long delays, so there's more phenominal content present than can be reported at any one moment, while side B says the coupling is trivially expected because both responses are reading off the same sparse store, and predicts the visibility ratings collapse toward something like a four-item limit much faster than side A wants. I'm honestly not sure I've got that right — fix it if it's wrong, and say what an outcome that would surprise both sides looks like, since that's the kind of thing that makes a pre-registered prediction worth writing down rather than a hedge. Finally, flag anything in the document that neither side can currently sign. I'd rather that be explicit on the page than have them discover it halfway through the session with the dean watching. No references or bibliography needed, I just want the text both of them could put their names on.
