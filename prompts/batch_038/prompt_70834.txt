Six months ago I left a lectureship in England for a research post at a German university, and the move has made the financing of higher education something I can no longer treat as a policy abstraction. Three systems now bear on my life at once, and I find that I cannot get them to sit consistently together in my head. I should like a philosophical reconstruction of what each one presupposes, rather than a comparison of their fiscal merits.

First, my own. I still carry an English undergraduate loan from the post-2012 system. It is collected through the payroll by the tax authority as a fixed percentage of earnings above a threshold; there was never any credit assessment, there is no meaningful sense in which I can default, and the outstanding balance will simply be extinguished after a fixed number of years — a number that has been extended for the cohorts behind mine. British mortgage lenders do not treat it as a debt at all, but as a standing deduction from my take-home pay. Meanwhile the state's own accounts, as I understand them, charge the portion of each year's lending that is not expected to come back to public spending at the point the loans are made — which reads to me like an official admission that, for most borrowers, this was never a loan.

Second, the system I now work inside. No tuition fees at public universities in almost every Land; a modest per-semester contribution from each student covering student services and a regional transport ticket; admission restricted by numerus clausus in oversubscribed subjects; and, in one Land only, substantial per-semester fees charged to students from outside the European Union.

Third, the arrangements I keep meeting by comparison. A Danish colleague who was paid a monthly state grant to attend university, with an optional supplementary state loan, in a country that recoups its outlay through a steeply progressive income tax. And an American sister-in-law whose federal student loans survive personal bankruptcy, where her credit-card and medical debts would not, unless she can satisfy a hardship test that courts apply very stringently indeed.

What I want is the implicit theory behind each of these designs. Specifically:

1. For each system, reconstruct the view of higher education that the funding design only makes sense under. Is education here an investment in the student's human capital, a private consumption good purchased in a market, a public good whose benefits diffuse beyond the beneficiary, a positional good whose value depends on scarcity, a right, or a gift? And pair that with the view of obligation it assumes: what claim, if any, does the polity have on a person's future earnings, and on what grounds — reciprocity, benefit received, membership, or the fact that the state produced the earning capacity in the first place?

2. Distinguish carefully between what each practice strictly presupposes and what it merely fits comfortably with. I suspect a great deal is underdetermined here: an income-contingent repayment collected through the payroll seems licensable both by a human-capital view and by a redistributive one, and I want to know which features of each design genuinely force a theoretical commitment and which are compatible with several rivals. Where the practice cannot be made to decide between theories, say so plainly.

3. Tell me which rival views each design quietly rules out. What has to be false about education, or about the ownership of one's own talents, for the German arrangement to be coherent rather than merely cheap? What has to be false for the Danish stipend to be anything other than an expensive way of administering a tax rebate in advance?

4. Address the internal tensions I cannot resolve. The English system treats a single instrument simultaneously as a debt of the individual, as a hypothecated tax on earnings, and as a subsidy that the Treasury has already conceded will not be returned: is that incoherence, or a stable hybrid that expresses a genuinely mixed view of who owns the graduate premium? And in Germany, why is education free for citizens and EU students but priced for everyone else — what does that exception presuppose about the reason the domestic system is free? If the justification is recoupment through a lifetime of German taxation, then the fee is a price on expected exit; if the justification is membership or citizenship, then it is not a price at all. Which of these does the practice actually commit the state to, and can it commit to both?

5. Test my tentative reading of the American asymmetry. My conjecture is that non-dischargeability presupposes that human capital cannot be repossessed and has already been delivered, so the only available collateral is the borrower's future earnings in perpetuity; on that reading, the obligation is not really contractual at all, but closer to a status one attaches to by acquiring an education. But this sits oddly beside a system that otherwise presents higher education as a competitive consumer purchase freely made in a market. Is there a theory of responsibility or of benefit under which the asymmetry is defensible, or is it creditor power dressed in the language of obligation? I would like the strongest defensible version and then an honest verdict.

6. Evaluate the framing I have been reaching for, and tell me where it is a rationalisation. I keep thinking that each system is answering a prior question — who owns the graduate premium? On my reading: the English design assumes the state substantially created it and may therefore recapture part of it from the individual who happens to hold it; the German design assumes it is not separable from the social product generally, so that earmarked recapture is conceptually confused and general taxation is the only honest instrument; the Danish design assumes study is itself socially productive activity, so the student is paid as a contributor rather than charged as a consumer; and the American design assumes the borrower bought an asset in a market and simply owes the price. Where does this hold up, where does it not, and is there a feature of one of these systems that the schema cannot capture?

7. Take up the signalling objection directly. If a large part of the graduate premium is positional — a screening effect that leaves aggregate productivity unchanged and merely redistributes access to good jobs — does the fairness-to-non-graduates argument that is customarily offered for graduate contributions still hold, or does it depend on a productivity story the design cannot support? Under that alternative assumption, what would each of the three designs have to become?

8. For each system, describe what would actually have to change institutionally if we replaced the presupposed view with its most serious rival: repayments in England acknowledged openly as an earmarked tax on graduates with all that implies for who bears the cost; German higher education treated as a club good priced at the margin and rationed by price rather than by numerus clausus; American student debt made freely dischargeable and the shortfall funded from general taxation. I want to see which of these is a cosmetic relabelling and which would alter who pays, and why.

9. Finally, be candid about where there is no theory at all — where a feature of these systems exists only because of fiscal path dependence, administrative convenience, or the terms on which a statute was passed, and where any reconstruction I am offered would be an imposition rather than a discovery. I would much rather be told that a practice is philosophically idle than be handed a tidy account it does not require.

Please write in British English, in a structured form with headings, of a length proportionate to the number of questions — I would expect something substantial rather than a summary. I am not after a bibliography, a reading list or citations of any kind, and please do not supply them; argue from the design features I have described and from first principles. If any of my characterisations of these systems is inaccurate in a way that matters to the analysis, say so at the outset and tell me how the analysis would change under the correct description.
