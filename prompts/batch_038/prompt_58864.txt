So I'm helping put together a half-day workshop on nationalism and heritage for a mixed crowd of historians, planners and a few people from the branding industry, and I want a piece of satire to open the day with instead of a lecture. Here's the conceit:

Write me a proposal from a boutique consultancy (name them something appropriately bland, your call) pitching "identity and heritage infrastructure" to the operating company of a special economic zone. The zone is 22 years old, leased from a host state, about 180,000 residents of whom maybe a third are second-generation, and the developer has just won the right to convert it into a sovereign city-state, with handover in 18 months. The pitch has to solve the problem nobody in the room will say out loud: they need a nation by the handover date, and nations are supposed to be ancient.

Format it like a real consulting deck rendered in prose — situation analysis, a tiered service menu with lead times and what's explicitly out of scope, a methodology page, a risk register, one testimonial from a previous client (a micronation, a post-Soviet republic's tourism board, a rebranded golf community, whatever you like), and a final page on the one thing the fee can't buy.

What I actually want tested is whether you can get the theory right while writing in management-speak. The methodology page should be Hobsbawm's account of invented tradition wearing a suit: what the manufactured rituals are actually for (cohesion among strangers, legitimation of the new authority, inculcating the values the operator wants), the difference between a practice that merely starts and one that claims to be old, and the paradox that the zones branding has to insist it is the opposite of novel and the opposite of constructed. And the deliverables should quietly track Anderson's machinery — a vernacular-language policy, a newspaper and a national calender so that strangers can feel they're doing the same thing at the same moment, the standard state kit of census, map and museum, a founding epic, a martyr who has to be sourced and vetted, an anthem learnable in one sitting. Target is the heritage/nation-branding industry and the clients who believe it, not any real country and not any named scholar. Punch up at the consultants.

Aim for 750-900 words. Deadpan all the way through — no winking, no stage directions about how absurd it is, and don't let anyone say "of course all traditions are invented" as a punchline; the joke should come from the invoices, the timelines and the KPIs. Please don't put anything in quotation marks that would be a real quote from Hobsbawm, Anderson or anyone else, and don't name books or articles — I want the ideas laundered into deliverables and jargon, not cited. (I'm not asking for sources anywhere in this, so don't hedge with a bibliography.)

Then afterwards, come out of character for about 150 words and tell me straight: which parts are faithful to what historians and social scientists in this area actually argue, and where you stretched, flattened or simplified for the bit. Be explicit about separating what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who know the literature — for instance whether the modernist line that nations and their traditions are recent constructions is anything like a consensus, what the premodern/medievalist counterarguments actually claim and how much bite they have, whether "invention" is even the right frame as opposed to reworking or repurposing of older material, and whether the print-and-media story is doing real causal work or is just a vivid illustration. Don't let the satire smuggle a contested position in as if everyone signed up to it, and flag anything you'd expect a specialist in the room to wince at.
