I teach a joint graduate seminar on the philosophy of mind of sleep, and for the opening session I want a decision tree that sorts participants into named positions on the question of whether dreams are experiences — the dispute that runs from Malcolm's denial, through Dennett's defence of that denial, to the present-day disagreement over immersive dream experience and over the existence of dreamless sleep. The cohort is a mix of philosophers who have read neither Malcolm nor Dennett and sleep researchers who have read neither party's philosophy, so the instrument has to be answerable with no prior exposure to the debate: every node must be a question about a concrete case or a bare claim that someone could simply react to, and no node may contain a philosopher's name, a theory label, or the word scepticism.

Please lay out the logical space before drawing the tree. I want the axes on which positions can differ listed first, and I want you to tell me if any of them collapse into one another. At minimum, cover these: whether a dream requires that something was going on during sleep, or only that something happens at waking; what makes a dream report true; whether first-person evidence generated from inside sleep can be trusted as evidence, or is always a waking reconstruction; whether the question of what happened during sleep is a substantive empirical question or one dissolved by the way the concept of dreaming works; whether dream content is perception-like or thought-like, and whether that distinction can really be kept neutral with respect to the first question; and whether a subject can be mistaken in taking themselves to have dreamt.

Then the tree: eight to twelve nodes, each with two or three answer options and an explicit pointer to the next node or leaf. Build the diagnostic questions out of the following cases, adding at most one case of your own, and it must be one a person could react to without knowing anything about sleep research. The cases are: the lucid dreamer who signals in real time with eye movements and whose estimate of elapsed dream time matches the clock; the old anecdote of the long trial-and-execution narrative whose closing image, the falling blade, coincides exactly with the instant the sleeper is jolted awake; the insistent non-dreamer who nonetheless produces a report after every single awakening; the report that has grown new episodes when the subject is questioned again an hour later; the false awakening in which getting up and making coffee is itself later reported as dreamt; and the fluent, confident explanation a patient offers for an action whose real cause they cannot know.

Leaves should carry the name of the position where the literature has one and be marked unnamed where it does not. Malcolm's position and Dennett's must not be merged: if they differ, show the node that separates them; if they do not differ, say so and locate the real fault line instead. I also want a leaf for a combination I suspect several of my students in fact hold and have never seen defended in print — the person who concedes that there are genuine experiences during sleep but denies that ordinary dream reports are made true by them. Give that position a name of your own coinage and tell me whether it is stable or collapses into one of its neighbours under pressure. Finally, flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate, and identify at least two answer-combinations that no position occupies, each with a one-line diagnosis: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

No bibliography, no citations, no page references. I want the classification instrument, not a survey of the debate.
