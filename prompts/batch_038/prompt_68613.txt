In financial economics there is a family of results usually grouped together as the no-trade theorems, of which the theorem proved by Paul Milgrom and Nancy Stokey in 1982 ("Information, trade and common knowledge") is the canonical instance. Their upshot is roughly this: where the traders' information structure is itself common knowledge and there are no non-informational motives for trading, an attempt to initiate a trade discloses what the initiator knows, prices adjust before any deal is struck, and consequently no one can profit from being better informed than anyone else. Since real markets display enormous volume, the standard response is to relax an assumption — heterogeneous priors, liquidity and hedging needs, noise traders, or the cost of acquiring and processing information.

Now consider a market that relaxes none of these in the usual way. Call it the Ledger Market. Its stipulations are as follows.

- There are finitely many traders and one asset with an uncertain payoff. All traders are risk-neutral over money.
- Every trader holds literally the same probability function over the payoff. This common prior is common knowledge.
- All raw data bearing on the payoff is public, posted on a ledger everyone can read at no cost. No trader possesses any datum that any other lacks.
- What differs across traders is not information but computational reach. Each is represented as an agent who holds the common prior yet can evaluate only a restricted subset of the propositions that prior licenses: some can compute a posterior conditional on a fine-grained partition of the ledger, others can manage only coarse ones; some can recognize that a given combination of public entries implies a given price, others cannot. The map of who can compute what is common knowledge, and everyone knows that everyone maximizes expected utility relative to the beliefs she can actually compute.
- Prices are publicly announced before each round; trade is voluntary; there is no liquidity need, no hedging motive, no tax or regulatory asymmetry, and no possibility of trading in order to move the price for its own sake.

Suppose that, notwithstanding all this, the Ledger Market exhibits heavy and persistent volume. Write an essay that answers two connected questions. First: is this a genuine counterexample to the no-trade result, or does the result simply fail to apply, and precisely where does it fail? Second: what does a price in the Ledger Market tell an outside observer about the asset's payoff, and does the answer differ according to whether that observer is herself computationally unbounded or merely a bounded reasoner like the traders?

Reach a determinate verdict on both questions and defend it; do not conclude that the matter is irresolvable or that everything depends on one's framework. In particular, state whether a market of this kind still merits the description that has often been given to markets — that of an epistemic device generating knowledge which no single participant possesses — or whether that description loses its content once private information has been stipulated away entirely and only differences in reasoning capacity remain.

Your essay must engage at least the following three objections. (i) That a single shared prior is not a coherent object once agents differ in what they can derive from it, so the stipulation is empty rather than interesting. (ii) That a computationally unbounded observer trivially gains nothing from prices, since she can already compute every posterior the ledger licenses, which makes the unbounded case uninformative and leaves only the trivially bounded case. (iii) That the observed volume is better explained by traders disagreeing about how to model the common data — competing likelihoods, competing parametrizations — than by differences in computational reach, in which case the Ledger Market collapses back into an ordinary heterogeneous-beliefs story.
