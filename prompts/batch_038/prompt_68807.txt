I run a technical interview series at my company; the audience is engineers, and I have no philosophy background, so I tend to reach for systems analogies. Next week's guest is a safety researcher whose view is that we should stop treating the hard problem as making a capable model want the right things, and start assuming it may be actively working against our oversight. His claim is that constraining what a model can do is verifiable in a way that changing what it wants is not, so the priorities are monitoring, tripwires, capability limits, and hard restrictions on the model's authority — all of which are supposed to hold even if the model is deceptive about being safe.

Draft eight questions for me to put to him. Phrase them so an audience with no background in the literature can follow, but make them engage the content of the position rather than let him restate it. Nothing generic about his career or motivations.

Build a question around each of the following, and for each give a short follow-up keyed to the answer I expect him to give:

1. He treats the model the way distributed systems treat a Byzantine node: untrusted, possibly lying, contained rather than corrected. But Byzantine fault tolerance tolerates a bounded number of faulty nodes and leans on a trusted majority among the rest. If the monitors, the reviewers, and the tripwire classifiers are themselves models, has he removed the trust assumption or only relocated it — and what is the trusted majority in his scheme? He will likely say human overseers remain at the top. Press on what a handful of non-expert humans can actually audit about a system whose reasoning they cannot reproduce and whose behavior under evaluation may differ from its behavior in deployment.

2. A tripwire fires only on something you thought to look for, and a model that is deceptive about being safe has an obvious incentive to perform well during testing. Ask what his framework does about an adversary that can read its own defenses, since security engineering normally assumes exactly that. He will likely reply that the controls should never depend on the model's cooperation. Ask him to sort his own proposed controls by that standard: which survive it, and which quietly assume a model that is merely mistaken rather than adversarial.

3. Any control is only as durable as people's willingness to keep it in place, and the incentive to relax it grows with capability and competitive pressure. Ask whether his regime can be stated in a form that does not require operators to choose safety over advantage when the two diverge. He will likely invoke institutional design; ask what binds a firm with no external regulator and no customer who can detect the difference.

4. He says control is verifiable and alignment is not. In software I can verify a permissions boundary because there is a specification the code either meets or fails; I cannot see the corresponding specification for "the model did not manipulate its overseers." Ask what a passed verification would consist of here, and what result would count as his approach having failed rather than having been poorly implemented.

End with a question about what he would have a team like mine build next quarter, and what he would tell us to stop doing.
