ok so slightly weird request. I'm an undergrad in Professor Adaeze Vance's last-ever run of her seminar on how groups make decisions — I'm the one who still technically owes her a paper, due the week after her retirement dinner. The department is throwing that dinner on the 14th, and becuase she flatly refused to let any faculty member speak, they asked me to give the toast. Five minutes, spoken out loud, about 750 words. I want you to actually write the speech, not an outline of it.

Who's in the room: ninety-ish people. Maybe a third are philosophers and economists who took her class or co-wrote something with her, and they know her work cold — she spent forty years on the problem where a panel votes on the reasons behind a decision and gets one answer, votes on the decision itself and gets another, and both votes were perfectly reasonable. The other two thirds are her husband, her two grown kids (the older one does not think the profession treated her well and isn't going to pretend otherwise at dinner), neighbors from her town, a table of former students from a community college she taught at for eleven years before anyone knew her name, and our dean, who startles easily. Also invited, at his own request: a guy she failed in 1994 who is now a fairly successful lawyer.

Here's the structure I want and it's the whole point, so please don't soften it. The toast runs three live show-of-hands votes on her, phrased so that anybody can answer them instantly with zero context — the sort of thing where I say something like "hands up if you ever got a draft back from her with one word written on the whole thing." The third vote is the one where the room goes the other way, so that every person in that room who knows what she works on can see, in real time and about her, that the room has just done the precise thing she built her career arguing you cannot do coherently. The people who don't know the work should hear only a compliment, and laugh. The people who do should get it before the laugh finishes. And the two earlier votes need to be carried by *different halves of the room* — seminar people yes to one, community-college table yes to the other, nobody yes to both. That's what makes it bite and it's the part I most want you to get right.

Then the speech has to say what I'm going to do about it. Not "and that's why we love her," I'd rather not give a toast at all. Her position is that you vote on the reasons and let the conclusion fall where it falls, and that doing anything else isn't taking the group seriously as a group. I want the toast to tell her, to her face, that in her case I'm not running any procedure at all — that she'll say that isn't a position, it's a failure to have one, and that I've thought about it and I'm fine with that. It should be obvious I know I'm being inconsistent and that it's deliberate.

Hard rules. No jargon whatsoever: none of these words appear anywhere in the speech — aggregation, discursive dilemma, doctrinal paradox, collective rationality, impossibility, Arrow, social choice. I can say "her work" or "the thing she does." No superlatives about her: she has a standing joke-rule that she genuinely means, that nobody is allowed to say anything about her beginning with "no one has ever," and the toast should visibly obey that rule, ideally at some cost to itself. It has to get through the 1994 thing without a joke at his expense and without acting like it didn't happen. Two real laughs minimum, and the last sixty seconds should be the part her husband likes best.

Also she cries at nothing, so don't try to make her cry, that's cheap. Just make it true.
