I was present at a discussion in my department's common room last week and would value an impartial adjudication of it, since I was unable at the time — and remain unable now — to work out which of the two participants had the better of the argument.

The occasion was an incident described by one of them, Miriam. Her nephew, aged fifteen, had spent three years building up an estate in a persistent online world: a house, its furnishings, a stable of mounts, and a collection of items assembled through trading. Roughly £1,400 of his own money had gone into purchases along the way. Another player then used a trading mechanic that the game's terms of service expressly permit to acquire the entire estate for a nominal sum, and dismantled it within the hour. The boy was distraught for several days and said he had been robbed. Miriam's colleague Daniel said he had not been robbed of anything, on the grounds that there was nothing there to steal.

Their subsequent exchange ran as follows.

(1) Daniel: the estate was never a house and the items were never possessions. They are database rows and rendered pixels standing in for a house. What the boy actually lost was anticipated future use and standing among his peers — both genuine, both located in him rather than in any virtual thing. The harm is real; the objects are not; and running the two together is precisely what makes the story sound like theft.

(2) Miriam: those database rows are ordinary physical structures doing ordinary causal work. They persist unobserved, they constrain what any player can do next, they are traded for sums convertible into sterling, and they sit on servers in a building somewhere. Anything with a location, a history, a price, and the power to make a difference to what happens next is real, and the demand for some further ingredient before we say so looks like stipulation dressed as argument.

(3) Daniel: that proves far too much. The same description fits the tokens in a game of Monopoly and the ink in the first printing of Anna Karenina, and he was not prepared to grant that Anna Karenina is a real person located in a building in Moscow. The difference between a real house and a Monopoly token is not the presence or absence of causal powers but whether a thing's properties are settled by the world or by conventions that could trivially have been otherwise. Virtual objects are convention-determined through and through, and that is what makes them fictional.

(4) Miriam: a balance in a current account is convention-determined through and through — its properties depend entirely on ledger entries and legal rules that could easily have been different — and yet nobody supposes a bank balance to be fictional, or that a fraudulent transfer out of it is not a real loss. If convention-dependence entailed unreality, most of what people in this country own would be unreal, which she took it that Daniel did not believe.

(5) Daniel: then consider what Miriam's view commits her to. The same game engine generates several thousand simulated deer a day and deletes them as the player's region loads and unloads. If virtual things are real, the game is bringing thousands of real deer into and out of existence daily, and her nephew's three years of play have been a scene of continuous mass extinction. He was willing to bite some bullets, he said, but not that one — and the alternative is to concede that the estate was no more real than the deer.

(6) Miriam: this conflates two questions she had kept apart, namely whether something is real and whether something matters. She allowed that the deer raise a further issue — were any of them conscious, the deletions would be a moral catastrophe, and she would then want to revise her view of these games entirely — but the estate case never turned on consciousness. It turned on a person's having made, owned, and lost something. Daniel's deer, she said, were a thought experiment about sentience smuggled into an argument about objects.

(7) Daniel, finally: if 'real' is compatible with 'conventional', 'fictional', 'deletable at the whim of a server administrator', and 'worthless except insofar as someone happens to care about it', then Miriam has not established the reality of virtual objects but simply reassigned the word, and the question worth asking — whether the boy was robbed — has not been engaged with at all.

They circled this loop three times without moving. I did not intervene, and I have no position of my own to argue for here, so I should like yours.

Please work through the seven moves in order. For each, say which side has the better of that particular exchange, or whether it is a draw, and justify the verdict briefly rather than merely announcing it. Where a move is right about one thing and wrong about another within the same breath, please separate the two. Then address three further questions: whether either participant is at any point answering a question the other did not ask; whether move (4) and move (5) are genuinely inconsistent with one another or merely in tension; and what, if anything, important is overlooked by both of them — including whether the incident that started the whole thing is properly described as theft. I am after an assessment of these arguments as actually made, not a general essay on the ontology of virtual worlds.
