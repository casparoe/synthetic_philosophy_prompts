so I keep hitting the same wall whenever I try to test lottocratic theory against actual history, and Venice is the case that should settle it. Here's the problem. Basically every modern defense of sortition wants the draw to do two seperate jobs at once: (a) make the ruling body a microcosm of the people — descriptive representation, cognitive diversity, the epistemic upside Landemore gets out of the diversity-trumps-ability result — and (b) defuse faction, campaigning and capture, which is Guerrero's lottocracy case and Dowlen's point that the lottery's political value lies precisely in its being blind to reasons and merit. Manin's older observation cuts across both: the tradition used to treat lot as the democratic device and election as the aristocratic one.

Venice looks like it bought job (b) and skipped job (a) for about five hundred years. Facts, since you can't look any of this up: after the Serrata at the end of the thirteenth century the Great Council was closed to a hereditary set of families, so eligibility was a birthright entered in a register rather than anything resembling citizenship. The doge was chosen by a long alternating sequence of draws and ballots — I think it ran something like: the Great Council draws 30 by lot, those 30 reduce to 9 by lot, the 9 elect 40, who reduce to 12 by lot, elect 25, reduce to 9 by lot, elect 45, reduce to 11 by lot, and a final committee of roughly forty elects the doge. Treat those numbers as my hazy recollection and don't hang anything on them; the only load-bearing fact is that lot and election alternate many times and the last step is a vote. Each incoming doge swore an oath the electors kept lengthening and tightening until the office was close to ceremonial. The republic outlived nearly every other Italian state and ended in 1797 under Napoleon rather than by coup.

What I want:

- Walk the claims above through the Venetian machinery one at a time — which does the case actually support, which does it strain, and which can it not speak to at all?
- The crux: if the anti-faction payoff arrived with essentially zero descriptive representation and zero diversity (a few hundred intermarried nobles who all knew each other and shared one commercial interest), are the two benefits separable in principle? If they are, which one is doing the real work in the contemporary arguments — is Guerrero/Dowlen basically a thesis about the selection mechanism that's indifferent to the pool, with the epistemic stuff bolted on afterward?
- The pool problem. Venice suggests who is eligible matters far more than how you choose from among them. Is that a friendly amendment (fix the pool, keep the draw) or does it collapse lottocracy into an argument about the franchise wearing a different hat?
- Where the theory is silent: real power. The doge was a figurehead; the Senate and the Council of Ten ran things. Does sortition into a powerless office even count as a case of lottocracy, or is Venice better described as election-and-cooptation with a randomizer bolted on top purely as an anti-bribery device?
- And the uncomfortable flip side: does the sheer durability of that closed oligarchy make sortition look like a conservative technology — a way for a narrow elite to freeze itself in place and stop its own members from defecting into factions? What would that mean for people proposing to replace legislatures with citizen assemblies now?
