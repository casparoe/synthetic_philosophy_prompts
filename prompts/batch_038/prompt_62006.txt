I run a small volunteer group that reviews municipal street and transit projects on behalf of the people those projects tend to leave out — wheelchair users, people who don't drive, night-shift workers whose last bus leaves before midnight. Next month I have fifteen minutes plus questions in front of our state's design standards committee: mostly district engineers and public-works managers, one procurement officer, and two accessibility consultants who bill to the department. This committee adopts and revises the manuals that every project in the state gets drawn against.

I am not going to argue that any given artifact has politics. The familiar low-overpass story is disputed, and I don't want my case resting on it. My claim is narrower and, I think, harder to dodge: the politics sits in the standard, not the project. A minimum clearance, a signal-timing assumption, a default walking speed for a "typical" pedestrian, a lot-width requirement — each one decides who the system is built for, and each is invisible at the project level, because the engineer drawing the plan can truthfully say they were only following the manual. From that I argue that any standard the state adopts should carry a short written statement of whom it fails to accommodate and why that failure was judged acceptable, and that the statement should be open to public revision rather than controlled by the committee alone.

Give me the question period. I want the seven or eight objections this room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each does to my case, phrased in their vocabulary — level of service, liability, ADA compliance, value engineering, design exceptions. For each, a reply I can deliver in under a minute and an honest note on where that reply is thin. Tell me which objections I should partly concede before they are made, which rest on a confusion I can dissolve in one sentence, which of my claims they will hear as an accusation against them personally, and the single question I am hoping nobody asks. Assume the committee is competent and well-intentioned; the objection that will hurt me most is the fair one.
