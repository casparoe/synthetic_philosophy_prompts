Act as a senior epistemologist who has set and marked a lot of university exams and is allergic to questions that reward regurgitation. Think carefully, and don't rush to the deliverable.

I teach an upper-level (4th year/MA) epistemology seminar and I'm writing the final. We've spent two weeks on Moore's proof of an external world — the holding-up-the-hands gesture, his conditions on what counts as a rigorous proof, and the usual trio of complaints (it begs the question, it doesn't engage the sceptic, it's dialectically useless). They've also had a bit of Wittgensteinian hinge material and some Crispin Wright, but I do *not* want the exam to be a memory test about whose name attaches to which label. So: no citations, no reading list, and the marking scheme should not hand out marks for name-dropping. Students get 45 minutes, no books.

What I want is ONE essay question, self-contained enough to paste straight onto the paper (call it 250 words of question text), plus a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme totalling 25.

The device I want to build it on: two short responses to Moore's proof by two philosophers I've invented, and the student has to adjudicate. Roughly —

(A) "Okonjo": the proof is perfectly good as an argument; what's broken is our standard for what would count as answering a sceptic. Demanding that Moore establish his premises without relying on anything that already presupposes the framework under dispute is a demand nobody meets anywhere in inquiry, so the failure sits in the job description of "answering scepticism", not in the argument.

(B) "Halvorsen": the proof is valid and its premises really are known, but it cannot extend knowledge, because the warrant for the perceptual premise is only available if you're already entitled to something of the kind the conclusion states. Non-transmissive, not viciously circular — the defect is in the structure of warrant, not in conversational norms.

The crux I want the question to turn on is whether (B)'s concession that the premises are known is actually compatible with (A)'s claim that the proof answers nobody — i.e. are these two descriptions of one phenomenon, or rival claims about where knowledge stops? — and then a concrete case to force the issue: a *local* sceptic about this particular hand (hologram, cleverly disguised prosthetic, whatever you think works) where the warrant-structure story and the conversational-norms story look like they give different verdicts on whether Moore could come to know something by arguing for it. Students should have to say what would settle the dispute, not just describe it.

Deliver, in this order:
1. First brainstorm out loud three or four candidate cruxes this question could hang on, say which one you're taking and why the others are weaker. Don't skip this, it's how I'll know you actually thought about it.
2. The exam question exactly as it would appear on the paper, including the two responses in the students' own words (short, quotable-sounding but invented).
3. The model answer, ~700 words, written in the voice of a very good candidate rather than a professor — it should argue through to a verdict, not survey the terrain.
4. A marking scheme: five must-hit points with marks adding up to 25, and for each point one line on the specific way candidates typically botch it.
5. A short paragraph on what a genuine A answer does that a competent B doesn't.

Two extra things. Keep the philosophers invented — if your model answer starts gesturing at real people's views as though the student had read them, you've missed the point of the exercise. And be honest about the local-sceptic case: it's the bit I'm least sure holds together. If you think the two responses actually converge there, say so and give me a better case that separates them instead of papering over the problem.
