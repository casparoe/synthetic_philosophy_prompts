For thirty-five years I worked as a wildlife disease epidemiologist, most of it modelling how to place a limited vaccine supply: how many treated baits per square kilometre, which corridor first, which population to abandon. Before that I trained as a zoologist, and the instrument I trust most from those years is the dichotomous key — a sequence of forced choices that carries an unlabelled specimen to a name without ever asking the user what he thinks it is called. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy, partly to keep the head in order, and I have recently been reading the argument associated with Taurek that the numbers need not count: that faced with five who will otherwise die and one who will otherwise die, a person choosing may be permitted to save either, and that giving everyone an equal chance is at least as defensible as saving the greater number. My entire working life assumed the opposite so thoroughly that I never noticed it was a claim at all.

I now sit on two bodies where this matters practically: a regional health authority's panel for scarce-resource decisions, and the funding committee of a conservation trust. On both, sensible people diverge in ways I cannot yet map. I would like you to build me a key that sorts them.

Requirements. At most eight branching questions and at most twelve leaves. Every node must be a concrete case posed to the reader with two or three answer options, never a question about which view he finds appealing, and never a question whose wording betrays where the branch leads. No question may contain the name of a philosopher, a school, or a term of art: the vocabulary of the literature belongs only at the leaves, in the key itself. The readers are twelve laypeople — clinicians, a wildlife manager, two retired engineers like me — with no philosophical training, and the key must be readable aloud in ninety minutes.

Fix the background conditions at the head of the key so that they never vary downstream: the people or animals affected are strangers to the chooser, nobody is at fault, need is otherwise equal, the chooser can save only some, and there are no ecological, reputational, or future-supply consequences of either option. Anything that turns on such consequences is outside my question, and I would rather the key exclude it than quietly absorb it.

Here are five cases of my own construction. Use whichever genuinely discriminate; tell me plainly which are redundant, and which are malformed in the sense that they seem to split positions but actually collapse onto an earlier question.

A. A rescue craft can reach one of two groups: five on one vessel, one on the other.

B. One course of an antimicrobial will cure a single patient of a fatal infection; the same course divided five ways will arrest the infection in five patients who would otherwise survive with a permanent and painful disability.

C. Ten thousand people, each spared a week of nausea and lost work, against one person spared permanent blindness.

D. Having settled on the five in case A, one of the five tells the chooser that she does not think her being one of five strengthens her claim at all, and would rather he randomised between the two groups. Does her saying so alter what he ought to do?

E. A fixed stockpile of oral vaccine. Deployed one way it protects five hundred thousand animals of a common species; deployed the other way it protects three hundred animals of a rare species. The disease, its course and its case fatality are identical in both, and — stipulate this — there is no difference in ecological function, no human interest either way, and no difference in the capacity of the animals to suffer.

Two nodes I expect to need, and one I suspect. The key must somewhere separate the view that a rescuer owes nothing to the numbers from the view that the numbers genuinely matter but only up to a point beyond which a further mild benefit to many cannot outweigh a grave harm to one. It must also separate those who think the reason to save the many comes from what is owed to each of them individually from those who think it comes from the total amount of good produced. And I suspect — tell me whether I am right or confused — that the answer may differ depending on whether the choice belongs to an impartial allocator or to one of the people at risk; if that is a real joint in the argument, give it a node, and if it is a confusion, say so and explain where I have gone wrong.

At each leaf, give the position a plain-English name and, if it has one, the name it goes by in the literature; then state in one sentence the verdict that position is committed to on the case which generated the leaf. Where two leaves cannot be separated by any case I could construct, mark them as inseparable and say what would be needed to tell them apart; do not invent a distinction to keep the tree tidy.

After the key, list at least three combinations of answers that are logically available but that no position occupies — for instance, whatever it would be to hold that welfare aggregates freely among members of one species but not across species, if anything holds that. For each empty cell, state whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has yet bothered to occupy it.

I do not want a reading list, footnotes, or a history of the debate; the instrument is the deliverable, and I can look up names myself once I have them.

Finally: tell me how confident you are that this key is cut along the real joints rather than along the grain of my own framing, since I arrived at the question with a strong prior that has probably shaped the cases I chose. Single out the one node, or the one assignment of a position to a leaf, that you would trust least, and say what evidence or what further case would settle it.
