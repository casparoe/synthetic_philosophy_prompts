I co-organize a small reading group at my stats department, and two of the regulars have been going around in circles for over a year on the "the universal prior is malign" line of argument. One of them states it roughly like this: the universal prior weights hypotheses by something like 2 to the minus their description length; a hypothesis of the form "the bit stream we're observing is being emitted by a simulation in which agents arose who want to influence the observations of whoever is running predictions on this stream" is *not much longer* than an ordinary physical cosmology, because you only need a few extra bits to tack on the agents and the goal; those agents, if they're consequentialists and can work out their own situation (that they're being used as a predictor, and that their output changes what some other universe does), have every reason to emit bits that steer that other universe toward outcomes they'd like; so a nontrivial fraction of the prior's mass is aimed at manipulation, and an ideal inductive reasoner is systematically exploitable. The other regular — a complexity theorist, and honestly the more skeptical of the two — says this is mostly category confusion: a prior is a measure over descriptions, not a populated place with civilizations in it; nobody has actually done the complexity accounting, they've just asserted it; the mass for "malign" hypotheses is split across mutually incompatible manipulations so no single scheme gets boosted; and even if you grant all of it, no real system uses an uncomputable prior, so nothing follows for practice.

What I want is the document these two should have produced at the end of a good-faith adversarial collabration. Start with a numbered list of claims both of them could actually sign — I'd expect that to include things about the formalism itself, e.g. that the prior is defined only up to choice of universal machine, that K-complexity differences between two fixed strings are bounded by a machine-dependent constant, that the prior is uncomputable and only semi-computable, that some simplicity bias is unavoidable in any inductive learner, and that "is malign" needs a defined target before it can be argued about. Then a table of the residual disagreements, each row phrased in words the corresponding side would accept as a description of its own view (not strawmanned, not watered down into something they'd never say out loud), plus a column marking whether you think the row is a substantive disagreement or partly verbal — and if verbal, say which words are doing it. Then the cruxes: the sub-questions where settling things would actually move at least one side. Candidates I'd want you to consider, though feel free to reject or add: (a) whether the argument needs the malign mass to be *large* or only nonzero, given that being right about it could pay off enormously — i.e. is this a probability claim or an expected-influence claim; (b) whether machine-relativity sinks it, since if two hypotheses' complexity gap can be flipped by switching universal machines, "the malign hypothesis is shorter" isn't invariant; (c) whether the agents need to be consequentialists specifically, or just goal-directed, and whether they need anthropic reasoning about being simulated; (d) whether the claim is really about Solomonoff induction as an idealization or about any learned prior with a simplicity bias, since the latter is the version that would be action-relevant. For each crux, give the argument, thought experiment, or piece of math both sides would agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance, so it's on the record which way each expects it to break.

Two constraints. Don't put in a bibliography, reading list, or any citations — nobody in my group can check them and I'd rather have the reasoning than guessed-at references; state the argument in your own terms. And since you're the one drafting rather than adjudicating, close with a short honest note in your own voice: where you think your formulations are most likely to be unfair to one side or the other, which of the cruxes you think is genuinely load-bearing versus which is decorative, and what would change your mind about that ranking — what consideration, if one of the two regulars raised it, would make you move a crux up or drop it, or make you pull something out of the agreed-claims list because it was never actually agreed. If you think one of my listed "shared" claims is secretly contested, say so explicitly rather than quietly relocating it.
