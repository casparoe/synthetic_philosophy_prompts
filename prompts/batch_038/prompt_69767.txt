I am a fourth-year graduate student in history and philosophy of science, and in three weeks I give a forty-five-minute paper in our philosophy of physics colloquium, which this term is co-listed with the general relativity group's seminar. I would like a preparation sheet for the question period.

The paper defends a deflationary reconstruction of Gödel's rotating-universe argument. The received reading is that Gödel refuted the reality of time by exhibiting an exact solution of Einstein's field equations containing closed timelike curves through every point, so that an inhabitant of such a universe could return into his own past; the idealist conclusion is then drawn that objective becoming — the lapse of time — is not a feature of the world at all. My claim is that this gets the argument's structure backwards. On my reading the load-bearing premise is not the physical possibility of such voyages but a structural point about the theory: the field equations, taken as a complete physical description of a world, are compatible with models in which no global temporal ordering is definable, and so cannot underwrite the claim that time objectively lapses. The closed curves dramatize that compatibility; they are not themselves a premise. It follows, I argue, that the familiar objections miss their target — that our universe is expanding rather than stationary, that the solution requires pressureless dust at rest and a negative cosmological constant, that chronology protection or semiclassical instability rules such models out, that the voyages would be ruinously expensive in accumulated acceleration. None of these bears on a modal premise about what a theory permits. But the reading costs Gödel his idealism: what it yields is a four-dimensional world with no privileged foliation and no objective becoming, which is very nearly the B-theoretic picture already on the table, so the argument's interest is historical rather than metaphysical.

The argument I will present runs in three steps. First, an interpretive step: the operative premise in Gödel's 1949 material concerns what the space of solutions licenses, not what a traveller could do. Second, a reconstructive step: from the compatibility of the theory with models lacking any objective temporal order, plus the assumption that the theory is the whole physical story, to the conclusion that the lapse of time is either not part of the world or something physics is in principle silent about — a disjunction Gödel, for independent reasons, resolved in the first direction. Third, a diagnostic step: the usual objections all attack the dramatization rather than the premise, which is why the literature has produced so much heat about time travel and so little about the inference.

The room will contain two working relativists, one on causal structure and singularity theorems and one on numerical cosmology, who know the Gödel solution as a textbook curiosity and are impatient with philosophers' use of exact solutions; four metaphysicians of time, all B-theorists or eternalists of some stripe, two of whom think talk of objective lapse was never a coherent posit and will suspect me of refuting a view they never held; a senior historian of twentieth-century logic who knows the Princeton material and the Einstein–Gödel relationship and regards rational reconstruction as a species of theft; and two graduate students working on chronology protection. There will be no Kant scholars, no idealists about time, and nobody inclined to defend Gödel's metaphysical conclusion.

Please give me eight to ten objections ordered by likelihood times damage, each attributed to a segment of the room and phrased in that segment's idiom — the relativists will talk about time functions, stable causality, and distinguished congruences, not about the flow of time; the B-theorists will ask which primitive my reconstruction leaves Gödel with; the historian will ask what Gödel wrote and when. For each objection I want a reply I can deliver standing up in about two minutes, a statement of what I should concede before delivering it, and one sentence identifying where the reply is softest. Then tell me which single reply has to land for the paper to survive, which two or three objections I should preempt inside the talk rather than save, and what the question I am hoping nobody asks actually is, together with an honest account of what I would say if it came.

Two further requests. Flag every place where my reconstruction rests on a technical claim about causal or temporal structure that I have stated loosely and where a relativist will insist on precision. In particular, I have a recollection of a result of Malament's to the effect that in Gödel spacetime the causal order alone picks out the family of closed timelike curves of minimal accumulated acceleration, and I cannot tell whether that is fatal to my claim that the solution lacks privileged temporal structure; tell me how much to worry and what to say. Finally, please give me no references, no bibliography, and no suggestions about what to go and read — assume I have forty-eight hours and no library access.
