For fifteen years the Anthropocene Working Group, established in 2009 under the Subcommission on Quaternary Stratigraphy, tried to settle when the Anthropocene began by the same procedure used for every other boundary on the geologic time scale: in 2023 it proposed a golden spike at Crawford Lake, Ontario, with the epoch beginning in 1952 on the strength of plutonium from thermonuclear fallout preserved in the lake's sediment. In March 2024 the subcommission voted the proposal down, 12 to 4, with the youth of the proposed boundary among the stated worries. I want the philosophical commitments of that practice rather than a history of the debate. What must be true for one drill core in one lake to be the authoritative place to look for a planetary, species-level transition — and what does the golden-spike convention presuppose about synchrony, given that the Anthropocene's markers are globally distributed but registered at different times in different places, and that the underlying human transformation of the Earth system may have no first moment at all? What conception of agency does the practice assume in treating our doings as the sort of thing that leaves a boundary the way tectonics or an impact does, and is that boundary understood as discovered or as stipulated? Which rival conceptions does it quietly rule out — the 1610 Orbis proposal, Ruddiman's early-Anthropocene thousands of years, and above all treatments of the Anthropocene as a historical or political concept (a Capitalocene) rather than a stratigraphic unit? Finally, separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely sits comfortably with it: could the same stratigraphers have ratified a 1952 boundary while rejecting nearly all of that baggage, and what would a dating practice built on one of the rival conceptions have to look like instead? Please write in American English.
