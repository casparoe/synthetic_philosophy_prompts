QUESTION 3 (25 points). State the synchronic Dutch book argument for probabilism, and illustrate it with a concrete set of bets. Then state the diachronic version of the argument and identify the norm of belief revision it is intended to support. What idealizing assumptions does each version depend on, and what happens to the conclusion if one of them fails? Finally, say whether you think these arguments show that incoherent credences are irrational or merely that they are imprudent, and justify that judgment.

RESPONSE. The synchronic argument starts from the Dutch book theorem: if an agent's betting quotients for a set of propositions violate the axioms of probability, then there is some set of bets at exactly those quotients that guarantees her a net loss, no matter how the truth values come out. For example, let A and B be mutually exclusive, and suppose the agent has credence 0.6 in A and 0.5 in B. Because A and B cannot both be true, she will have paid $1.10 for a pair of bets that pays out at most $1.00, so she is certain to lose ten cents. A bookie who knows her rates can construct such a package whenever finite additivity fails, and analogous constructions handle violations of non-negativity and of normalization. Probabilism follows: the credences of a rational agent must form a probability function.

The diachronic argument concerns revision rather than a snapshot, and de Finetti introduced it together with the synchronic theorem; its target is conditionalization. If an agent with credence function c learns E with certainty, rationality requires her new credence in H to be c(H|E). An agent who revises by any other rule can be exploited across time: the bookie sells her, now, a bet whose payoff depends jointly on H and on E, and then, once she has learned E, offers her further bets at her revised rates. Because the revised rates do not line up with the conditional rates she already had, the two packages together guarantee a loss regardless of how the world turns out. Conditionalization is therefore the unique rule that is not exploitable over time, which is why Bayesians treat it as a norm of rationality and not as a heuristic.

Both directions of the theorem hold: incoherence implies that a book can be made against you, and coherence implies that none can. Within the betting setting the norm is therefore a complete guarantee — a probabilistically coherent agent cannot lose money no matter which bets she accepts, and an incoherent agent will. Rationality is always rewarded and irrationality always punished, at least where money is concerned. That is what gives the argument its rhetorical force: unlike appeals to accuracy or to abstract consistency, it shows the cost of violating the axioms being paid in cash.

The assumptions do matter, though. The theorem requires that the agent accept each bet at odds she herself regards as fair, that she evaluate the bets one at a time rather than as a package, and that her credences stay fixed while the book is being laid. It also requires the stakes to be small enough that she is approximately risk-neutral with respect to them, since otherwise the fair-odds premise breaks down in the face of diminishing marginal utility for money. And it requires that credences be the kind of thing that shows up in betting dispositions at all, which some philosophers deny. Given all that, I think the argument establishes that incoherent credences are imprudent rather than irrational in any robust sense: what is shown is a form of pragmatic self-defeat, not a logical contradiction, since an incoherent agent's beliefs need not be inconsistent with one another and she may never encounter a bookie. The accuracy-dominance argument is usually offered as a replacement that dispenses with the betting machinery altogether, though it carries its own assumptions about what counts as a good measure of inaccuracy.
