So I'm the token academic on our state's corrections policy panel — seven voting members, I teach part time at the community college, nobody else has done any philosophy and two of them think that's an advantage — and on the 14th we vote on a pilot that has basically split us down the middle. The proposal is to convert one 40-bed housing unit at our only max-security prison into what the warden calls a "normality unit," loosely copying the Norwegian thing. A consultant's report on Halden got passed around at the last meeting and the details that stuck with people are that residents there buy groceries and cook their own meals in a real kitchen with real knives, that there are no cameras in the cells or the common rooms, that staff are unarmed, and that people are still locked in their rooms overnight — something like half the day, so it isn't a freedom program, it's a furniture-and-friction program. Our version would screen residents for it, require that they be inside five years of a release date, keep their current work and program assignments, put staff in street clothes instead of uniforms, and disable the interior locks on the common areas during the day.

The complication is that our enabling statute hands us two standards that, applied literally, point in opposite directions, and the memo has to actually settle the conflict rather than note it and move on. Section 4 says conditions of confinement for people serving sentences may not be "more eligible than the conditions habitually available to the lowest-income law-abiding residents of this state," which is transparently the old poor-law idea that relief has to be worse than the worst job outside. Section 9 says the department shall not impose conditions that "impair an inmate's capacity to function as a free person upon release." Where I live, the people Section 4 tells us to compare against often share apartments with relatives, work schedules that change weekly, and eat worse and less safely than that unit would eat — so a faithful Section 4 comparison might condemn the pilot while blessing the status quo, and I know at least two colleagues are leaning on exactly that without saying so out loud. I want the memo to say what Section 4's comparison class is really doing: is it a floor, a ceiling, a deterrent constraint, or a fairness constraint against giving prisoners things working poor people don't have, and can it be reconciled with Section 9 at all or does one of them have to give way? If the honest answer is that they can't both govern, pick one and defend it, and tell me what that does to the rest of our docket, because we use Section 4 to strike down everything from better mattresses to tablet access and I don't want to discover the implications in March.

Two things I want handled carefully. First, a much smaller version of this ran three years ago at a medium facility and got shut down after a resident was stabbed in a common area during a stretch when the interior locks were off; the warden's staff insist the same assault would have happened in the shower or the yard under the old regime and that the victim had been marked for weeks, so the incident doesn't show the pilot increases harm, only that it relocates it. Take a position on how much weight that gets: what the relevant baseline is, whether harm that is displaced rather than added counts against a program, and what we'd have to measure during the pilot to answer this with numbers instead of shouting. Second, the officers' union filed a statement arguing that the whole design shifts risk onto staff — unarmed, in street clothes, slower to respond — so that people who did nothing wrong pay the cost of a benefit aimed at reducing future crimes by third parties. That is honestly the strongest thing in the file and it can't get one dismissive paragraph; it needs a real answer, including whether the panel is entitled to impose that risk in the first place and what would make it permissible (consent, hazard pay, transfer rights, staffing minimums, something else entirely), and whether an officer who objects should be able to opt out of the unit without being treated as not a team player.

Deliverable is a memo of maybe 1,200 to 1,500 words I can circulate to the panel and the warden's office before the meeting. It needs a bottom line — authorize, authorize with conditions, or reject — and if it's conditions they should be things a warden could implement and a court could check: eligibility, phase-in schedule, the specific events that would automatically suspend the pilot and who is allowed to restart it, staffing minimums, and what gets reported at month six and month eighteen. Don't hedge into "more study is needed," that's how we've killed the last four of these. No citations or footnotes, I'll square references myself, and please don't invent case law or pretend our statute says something it doesn't. Write it like a document that has to survive being read aloud by someone who thinks the whole thing is coddling, seperate typo in my notes aside, because that person will be sitting across from me.
