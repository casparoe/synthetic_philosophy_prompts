I am a senior in high school, and for my capstone seminar each of us has to produce a long argumentative essay and then revise it in response to outside commentary. My topic grew out of a unit on twentieth-century political thought in which we spent a single class on Simone Weil's short wartime essay arguing for the abolition of political parties. I read that essay twice in a paperback anthology from my school library, and I have not been able to let go of a difficulty in it that our class discussion skipped over. I have drafted roughly half of the essay and condensed it below into six numbered sections; because these are summaries of paragraphs rather than finished prose, I would ask that you comment on the argument and the architecture, not on sentence-level style. For what it is worth, the two people who have seen the draft disagree about nearly everything: my history teacher thinks Section 1 is already an uncharitable reading of Weil and that everything after it is built on sand, while my cousin, a second-year political science major, thinks Section 4 is the only part worth keeping and describes Sections 1 through 3 as a book report with a paradox stapled onto it.

1. Weil's argument as I understand it. Three features make a political party what it is on her account: it aims at its own indefinite growth; it exerts collective pressure on the minds of its members; and it demands a loyalty that overrides a member's own judgment about particular questions. Because growth is the operative aim, doctrine becomes instrumental — positions are adopted insofar as they recruit and are dropped when they cost members — so the party is, as she puts the point, totalitarian in relation to its members; I read that structurally, not as a claim about regimes. Underneath the political claim sits an epistemological one: attending to what is just requires a disinterestedness that collective passion fills up in advance, so the partisan does not merely reach different conclusions but has damaged the faculty by which conclusions are reached. Her remedy is an assembly of individuals voting as they judge best, with informal currents of opinion that she distinguishes from organized parties, and with no bodies that recruit, discipline, or campaign.

2. The difficulty I want to press. Suppose the essay succeeds and abolition becomes a cause. Causes get organized. Any movement pushing this one must recruit supporters, must ask them to treat the cause as prior to their private judgments about tactics, and must bring some pressure to bear on members who defect at an inconvenient moment. By Weil's own three criteria, an abolition movement is party-shaped. So either the movement falls under her condemnation, in which case her proposal has no possible agent and is inert; or it does not, in which case her criteria were never criteria of condemnation but a description of electoral parties specifically — and the argument then rests on an undefended premise, namely that the corruption of judgment matters most at the moment a vote is cast.

3. My attempted repair, which I call the tendency escape hatch. A movement could refuse the party form outright: no dues, no roster, no endorsements, no officers, no position on anything except the single institutional change, and a standing commitment to dissolve once the change is enacted. Such a body does not aim at indefinite growth, since it aims at its own obsolescence, and having no members it has nothing to pressure. It therefore fails all three criteria and escapes condemnation. The distinction I want to draw is thus not that Weil contradicts herself. Her proposal is not self-refuting but self-defeating: the only agents realistically able to enact abolition are agents her criteria disqualify, because enacting it requires winning elections and winning elections requires precisely what the escape hatch forbids. The logic survives; the feasibility does not.

4. Why the remedy may matter less than the diagnosis. Weil's prescription is aimed at the vote: unbind the representative and judgment is restored. But the organizations that most shape political thinking among people my age do not work primarily on votes. They work on what counts as a live question at all — which people are treated as candidates, which issues are treated as serious, which facts are merely salient. A recommendation feed is, I want to say, a party without members: no roster and no pledge, yet continuous pressure on minds, plus an explicit optimization for its own growth in which any content whatsoever is instrumental. Her three criteria fit platforms better than they fit parties, since platforms pursue growth openly and by design. Her remedy does not transfer, though. Abolishing parties would not abolish the shaping of the agenda; it would leave a vacuum that whatever controls salience would fill. The diagnosis generalizes; the prescription does not.

5. The hardest objection, and my probably too quick reply. Parties are epistemic shortcuts: no representative can investigate everything, so disciplined blocs let an individual defer to people who specialize, and a bloc's label carries information to voters who cannot check the record. Weil's assembly seems to demand a self-sufficiency of judgment that is impossible at scale. My reply is that the shortcut argument justifies relying on other people's analysis, not on other people's incentives, and that a tendency can supply expertise without supplying sanctions, so the division of cognitive labor survives abolition intact. My teacher's counter, which I have not been able to answer, is that I have only relabeled the problem: expertise is produced by institutions with staffs, budgets, and continuity, which is to say by organizations that recruit and grow.

6. What remains. I plan to add (i) an examination of whether Weil's epistemology — the claim that attention requires a kind of emptiness — can be prised apart from her institutional proposal, so that one could accept the diagnosis and reject the remedy without contradiction; and (ii) a test case asking whether a randomly selected, non-renewable citizen panel with no reelection incentive satisfies her three criteria, which I suspect it does, and which would supply an institutional form for the remedy that my reading of the essay does not find there. Neither part is written yet.
