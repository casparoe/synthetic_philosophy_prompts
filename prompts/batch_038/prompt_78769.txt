My doctoral supervisor convenes a long-running interdisciplinary seminar on animal and human signalling, and she closes every session by ruling, exhibit by exhibit, on whether the trait under discussion counts as an honest costly signal. I have kept a record of this term's rulings, together with the reason she gave in each case:

1. The peacock's train: accepted. Only a male in good condition can grow and carry it, and it invites predators; a weak male cannot afford the same extravagance.
2. Carotenoid-based plumage redness in finches: accepted. The pigments must be obtained from food and are also wanted elsewhere in the body, so a dull male cannot buy his way into the same display.
3. The satin bowerbird's bower: accepted. Weeks of construction, constantly raided and rebuilt, and only a subset of males keep one in condition through the season.
4. Stotting in gazelles: accepted. It spends time and energy at exactly the moment when flight would pay, and an animal in poor condition cannot produce a convincing stot at all.
5. The artificially lengthened tail of a widowbird: rejected, even though the male so treated wins more territories and more matings than his neighbours. Her words were that he paid nothing, and that the female's preference has been exploited rather than informed.
6. A cheap rented suit worn to a job interview: rejected. Any applicant can rent one; the outlay does not vary with the quality being advertised.
7. The alarm call of a ground squirrel: accepted as honest, but not, she insisted, as a costly signal. It costs almost nothing, and its reliability comes from elsewhere — the caller's own interest in the response of the listeners, who are largely relatives, and the fact that anyone present can see for themselves whether a predator is really there.

Next week's session has three exhibits, and I have promised to predict her rulings in advance:

(a) In one species of fiddler crab, a male that loses his single enlarged claw regrows one after the next moult. The replacement is about the same length as the original but has less mass, contains less muscle, is a markedly worse weapon, and is cheaper to wave in the same display. Rivals with original claws do not avoid fighting these males, though they would usually beat them, and females choosing mates show no aversion to them. In some populations close to half the males carry a regenerated claw.

(b) The human blush following a minor transgression — being caught in a small lie, or a social blunder at a dinner table. It is involuntary, essentially impossible to summon at will, and costs the blusher nothing material; observers nonetheless report being readier to forgive and to trust again someone whose face gives them away. There is a further wrinkle: it is not obvious that a blush is a display at all, rather than a physiological leak that onlookers have simply learned to read.

(c) The founder of a company everyone has heard of, who turns up in an unmarked grey t-shirt, wears no watch, drives a fifteen-year-old car, and mentions none of these facts. A newcomer wearing precisely the same clothes would be read as a nobody.

For each exhibit, predict her verdict and give the reasoning she would most likely offer. Then set out the candidate principles her record is consistent with — at least: that honesty requires genuine waste; that it requires the marginal cost to fall harder on low-quality signallers; that it requires faking to be physically impossible; that it can be secured by the consequences of being caught — and say which of these her seven rulings have already ruled out, and which remain live. Identify which of the three new exhibits is the most diagnostic, and say precisely where the surviving principles part company on it. Where the record genuinely underdetermines her verdict, say what she would be committing herself to by ruling each way, rather than pretending to a confidence you lack. Finally, propose one further exhibit that would separate the surviving principles, and state what would have to be true of it for the test to work.
