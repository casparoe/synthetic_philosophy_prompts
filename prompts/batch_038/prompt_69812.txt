I'm on the board of a county historical society and Thursday we vote on an exhibit that's split us straight down the middle. I need something to circulate the night before, because right now I'm just absorbing other people's arguments in the parking lot.

Background on what we actually hold. For roughly 1880 to 1930 our collections are almost all institutional paperwork: poorhouse admission registers, the county jail blotter, a batch of state hospital commitment files that got transferred to us in the seventies, truancy records, charity-relief case notes. We have essentially no diaries or letters from working people in that period. What we have is what clerks wrote about them, in forms the clerks designed.

Our curator has proposed a one-gallery exhibit built around a single woman in the commitment files, admitted in 1901. She picked her because the file is unusually thick: three pages of a statement the woman dictated to a clerk, letters her sister wrote to the superintendent begging to have her released, and the complaint from the farm family she worked for that seems to have started the whole thing. The curator's pitch is that this is our one real chance to let an ordinary person from that era speak in her own words on a public wall, and that if we pass on it the gallery stays about landowners and railroad men forever, because those are the only people who left us papers on purpose. She also says its a weird case rather than a typical one, and she thinks that's an advantage, which I don't fully follow.

Two objections came up and I can't tell how much weight they carry.

First, a retired school principal on the board says the whole thing is a trap. The woman is visible to us only because the state was processing her. Her "own words" were taken down by a clerk working from prompts we can't see. The sister's letters were written to persuade an official who held her sibling's fate. So the exhibit will be the institution's voice with a woman's name attached, and dressing that up as history from below is worse than admitting we don't have her. This trustee would rather we made the records themselves the subject — the poorhouse, the hospital, who got written down and why — and left the individuals mostly anonymous.

Second, a great-granddaughter found the file last spring and objects to her ancestor's name and the diagnosis in it going up where school groups will read them. A different branch of the same family wrote to us supporting the exhibit and saying she'd be proud. Our written policy only says institutional records over seventy-five years old are open to researchers, which was drafted for genealogists and says nothing at all about panel text.

Underneath both, the chair is being pushed by a donor crowd who'd prefer a founding-families gallery, and one trustee keeps insisting this is politics rather than scholarship. What the chair actually wants from Thursday is a decision plus a rule we can apply to the next ten proposals, because a local history group is already pitching us a jail-blotter exhibit and I guarantee this exact fight repeats.

Draft the memo. I want a clear bottom line — do it, don't, or do it with named changes — and the conditions specific enough that the curator could build to them: name or no name on the labels, what we do with the dictated statement and how we'd have to present it, whether the institutional frame gets space in the same gallery or a separate one, what we owe the objecting descendant versus the supportive one, and what the reusable rule should say about cases where the only surviving record of a person is a record made about them by someone with power over them. Then give me the strongest honest version of the principal's objection and say whether you think it's fatal or survivable — I genuinely can't tell yet if she's making a good methodological point or just a gloomy one. And please don't hand me a bibliography or a list of books to read, nobody on this board is going to look anything up and I don't want to be the one assigning homework.
