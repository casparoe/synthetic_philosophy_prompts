You're a moral philosopher who has spent too long inside the aggregation debates — sketch out the terrain out loud for a few lines before you commit to a line of attack.

Here's what I want argued, and argued properly rather than nodded at. Taurek's anti-aggregationism — five people each needing a fifth of the drug vs. David needing all of it, no adding up the five, just give everybody a chance — gets knocked over with one standard objection: if you refuse to aggregate across people you can't do risk policy at all. No speed limits, no bridge inspection schedules, no vaccine allocation. The numbers-sceptic is supposedly stuck.

Argue that this objection misses, and argue the stronger version: the numbers-sceptic can run full-blooded expected-value aggregation in ex ante policy choice while still refusing to aggregate in the ex post rescue case, and its not a fudge or a special plea — it falls out of Taurek's own reasons. My hinge, sharpen it or replace it with something better: in the rescue case there is a determinate someone who dies unless I act, and that determinacy is what generates the non-comparative duty; ex ante there is nobody who is "the one" yet, so the aggregation is across possible futures rather than across actual persons. Two different questions, so no inconsistency to expose.

Then steelman the reply I expect — that a policy is just a bundle of future rescue cases, and if you'd refuse to aggregate inside each one you can't aggregate across them — and either answer it or concede it cleanly. No retreating into "it depends on your metaethics."

Last line, exactly: Verdict: defensible / indefensible — plus one sentence on whether you actually buy the thesis after having made the case for it.
