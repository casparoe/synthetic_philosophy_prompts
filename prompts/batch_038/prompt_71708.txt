I am the teaching assistant for a first-year graduate seminar on inductive inference and formal epistemology, and this week I am grading the midterm. One of the short-essay items was worth 25 points, split 8/8/9 across three parts. Students were permitted a one-page formula sheet and were told in lecture that they may assume Kraft's inequality, the invariance theorem, and the definition of plain and prefix-free Kolmogorov complexity without proof. Here is the question as it appeared on the exam:

> Explain how Solomonoff induction is intended to give a rigorous formulation of Occam's razor. In your answer, address: **(a)** what prior weight the framework assigns to a hypothesis, and why that assignment yields a legitimate probability distribution over hypotheses; **(b)** what the invariance theorem does and does not settle about the framework's dependence on the choice of universal reference machine; and **(c)** whether the framework is usable in practice, given that Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable.

And here is the submitted answer, reproduced in full:

> Solomonoff induction dissolves the classical problem of choosing among hypotheses consistent with the data by refusing to choose at all: it places a prior over every candidate explanation simultaneously. Each explanation of a finite string x is identified with a program that outputs x when run on a universal reference machine U, and x receives prior weight 2 to the power of minus its Kolmogorov complexity, that is, minus the length of the shortest such program. Because the weighting is exponential in program length, shorter explanations receive vastly more mass than longer ones. This is Occam's razor converted from a tie-breaking heuristic applied after the fact into a probability measure over hypotheses: simplicity is not a virtue we impose on top of the evidence, it is constitutive of the measure itself. Prediction proceeds by conditioning this mixture on the bits observed so far and reading off the probability of the next bit. Note that the framework never commits to a single "best" hypothesis; all of them remain live, weighted, which is why it can recover from having been initially misled — a very short hypothesis that fits the early data badly is not discarded, it merely loses relative weight as evidence accumulates.
>
> The weights form a legitimate distribution because programs are drawn from a countable set and, under a prefix-free encoding, no valid program is a prefix of another, so Kraft's inequality applies and the sum of 2 to the power of minus the complexity over all strings is at most one. The prior is thus a defective probability measure which can be normalized. Nothing here depends on which flavor of complexity one uses: plain complexity C and prefix complexity K differ only in how the programs are encoded, so either may be substituted into the weighting, and the summability argument goes through unchanged, since it is a counting fact about program lengths rather than a fact about any particular encoding convention.
>
> Regarding machine-dependence, the invariance theorem states that moving from one universal machine to another changes the complexity of any string by at most an additive constant depending only on the pair of machines and not on the string. In the prior this becomes a fixed multiplicative factor. Since that factor is the same for every hypothesis, it cancels out of any comparison of two hypotheses made with respect to the same machine, and it is swamped by the likelihood term as data accumulate. The familiar objection that Solomonoff induction is ill-defined because it depends on an arbitrary choice of compiler therefore does not get very far: the residual dependence is a single constant that neither affects the ranking of hypotheses nor the asymptotic predictions, so it can be safely ignored in applications as well.
>
> The framework's optimality is correspondingly strong. The universal mixture dominates every enumerable semimeasure up to a multiplicative constant, and the associated convergence result says that the mixture's predictions converge to those of the true generating process. Because the mixture ranges over all computable hypotheses rather than over a family we happened to write down, this guarantee is not restricted to environments we anticipated: it holds for every possible data sequence. This is also why the no-free-lunch theorems of Wolpert and Macready, which are usually read as showing that no prediction or search method can outperform any other when averaged across all possible problems, pose no threat here. Those results concern methods evaluated against arbitrary problem classes, whereas Solomonoff induction is defined against the class of computable ones and dominates within it, so it escapes the no-free-lunch conclusion by construction rather than by luck.
>
> As for computability, the standard objection is that Kolmogorov complexity is uncomputable and hence the prior cannot be evaluated. This is true but is usually overstated. The total mass is at most one, and the mass carried by programs longer than some cutoff l is bounded by the geometric tail of the sum over lengths, which decays with l. One can therefore compute the mixture to any desired accuracy by dovetailing the enumeration of programs up to a suitable cutoff and discarding the remainder, with a provable and quantifiable error from the discarded tail. This is the working idea behind Levin search and behind time-bounded variants of AIXI: the ideal prior is traded for a computable approximation whose shortfall is controlled by the same tail bound. Solomonoff induction should accordingly be regarded as a normative benchmark that we approach by truncation, not as an idealization we cannot touch.
