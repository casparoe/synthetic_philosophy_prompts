> &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;L&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;C&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;R
> T&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;(2, 3)&nbsp;&nbsp;(4, 0)&nbsp;&nbsp;(0, 0)

> (a) Find the set of strategy profiles that survives iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies. A strategy may be dominated by a mixed strategy as well as by a pure one; show your working. [8]
> (b) Does the order in which the eliminations are carried out affect your answer to (a)? Justify your answer. [5]
> (c) Suppose instead that at each round we eliminate weakly dominated strategies. Would your answer to (b) change? Would the set of surviving profiles still contain every Nash equilibrium of the game? Justify both answers. [7]

And here's the script, transcribed more or less as written:

> (a) First look for domination by pure strategies. For player 1, T doesn't dominate M, because T gets 0 at R where M gets 1, and B doesn't dominate M either, because B gets 0 at C. But the mixture ½T + ½B gives (2.5, 2, 2) across L, C, R, which beats M's (1, 1, 1) against every column, so M is strictly dominated and goes. Symmetrically for player 2: neither L nor R dominates C on its own, since L only ties C at B and R only ties C at T, but the mixture ½L + ½R gives player 2 (1.5, 2, 1.5) against T, M and B, all strictly better than C's (0, 1, 0), so C goes as well. In the reduced game B strictly dominates T, since 3 > 2 and 4 > 0, and R strictly dominates L, so both of those go too and we're left with the single profile (B, R). Since only one profile survives, that must be the unique Nash equilibrium of the game.
>
> (b) No. Strict dominance is a relation between two strategies belonging to the same player, and it's defined by comparing their payoffs against the opponent's strategies, so crossing some of the opponent's strategies off the list can't alter the comparison between the two we're actually looking at. Nothing that fails to be dominated at the start can become dominated further down the line, so whichever order you do the deletions in you end up with the same surviving set.
>
> (c) For this particular game it wouldn't make any difference, because every strategy we deleted in (a) was strictly dominated and strict dominance implies weak dominance, so exactly the same sequence of deletions is still available. In general though the answer to (b) would change, because with weak dominance the order can matter: deleting a strategy that was only weakly dominated can get rid of a tie that was the reason some other strategy was weakly dominated in the first place. On the Nash equilibrium question, no change either — a weakly dominated strategy is still never a best response to anything the opponent might play, so it can't carry positive weight in an equilibrium, and the surviving set will still contain every Nash equilibrium of the game.
