I run the annotation pipeline for a municipal dataset that will drive automatic enforcement of a crowding bylaw. Our guideline asks annotators to label each still image with the predicate "the image shows a crowd." We currently publish a single bright line — twelve or more clearly distinguishable people counts as a crowd, eleven or fewer does not — and we are considering replacing it with a three-band rule: clearly a crowd, clearly not a crowd, and a middle band routed to a second reviewer, with residual ties broken by a coin flip so the training set stays class-balanced.

Two philosophers have been asked to advise, and they disagree about what a guideline like ours is doing. Margit is an epistemicist: she holds that "crowd" has one sharp extension, fixed by our actual patterns of use, that every image either shows a crowd or does not, and that our inability to say where the boundary falls follows from a margin-for-error constraint on knowledge rather than from any gap in the world or in the language. Rao is a supervaluationist: he holds that there are many admissible precisifications of "crowd," each with a different threshold, that an image falling under some of them but not all is neither truly nor falsely a crowd, and that our three-band scheme merely writes down a semantic structure that was already there.

Write a dialogue between Margit and Rao, set in the meeting where the guideline is being revised, in which each tries to move the other on whether the authority should adopt the three-band rule — and, underneath that question, on which picture of vagueness is correct.

Constraints on the dialogue:

- Between 1,000 and 1,300 words. Minimal stage direction, no narrator commentary, no summarising of what has just been said.
- Both are equally able and equally well informed, and neither is permitted a weak version of the other's position. Before answering an objection, each must state it in a form the other accepts as fair; have each explicitly say whether the restatement is fair.
- Work through three cases, in this order:
  1. **The band-boundary case.** The three-band rule needs a number for where the clear cases stop and the middle band begins. Rao must explain why that second threshold is not as arbitrary as the first, and Margit must press whether the problem simply recurs one level up, and again above that, without end.
  2. **The coin-flip case.** For a middle-band image, the tie is broken by a coin. Does the coin make true what was neither true nor false, or did the coin flip alter our practice and thereby move a boundary that was already determinate? Each must say what their view predicts the guideline's audit log should show over many such flips.
  3. **The coherence case.** The bylaw also uses "large gathering," which is entailed by "crowd" but not conversely. Each must explain how their semantics secures that entailment for borderline images, and must face the worry that their rival secures it only by accident.
- Each side concedes at least one sub-point during the exchange and changes their mind about it, while the central disagreement survives intact. Neither converts the other.
- Before the end, they must confront the possibility that the practical recommendation — adopt or reject the three-band rule — comes out identically on both semantics, and therefore that nothing turns on their dispute. Each must give a reason it is not idle, and each must say whether the other's reason succeeds.
- No citations, no bibliography, no named papers, schools, or historical figures other than the two speakers. The arguments must carry their own weight.
- Mark with an asterisk any argument that you take to be a construction for this scenario rather than a move from the standard dialectic. I want at least two asterisked moves per speaker.

End with one line outside the dialogue, beginning "Verdict:", stating in a single sentence whether Margit and Rao's disagreement is substantive or merely notational and, if substantive, the one claim on which it turns.
