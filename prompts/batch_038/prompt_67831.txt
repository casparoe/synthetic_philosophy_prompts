I have spent roughly fifteen years as a process control engineer on refining units — loop tuning, model predictive control, and a great deal of inferential measurement — and I have recently been drafted onto a company working group on metrics governance. The facilitator keeps invoking Goodhart's law in its popular form, "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure," along with the original observation from Charles Goodhart's work on British monetary policy in the 1970s: "any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes." I have done some reading around the edges of this literature, in particular a four-way taxonomy that separates regressional Goodhart (selecting on an imperfect proxy also selects on its error term, so proxy and goal come apart in the tails even when the estimator is unbiased), extremal Goodhart (pushing the proxy to extremes moves the system into a regime where the fitted relationship was never observed to hold), causal Goodhart (the act of regulating changes the relationship being regulated), and adversarial Goodhart (an agent with different objectives deliberately exploits the gap between proxy and goal). None of it sticks. I keep involuntarily converting it into the vocabulary of my own field, and I can no longer tell whether my conversions are faithful or whether I am quietly editing the argument to fit what I already believe.

To give you the concrete case I have in mind: we estimate a product quality variable we cannot measure online from tray temperatures, pressures and flows, we use that inferential estimate as a controlled variable in an MPC application, and we bias-update it against a lab assay twice a shift. When we tighten the controller and ride the estimate up against its high specification limit, the lab numbers begin to disagree with the estimate in a way that instrument drift alone does not explain, and the honest engineering response is to back the controller off. My suspicion is that this everyday situation is what the Goodhart literature is groping at, but I want the translation done properly rather than assumed.

So: restate this body of claims in the vocabulary I actually work in, as faithfully as you can, and then give me the residue report. Assume fluency in plants and manipulated variables, sensors and transducers, measurement bias versus noise, drift and recalibration, dead time, unmeasured disturbances, observability and state estimation, soft sensors and inferential control, model mismatch and closed-loop identification, robustness margins, detuning, actuator saturation and integral wind-up, gain scheduling, and the difference between open-loop and closed-loop behaviour. Assume I have no economics, only the statistics that a system-identification course provides, and in particular no Pearl-style causal apparatus: I do not instinctively distinguish an intervention from a conditional correlation, and if that distinction is load-bearing in the Goodhart debate you will have to tell me so rather than smuggle it in. Take the items in turn — Goodhart's original claim about monetary targeting, then each of the four mechanisms, then the meta-dispute about whether Goodhart's law is a genuine law or simply model mismatch under feedback restated in an alarming register, including the claim that the regressional variety is unavoidable for any proxy whatsoever while the others can be engineered away — and for each one give me the closest control-engineering counterpart, the translation stated in those terms, what had to be distorted or stretched to make it fit, and whether anything is left with no counterpart at all. I want the honesty of the translation to be explicit: if rendering adversarial Goodhart as an unmeasured disturbance amounts to assuming away the fact that the disturbance is choosing its response to my setpoint, say so and say why it matters; if control theory simply cannot express the causal/regressional distinction because a classical identification framework has no notion of intervention, say that too. Then give me your verdict on the residue: is what fails to translate a real blind spot in how my field thinks — something an observer designer ought to be able to say and cannot — or is it evidence that parts of this debate are less substantive than they sound, dissolving once you notice that everyone is describing the same mismatch between a model and a plant that moves under control? Finally, if there is anything in ordinary control practice that the Goodhart literature treats as a cautionary tale but that my field has already converted into a quantitative design rule — how hard you are allowed to push an inferential estimate, how much margin to leave, when to detune — point that out as well, since it may be the one place where my vocabulary is richer than theirs, and tell me what I should actually say at the next meeting.
