I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am working on a long piece about whether the present is real. In reporting it I have absorbed a position that nearly every physicist I spoke to stated as settled rather than argued for: that special relativity disposed of presentism decades ago. The reasoning, as I now hold it, is the one usually traced to Rietdijk and Putnam in the mid-1960s and popularized through Penrose's image of two passers-by on a street whose respective "nows" slice the Andromeda galaxy at different stages of a decision. Because simultaneity is frame-relative, no privileged set of events constitutes the present; because what exists cannot coherently vary with the state of motion of whoever asserts it, whatever is real relative to one observer's now is real simpliciter; so past, present, and future events exist on the same footing. I want to know what would count against that.

Please take the view seriously and give me a map of its exposure:

- Separate the load-bearing premises from the ones doing quiet work. In particular, distinguish the empirical claim (no experiment picks out a frame) from the metaphysical step (therefore no foliation is objectively the present), and tell me what would have to be true for each to fail.
- Rank the candidate defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and for each say whether it is a live possibility or merely a logical one, and whether the work would have to be done by physicists, philosophers, or both.
- Assess the escape routes honestly: local "nows" confined to an observer's backward light cone, as proposed by Stein and developed by Savitt; non-standard synchrony conventions in the Reichenbachian sense; a privileged foliation handed to the presentist by cosmology, such as the cosmic microwave background rest frame or constant-mean-curvature slicing; and the view on which a real frame exists but is empirically undetectable in principle. For each: what the world would have to look like, and whether it genuinely saves presentism or merely relocates the problem.
- Are there results already on the table that cut one way or the other — the use of a 3+1 split in canonical formulations of gravity, the tightening experimental bounds on Lorentz violation, collapse models that appear to want a preferred slicing — and how much weight would my sources allow them?
- Is there any conceivable observation that would distinguish eternalism from presentism-plus-privileged-frame? If not, say so plainly, and tell me whether the framing my sources reach for is a category error, or whether the absence of empirical leverage is itself a reason to prefer the view that needs none.
- Which of these would a committed eternalist concede as genuinely counting against them, and which would they dismiss as failing to engage the view at all? Does the position risk anything, or has it been qualified into a shape no argument can dent? If it is well insulated, tell me whether that is a defect in a metaphysical thesis or precisely what one should expect of one.
- And does the phenomenology bear on any of this — the felt passage of time, the extended "specious present" — or can both sides explain it equally well, in which case I should not imply otherwise?

I am not after references or a reading list; I want the reasoning laid out so that I can carry it to a physicist and see which parts survive the encounter. What I most want to know is whether the sentence that physics has shown the present not to be objectively real can stand in my piece, and if not, what the strongest claim I can honestly print in its place would be.
