Retired marine biologist here, 68, spending my days reading history and philosophy for fun, which is a blast. I keep running into an argument pattern I don't have a word for, and I want to know whether it's a recognized thing or whether it needs one.

Two examples. First, from my old working life: a colleague argued that a certain kind of fishery collapses within roughly 30 years once harvest passes some threshold, and he had a list of about a dozen historical cases behind him. I pointed at one on his own list that had passed the threshold and was still going strong after 60 years. Fine, he said, but that one had a different upwelling regime, so it wasn't really comparable, and he dropped it. A few weeks later someone else flagged a second case, and that one went because the stock assessment methods weren't consistent across the record. By the end there were four cases left and he was more confident in the 30-year figure than he'd been with twelve. Nobody lied, nobody invented anything, and every single exclusion was on its own perfectly reasonable and based on real knowledge of the systems.

Second, from the book group I'm in now — we've been reading about the rise and fall of great powers. Someone makes the classic move: don't get lost in the details of the present situation, look at the base rate, every empire in history has gone, average lifespan is such-and-such, so here's your forecast. Another member says, what about the one that lasted 800 years? Answer: that wasn't really an empire, it was a tribute system with a ceremonial center. What about the one that survived its own collapse and reconstituted? Doesn't count, no continuity of administration. Same shape as the fishery: the comparison group gets trimmed every time a member threatens the pattern, the trimming is done with genuine expertise, and confidence rises as the group shrinks, all the way to the point where there's one case left and the "base rate" is just that case.

So — is there an established name or literature for this? I know a few near neighbours and none of them sit right. No true Scotsman is closest in spirit, but that's about rescuing a universal claim by redefining a word, and here nobody's defending a definition, they're defending a statistical forecast, and the definitions get narrower rather then the claim gets vaguer. The Texas sharpshooter is drawing the target around the bullet holes, but that sounds like a one-off act on data you already have, not this slow reactive carving under challenge. Cherry-picking is picking your sample up front. Overfitting is in the neighbourhood, but no model is being fit — it's the sample being sculpted. And I gather there's a general puzzle about which class of past cases you should take a base rate from at all, but I'm not asking about that; I'm asking about this specific dynamic where the class is revised downward in response to objections and confidence goes up as a result.

If there's no name, coin one, but honestly I care much more about the carving than the label. What exactly is this thing, what is it not, and where's the line between it and legitimate narrowing of a comparison group? Because surely sometimes you should throw out a bad comparision — twelve sloppy matches can be worse than four good ones, and I'd have defended my colleague's first exclusion on its merits. So what makes an exclusion legitimate rather than symptomatic: timing (criteria fixed before you see the cases)? symmetry (would you exclude a case that helps you)? some floor on how many cases are left? And is there a point where the shrinking itself counts as evidence against the forecast, no matter how reasonable each individual exclusion looked?
