I sit in on a reading group about heuristic arguments in number theory. Whenever someone asks whether a heuristic derivation should be trusted, the person running it gives the same one-line answer: the primes are random, so unless there is a positive reason for correlation, treat the relevant events as independent. Last week he used it to deflect an objection to the usual derivation of the Hardy–Littlewood twin prime constant — a visitor suggested the model might be blind to correlations it has no way of registering — and he replied that any departure from the random prediction would itself have to be explained, so the burden of proof sits with the objector.

Please unpack that sentence. I want the distinct precise theses it could be standing for, running from near-platitude to wildly ambitious, each stated as something that could actually be checked or refuted: a claim about which asymptotics a Cramér-style model reproduces, a claim about the absence of bias in any residue-class sense, a claim about where the burden of proof lies, a claim about how much evidential weight a successful heuristic confers on the conjecture it suggests, plus any other reading you think the sentence gets stretched to cover. For each, say whether it is true, false, or not the kind of thing that could be either, and whether it would in fact license shifting the burden onto the objector. Finish by sorting the standard caveats people attach to the slogan — congruence obstructions, the parity barrier in sieve theory, irregular behaviour at very short scales — into those that are genuine counterexamples to some reading and those that are simply why the slogan has to be qualified at all.
