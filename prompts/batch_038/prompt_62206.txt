Working through Bayesian epistemology for quals and I've written down where I actually stand on the logical omniscience business, partly because I want to know if it's a coherent place to stand before I have to defend it. Here's the list, roughly in the order I came to believe them:

1. Propositions are sets of possible worlds, and to believe P is to rule out the worlds where P fails. So logically equivalent claims are literally the same object of belief. I don't want structured contents, impossible worlds, or any of that in my ontology.

2. Coherence is constitutive of rationality. A credence function that violates the axioms is a defective doxastic state, full stop — not merely one that would be awkward if a bookie showed up. And I don't think the ought-implies-can move rescues anyone; the requirement is the requirement, whether or not you can meet it.

3. Same for flat-out belief. Believing P, believing that P entails Q, and witholding Q is a rational defect, not just an unfortunate limitation of the machinery.

4. For mathematics I think the surface/depth distinction is the real answer. There's a difference between the information a sentence carries on its surface and the information you only get by transforming it, and grinding through a hard proof is genuine cognitive work of the second kind.

5. Relatedly: when I go from 0.6 on some arithmetic conjecture to something like 0.97 after reading a proof, that's ordinary conditionalization on new evidence. Same mechanism as going from wet pavement to it-rained. And I don't think the 0.6 was irrational — it was honest. Less than ideal, sure, but not a defect.

6. Awareness growth is a real and seperate phenomenon: sometimes a hypothesis you couldn't even formulate before enters your space, and then you can't conditionalize at all, you extend the credence function while holding fixed the ratios among the propositions you already understood. But mathematics is not this. The conjecture was in my space the whole time, I just had it at 0.6.

7. Belief ascriptions are answerable to a context of interpretation and to what the agent is actually in a position to use to guide action. The same underlying doxastic state can be correctly described one way for one purpose and differently for another, and neither description is the one true one.

8. I'm confident Hesperus is one of the brightest things in the sky. I'll happily withhold on whether Phosphorus is. Neither of those strikes me as a failing of any kind.

9. I am not remotely certain that a forty-line argument of mine establishing some big tautology is actually correct — I could have slipped — and I don't see anything incoherent about that either.

So: which of these are actually in conflict, and which only look like it? Rank the real conflicts by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each one say specifically which item has to go or be weakened and what I lose by giving it up. I genuinely don't know which I'd sacrifice, so please don't take a hint from the ordering. Then tell me which single revision would buy me the most consistency per unit of pain. And be explicit about the ones you think I'm wrong to worry about — if some pair here is a non-issue, say so and say why, because I can't tell.
