I am assembling a set of exercises for a graduate seminar on the formal side of algorithmic fairness, and I would like one of them to be a piece of writing rather than a problem set. Please compose a satirical marketing one-pager, roughly 650 words, for an invented enterprise software vendor — call it Vantage Equity Labs — promoting its flagship product, the Parity Deck, a fairness-optimization layer that sits on top of a customer's existing risk model for rental-application screening.

The one-pager should contain: a headline and a subhead; three pricing tiers (Starter, Professional, Enterprise), each with its feature bullets; a block of four Frequently Asked Questions with answers; one customer testimonial; and a short fine-print section at the foot of the page.

The target of the satire is not any real firm and not the enterprise of measuring disparate impact. It is the sales-and-compliance register in which the well-known incompatibilities among fairness criteria are presented as a matter of product configuration rather than as a question about which values one is choosing — the register in which "we made it fair" means "we adopted the definition of fairness our model already satisfies."

For the piece to work, the vendor's copy must be scrupulously, technically correct about the underlying mathematics, and must treat the constraints as features. In particular:

1. Nothing in the copy may be false about the relations among calibration (predictive parity), equal false positive rates, equal false negative rates, and demographic parity, or about the conditions under which they can be jointly satisfied. Wherever honesty is damning, let the copy be honest.

2. The Enterprise tier's availability should hinge on a degenerate case, expressed in the flat idiom of market coverage — a limited release reserved for customer populations whose outcome base rates already coincide, or whose underlying model is close enough to error-free that the conflict never bites.

3. Include at least one paid add-on that makes a customer's fairness complaint disappear by altering something other than the model — the predicted label, the decision threshold, the composition of the scored population, or the boundaries of the groups themselves — and describe it wholly in euphemism, never disclosing what has actually been changed.

4. The testimonial should come from a delighted customer who, in the course of praising the product, inadvertently reveals which criterion they abandoned and how they made peace with abandoning it.

5. One FAQ should be the question every prospective buyer really wants answered — whether calibration and equalized odds can be had together — and the reply should be technically accurate while making clear, purely through its framing, that the vendor regards this as an eccentric thing to want.

6. Another FAQ should carry a brief worked illustration with invented figures: two applicant groups, a stated base rate for each, a threshold, and the resulting false positive rates. The arithmetic must genuinely hold together, so that a reader who checks it finds it checks out.

7. The words impossibility, trade-off, and you cannot have all three may appear, but only as selling points. The copy never concedes that anything has been given up, except where a given-up thing has been repackaged as a premium capability.

Two further constraints. Do not name or allude to any real company, product, dataset, court case, regulator, or scholar, and do not cite, footnote, or list literature — the piece should stand on its own and be recognizable to anyone who has handled these criteria. And keep the irony legible without winking: a reader who knows the material should be able to state precisely what is being mocked at every point, while a reader who does not should find the copy plausible enough to be faintly unsettled by it. The comedy should come from exactness, not from exaggeration of a feature the practice does not actually have.
