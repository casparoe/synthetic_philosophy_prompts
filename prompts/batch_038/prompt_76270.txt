Teaching the free will seminar again and the last meeting is on sphexishness — Hofstadter's name for what the digger wasp does: she drags a paralized cricket to the mouth of the burrow, goes down alone to check the nest over, and if you've nudged the cricket a few inches while she was in there she hauls it back to the entrance and runs the checking routine again, and again, instead of just carrying the thing inside. I want to hand the students a poem on their way out, and I want the form to carry the argument instead of the poem explaining it.

So: 18 lines, three six-line stanzas, and each stanza uses the same six end-words as the one before it, rotated one position round (last end-word becomes first, and so on), so the loop is sitting there on the page. On top of that, exactly one word per stanza that isn't an end-word has to be new — a word appearing nowhere earlier in the poem — and it should land where someone following the pattern notices it as an intrusion. By the third stanza the students should be feeling the worry that "introduce exactly one new word per stanza" is itself just a longer routine the poem cant get out of, which is roughly where the class always ends up. No Hofstadter, no Dennett, no latin binomial, no lecture — and it still has to work as a poem for someone who's never heard the term.
