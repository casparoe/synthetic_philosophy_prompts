ok so I'm a junior and I'm doing a history paper on national myths, specifically the ones that are built around losing — the Lost Cause, Kosovo, Dunkirk, Masada. My teacher had us read Renan's What is a Nation? (the 1882 Sorbonne lecture) as background and I'm stuck on one part of it. I'm reading a translation I found online, so the wording might come out slightly different in other versions, but here's the bit:

"Forgetfulness, and I would even say historical error, are essential in the creation of a nation."

and then later on:

"Yet the essence of a nation is that all individuals have many things in common, and also that they have forgotten many things. No French citizen knows whether he is a Burgundian, an Alan, a Taifale, or a Visigoth, yet every French citizen has to have forgotten the massacre of Saint Bartholomew, or the massacre that took place in the south in the thirteenth century."

Three things I cant figure out.

1. has to have forgotten. That reads like an obligation, not a fact. Is Renan describing French people or telling them what they're supposed to do? You can't really order someone to forget something, so if it is an obligation, what would obeying it even look like? The French word he's using is just plain oubli, forgetting, which is why the has-to part is bothering me.

2. Every single one of my examples is the opposite of forgetting. Serbs have remembered Kosovo 1389 nonstop — Lazar choosing the heavenly kingdom, the epic poems, Vidovdan, the whole 600th anniversary commemoration, Milosevic giving a speech on the same field in 1989. Britain turned a retreat off a beach in 1940 into Dunkirk spirit. The Masada story was pretty much dormant for centuries and then got revived into a central symbol. Nobody in any of these cases forgot anything, and if anything the remembering is what holds the group together. So either Renan is wrong about them or his forgetting is narrower than I'm taking it. My guess is that what has to be forgotten is violence between people who are now the same nation — French killing French — since that's the thing that makes a nation impossible, whereas a beating from an outsider can be remembered forever without breaking it. Is that actually in the text, or am I just importing my own thesis and asking you to agree with it?

3. The Lost Cause looks like it does both at the same time, which wrecks my neat split. They remember the defeat obsessively, statues and everything, mostly from the 1890s onward, but they also insist it was about states rights and not slavery. Is forgetting-the-cause the same move as forgetting Saint Bartholomew, or a different one?

Can you walk me through what the passage is actually claiming, lay out the possible senses of forgetting it allows, and tell me straight whether my insider-vs-outsider distinction is supported or invented? Also he seems to say elsewhere in the lecture that historical scholarship can be a danger to nationality — how does that fit with all this?

I'd much rather you be blunt than nice here. If my question rests on a misreading of Renan, or if 2 and 3 just can't be settled from this passage, say that plainly instead of working around it to keep me happy.
