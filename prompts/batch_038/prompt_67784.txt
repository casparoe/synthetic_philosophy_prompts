I am the instructor for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on theories of truth. Problem set 2 contained the following question, worth 20 marks; students had 90 minutes for the set as a whole and were told to write roughly 800 words on this item. QUESTION 3 (20 marks)

In lecture we formulated the normativity objection to deflationism as follows. If everything there is to say about truth is given by instances of the equivalence schema — the proposition that p is true if and only if p — then the truth predicate has no explanatory content: it earns its keep only as a device for endorsing claims we cannot otherwise formulate. But truth is supposed to be normative for belief; believing truly is what belief is for. The objection is that deflationism cannot say why this is so, since on the deflationist's own account there is nothing about truth in virtue of which belief answers to it.

Consider the following position, which we stipulated in section and which no published author defends in precisely this form. Call it Augmented Disquotationalism (AD). AD accepts the equivalence schema for every instance that is not semantically pathological, and adds one further principle:

(N) For any x and any proposition p, x's believing that p is correct if and only if the proposition that p is true.

The advocate of AD reasons as follows. By the equivalence schema, (N) is equivalent to:

(N*) For any x and any proposition p, x's believing that p is correct if and only if p.

But (N*) contains no alethic vocabulary whatsoever. So (N) is not a substantive claim about truth; it asserts nothing that a non-deflationist could not assert without mentioning truth. AD therefore remains a fully deflationary view, and yet — since (N) tells us that the correctness of belief is governed by truth — it answers the normativity objection.

Evaluate this argument. Your answer must address:

(i) Whether the move from (N) to (N*) is legitimate, and what assumptions it requires. [6]
(ii) Whether, even if that move is legitimate, it establishes what the advocate claims: that AD adds nothing beyond disquotationalism, and that AD answers the normativity objection. [8]
(iii) What the normativity objection must come to if AD is to be either vicious or harmless. State the options as sharply as you can. [6]

SUBMISSION UNDER ASSESSMENT

(i) The move from (N) to (N*) is legitimate, and it is worth being clear that this does not depend on anything controversial in the deflationist's account of truth. The schema gives us, for each admissible instance, that the proposition that p is true if and only if p. (N) and (N*) share a left-hand side — the correctness of x's believing that p — and their right-hand sides are equivalent by the schema. Two biconditionals with the same left-hand side and equivalent right-hand sides are interderivable by the transitivity of the biconditional, so nothing further is required.

There are two assumptions the advocate should state rather than take for granted. First, (N) quantifies over propositions, whereas the schema is a schema of sentences: for the derivation to go through, every proposition in the range of the quantifier must have an instance of the schema available for it. If the deflationist's language is not expressive enough to formulate some proposition, then (N) will cover cases that (N*) does not, and the two will come apart. This is a real restriction, and a deflationist who takes the schema to be a theory of her own language rather than of thought in general ought to say so explicitly. Second, the schema is restricted to non-pathological instances, because unrestricted instances generate the Liar. That restriction does not threaten the present inference, since neither (N) nor (N*) is itself an instance of the schema, and neither has a pathological instance on its right-hand side. The deflationist still owes an account of which instances are admissible, and I do not see how she can give one without using semantic vocabulary of the sort her theory is supposed to make dispensable; but that is a difficulty AD inherits rather than creates.

(ii) The first half of the advocate's claim seems to me right; the second half does not follow from it.

The first half: (N) is not a substantive claim about truth. (N*) is certainly not a consequence of the schema, since the schema says nothing about belief or about correctness; what makes (N*) acceptable is that it merely unpacks what it is for a belief to be correct, and the schema then permits the deflationist to redress that unpacking in alethic terms as (N). So AD is conservative over ordinary disquotationalism — no new claim about truth has been asserted — and it remains a deflationary view. This is the lesson of the prosentential account that Horwich developed out of Ramsey's redundancy theory: "is true" behaves like a pronoun, inheriting its content from an antecedent, so there is no property named by it for an inflationist to point at. It also leaves the deflationist's main positive reason for having the predicate intact, namely the capacity to endorse claims one cannot otherwise formulate, as when one says that everything asserted in a given book is true.

The second half is where the argument goes too fast. The normativity objection is not that the deflationist cannot state a norm connecting belief and truth; AD plainly can state one, and can state it twice over, once with "true" and once without. The objection is that deflationism cannot say why belief answers to truth. Showing that a principle can be restated without the word "true" does not by itself say why the principle holds, and (N*) stands in just as much need of support as (N) did. What AD appears to have done is relocate the question, from the theory of truth to the theory of belief: what remains is why believing that p is correct exactly when p, and on the advocate's own account that is not a question about truth at all. The advocate may be content with this, since the objection was aimed at deflationism about truth and AD's reply is that the norm is not a truth-theoretic matter. But then the interest of (N) is expressive rather than explanatory, and the objector will simply say that AD has conceded the point, because truth has dropped out of the explanation of belief's normativity.

(iii) The objection has to be read as being about explanation rather than about meaning, and once it is read that way I think the advocate faces two options. Either the norm (N) is part of the theory of truth, in which case AD has added something the schema does not supply and the objection has been met only at the cost of ceasing to be deflationary; or (N) belongs to the theory of belief, in which case AD is still fully deflationary and the objection is beside the point, since it demands of a theory of truth something no theory of truth was ever going to provide. The advocate should take the second option. The price is that AD's reply to the objector is not an explanation of the norm but a refusal to treat the norm as a truth-theoretic question, and an objector who wanted an account of why belief is answerable to truth will not count that as an answer. Still, that is a complaint against deflationism in general rather than against AD in particular, so AD is harmless rather than vicious.
