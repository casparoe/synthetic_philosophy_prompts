Retired last March after 37 years running a cryogenics lab, and I've been reading philosophy properly ever since — badly, probably, but enthusiastically. I worked out an argument in my notebook over a couple of weeks and I'm now in the uncomfortable position of finding it more convincing than I want it to be, because the conclusion would mean most of what I said at group meetings for three decades was, strictly speaking, nothing. Maybe you can find the loose bolt.

What it's supposed to show: that the difference between "if X happens, Y happens" and "if X had happened, Y would have happened" is only grammar, and that once you see that, neither kind of sentence has truth conditions, and then laws of nature go down with them.

1. Both kinds of conditional get evaluated the same way. To take either seriously you do the Ramsey thing: suppose the antecedent, and see whether the consequent comes out on that supposition. That's how I'd check a counterfactual about my dilution refrigerator and its how I'd check an indicative one.

2. If two sentence forms are assessed by the same procedure, and the only difference between them is verb mood plus the speaker's private opinion about whether the antecedent actually holds, then they have the same content. Mood isn't a semantic category, it's a register.

3. So indicatives and counterfactuals are truth-conditionally the same animal, and anything proved about one transfers to the other.

4. For indicatives there's Adams's thesis: a rational person's degree of belief in "if A, B" should be the conditional probability of B on A. Not the probability of some proposition, just P(B|A).

5. Lewis's triviality result says you don't get that for free. In any language with real content in it, there's no connective whose probability equals the corresponding conditional probability under every probability function. So whatever it is that we assign P(B|A) to in (4), it cannot be a proposition with truth conditions. Indicatives aren't truth-apt. (I'm paraphrasing Lewis hard here, but I think thats the shape of it.)

6. By (3), counterfactuals aren't truth-apt either.

7. But the standard way to tell a law from a mere regularity is that laws support counterfactuals. Every sphere of uranium-235 is less than a mile across is law-ish precisely because it licenses if this lump were U-235, it would be under a mile across; the parallel generalization about gold spheres is accidental because it licenses nothing.

8. If counterfactuals have no truth conditions, nothing can support them. So the law/accident distinction has no content.

9. Therefore there are no laws of nature, only regularities, and every equation I ever wrote is at best compact notation for a table of conditional probabilities, some of which we sampled and most of which we didn't.

Where does it break? My instinct is (5). I never much liked the idea that degrees of belief are about sentences anyway, and I'd happily treat P(B|A) as a fact about my betting dispositions rather than about the world. But if I give up (5) I seem to lose the whole bridge from (4) to (6), and then (7) through (9) just hang there with nothing holding them up. My second candidate is (2), though (2) feels like the most scientifically respectable thing on the list — why would the grammar of a sentence change what's true?

So: which step would you reject, why did it look safe to me, and what does each possible rejection cost? And if you think the thing can be repaired on weaker premises so that some version of (9) survives, I'd want to know what those premises are. I'd much rather end up with a defensible instrumentalism than an unsound metaphysics, and right now I can't tell which one I'm holding.
