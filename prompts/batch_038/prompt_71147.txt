Sen's impossibility of a Paretian liberal has been picked over for half a century, and as far as I can tell nobody has been moved by anything. As I read the result, it is entirely framework-bound: two people, three states (Lewd reads the book, Prude reads the book, nobody reads it), each person decisive over the single pair of states that varies only with whether he himself reads, plus Pareto, plus an unrestricted domain. The contradiction appears only once the social ranking is required to be transitive, since Lewd's protected pair ranks Lewd-reads above nobody-reads, Prude's protected pair ranks nobody-reads above Prude-reads, and Pareto ranks Prude-reads above Lewd-reads. The stock replies are equally familiar: rights should be modelled as permissions over choice procedures rather than as decisiveness over outcome pairs; the offending profile should be excluded because a preference whose object is someone else's private conduct should never have entered the aggregation at all; rights are side constraints on action rather than terms in a ranking of end states, so the theorem attacks a view nobody need hold; and if transitivity is dropped the cycle simply stops mattering. Each has a standard rejoinder, and the discussion has hardened into mutual non-engagement.

My hypothesis is that the dispute was never about liberty but about the representational license of the social-preference framework — about whose preferences over what count as admissible input to a social ranking — and that the four replies differ mainly in how much of that framework they are willing to surrender. Test this. Is the hypothesis right, or is there a substantive disagreement about the nature of rights underneath it, or a merely verbal one about what the liberalism axiom was ever supposed to mean, or a disagreement about whether a theorem inside one formal apparatus can constrain a moral concept at all? For each of the four replies, state what it must assume in order to work and identify precisely where the rejoinder bites — whether it targets the formalism, the admissibility claim, or the interpretation of the axioms. Then tell me what would count as progress here: a rights formalism that provably escapes the result while preserving its content, a principled and non-question-begging restriction on admissible preferences, a reframing that dissolves the appearance of paradox, or nothing whatsoever. If nothing, argue that the debate should be abandoned rather than continued, and say what the persistence of the disagreement nevertheless reveals about how rights and welfare are related in social choice theory.
