**The question as set (25 points).** Consider the following two-player strategic-form game. The row player chooses among U, M, D; the column player among L, C, R. Each cell lists (row payoff, column payoff).

```
            L          C          R
   U     (0, 4)     (5, 1)     (0, 3)
   M     (2, 2)     (2, 2)     (2, 2)
   D     (5, 3)     (0, 1)     (5, 4)
```

(a) [4 points] For each player, determine whether any pure strategy is strictly dominated by another pure strategy.
(b) [7 points] Determine whether any strategy is strictly dominated by a mixed strategy over that player's other strategies. Wherever such domination holds, characterise the full set of mixing weights that witness it.
(c) [6 points] Carry out iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies, allowing domination by mixed strategies, and state what survives. Each deletion must be justified by reference to the game as it stands at the step at which the deletion is made.
(d) [4 points] Does the order in which deletions are performed affect the surviving set? Explain.
(e) [4 points] What does your answer to (c) imply about the Nash equilibria of this game?

**The student's answer.**

(a) For the row player no pure strategy is strictly dominated by another. U pays 5 against C, more than either M or D manages there, and D pays 5 against L and against R, more than either U or M manages there, so neither U nor D can be dominated; M pays 2 against everything and is beaten by U at C and by D at L and R, so it dominates nothing either. For the column player, L pays at least as much as C against every row and strictly more against U and against D, and R does the same, so L and R each dominate C — but only weakly, since all three pay 2 when the row player chooses M. L and R do not dominate one another. So no column strategy is strictly dominated by a pure strategy either.

(b) Mixtures change the picture. For the row player, ½U + ½D pays 2.5 against L, 2.5 against C and 2.5 against R, each strictly more than the 2 that M pays, so M is strictly dominated by a mixed strategy. More generally, any mixture pU + (1−p)D with 1/3 < p < 2/3 does the job. For the column player, C is strictly dominated by ½L + ½R, which pays 3.5 against U and 3.5 against D, in each case more than the 1 that C pays.

(c) Since the order of elimination does not matter for strict dominance, I delete M and C in the first round. What remains is

```
            L          R
   U     (0, 4)     (0, 3)
   D     (5, 3)     (5, 4)
```

in which D strictly dominates U, because 5 > 0 against both L and R, so U goes. That leaves the column player facing D alone, and R pays 4 against D where L pays 3, so L goes. The unique surviving profile is (D, R), with payoffs (5, 4).

(d) No. Provided each deletion is by strict dominance, the surviving set is independent of the order in which the deletions are made. That is why I was entitled to check the domination of C in the game with M already removed, and it is why deleting M and C together in the first round costs nothing. Weak dominance is a different matter: there the order of deletion can matter in general.

(e) A strictly dominated strategy is never played with positive probability in a Nash equilibrium, so every equilibrium of the original game survives into the reduced game, and the reduced game has only the one cell. (D, R) is therefore the unique Nash equilibrium, and it is a strict one: the best the row player can do against R is the 2 that M pays, and the best the column player can do against D is the 3 that L pays. The same fact can be seen from the other side, since C is never a best response to any belief the column player could hold about the row player's play; by the standard equivalence between being strictly dominated by a mixed strategy and never being a best response, the removal of C was licensed from the very first round. One point of interpretation worth making: nothing in (b) requires the row player to be indifferent between U and D, because dominance is a comparison of payoff vectors rather than a claim about what she will choose, so ½U + ½D is available as a witness even though she ends up playing D with certainty. Finally, the game is not zero-sum, so none of the above leans on minimax reasoning.
