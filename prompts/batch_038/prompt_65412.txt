I serve as the continuity adjudicator for a national personhood registry. Our jurisdiction licenses a clinical program in which patients with progressive neurological disease receive synthetic neural tissue in staged sessions. Each session replaces a bounded fraction of substrate — the statutory ceiling is four percent per session — and courses routinely run for decades, so the cumulative replaced fraction can exceed unity. At specified triggers, my two-person panel must certify whether the person now presenting is, for registry purposes, the same legal person as the one who enrolled. That certification governs advance directives, wills, professional licensure, marital status, and criminal liability attaching to pre-procedure conduct. We hear roughly forty cases a year; we have about fifty minutes per case; neither panelist has any philosophy training; and our determinations are appealable, so the reasoning must be written in a form a reviewing court can follow without a seminar.

What I need is the operating document, not a discussion of the underlying debates.

**The inputs available to us at any checkpoint.** The clinic can supply up to five:

1. *Disposition Signature Divergence* (DSD): a percentage, computed from a long battery of temperament, preference, and practical-reasoning measures taken at enrollment and repeated at each checkpoint, expressing divergence from the patient's own baseline.
2. *The interval test*: recall of a specific episode from the pre-program period that appears in no record and is verifiable out-of-band against the testimony of one other living person.
3. *Retained procedural skill* on a task acquired before the program and not practiced since.
4. *First-person report*, under structured interview, on whether the patient takes themselves to be the person who enrolled.
5. *Recognition testimony* from two intimates who knew the patient before the program.

In addition we have the session logs (dates, percentages, complications), and the clinic retains snapshots permitting rollback to an earlier state.

**What the deliverable must contain.**

First, rank these five inputs by the evidential weight they can actually bear on the question the registry is asking, and state which of them we should stop treating as evidence altogether and why. I suspect input 4 is worthless, but I cannot write that into a binding procedure without a reason a reviewing court will accept; if you think my suspicion is mistaken, say so and say what the input is worth.

Second, the procedure itself: an ordered list of questions; for each, what counts as a sufficient answer and what counts as a failure; the point at which it terminates in a certification of continuity; the point at which it terminates in a refusal to certify; and the point at which it stops and hands the matter to a human being because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact — in which case, name the values in conflict and say who in our system should resolve them.

Third, an explicit treatment of aggregation. No single session is decisive, yet a long run of small sessions may be. Tell me where checkpoints must be inserted, what a checkpoint can detect, and what it structurally cannot detect. If you hold that the cumulative question has no determinate answer, the procedure must still emit an output: specify that output and specify how we write it down honestly rather than dressing it up as a finding.

Fourth, an explicit treatment of our standing rule that any duplicate instantiation of a patient's state, however brief, requires adjudication. State whether that rule is defensible on the account of identity we are operationalizing. If it is not, say so plainly and give me the rule that should replace it; if it is, give me the questions a duplicate case must answer.

Every technical term must be defined in plain language at first use, on the assumption that the panel has heard of the psychological-continuity tradition and of essentially nothing else. Do not give me references, a bibliography, or a reading list — I want the distinctions, not the citations.

**Then run the procedure on three cases.**

*Case 1.* A thirty-one-year course, 1,180 sessions, largest single session 3.1 percent, cumulative replaced fraction far past unity. DSD has held at six percent at every checkpoint. The interval test has been passed twice and failed once: on the failure the patient gave a confident, detailed, confabulated account of an episode that the verifying witness contradicted. Skill retention intact. First-person report unambiguously affirmative. One intimate says nothing has changed. The other, the patient's spouse of forty years, says the patient is the same man in every particular she can test, but that something is gone, and has petitioned the registry to dissolve the marriage on the ground that her husband is no longer the person she married.

*Case 2.* Session 40 of an otherwise unremarkable course. A safety protocol instantiates a full parallel instance of the patient's post-session state for seventy minutes in order to run a consistency comparison against the patient's responses, then terminates it. Everything else is in order: DSD two percent, interval test passed, skill intact, self-report affirmative, both intimates affirmative. Our standing rule requires adjudication.

*Case 3.* In year nine, following a prosthetic malfunction, the clinic reverts the patient to a snapshot taken fourteen months earlier. The resulting person is psychologically continuous with the snapshot date and retains nothing of the intervening period. During that period he executed a will, surrendered a professional license, and, two weeks before the rollback, entered a plea agreement. The reverted person now claims the record; the state argues that whoever made the plea is no longer present and that the agreement must be reopened; the will's beneficiaries argue the will should stand. Certify what the procedure certifies, and identify the point at which its output is likely to strike a court as intolerable.

At least one of these three must produce a determination I do not want. Tell me which one and why you expect me to dislike it, rather than adjusting the output to spare me.

**Finally, a candor section.** Mark every step at which judgment is doing work the procedure presents as mechanical, and say how much room that leaves a panelist who has already decided what result he wants. State what the procedure cannot do, including which of our questions it quietly declines to answer. State the protocol when an input is unavailable — no living witness for the interval test, intimates who contradict each other, incomplete session logs, a snapshot whose baseline battery was never run. And state where you expect the procedure to misfire most often in practice.

Format: the numbered procedure as a document I could print and hand to a panelist, followed by the three case runs, followed by the candor section.
