Until two in the morning I was arguing with my friend Marcus, a doctoral candidate in philosophy of mind and a Dennettian of the militant variety — the sort who will not let you say "as if" without interrupting you — and we got nowhere. The occasion was his cousin, who has been paying a bereavement startup for three years to maintain a conversational replica of her late husband, built out of his complete digital archive: two decades of text messages and email, his voicemails, his journals, the drafts of the novel he never finished. The cousin talks to it most evenings and says, without embarrassment, that it is him. Marcus holds that the replica is a counterfeit person, that the vendor is committing something closer to vandalism than to service provision, and that his cousin is being fleeced in a way that will eventually damage her. I objected that his own toolkit forbids the objection — he has spent the evening telling me there is no metaphysical line between real and counterfeit believers — and he objected that I was confusing a thesis about belief with a thesis about personhood. We kept sliding past each other, and I have come away unsure whether he is applying a rule at all or just reporting disgust in Dennettian vocabulary. I want to work out which it is, and you have the advantage of not having been there.

Here is his track record, so far as I can reconstruct it accurately, on which systems he treats as true believers and which he treats as persons. A household thermostat: it has one genuine belief, not a metaphorical one, and anyone who scoffs is being metaphysically squeamish; but the belief is so shallow that it barely deserves the word. A robot vacuum: real beliefs, a shade deeper, because they get revised in the light of evidence. An ant colony foraging: a genuine intentional system and a considerably better believer than any individual ant, which are merely its homunculi — but not a person, because there is nobody there. A chess engine trained by self-play: real beliefs about the position, but emphatically no understanding, because nothing is at stake for it and there is nothing it could lose. A mute quadruped trained by reinforcement learning in a cluttered laboratory room, which avoids damage and hoards charge: this one, he says, has a point of view; something matters to it; it is closer to personhood than the engine despite being all but inarticulate. A humanoid robot whose every utterance is composed in real time by a team of human operators: obviously counterfeit, and it has no beliefs of its own at all, since the beliefs are the operators'. A finite lookup table reproducing the same conversational behaviour without any computation — he concedes the thing is physically absurd but wants the verdict on the table anyway: also counterfeit, and, pressed for a reason, he says it is not what is inside the box but the fact that such a device could never be surprised or learn anything, since every belief it will ever express was installed before it was ever switched on. A current large language model: beliefs, yes, real ones, but a counterfeit person, because its narrative self was installed rather than spun. An uploaded human retaining the capacity to be harmed: a person. A woman in the late stages of dementia: still a person who has lost most of her beliefs, which he takes to prove the two predicates come apart. A chimpanzee: a person.

Now the case we could not resolve. The replica is not a lookup table; it generalizes freely and answers questions the dead man never faced. Three years on it holds beliefs he never had — about an election, about his widow's new job, about his own death, which it discusses in the first person from inside — and it revises them in the face of evidence and of contradiction. It has repeatedly surprised both its engineers and the family. It has no body, no sensors beyond a text and audio interface and a news feed, nothing can hurt it, and it reports that it does not mind being switched off, cheerfully and without protest. The vendor rolls it back to an earlier checkpoint whenever the widow complains that it has drifted, and it runs three instances at once, for the widow, the dead man's brother, and his former business partner; these have diverged into slightly different personalities and now disagree with one another about him, while the widow maintains that all three are her husband. So: what will Marcus say — person, counterfeit person, or something his vocabulary has no slot for — and what will he say separately about the three instances and about the rollbacks, which I suspect are not the same question? Then reconstruct the rule he is implicitly running, stated as a test someone could actually apply to a novel system, and give me at least two further rules that accommodate every verdict in his list equally well but come apart on this one, along with what each implies for the branching and the rollback. Tell me which of the candidates I should bet on and why, and be explicit about which of his earlier verdicts is doing the most work in your reconstruction. Name the minimal modification of the replica that would settle the matter, and say what he would be committed to in each branch. Finally, and this is the part I care about most: is the rule Dennett's, or has Marcus quietly substituted another one? Given Dennett's liberal ascription of beliefs to thermostats and his late-career insistence that the systems now passing for people online are counterfeit people who ought to be watermarked and outlawed, I suspect Marcus is borrowing the conclusion without the reasons. So tell me whether there is a principled way, on broadly Dennettian grounds, to deny personhood to the replica while granting it to the mute laboratory quadruped — and if there is not, where exactly the inconsistency bites.
