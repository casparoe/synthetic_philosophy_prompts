I build infrastructure software for a living — pipeline tooling, mostly, and before that a few years on distributed storage — and I have never taken a single course in philosophy or political theory, so I want to be upfront that this request may be an odd one. My mother is retiring at the end of June after thirty-one years as the executive director of a very small nonprofit that worked on nuclear risk reduction: notification agreements, inspection schedules, the tedious back-channel business of getting two governments that were not speaking to agree on the wording of a paragraph. There is a dinner, and I have been asked to speak for the family for about six minutes. The room will be roughly ninety people: former negotiators and analysts who spent their working lives inside this subject, my two sisters, my mother's brothers, and a good many neighbours and bridge partners who know her only as a woman who grows an unreasonable number of tomatoes and is never late returning a phone call. I do not want to give the speech that gets given at these things — the chronology of positions held, the anecdote about her handwriting, the sentence about how she was tough but kind.

To understand what her job actually was, I spent the winter reading about the logic of deterrence, and I have become unable to stop thinking about one feature of it. The threat at the centre of the whole arrangement is rational to make and, if it were ever actually executed, would be an act of complete self-destruction for the person executing it — so the system's safety depends on everyone believing a promise that no sane person would keep, and on that promise never being tested. I also came across two refinements that struck me as more than technical. One is that the most credible posture is not a clean ultimatum but a deliberate slide toward shared, partly uncontrolled risk: you make the outcome uncertain for everyone, including yourself, precisely because a threat you fully control is a threat you could decline to carry out. The other is the objection that a perfectly automatic and therefore perfectly credible deterrent is the worst design anyone could choose, since it removes the last human decision at exactly the moment when a human decision is the only thing standing between an accident and a catastrophe. And then there is the uncomfortable corollary I could not shake: a balance that is too dangerous to disturb at the top level becomes a place where smaller, crueller conflicts are comparatively safe to have. My mother's view, which I have heard her state at dinner tables for twenty years, is that all of this is a moral scandal rather than a clever puzzle, and that the work consisted in making sure the test never came. She cannot stand being called a hero and she never used the word win.

Please write the speech, about eight hundred words, to be read aloud by someone who is not a performer. I want the ideas to carry the structure rather than decorate it: the shape of the paradox should be what the talk is about, and her life should be what illustrates it, not the reverse. A few firm constraints. No names, no book titles, no dates, and no quotations of any kind — please put nothing in quotation marks, because I cannot verify a quotation from memory and there will be people in the room who were in the meetings I am describing. Every idea must be rendered in a plain sentence a fourteen-year-old could follow before it is asked to do any work, and the words bomb and nuclear should not appear in the first third of the speech at all. Include one moment of genuine warmth and humour that the neighbours carry, and let it be about the family rather than about the end of the world. The speech must not resolve the paradox and must not end triumphantly; it should acknowledge that the arrangement my mother served was not wise, only that it held, and that a great deal of uncelebrated labour went into the holding. Finally, it needs to work on two audiences at once: the people who spent their careers on this should recognize that whoever wrote it actually understood the logic and got it right, and my mother's bridge partners should never once feel that they are being lectured. Useful concrete details if you want them: she kept yellow legal pads, she once spent two years on a single sentence of an agreement, she answered the phone the same way for thirty years, and the garden is real.
