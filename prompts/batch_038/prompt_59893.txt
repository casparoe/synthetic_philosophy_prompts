**Draft: "Establishment, belief, and the tyranny of cross-order demands"**

Readers of the *Pensées* on justice and force divide into two camps. The first takes Pascal for a quietist conservative: law originates in force, the origins must not be examined because examination breeds sedition, and therefore the established order is to be obeyed. The second takes him for a masked subversive: the fragments dissolve the legitimacy of every actually existing order, and obedience survives only as prudence. My claim is that both camps rest on the same conflation, and that once it is undone the choice between them dissolves too.

The conflation is between a constitutive thesis — that establishment makes law just — and a doxastic one — that establishment makes law *be believed* just. Pascal asserts the second, not the first: « La justice est ce qui est établi ; et ainsi toutes nos lois établies seront nécessairement tenues pour justes sans être examinées, puisqu'elles sont établies. » The second clause describes what will be held, and by whom, and under what conditions of scrutiny; it does not describe what justice consists in. The constitutive reading is usually imported from the more quotable « ne pouvant fortifier la justice, on a justifié la force, afin que le juste et le fort fussent ensemble, et que la paix fût, qui est le souverain bien » — but that sentence reports something human beings have *done* to force, and it states the purpose for which they did it. Its logic is instrumental, and its terminus is peace, not legitimacy.

I want to call the resulting picture an **installation asymmetry**: force is indispensable for installing a legal order and progressively less indispensable for maintaining it, because custom inherits the work of securing compliance. On this view the well-known injunction that it is dangerous to tell the people the laws are unjust — that they must instead be told to obey them because they are laws, as one obeys superiors not because they are just but because they are superior — is not a claim about the ground of obligation. It is a claim about the fragility of the installed state. Belief is what carries the order once force has retired from daily use, which is why belief, and not justice, is the object of political management.

Pascal's only positive criterion of illegitimacy is then supplied by the tyranny fragment: « La tyrannie est de vouloir avoir par une voie ce qu'on ne peut avoir que par une autre. » Different duties answer to different merits — love to agreeableness, fear to force, assent to knowledge — and the tyrant is the one who demands the currency of one order in payment for goods belonging to another. Tyranny, on this reading, is a category error rather than an injustice; and that is why the same fragments can press obedience to superiors while refusing to call the resulting arrangement justice. Pascal is neither legitimating the established order nor debunking it. He is specifying when authority becomes intolerable: when it crosses orders.

The consequence is that Pascal has a theory of the second best rather than of legitimacy. The question "is this law just?" is displaced by "does this authority demand love where it can supply only fear?" The difficulty I cannot resolve is methodological: these are copied, cut and rearranged fragments, published posthumously in 1670 and ordered differently by every modern editor, so it is unclear how much of the argument above is Pascal's and how much is an artefact of the sequence I have chosen to read them in.
