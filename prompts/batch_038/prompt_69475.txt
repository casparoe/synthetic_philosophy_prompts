Not an academic, just somebody who reads about this stuff late at night. I've been stuck on the Condorcet jury theorem lately — the one that says if every voter has a better-than-even chance of being right about a yes/no question, and they vote independently, then the majority's answer gets more and more likely to be correct as the group grows. It reads like a proof that groups are smart. Then I think about Challenger and it doesnt feel like it explains anything, and I can't quite say why, which is annoying.

Here's what I remember of the story, so correct me if I've got it wrong: January 1986, coldest launch morning there had ever been, engineers at the booster contractor tell NASA the night before that the rubber seals won't be safe in that kind of cold, NASA pushes back hard, the contractor's managers go off by themselves for a few minutes, come back and approve the launch, and the engineers who objected are not the ones signing anything. Shuttle breaks apart 73 seconds in. There's also a reported remark where one of the company's managers told another to stop thinking like an engineer and start thinking like a manager — I'm going from memory so the wording is probably off.

So run the theorem over that night as carefully as you can. Who exactly is the jury — the engineers, the managers, everyone on the phone call, or all three organizations together? What yes/no question was each of them actually answering, because I suspect "will these seals hold at freezing" and "should we fly tomorrow morning" are not the same question and the slide from one to the other is doing alot of the work. What was each person's competence, and did independence survive at all when everyone was reacting to pressure from the same customer on the same call? Then tell me plainly which features of the disaster the theorem really explains, which ones it can't even describe, and whether it was just never built for groups this small, where rank swamps the averaging.

After that: is there something better? I've half-read about a puzzle where a group votes on the reasons and gets one answer but votes on the conclusion and gets the opposite, and about something called the normalization of deviance. Say which of those — or anything else you think fits better — actually does more work here, and don't just pick one to keep things tidy.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind. Which consideration would make you say the jury theorem does explain Challenger after all, and where in what you just wrote are you most likely to be wrong? I'd rather see the weak point than get a confident answer.
