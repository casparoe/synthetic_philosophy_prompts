I have just finished six weeks of clearing my late uncle's house for probate, and the task that nearly cost me the contract was the schedule of contents the court required: a count of items, room by room. In the garage I had to decide whether the bicycle he stripped down every November and rebuilt every April was one item or twenty-six; whether a jar of forty unmatched screws and brackets was one item or forty; and whether the shelving unit he started in 1998, never finished and by all accounts never intended to finish, was an item at all. My colleague said the answers were obvious. I have stopped believing that, and I now suspect that the question of when some things compose a thing was silently doing all the work in that inventory while nobody was arguing about it.

Please write a dialogue of roughly 1,000 words between a mereological universalist and a mereological nihilist who have been asked to produce that garage inventory together and to sign off on a single item count.

Constraints I care about:

- Neither may be a crank. The nihilist must not collapse into denying that anything exists or that sentences about the garage can be true; the universalist must not get away with merely gesturing at arbitrariness in where anyone would draw a line.
- The nihilist has to show her work: offer explicit paraphrases of at least three ordinary claims about the room — that the bicycle is broken, that the shelving unit is unfinished, that there are more screws in the left jar than in the right — in terms of simples arranged a certain way. The universalist should then press on whether those paraphrases preserve what the claims were being used to say, or whether composition has been smuggled back in through the arrangement relation itself.
- The universalist has to face coincidence in that particular room: two objects apparently in one place at once, the shelving unit and the mere sum of its parts, and what that does to an item count that a court will act on.
- At least once, one of them should propose the intermediate view — that composition occurs only where the result is a life — as the position that actually gets the inventory right, and both should explain why they reject it rather than ignore it.
- Neither character may simply restate their thesis; each must concede one genuine point and say which of their own commitments the concession costs them.
- No tidy conversion at the end. They should either agree on a number for a reason both can accept, or state precisely which sub-question has to be settled first — and it should be clear to the reader which of the two is worse off.

Keep returning to the actual objects on the shelves; I want the metaphysics to be forced by the garage, not illustrated by it.
