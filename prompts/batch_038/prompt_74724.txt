I am drafting a paper on the epistemology of peer disagreement and would like detailed comments on the draft below. Please treat it as a genuine submission: I want criticism of the argument rather than encouragement, and I want to be told where it fails.

DRAFT — Disagreeing About the Question

1. Setup. The debate between conciliationist and steadfast positions is standardly staged on a case with a fixed architecture. You and an epistemic peer have reviewed the same body of evidence; you have come to believe P, they have come to believe not-P; the question is what you ought now to do with your credence in P. Conciliationists hold that learning of the disagreement requires substantial movement toward your peer's credence, in the limiting version giving the two judgments equal weight. Steadfast views deny this: on a total-evidence picture, the bare fact that a peer disagrees either lacks the weight to compel revision or is outweighed by your first-order grounds. Both camps then argue about the price of their view. Conciliationism is charged with spinelessness and with self-undermining, since its own recommendation is itself a disputed matter among its peers. Steadfastness is charged with arbitrary self-preference, since it licenses you to discount a person you have just described as your equal.

My claim is that this shared staging already settles something it should not. Both sides assume that the two agents are answering one and the same question, so that the only remaining issue is how to combine two answers to it. I argue that in a large and philosophically important class of cases the parties are not answering the same question, and not merely in the sense that their words carry different contents.

2. Partition mismatch. Take a judgment to be a location within a partition: a set of mutually exclusive and jointly exhaustive hypotheses that the agent treats as the live options, the uttered sentence reporting which cell she takes the world to occupy. Two agents can share a vocabulary, share the evidence, and be equally competent, sincere, and reflective, and still be locating the world within different partitions, because they have drawn different distinctions among the live options.

Consider two moral philosophers assessing the same act. My judgment is sensitive to a distinction between what the agent intended and what she merely foresaw; theirs does not draw that distinction, but is instead sensitive to whether the agent could reasonably have been expected to notice a risk, a distinction my partition collapses. Each of us is answering a well-posed question, and each answers it competently. But there is no single query to which my confidence of 0.8 and their confidence of 0.2 are both answers.

3. Consequence for conciliationism. If that is right, the conciliationist prescription is not merely costly but inapplicable. Averaging, halving the difference, and equal weighting are operations on numbers, and they presuppose that the numbers are attached to the same content. Under mismatch the resulting figure is attached to nothing: it is not a credence in any proposition the agent is in a position to entertain. I want to stress that this is a technical complaint rather than a moral one. It does not depend on the spinelessness or self-undermining charges, and it bites before those charges get a chance to run.

4. Positive proposal. The correct response to a mismatched-partition disagreement is not doxastic revision but partition negotiation: identifying which distinctions each side's judgment is tracking, and asking whether the finer partition is worth what it costs in the reliability of the judgments made within it. Partitions are instruments, and instruments trade resolution against reliability; two instruments can be differently calibrated without either being defective. Mismatch can therefore be reasonable on both sides even where there is a single true answer to the underlying substantive question. I take this to be a claim about the structure of inquiry rather than a variety of permissivism about credence.

5. Anticipated objection. The obvious reply is that such cases are not disagreements at all: if the questions differ, the parties are talking past each other, and the case falls outside the conciliationism debate rather than undermining it. I have two responses. First, the disagreement is real in every sense that carries dialectical and practical weight — the parties contradict one another, would coordinate differently, and each takes the other to be making a mistake; what is missing is only the assumption of a shared partition. Second, and more importantly, the conciliationist who restricts her view to shared-partition cases owes a procedure for determining, from inside a disagreement, whether she is in such a case. The familiar tests — same evidence, equal intelligence and sincerity, mutual awareness of the argument — do not discriminate, since all of them can be satisfied under mismatch.

6. Conclusion. The debate has been conducted over a special case. The general question, namely how to compare and combine answers to different questions, is asked by neither camp.

END DRAFT

The comments I want, in this order:

1. Accuracy of the framing in section 1. Is that a fair statement of the two positions and of the standard objections to each, or have I caricatured one of them? In particular: is it true that conciliationist prescriptions are standardly formulated as operations on numbers attached to a single proposition, or can the equal-weight idea be restated so that it does not presuppose a shared partition — for example by appealing to the total evidence, or by locating the disagreement at some coarser proposition that both partitions refine? If such a restatement exists, give it explicitly and say whether section 3 survives it.

2. The common-refinement reply. Someone will object that any two partitions have a join, so the parties can always move to the coarsest partition refining both, at which point averaging is well defined again and my complaint reduces to a remark about how hard the refinement is to reach. Is that fatal? If it is not, help me state precisely what residue of the objection survives it. I suspect the residue concerns the cost of the refinement and which refinements are cognitively available to a situated agent, but I have not been able to get it clean.

3. Collapse worries. Does section 2 collapse into either of two diagnoses already on the market: (a) the verbal-dispute diagnosis, on which the parties attach different contents to the same words and can be separated by a method of elimination, and (b) permissivism, on which one body of evidence licenses more than one doxastic response? For each, say whether the collapse succeeds and, if so, point to the sentence in my text that licenses it. Characterize both positions in your own terms rather than by attribution, and tell me what work my notion of a partition is doing that theirs is not.

4. The example. Does the intended-versus-foreseen and reasonable-foreseeability case genuinely illustrate two different partitions, or does it illustrate two different weightings of one partition, in which case section 2 loses its only support? If it fails, propose a replacement example, preferably from philosophy rather than from the empirical sciences, and say what makes it better.

5. Sections 4 and 5. Is the resolution-versus-reliability tradeoff coherent as I have stated it, or am I leaning on an instrumentalist metaphor that cannot carry the argument? Separately: is the objection anticipated in section 5 the strongest one available, or is there a better objection I have omitted? If there is a better one, state it and give the best reply I could make.

6. Overall assessment. Tell me whether this is a thesis or a distinction in search of a problem. If it is the latter, say what would have to be true for it to become the former, and identify the one section whose deletion would cost the draft the least.

Please organize your reply as numbered comments keyed to the sections above, quoting the sentence you are commenting on wherever that makes the comment clearer, and close with a short paragraph of overall assessment. Do not pad: if a comment amounts to the draft being fine as it stands, say so in one line and move on.
