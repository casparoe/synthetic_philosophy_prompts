I am a novelist, some four hundred pages into a third book, and I have run up against a difficulty that is less a matter of craft than of moral psychology, so I am bringing it to you rather than to my agent. Two of my characters do something I can describe vividly but cannot name. The first is a man whose marriage has been finished for years in every way a spectator could measure; he has arranged his week — the late office, the standing Thursday fixture, the habit of taking calls in the kitchen — so that he is never alone with his wife long enough for a certain question to become available to him. He does not tell himself the marriage is sound. He tells himself nothing whatever on the subject. Put the question to him directly and he will answer, and answer accurately, in the register of a man mildly surprised by it; the machinery is intact, the input has simply never arrived. My second character is a mother who has ordered her household so that no tally of hours exists to be kept: not a suppressed tally, not a tally she keeps and then discards, but no tally at all. Each individual decision that sustains the arrangement is innocent on its own terms, and she would in good faith deny having decided anything; yet the shape of the life is legible from outside, the way a moat is legible to someone who never watched it dug.

I know the neighbourhood. I have read enough about self-deception to be wary of its standard puzzles, about how a person is supposed both to know and not to know; I have Sartre's bad faith and Orwell's doublethink in mind; and I understand that in the law there is a doctrine concerning the defendant who suspected something and chose not to look. None of these appears to be my case. In the puzzles, and in bad faith, and in doublethink, there is a belief, or a pair of beliefs, doing the work, and the difficulty is how they manage to coexist. In the legal doctrine there is a suspicion that the person declines to confirm. What my characters do is quieter: there is no false belief to be had, because the proposition never enters, and no suspicion, since suspicion would already amount to a kind of knowing. Nor are they repressing anything, if repression means material that was once present and has since been driven down; here nothing has ever been present. They manage their circumstances so that a question is never put, and in the absence of the question there is nothing for them to get wrong.

So: does this have a name? I want the specific concept, not the general area, and I shall not be satisfied by a label alone — I want to know where its edges are. Tell me which established idea comes closest, and say precisely how my case departs from each candidate, whether by degree or by kind. If there is genuinely no concept for it, say so plainly and then coin one: define it, and give me the boundary cases — the person who has the thought once a year and dismisses it, the person who cannot entertain it because of trauma rather than arrangement, the person who has delegated the question to a spouse or an accountant, the person whose ignorance is imposed from outside rather than curated — and tell me which of those fall inside. And I would rather have your verdict than your inventory. Take a position on whether what my characters do counts as self-deception at all, or as something that sits beside it, and give me the consideration you find decisive; if you think I have described a confusion rather than a phenomenon, say that instead, and show me where the description breaks.
