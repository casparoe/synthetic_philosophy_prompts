I'm serving on a seven-person working group that our city council asked to advise on whether the city should formally apologize for an early-1960s highway project that razed roughly nine hundred homes in a predominantly Black neighborhood on the east side. The council wants a recommendation before the end of its term, and the group includes two historians, a city attorney, a retired council member, a housing economist, and a representative of the neighborhood association that has kept the grievance alive across three generations. There is broad agreement that the facts are not seriously in dispute and that something ought to be said publicly; the real disagreement is about who exactly is doing the saying, and why they are the ones who have to say it. At our last meeting one member — a former law professor, easily the most articulate person in the room — offered an analogy that effectively ended the evening's argument, and I want to know whether it deserved to.

Her version went roughly like this. When you buy a building, you take it subject to the encumbrances already on the title: an easement a previous owner granted, a mechanics' lien for work a previous owner commissioned and never paid for, a covenant that runs with the land. Nobody thinks the new owner is a bad person on account of any of these, and nobody thinks she signed anything herself; the obligation attaches to the property, not to the character of whoever happens to hold it at the moment. She cannot say the boiler is not her problem, because the tenant's claim was never about the boiler-breaker's soul — it was about the building. And crucially, she took the rent roll: the income stream she now enjoys was built partly by the very transaction that created the encumbrance. So the city, she said, is a landlord who acquired a portfolio with liens on it; the apology is the acknowledgment of the lien, and the housing trust fund some of us want attached to it is the payment. Residents who object that their families were not even in this country in 1962 are making the purchaser's mistake — trying to take the rents and refuse the encumbrances — and the objection dissolves once you see that the city is the same title-holder now as it was then, however much turnover there has been in the people occupying its offices.

Our youngest member pushed back with what I think is the sharpest counter, and he stayed deliberately inside her analogy: property law also recognizes the bona fide purchaser — someone who buys for value, searches the record, finds nothing, and therefore takes free of unrecorded interests. His point was that most current residents are in something like that position: they bought in at market price, they did not receive the neighborhood for free, and the appreciation they captured is not the same thing as receiving the proceeds of the original taking. If the landlord analogy is correct, he said, it may exempt precisely the people it was meant to capture. I'd like you to audit the analogy itself rather than referee that exchange. Lay out the correspondence explicitly — what in the city plays the role of the title, the building, the easement, the lien, the rent roll, the tenant, the registry, the act of purchase — and then separate the mismatches that actually bear on the use she is making of it from the ones that are harmless decoration. In particular: does the analogy smuggle in the idea that the obligation runs to or through a *thing* rather than to persons, some of whom may no longer be around to receive anything, and whose descendants may have no standing that a tenant's assignee would have? Does the absence of a registry — no determinate quantum, no recorded date, no counterparty entitled to enforce — matter for what an apology can honestly promise as opposed to gesture at? And is the identity claim (the city now is the city then) doing independent work, or is it merely a restatement of the benefit claim, such that if you grant the benefits you never needed the identity and if you deny the benefits the identity buys you nothing? Then tell me whether each of the two conclusions survives on its own — that an apology is owed, and that it must carry a funded material component — because I suspect the analogy supports them to very different degrees. Finally, if it fails, is there a better one: something the group could actually put in a public document that makes the "I wasn't there" objection feel confused rather than merely overruled, without importing whatever this one imports.
