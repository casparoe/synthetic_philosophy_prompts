I spent forty years as a quantitative geneticist — first in livestock breeding, later advising on selection programmes for a seed company — and since retiring I have taken up philosophy with more time than sense. The literature I keep returning to is that on so-called liberal eugenics, which I find professionally absorbing and, in one respect, philosophically careless. I would like you to write me an essay that settles a question in that literature. Please argue to a verdict; a balanced survey of the positions would be of no use to me at all.

The starting point is Julian Savulescu's principle of procreative beneficence, which he states as the claim that "couples (or single reproducers) should select the child, of the possible children they could have, who is expected to have the best life, or at least as good a life as the others, based on the relevant, available information." Savulescu and Kahane later softened this into an obligation only where the expected net benefit is significant. I want to press on a different softening, one that comes out of my own trade rather than out of the papers.

In a breeding programme one never selects on the trait itself. One selects on an index — a ranking with a measurable reliability — and the expected gain is the product of the selection differential and the accuracy of that index. Where accuracy is poor, gain is small but not zero, and the breeder simply tolerates a slower rate of progress. Now, what actually reaches prospective parents today is of this second kind: polygenic scores that rank embryos, whose predictive power for any given life-outcome is modest and whose transferability across populations is worse. Savulescu's principle, by contrast, is phrased entirely in terms of an expectation about a whole life, with no apparent place for the reliability of the instrument by which the expectation was formed beyond the words "available information."

So consider a proposal you may call the principle of procreative proportionality: the stringency of any obligation to select the best-ranked embryo is proportional to the predictive reliability of the index used to rank it, and below some threshold of reliability the obligation does not merely weaken but lapses, leaving a bare permission. Stipulate two cases and reason through them. In the first, a clinic can rank ten viable embryos by an index that accounts for a few percent of variance in a broadly desirable trait. In the second, the same ranking is available but the index is, ex hypothesi, near-perfectly predictive of that trait. I want the essay to answer the following, in whatever order makes for the best argument:

Is proportionality a substantive normative thesis, or a trivial evidential caveat — a restatement of the platitude that one cannot be obliged to act on information one does not have? If it is trivial, say so plainly, and then say what would have to be added to make it non-trivial.

Must the lapse be sharp or can it be gradual? A threshold looks arbitrary, and arbitrariness in a principle governing who is brought into existence is not a small defect. A gradual lapse, however, seems to leave Savulescu's principle merely weakened rather than replaced, which is not what I intend. Which horn is survivable, and why?

In these cases the choice determines which particular person exists, so no candidate child can complain of having been ranked second. Does proportionality require a different account of what the obligation is owed to, or against whom it can be wrong, than the impersonal comparison the principle seems to assume?

Most importantly: the objection associated with Habermas — that a person whose endowment was deliberately designed cannot understand herself as the undivided author of her own life, and that this erodes the symmetry of relations among free and equal persons — is usually advanced as a claim about the meaning of intervention rather than its causal efficacy. Does it survive contact with what we know about effect sizes, or is it parasitic on an implicit determinism that no working geneticist would accept? Or does it hold even in the weak-index case, on the ground that what matters is the parents' intention rather than the upshot? I regard this as the crux, and I want it decided rather than noted.

Finally, address whether any of this is disturbed by the view, defended by some philosophers of disability, that many impairments are not in themselves features that make a life worse — and whether that view, if accepted, changes the analysis of ranking as such or only of the traits it is legitimate to rank on.

Conclude by stating whether the principle of procreative proportionality should be adopted, adopted in amended form, or rejected in favour of something like the Savulescu–Kahane revision, and justify that conclusion. Please do not append a bibliography or a list of further reading; I have the papers. Argue from the concepts and the cases.
