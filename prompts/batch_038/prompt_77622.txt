I'm on the Clinical Prediction Model Review Panel at a mid-sized regional health system — six of us, and I'm the token ethicist, which mostly means I get asked to write things up when the two statisticians on the panel stop listening to each other. That's happened again and I've been volunteered to circulate a decision memo before Thursday's meeting. Here's the situation. We have to decide whether to approve a vendor's gradient-boosted readmission-risk model for a two-hospital pilot, or stick with the homegrown logistic regression our analytics team built. On our held-out 2019–2023 sample the vendor model wins by about .02 AUROC, but all its features are locked inside an engineered pipeline nobody here can fully audit, whereas the internal model's predictors are things a hospitalist can look at and object to. Our standing policy makes the benchmark comparison a gate: whichever model cross-validates better on our own data gets deployed, full stop. One of our statisticians now wants to kill that gate, and he's arguing it with the no-free-lunch theorem. He keeps repeating a line he picked up off a conference slide about hyperparameter tuning — something about how any two optimization algorithms are equivalent when their performance is averaged across all possible problems — and from there he goes: averaging over all possible target functions is what makes the theorem true, so a benchmark win on our finite historical sample licenses no inference whatsoever about future patients, so ranking models by held-out performance is numerology dressed up as science, and we should instead choose on interpretability plus a written argument for why this particular problem is learnable. The other statistician says that's a parlor trick, that NFL is a trivial counting result about a hypothetical universe of all functions that has zero purchase on our actual question, and that dropping the benchmark gate would hand deployment decisions to whoever tells the most reassuring story.

Where it gets muddier: our own policy manual, section 4.2, already leans on NFL. It's the stated rationale for requiring each modeling team to submit "a plain-language argument for why the structure of this problem licenses inductive inference beyond the sample we have," and the vendor's submission basically says NFL doesn't apply to them because their algorithm was selected for tabular clinical data. So both camps have a piece of the theorem on their side, and there's also a live complication I keep raising and everyone talks over: our discharge criteria changed in mid-2024, so the distribution the benchmark measures isn't quite the distribution the pilot will run on, which feels like the real inductive problem here and doesn't seem to be what either of them is arguing about. Some people also insist NFL is just the formal version of Hume — that it settles the problem of induction by proving it — and others say it's the opposite, that it quietly assumes the very uniformity of nature that Hume is accused of leaving unjustified. I genuinely can't tell which of these is a deep point and which is a slogan being used as a conversation-ender, and I don't want to circulate a memo that just splits the difference to be polite.

So: draft the memo I'll send around Wednesday night. It needs an actionable bottom line — approve the vendor model, keep the internal one, or approve with conditions that are actually specified, plus a recommendation on whether section 4.2 and the benchmark gate survive as written — and it needs the reasoning behind it in terms the other five panel members can push back on rather than wave at. Please take the NFL argument apart properly: what the theorem does and doesn't establish, whether the version he's quoting even covers prediction rather than search or optimization, whether it says anything about a single real problem instance versus an average over all conceivable ones, and whether it gives Hume's skeptical challenge any new force or just restates it with subscripts. Then tell me what the panel should require of modeling teams instead of, or in addition to, a leaderboard number, and give me two or three criteria a non-expert like me could actually apply to the next proposal without needing to referee a philosophy paper. Give the losing side its strongest hearing — if dropping the benchmark gate really is defensible, say so and say under what conditions — but land somewhere and defend it.
