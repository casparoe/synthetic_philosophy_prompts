A consumer platform I use offers three things side by side in its account settings. A user can arrange for their profile to be frozen and labelled a memorial on their death, with all content preserved and friends invited to post tributes; or can instruct the platform to delete everything permanently; or can authorise a conversational replica fine-tuned on their own messages and posts, which the bereaved may continue to talk to — a replica whose licence permits the family and the platform to run separate instances of it simultaneously. Elsewhere the same company treats the death as terminating every contract, obligation, and legal capacity the user had. What picture of what a person is, and of what survival consists in, does this bundle presuppose? I have been reading Parfit on the teletransporter, and it makes me suspect these are not merely three products but three rival theories of what matters. Please reconstruct the implicit theory: what does the frozen memorial strictly presuppose that the replica does not, and vice versa; which accounts of identity does each quietly rule out; and is licensing several simultaneous replicas coherent with the way the platform treats the deceased person everywhere else? Then tell me where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — licensed equally well by several theories of survival — and what these features would have to look like if the company adopted the view that identity is not what matters, only psychological continuity with the right kind of cause.
