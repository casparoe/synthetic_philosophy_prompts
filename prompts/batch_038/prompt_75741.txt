Last night a friend and I spent four hours on transformative experience and arrived somewhere neither of us intended. She is not a specialist, which may be why she reached for the blunt version of the problem; I have since written her argument out as carefully as I can, and although its conclusion struck me as unacceptable at the time, I am now unsure whether my objection is anything more than taste. I should like an adjudication.

Her argument, reconstructed:

1. On the account developed in L. A. Paul's *Transformative Experience*, some experiences are epistemically transformative: you cannot know what they would be like for you until you have had them.
2. Choosing rationally among options requires that you be able to assess what each outcome would be like for you, since that assessment is what supplies the utilities being weighed.
3. So where an option's outcome is an epistemically transformative experience, you cannot choose rationally on the basis of expected outcomes.
4. But every option whose outcome includes an experience you have not yet had is in that position: a first child, a first war, a bereavement you did not foresee, an emigration, a conversion, a diagnosis, and equally a first taste of something unfamiliar or a first night in a strange city. Novelty comes in degrees, and nothing in the framework marks a principled cutoff.
5. So outcome-based rational choice fails across a very wide range of ordinary decisions — arguably most of the ones that matter most.
6. Were that so, we would expect to observe it: agents facing such choices would either stall or concede that they were leaping rather than deciding.
7. Instead they deliberate fluently, cite considerations, revise their minds for reasons, and afterwards report that the deliberation tracked something — parents who agonized and were glad, converts who tested a creed before committing to it, patients who weighed a treatment and stand by their weighing.
8. A theory of choice that pronounces a great deal of visibly successful deliberation impossible is not a deep theory but a false one.
9. Therefore either (2) is wrong, or (1) applies to far fewer cases than the framework's own showcase examples suggest — and on either disjunct there is no distinctive problem here.

Two further moves belong in the record, and I want them examined as well. When I objected at (4) — that tasting a durian is not on all fours with becoming a parent — she asked me for a criterion, and the best I could produce was what I have been calling the discordance test: an experience is transformative for you just in case competent, sincere, and articulate people who have had it disagree radically about what it was like. She replied that the test must misfire precisely in the cases that matter, because testimony about a transformative experience comes from someone the experience has already remade; the reporter is not the person who faced the choice, so her report cannot tell you what facing it would have been like from where you now stand. Discordance on this account is evidence only that reporters changed, and agreement would be equally uninformative. I had nothing to offer at two in the morning.

What troubles me is something I cannot state cleanly: (5) reads as plausible and behaves as absurd the moment I try to act on it. Tell me which step you would reject, why that step looked innocent going in, and what the rejection costs — including whether the framework can simply bite the bullet at (7) and concede that most weighty deliberation is not rational in the outcome-based sense, or whether that concession collapses into the charge that the theory is unfalsifiable, since any evidence of successful deliberation can be re-described as a leap. Say also whether the discordance test can be repaired against her objection, and if it cannot, whether her objection disposes only of the test or of the more modest claim that some experiences are transformative while others are merely new.

Of these claims, (1) and (7) are the ones I am least willing to surrender. I hold (2) more loosely, though I do not know what I would put in its place.
