I am a third-year student in an aquaculture science programme, and this term I am also taking an applied ethics elective. Over the summer I worked at a coastal farm raising Pacific whiteleg shrimp in indoor tanks, and the owner has asked me to help him think through next season's stocking plan — partly, I suspect, because he knows I have been reading about animal welfare.

Here is the situation. We currently stock the tanks at roughly 300 animals per cubic metre and run three production cycles a year, which comes to about seven million shrimp harvested annually. At that density our survival rate is around 65 per cent, growth is slow, and most of the veterinarian's visits concern disease and shell damage. A trial we ran this year at half the density showed survival nearer 85 per cent, faster growth, and far fewer injured animals. His own welfare scoring sheet — the one the buyer requires — puts the animals' quality of life at about four out of ten at the high density and six out of ten at the low density. Because the tanks and the delivery schedule are fixed, halving the density would roughly halve the harvest; he allows that the better survival would put the figure somewhat above half, and settles on three and a half million.

He has done the arithmetic and considers the decision closed. Seven million animals at four, he says, is more welfare than three and a half million at six, so the dense plan produces more of what actually matters. When I said that every single animal is worse off under that plan, he replied that the shrimp which would have existed under the low-density plan are not harmed by never existing, so only the total can count. He added that if we shrink, the buyer will simply source from a denser farm elsewhere, and those animals will be produced anyway in worse conditions.

I should mention one complication: our lecturer has stressed that the evidence for sentience in penaeid shrimp is genuine but much thinner than it is for fish, and that there is no settled method for converting that difference into a weight. So I am not even sure the numbers on his sheet can be multiplied together as confidently as he does.

Something in his reasoning still strikes me as wrong, and I cannot tell whether that is a real objection or squeamishness. Is the argument sound? If it is not, where precisely does it break down — and is there a version of his arithmetic that survives the objection, since I do not want to dismiss a serious point merely because I dislike its conclusion? Finally, what should I actually put in my recommendation to him for next season? I owe him a decision, not a seminar.
