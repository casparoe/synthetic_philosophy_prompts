You are to write as a historian of mathematics and political thought who also has serious technical competence in social choice theory — someone equally at home in the French Enlightenment and in the mid-twentieth-century formal literature on preference aggregation. Please think carefully before answering, and work through the tasks in the order given rather than leaping to a verdict; in this case the obvious answer is probably not the interesting one.

The context is that I am an undergraduate in a seminar on the intellectual history of democratic theory, and my final assignment is a counterfactual reconstruction. I want your best reasoned reconstruction of a person's likely views, not a survey of what commentators have said about him, and not a hedge about the impossibility of knowing.

The scenario. Suppose the Marquis de Condorcet (1743–1794) were restored to life in full possession of the mathematical ability and the political convictions he held at the end of his life, and were handed a careful statement of Arrow's impossibility theorem in its 1951 form: over a finite set of at least three alternatives, there is no social welfare function that simultaneously satisfies unrestricted domain, unanimity, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship while always returning a complete and transitive social ordering. He is a first-rate mathematician, so assume he works through the proof himself and accepts it as correct. Alongside the theorem he is shown three further things: that the pairwise procedure he himself advocated fails independence of irrelevant alternatives, since the entry of a third candidate who cannot win may reverse the outcome between the other two; that the impossibility evaporates if the admissible profiles are restricted, for instance to single-peaked preferences; and that it can be sidestepped altogether by abandoning ordinal ballots in favor of rated or cardinal ones.

Task one: brainstorm before committing. List five or six genuinely distinct responses available to him — not six phrasings of the same response. Give each a short label and one or two sentences of exposition. Include at least one response on which he treats the theorem as a restatement of the cycling paradox he had already identified himself, and at least one on which he treats it as changing the subject away from the question he actually cared about.

Task two: commit. Which of these, or which combination, would he in fact adopt? Ground your prediction in his documented commitments rather than in generic Enlightenment character traits: his conviction that an assembly of independently judging individuals, each more likely than not to be right, converges on truth as its size grows; his criticisms of Borda's point-count; his confidence in the indefinite perfectibility of the human mind; his preference for a tiered, representative institutional order over direct mass voting; his ambition to make political science quantitative. Say explicitly which of these commitments is doing the most work in your prediction, and which one you think a biographer would object to your relying on.

Task three: the sharp question. Does Arrow's theorem address Condorcet's question at all? His central probabilistic result concerns a binary proposition put to a body of competent judges, and he appears to have thought of elections partly as a procedure for combining noisy estimates of a candidate's merit; Arrow's result concerns the construction of a transitive ordering over many alternatives from ordinal ballots alone. Would Condorcet accept the theorem as a refutation of his project, as a generalization of a difficulty he had already located, or as a formalization of a question he never asked? Take a definite position and argue for it.

Task four: consequences. After absorbing all of this, what would he recommend? Would he continue to defend his pairwise procedure, convert to some rated method, restrict attention to institutional designs that make cyclic profiles rare, or relocate his hopes entirely into deliberation, representation, and the education of judges and voters? And what would unsettle him more: the theorem, or the uses to which it has been put?

Throughout, mark clearly which elements of your reconstruction follow from positions he is on record holding and which are speculative extrapolation. Please do not append a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I cannot verify references and they would add nothing here. Aim for an account specific enough that I could defend it in seminar and committed enough to be wrong about him.
