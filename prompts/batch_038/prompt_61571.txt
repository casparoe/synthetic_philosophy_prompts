Compose a satirical document in the house style of a national government delivery plan: the Republic of Vestravia's *Life Evaluation Improvement Programme: Delivery Plan, Years One to Six*, issued by a newly created Minister of State for Aggregate Self-Report. Vestravia is an invented mid-sized country that currently sits forty-first in the World Happiness Report's national ranking and has been instructed by cabinet to reach the top ten. Aim for roughly 1,000–1,200 words, in numbered sections with headings: a short theory-of-change paragraph, five or six named workstreams each with an owner and a measurable indicator, a risk register with three entries, and a one-page annex titled "Measures Considered and Not Adopted."

The target of the satire is the practice of governing to a self-report index — not any real government, person, or the report's authors, who should be treated throughout as blameless technicians. Every joke must come from the measurement being right rather than from bureaucratic cliché, so build the plan on these true features of the instrument, deploying them without explaining them to the reader: the ranking is nothing more than the national average of a single zero-to-ten life-evaluation question, in which respondents place themselves on a ladder running from the worst possible life to the best possible life; the question deliberately avoids the words happiness, satisfaction, and well-being precisely so that it translates cleanly, which forecloses one obvious avenue of manipulation and visibly disappoints the minister; scores are pooled over three years from roughly a thousand respondents per year in each country; the six variables used to account for differences between countries (income, social support, healthy life expectancy, freedom, absence of corruption, generosity) never enter the score at all — they are fitted afterwards against a hypothetical benchmark country assembled from the world's lowest national averages; and both scores and ranks carry confidence intervals, so neighbouring countries in the table are frequently indistinguishable from one another.

The workstreams should accordingly aim at the survey rather than at life. Include: at least one measure that a reader recoils at — denominator management, control over who receives the interviewer's call, the treatment of persistent low scorers — presented as uncontroversial and costed to two decimal places; at least one flagship measure that is, on the minister's own stated logic, entirely inert, because it improves an explanatory variable rather than the ladder mean, with the national statistician's dry demurral confined to a footnote; a section on cross-national comparability, covering a "ladder literacy" campaign, the discovery that a neighbour's respondents simply use the top rungs more freely, and the minister's refusal to draw the obvious conclusion from that discovery; and a timeline shaped honestly by the three-year pooling, which lets the minister arrange for a successor to inherit either the credit or the shortfall. The annex should list and dismiss, in a line or two each, the alternatives an economist would press — day-to-day affect questions, the median rather than the mean, the distribution of life evaluations, multidimensional indices — for reasons that are technically sound and self-indicting. The risk register must include the risk that the programme succeeds and the country becomes less pleasant to live in, scored as low likelihood and low impact, with mitigation.

Hold the deadpan register throughout and never name the objection the piece is making: the reader should conclude, from the plan's own arithmetic and its sincere tone, that the ranking has stopped tracking anything. It should be funny to someone who has read the methodological appendix and readable to someone who has not. Include no citations or reading list, and put no wording from the actual survey instrument in quotation marks — paraphrase it.
