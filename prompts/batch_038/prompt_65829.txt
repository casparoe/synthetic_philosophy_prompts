Reading group thing I can't get out of. We were working through the unilateralist's curse — the Bostrom-et-al. paper where a bunch of well-meaning agents can each undertake (or spoil) some initiative on their own, and because everyone's estimate of its value is noisy and independent, the initiative gets undertaken way too often. Moral version of the winner's curse: whoever ends up acting is disproportionately likely to be the one who overestimated.

Someone in the group pushed it further and half of us found the result convincing, which is the worrying part:

(1) In a unilateralist situation, the agent who acts is the one whose private estimate came out highest, and that estimate is systematically too high.

(2) So when an initiative looks clearly worth doing to me, that fact is itself evidence that I'm the outlier, and I should mark my estimate down by whatever amount depends on how many others were in a position to act.

(3) If I mark down by the full amount, anything that looked attractive only because I was optimistic about it stops looking attractive.

(4) And there's no principled place to stop the markdown, because the same selection effect applies to every judgment I'm leaning on, not just the first-order one: how many other agents there are, whether their errors really are independent of mine, whether this is even a unilateralist situation, whether the downside is real. Each of those is another noisy estimate, and I'm again the one whose whole set came out most favorable, so each of them gets marked down too.

(5) Those markdowns compound, so what survives is far lower than any single discount would suggest.

(6) Refraining isn't exposed to any of this. Abstaining doesn't require my estimates to be right, since nothing turns on them — I do nothing, nothing happens, and no error of mine gets realized.

(7) So in any situation where others could act, I should abstain unless the case for acting survives a markdown applied to everything I'm relying on, including the judgment that it's safe to act.

(8) That's very close to never, and it swallows exactly the acts that look like pure upside — warning people about a risk, publishing the result, being first to donate, blowing the whistle. Which is what you'd predict, since widely shared attractiveness is the signature of a curse case rather than an escape from one.

(9) And it swallows arguing for the principle, since telling everyone to conform is itself an initiative I'd be undertaking alone, on my own estimate that conformity is good.

Two of us think (9) is a feature — the rule silences itself, everyone just stops, no paradox left over. I think thats a reductio but I can't say where the break is. I'd really like to keep (1); it seems to be the actual content of the curse and I dont want to lose the winner's-curse analogy. And I'd like to keep something in the neighborhood of (7) as a habit, just not as a demonstration that I should never do anything at all.

So which step goes? Why did it look fine on the way down, and what do I actually pay for dropping it — is there a curse left standing after the repair, or does the whole thing deflate into "be humble about your own judgment," which I didn't need nine premises to be told?
