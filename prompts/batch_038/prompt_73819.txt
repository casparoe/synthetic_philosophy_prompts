My office is throwing a retirement dinner for a guy I worked under for twelve years — thirty-four years in the foreign service, nearly all of it in the unglamorous middle of conflicts that other people assume end in one dramatic signing. About ninety people: two dozen are people he sat across tables from, including two who were on the other side of a border commission in the nineties and are now in the room as friends; the rest are his wife, three grown kids, one nine-year-old grandkid who will be asleep before the mains, and a pile of very junior officers who know him only as the man who keeps a tin of butterscotch in his bottom drawer and who will stop a meeting to correct your use of the word leverage. I'm giving the speech. Five, six minutes, call it 800 words.

The thing that makes this hard: he's a Kant person, but specifically the perpetual peace essay and nothing else, and he has talked my ear off about it since I was a third secretary. He builds 1:700 scale models of container ships — never warships, not one — and his stock line about why, roughly, is that trade doesn't stop wars, it just makes them more expensive, and that's the only mechanism in the world that reliably does anything. Which is basically his version of the bit near the end of the essay about the commercial spirit being unable to coexist with war. He spent four years of his life on a river-crossing protocol and another two on a fertilizer tariff schedule and considers both of those more load-bearing for peace than any summit he attended.

So I want the speech to be built out of that essay's actual architecture, not decorated with it, and here's what I mean. Use at least three of these as structure: the point that peace is not a natural condition and has to be instituted, over and over, which means I cannot end on he made the world a calmer place; the point that the problem of founding a state can be solved even by a people of devils provided they have enough understanding, which means the praise has to be aimed at what he designed so that it would keep working even if he'd been a scoundrel, rather than at his goodness — and that has to somehow still be warm in a room containing his wife; the preliminary article that voids any peace treaty carrying a tacit reservation for some future war; and the publicity test, roughly that a maxim affecting other people's rights is unjust if it couldn't survive being stated openly. That last one has to bind the speech itself, since a farewell speech is the one occasion where a diplomat is being genuinely public about a career that was mostly conducted in rooms, so no wink that only the negotiators can catch, no joke at a named government's expense, nothing whose real job is to retract what it appears to assert. That's the treaty-of-the-evening rule: no secret reservations.

Ground rules. Don't quote Kant — I don't trust anyone's memory of the wording, mine included, so paraphrase, and say it as an idea rather than as a sentence in quotation marks; name him at most twice. No citations, no bibliography, no as he writes in the second supplement. Don't invent achievements or anecdotes beyond what I gave you above; you can use the butterscotch, the model ships, the river protocol, the tariff schedule, and the leverage correction, and you should probably use at least the ships and the tin, since those are the two things the family will recognize. It must not turn into a lecture, and a nine-year-old's grandmother should be able to follow every sentence even if she'll never know why half of them are doing double duty.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, no labelled sections, just the speech running start to finish the way you'd write a letter. Then, after it, a paragraph or two in the same plain prose telling me which stretches you built for the people who know the material and which for the family, which of the four structural points you actually used, and where you think it's most likely to land wrong in the room.
