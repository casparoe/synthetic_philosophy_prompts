Retired after 35 years in renal physiology, only recently reading philosophy seriously, so be patient with me. I stumbled onto Berkeley's pamphlet of several hundred numbered questions about the Irish economy (published in the 1730s, sketching among other things a national bank) and I'm hooked — partly because it reads like transport physiology with money instead of filtrate. He asks whether money is useful only so far as it "stirreth up industry," whether "less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating," and whether the true idea of money is that of a "ticket or counter" whose material makes no difference.

That ticket line has escaped into my family. My daughter's boyfriend is deep into crypto and at dinner he argued Berkeley is basically on his side: money is just a ticket, the material doesnt matter, so you don't need a bank or a sovereign behind it, only a ledger everyone accepts — and he thought it funny that the bishop nonetheless designed a national bank, which he took as Berkeley failing to follow his own logic. A woman in my reading group uses the same line the other way: if money is only tickets, then inflation is just rewriting the numbers printed on them, and nothing real is lost provided everyone's numbers get rewritten together.

Could you audit the analogy properly? I want the correspondence table — what plays the ticket, the game, the prizes, the issuer/gamemaster, the ledger, the moment of redemption — and tell me if any of those slots comes up empty in an actual economy, because I suspect at least one does. Then the disanalogies, sorted into ones that genuinely threaten one of those two conclusions and ones that are merely decorative. Then what the ticket picture quietly imports: that the prize stock is fixed and independent of how many tickets exist, that tickets get spent rather than held, that issuing them is costless and neutral, that there is always a game in progress. Finally, say plainly whther the crypto conclusion and the harmless-inflation conclusion survive the audit, and which of the two the analogy was actually carrying all along.

Two more things. My own field has candidates and I'd like them pressure-tested: is money better figured as ATP — a turnover currency, barely stored, its whole value in the cycling — or as a catalyst, not consumed, speeding up reactions that would otherwise crawl, saturable beyond some point? Tell me what each would license and where each breaks, or propose a better one. And a hunch I can't check on my own: is the ticket move the same move Berkeley makes about language, that a sign can do its work without any clear idea standing behind it, or am I reading my own connection into him?
