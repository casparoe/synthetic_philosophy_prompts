I have no academic training in this subject and never went to university; I have spent the last four years reading about computability on my own, in the evenings, and I have arrived at a problem I cannot solve with the material available to me. It concerns the argument over whether the Church–Turing thesis could be false of the physical world — whether there might be machines that solve problems no Turing machine can.

What troubles me is not any single argument but the shape of the whole dispute. For roughly thirty years, people have produced proposals for devices that would outstrip a Turing machine: a computer placed along an endless worldline whose result is received by an observer falling into a specially arranged region of spacetime; neural networks whose behaviour depends on weights given as exact real numbers with infinitely many digits; machines permitted to run through transfinite stages rather than stopping after finitely many. And for each proposal, someone else has said that it is not a counterexample at all, either because it smuggles in an idealisation no physical system could satisfy, or because what the device is doing should not be called computing in the first place, or because no finite, fallible observer could ever tell whether it had worked. Neither side ever seems to lose. I have read people on both sides saying, with complete confidence, that the other side has simply failed to understand the question.

My own suspicion, which I hold only as a hypothesis and cannot defend, is that there are two different claims being run together under one name. Call them reachability and usability. Reachability asks whether the laws of physics permit a process whose outcome is a value that no Turing machine could produce. Usability asks whether anyone could ever operate such a process, get the value out of it, and be entitled to believe the answer. My guess is that one camp is only ever talking about reachability, the other only ever about usability, and that the genuine disagreement — the place where the same question is being answered in opposite ways — is much smaller than the volume of argument suggests. Relatedly, I keep running into an objection that hypercomputation requires infinite precision, infinite time, or infinitely many components, and a reply that the ordinary Turing machine requires an infinitely long tape, so the objection proves far too much. That exchange in particular seems to go nowhere, and I do not know whether it goes nowhere because it is unanswerable or because both sides mean different things by an idealisation being physically legitimate.

What I would like is a diagnosis, not a verdict on whether hypercomputation is possible. Is this a substantive disagreement about physics, a verbal one about what "compute" and "effective method" mean, a clash of standards over what would count as evidence that a device had solved an unsolvable problem, two groups answering different questions under a single heading, or a sign that the question as usually posed is ill-formed? Please engage the positions as I have described them rather than surveying the field. Then tell me what, on your diagnosis, would count as progress: a distinction, a theorem, a thought experiment that both camps must answer, a reformulation of the question — or whether no progress should be expected, and why not.
