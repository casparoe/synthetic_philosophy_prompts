Our department got a weird little windfall grant and my PI has basically handed me the job of turning it into a PhD studentship proposal — one student, three years, mandate is something like "formal epistemology of bounded agents." Deadline's in six weeks and I'm supposed to say what the student will actually work on, not just gesture at a literature. The stuff I keep circling is logical omniscence: standard possible-worlds semantics makes an agent know every logical consequence of what she knows, plus every tautology, which is obviously wrong descriptively, and every fix I've looked at seems to trade one problem for a different one. I'd rather not write a proposal that amounts to "survey the fixes," so I want help picking an agenda.

Here's the menu as I see it — feel free to tell me I've mis-carved it. (1) Impossible/non-normal worlds, where the epistemic accessibility relation is allowed to reach worlds that violate logic; the worry is that the restriction on which impossible worlds get included looks ad hoc, and that you end up with a semantics whose expressive power nobody can characterize cleanly. (2) Awareness logics in the Fagin–Halpern line, where you add an awareness operator and only demand closure over what the agent is aware of; worry: awareness is usually cashed out syntactically (aware of a sentence iff aware of its parts), so you've smuggled syntax into the semantics, and it's unclear what to say about awareness of awareness. (3) Stalnaker-style moves where belief comes in chunks and you let a new content in by permitting it; worry: how do you specify the chunking without either trivializing it or re-importing omniscience through the back door? (4) The Bayesian wing: what does it mean to update on a hypothesis you couldn't previously represent at all (old evidence / new theory), and is there a non-arbitrary way to grow the algebra? (5) The computational wing, where you treat a logical truth as uncertain in proportion to how hard it is to derive; worry: the probabilities go resource-indexed, so an agent's epistemic state seems to depend on facts about the machine she's running on, and I can't tell if that's a bug or a feature. Plus a couple of applied hooks I keep getting pulled toward: group and distributed knowledge for committees and firms, and the legal fiction that everyone knows the law, which is basically mandated logical omniscience with actual consequences for people.

So: rank these for a three-year project with a student who's strong in modal logic but has zero ML background, and defend the ranking rather than just listing it. I care about (a) how many of the other items are downstream of getting it right, (b) whether a genuinely publishable result is reachable in three years versus it being a career-long swamp, and (c) whether a clean answer would change how anyone thinks or just add a fifth semantics nobody adopts. Tell me which one or two I should actively drop as traps — the ones that look like open problems but where any resolution just relocates the puzzle a level up — and what result over the next couple of years would make you reorder the whole list. And if you think the real frontier is something not on my list, argue for it. No bibliography or reading list please, I know how to find papers; I want the dependency structure and your bet on where to push.
