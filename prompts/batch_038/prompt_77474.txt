I lead a team of five at a company that makes an email assistant, and this year we've been moving it from a thing that drafts text to a thing that acts: sending on the user's behalf, cancelling subscriptions, disputing charges, booking and rescheduling meetings, forwarding documents to whoever the user names. We have roughly two hundred thousand active users, most of them at small businesses that use the assistant as a de facto front office. The product question in front of us is what the agent is permitted to do, and my team has split into two camps that I cannot reconcile and, more to the point, cannot even describe neutrally to my own manager.

Camp A wants to confine the agent to a fixed action space enforced outside the model. Recipients must be on an explicit allowlist the user maintains; anything involving money goes through a confirmation the user clicks; every send sits in a reversible queue for five minutes; and there is no code path by which the agent can delete, purchase, or authorize anything at all. Their claim is that we can then state something unconditional to a customer, and that the statement stays true no matter what the model does, because the constraint isn't in the model — it's in the harness, and the harness is a few thousand lines we've read. The cost is that the product feels like a form, retention on the pilot accounts is measurably worse, and our competitor shipped the unconstrained version four months ago. Camp B says this is theater: they fine-tuned a much stronger model on our support traffic and it handles 99.4% of the twelve thousand sampled conversations correctly, with the residual failures mostly trivial and all of them catchable from user reports; they want to manage risk the way you'd manage it anywhere else in production software, with monitors, anomaly thresholds, a kill switch, and a fast rollback. Their most senior engineer has spent two weeks trying to give us the kind of bound Camp A wants and came back furious, saying the technique that works on toy inputs produces certificates so loose that they license nothing, and that anyone promising an unconditional guarantee on a learned system is selling something.

What has me stuck isn't picking a side, it's that I've stopped believing the two camps are answering the same question, and I can't say what the difference is. Two things have lodged in my head. First, Camp B's 99.4% was measured on conversations sampled from last spring's traffic, i.e. from users who were not trying to break anything; the agent lives on the open internet, and at least one of the things that will shape its future inputs is actively optimizing against us — and, arguably, so is the agent itself, in the sense that we trained it against a metric and it has every incentive structure to satisfy the metric rather than the intent. Second, Camp A's guarantee is a guarantee about single actions, and I can't see how it survives composition: an agent that is only ever permitted to send an approved template to an approved recipient can still ruin someone's week by sending four thousand of them, and nothing in the allowlist forbids that. A friend of mine who does security engineering told me over dinner that we're arguing about whether to build a vault that can't be opened or a vault that is very rarely opened by the wrong person, and that in her line of work only the first one is allowed to be called a vault; I found that persuasive and then immediately suspected she was smuggling in an assumption about who the adversary is.

So: which way of thinking about what we owe our users is more defensible, and what would you actually have us build — including whether the honest answer is some third thing that neither camp will currently acknowledge? I want a recommendation and your degree of confidence in it, not a survey of considerations; if you think one of my engineers is straightforwardly right and that I'm wrong to be torn, say so. And please push back where I've earned it. If the way I've framed this is confused — if the two camps are talking past each other in a way that dissolves rather than resolves once you describe it correctly, or if my vault analogy is doing illegitimate work, or if you genuinely cannot advise without facts I haven't given you — I would much rather hear that plainly than receive a diplomatic weighing of both sides. Tell me which facts you'd need, if that's the situation.
