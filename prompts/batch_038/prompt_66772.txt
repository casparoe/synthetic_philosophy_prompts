I am spending the semester writing a comparative policy paper on the two largest electricity systems in the EU, and I have found that the standard analysis — levelised costs, capacity factors, grid stability, emissions per kilowatt-hour — leaves out exactly the part I cannot stop thinking about. Consider the German sequence in isolation from its economics. In the autumn of 2010 the federal government extended the operating lifetimes of the country's reactors, on the explicit grounds that they were needed as a bridge technology until renewables could carry the load. In March 2011, within days of the Fukushima accident, it suspended those extensions and shut the oldest plants for a three-month safety review. By the end of June it had legislated a permanent, irreversible exit, and the last three units were disconnected in April 2023. Notably, the body convened to advise on the reversal was not a panel of probabilistic risk analysts: it was an ethics commission, staffed largely with philosophers, theologians, a sociologist of risk, union and church and industry representatives, and chaired by two figures from politics and science administration. France, over roughly the same decade, moved in the opposite direction — full state ownership of its dominant utility, a new-build programme, and an explicit doctrine that a large, state-directed nuclear fleet is the backbone of decarbonisation and of energy sovereignty. Both countries face the same physical facts about uranium, reactor physics, and atmospheric carbon.

So my question is what theory of risk, and of the permissibility of imposing risk on others, the German sequence presupposes, and what theory the French one presupposes. Several features of the German case seem to me to strain against any straightforward expected-harm calculus, and I would like them addressed one by one rather than dissolved. First, the reversal happened within months, on the basis of an accident that occurred on another continent, in a reactor design unlike Germany's, caused by a natural event of a kind Germany does not face; no new technical finding about German plants seems to have driven it. Does a policy that changes that fast presuppose that risk judgment is properly responsive to what a society can vividly imagine, or does it presuppose something invariant that Fukushima merely revealed — that the relevant probability was always higher, or always less knowable, than the licensing regime admitted? Second, the commission's very composition suggests the state took itself to be deciding a question of value rather than of magnitude. Yet the same state kept lignite plants running and in reserve through the nuclear shutdown, and scheduled its coal exit for 2038, a decade and a half after the last reactor closed. Whatever one's numbers, the dispersed mortality from coal combustion per unit of electricity generated is not obviously smaller than the statistical mortality one attributes to a modern nuclear fleet. What theory makes concentrated, low-probability, technologically imposed, potentially transboundary harm categorically unacceptable while leaving dispersed, high-probability, familiar harm to be traded off against jobs and electricity prices? Candidates that occur to me, and that I would like tested rather than listed: dread versus familiarity; harm imposed on people who never consented versus harm incurred by a society that chose its own energy mix decades ago; doing versus allowing, on the thought that building new risk is different from declining to remove an old one; the structural difference between a harm that is spread thin across many lives and a harm that arrives all at once and cannot be undone; and insurability — the thought that a risk no private market will price at anything like its true magnitude is a risk the public is silently underwriting. For each, tell me whether the German practice strictly requires it, merely fits comfortably with it, or is indifferent to it, and say plainly where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that two or three incompatible theories would license exactly the same legislation.

Third, and this is the part I am least sure how to phrase: both systems rest on institutional arrangements that seem to me to carry heavy, unspoken commitments. Both countries sit inside an international nuclear liability regime which, as I understand it, channels claims exclusively to the plant operator and caps compensation at amounts well below the plausible cost of a severe accident — which is to say that the tail is borne by victims and treasuries rather than by the party whose decisions created it, and that the state, in licensing the plant, has in effect made a distributional choice on behalf of people who were never asked. If my description of that regime is simplified or wrong, correct me; I have not read the instruments themselves. Meanwhile Germany abandoned its search for a single repository site and restarted a comparative search across the whole national territory, with a requirement that the emplaced waste remain retrievable for an extended period. What does retrievability presuppose about our standing toward people who will not be born for centuries — is it a genuine way of leaving them options, or is it a way of transferring to them a decision they will be unable to refuse? Finally, tell me what would have to change in practice, not just in rhetoric, if the presupposed theory were replaced: sketch what German energy policy would look like under a rigorous expected-harm consequentialism, and what French policy would look like under a consent-based deontology that treats unpriced tail risk as a taking, and say which parts of each existing system would survive the substitution unchanged. I do not want a balanced survey of the candidates; I want you to commit to the reading you think best explains the pattern, and to be explicit about the two or three places where your reconstruction is most likely to be wrong — for instance, if the 2011 reversal in fact did rest on technical findings about German plants, or if the coal-versus-nuclear mortality comparison I have assumed is badly off. Close by stating what would change your verdict: which piece of evidence, or which argument I have not considered, would push you to a different account of what these policies presuppose.
