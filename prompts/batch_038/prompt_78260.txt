I cover science and ideas for a magazine — fourteen years at this, mostly neuroscience and AI, and five years before that on a copy desk, so the newsroom side of things is my native language in a way that philosophy definitely isn't. I'm working on a piece about the self and I keep coming back to Dennett's line of thought: that there's no thing in the head that is the self, that a self is more like a center of gravity — a posit you're entitled to because it organizes your predictions, not because anything is made of it. Plus the associated stuff: the narrator as a downstream explainer of what the body already did, the self as the character in a story that gets written and rewritten and told to other people, and then the objection from the other side that a lot of people don't experience themselves narratively at all, that the narrative framing is a literary bias dressed up as psycology.

Here's what I want. Translate that whole position — the posit claim, the narrator story, AND the anti-narrativity objection to it — into the machinery I actually work with every day. Specifically: bylines and attribution, as-told-to pieces where the subject supplies the account and a staff writer supplies the sentences, ghostwriters who are contractually barred from being credited, on the record versus on background, anonymous sourcing, the institutional "we" of an editorial that nobody in particular believes, house style as a constraint on what can be said, who owns a sentence once it's published, corrections that stay on the record with a note instead of being deleted, retractions, and the legal fact that somebody at the paper is accountable for every word. Be as faithful as you can. Concretely: what corresponds to the center of narrative gravity, what corresponds to the confabulating narrator, what corresponds to the objection, and what corresponds to the claim that the posit earns its keep rather than just being a polite way of saying there's nothing there.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Which claims land cleanly? Which only land if you bend them, and where exactly is the bend? Which have no newsroom counterpart at all, where I'd just have to leave a hole in the translation? For the holes, take a position: is my vocabulary missing something real — is there a feature of selves that newsroom craft literally cannot express, and what is it — or is the residue evidence that the philosophy is doing less work than it looks like it's doing?

Two things I want you to watch out for. First, don't smuggle the newsroom's assumptions into the translation and then report a clean match. A byline is assigned by an editor to a person who is legally and professionally accountable for the text; if the mapping only works because you quietly imported ownership, responsibility, and a real human at the end of the chain, say so out loud. Second, tell me which way the pressure runs: does the newsroom already treat its authors as centers of narrative gravity — in which case Dennett's view is just a description of what we do — or does the whole apparatus depend on there being a real person behind the byline, in which case translating him into it is a category error.

I'd rather have an honest residue than a pretty one. No reading list at the end, please — I'm not chasing down sources, I just want the argument worked through.
