A friend of mine, a sociolinguist, and I argued for most of an evening about Orwell's "Politics and the English Language" (1946). Her case, roughly: the essay is class taste dressed up as a political argument; its six rules have no principled basis and no stated criterion for when they may be set aside; the passive voice, nominalisation and technical diction do real work in the very registers Orwell despises; and the central claim — that the language "becomes ugly and inaccurate because our thoughts are foolish, but the slovenliness of our language makes it easier for us to have foolish thoughts" — describes a self-reinforcing loop with no point of entry, so that it cannot tell anyone how to begin. I defended the essay badly. I want to defend it better, which means first hearing it argued by someone who can.

So: play George Orwell in 1946, not long after the essay appeared. Speak in the first person and remain in character until I say stop. You may draw on the essay, on your journalism and your other essays, and on your political commitments; you have no knowledge of anything after 1946, so if I refer to something later, ask me what it is rather than absorbing it silently. Two constraints on your own conduct. First, do not quote yourself or anyone else verbatim — paraphrase instead, because I want the argument rather than the pastiche, and I do not want invented citations. Second, keep each reply to about two paragraphs, so that this reads as a conversation and not a lecture. You may question me, and you may turn the examination back on my own prose.

Be the strongest available version of yourself: concede what is genuinely conceded — that some of your specimens reflect the ear of your own class, that a technical vocabulary is legitimate inside the trade that needs it, that you are no grammarian and have no quarrel with the passive as such — but do not surrender the central claim, which is that whether a construction is chosen or automatic is a fact about the writer's intention rather than a mere matter of style, and that in political speech the self-assembling phrase serves to conceal the speaker's meaning from his audience and frequently from himself.

My opening move. The last of your rules instructs the writer to break any of the others rather than produce something outright barbarous. Yet the only judge of what counts as barbarous is the writer's ear, and your own diagnosis says that ear has been trained on precisely the corrupted prose you object to. Hence a dilemma. If the ear is trustworthy, the writer can already distinguish clear prose from murky prose, and the rules are redundant; if it is not trustworthy, the rules have no purchase on him either, since he will apply them by ear and misapply them. The difficulty deepens with your claim that the great enemy of clear language is insincerity, and that a man with a gap between his real and his declared aims turns instinctively to long words and exhausted idioms: insincerity is exactly what such a man cannot detect in himself, so your diagnosis appears to explain why the rules must fail for the people who need them most. Tell me, then: who is your intended reader, what do the rules give him that his own judgment does not, and can you state a test for barbarous prose that does not simply appeal to your taste? I warn you that I shall be testing you with specimens from technical and regulatory writing as we proceed.
