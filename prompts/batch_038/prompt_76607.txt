I have no academic training in history. I left school early, came to this subject late and from the side, and have spent the last several years reading unsystematically about the First World War and about the long argument over who was responsible for it. For about two years I have also been part of an informal reading group — eight of us, none of us professional historians, one of whom is a retired solicitor and another of whom reads German and keeps correcting the rest of us. What has become painfully clear to me is that we are almost never disagreeing about the same thing. One member will say the question was settled decades ago; another will say the question is a fraud that only exists because a treaty needed a legal basis for an invoice; a third will say that everyone was guilty and therefore no one was. Each believes he has refuted the others. I would like to stop that, and I would like you to help me build the instrument that stops it.

Please write a decision tree — or, if you judge that a scored quiz with a key would sort us better, a quiz with a key — that places a reader at a named position on what it means to hold a *nation* responsible for a war, using the outbreak of the First World War as the running example throughout. Nine to eleven questions. Each question should be a thin description of a concrete situation, not a statement of doctrine: give us the circumstances and ask what we would say about them, so that a person can answer honestly without having any idea which label their answer leads to. Where possible, use thinly described versions of things that actually happened — in July 1914, in the decades before it, in the years after it, and during the great historiographical fight of the 1960s — rather than invented science-fictional analogues; strip out the names and dates so that a reader who has never opened a book on the subject can still respond to the situation itself. Two or three answer options per question, each phrased as something a reasonable person might actually say out loud.

Build it in the right order. I do not want a list of famous books and famous historians with questions stapled on to justify it. I want you to start from the logical space: here are the questions, here are the combinations of answers they permit, and only then here are the positions that occupy various regions of that space. Some of the questions I want on the tree, in whatever order you think best:

- Whether a nation is even the sort of thing that can bear responsibility, or whether responsibility always bottoms out in particular persons making particular decisions.
- Whether the question under dispute is about *causing* an outcome or about *deserving blame* for it, and whether those two can come apart in a case like this one.
- Whether blame is a fixed quantity to be divided up, so that establishing that other powers were culpable reduces the share attributable to any one of them, or whether it is the kind of thing several parties can each bear in full.
- Which stretch of time the verdict is a verdict about: the five weeks of July 1914, the twenty or forty years of policy that preceded them, or the twenty years that followed the armistice.
- Whether the purpose the verdict serves changes what the correct answer is — an invoice, a moral record, a warning to future statesmen, a basis for reconciliation, a thing taught to schoolchildren.

And one question that is my own, which I want represented on the tree even if you think it is confused. I have come to think that a nation's *later* attitude toward its own war does something to the responsibility it bears for having fought it. A public that repudiates a war, mourns it, and refuses to glorify it seems to stand in a different relation to 1914 than a public that spends two decades insisting it was betrayed, then produces a movement that treats the same war as a robbed victory. I have been calling this, privately and no doubt pretentiously, the ratification question. Please make it a genuine branch, tell me which of the leaves you end up with actually turn on it, and tell me bluntly whether any position denies that it matters at all — because my suspicion is that several of them must, and I have never been able to get anyone in my group to say so plainly.

As for the leaves, there must be at least seven, and they must include: a position on which the whole enterprise is malformed because only individuals can be guilty; a position that separates the legal or liability sense of responsibility from the moral one; a position holding that the July crisis was a systemic failure in which no single power acted in a way that marks it out as uniquely culpable; a position of the kind that caused such bitterness in West Germany in the early 1960s, when a Hamburg historian argued for a deliberate bid for world power with lines of continuity running forward into the 1930s; and a position that treats 1914 and 1939 as a single object of blame rather than two. If my ratification idea corresponds to a real position, place it; if it corresponds to nothing anyone defends, say so and say why.

Three further things I want, and I want them rather than a tidy-looking tree:

First, identify at least two combinations of answers that the tree's logic permits but that no serious position occupies, and for each one diagnose the emptiness — is the combination incoherent, is it merely unattractive, or has nobody simply thought to defend it yet? I suspect the most interesting parts of this debate are the unoccupied corners.

Second, flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate. I would much rather be told that two of these positions collapse into the same thing under any question you can devise than be handed a flowchart that quietly pretends otherwise. If there is some further question that would pull them apart, propose it; if there is not, say that the distinction is verbal.

Third, tell me what to make of a reader whose answers land nowhere — inconsistent, or splitting every question down the middle. My guess is that this describes most of us, and the instrument should have something to say about it rather than defaulting to the nearest leaf.

On the separate matter of what I believe going in, please correct me rather than work around me. I had always assumed that the article everyone calls the war guilt clause actually accuses Germany of guilt; when I finally read it, it says only that Germany accepts "the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage," and the article immediately following narrows what is to be compensated. Tell me whether that gap between the nickname and the text is a real hinge for the debate or lawyerly hairsplitting. I also believe the same treaty arraigned the former Emperor personally, as an individual rather than as a head of state, for an offence against international morality, and that nothing ever came of it because the country he had gone to would not surrender him. If either of those beliefs is wrong or oversimplified, say so before you build anything on top of them.

Some practical constraints. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, no footnotes, no suggestion that we go and look something up — we will not be doing any of that, and I would rather have your own account of the positions than a set of pointers. Please do not put words in the mouths of historians or documents unless you are confident of the exact wording; paraphrase instead, since a garbled quotation would be worse than none. Define any term of art the first time you use it, and write for intelligent readers who have never encountered the technical literature. And if the shape of the tree is itself a substantive and contestable choice — if organizing the debate around my questions rather than around, say, the archival evidence for particular decisions already prejudges something — tell me that too, and tell me what a differently shaped tree would have sorted us into instead.

Finally, and this matters more to me than any of the above: keep a clear line between what is settled and what is still genuinely fought over among people who know this material properly. Where there is something like a professional consensus, say so and say what it is. Where there is not, do not let a contested position wear the clothes of one — I have been on the receiving end of that often enough, and it is exactly the failure my reading group keeps committing. Where you are reconstructing a position from the logic of the space rather than reporting what its defenders actually say, mark it as your reconstruction. And where you think I have simply got something wrong in my framing — including the ratification idea, which I am attached to and therefore most likely to be wrong about — say so directly rather than diplomatically.
