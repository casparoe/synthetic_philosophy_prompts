I teach a seminar on practical rationality and the first two weeks are always a mess, because half the room thinks weakness of will is a straightforward failure of rationality and the other half thinks it's just a person having different desires at different times, which is hardly a scandal. Before anyone learns the labels I want to sort them by what they actually say about cases, so the seminar isn't six weeks of people talking past eachother.

So: build me a sorting instrument. Eight or nine questions, each a concrete little scenario about one agent spread across time — not "is time-inconsistent discounting rational" but situations with some texture, the way the good diagnostic quizzes work. Nobody should be able to answer by guessing which camp sounds more admirable, so please keep the words rational, irrational, self-control, willpower, weakness, discipline and discount out of the question text entirely. Students say what the agent should do, or what they'd do, or what they'd tell her; the scoring key does the mapping.

The cases should turn on the claims that actually divide the field, and I'll tell you which ones I care about, since the point is to pull apart positions that intro treatments staple together:

- whether a whole sequence of choices can be assessable even when every single choice in it is the best one available to the agent at the moment she makes it — the money-pump style worry, roughly
- whether "you'll end up paying to switch back and forth and be worse off" is a reason to change now, or only a prediction about how things go, and whether paying to bind yourself is a cure or a symptom
- whether the January person who signs the contract and the April person who no-shows are one deciding agent, and whether anything in the verdict changes if they arn't
- whether foreknowledge does any work: the agent who knows she'll change her mind and plans around it versus the one who is genuinely surprised by herself
- whether a device — a penalty clause, a lock, a trainer you've paid up front, an app that donates to a cause you loathe — repairs the problem or just moves it to the moment you decide whether to honor the device
- whether adopted plans generate standing reasons that persist, or whether reasons always bottom out in what you want right now
- and whether steep near-term discounting might simply be what an animal that has to eat today rather than optimize should do, so that the whole framing is a category error

Leaves I want the tree to be able to reach, at minimum: the dynamic-consistency view, on which the sequence rather than the choice is the unit of assessment; the precommitor, the sophisticate and the naif as three separate destinations rather than one self-control bucket; the divided-mind or multiple-selves view where there is no single subject available to be inconsistent; the planning-agency view where the failure is not governing yourself by plans you adopted; the deflationary view on which there is no such thing as akrasia, only a contrary desire that happens to be stronger; and the adaptive view from the last bullet. If two of those genuinely cannot be separated by responses to cases, tell me which two and why, instead of writing a question that pretends.

Then the part I actually most want: give me the logical space as a grid with those claims as axes, before you locate anyone in it. I want to see which combinations of answers are live and which are empty. For each empty cell, say whether it is empty because the position is incoherent, because it is coherent and stable and nobody has bothered to claim it, or because it is simply unexplored. My hunch is that there's a perfectly coherent spot nobody occupies — the agent who says the sequence is the right unit of assessment, but that what gets assessed is her situation rather than her rationality, and who thinks binding devices are useful instruments with no normative weight whatsoever, so that a person who is pumped is unlucky and not defective. Tell me if I'm wrong about that, and if there are other vacant plots.

Scoring key maps answer patterns onto leaves, never the other way around. Mark any question where two different leaves would give the same answer so I can cut it. And no bibliography, no reading list, no name-dropping of papers — I have a syllabus, I want the instrument to stand on its own feet.
