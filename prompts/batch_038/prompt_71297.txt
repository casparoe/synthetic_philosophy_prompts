Slightly odd request. I write novels — literary-historical ones — and my new book just went to press. The last third is set in the Dutch Republic in the early 1670s and is built, structurally, around the fact that Spinoza's Political Treatise breaks off partway through its chapter on democracy. Somebody in a philosophy department read it and has invited me to give a forty-minute talk at their early-modern workshop in the spring. Theyve been clear it isn't a book-promo slot and isn't a literature-and-philosophy chat: they want me to defend the interpretive claim the novel rests on, on its merits. I need to know what they're going to do to me.

The claim, roughly: (1) the chapter doesn't break off merely because Spinoza died — the argument had already hit a wall, because the premise that makes democracy the most natural kind of state for him, pooled power staying the power of the whole multitude so that obeying a decree you helped constitute is obeying yourself, also leaves him unable to say what democracy *isn't*. There's no line left between the assembled people and a riot. (2) So the missing rest of the chapter would have had to be about bounding the multitude's own power, and I argue he already knew that, from the way the Theological-Political Treatise tells the story of the Hebrew state coming apart after Moses, and from 1672, when the crowd tore the De Witt brothers apart in the street — which is the set piece of my book. (3) Hence the last chapter of the novel stops mid-sentence, and I want to defend that as a reading rather than a gimmick.

The audience: six to eight early-modernists who work from the Latin and think the gap between the published, rhetorical, theology-arguing TTP and the unpublished technical TP matters enormously; two or three who read Spinoza through the French tradition where it's all affect and the power of the multitdo and who think institution-building misses the point; one historian of the Dutch Republic who cares about regent oligarchy and the Rampjar and won't let me get away with anything about what Spinoza took the United Provinces actually to be; a couple of grad students, one convinced he's a liberal democrat in modern dress and one who thinks every contested passage is esoteric writing aimed at somebody.

Give me a prep sheet, not an essay: the eight or so objections I'll actually get from this crowd, ordered by how much damage they'd do, phrased the way each of those people would phrase it, each with a two-minute answer I can deliver from a podium without notes, plus a flag on where each answer is softest. Then: what I should concede in my opening three minutes to buy credibility; the one question I'm praying nobody asks; which of my three claims above are simply wrong or would sound wrong to them, so I can quietly drop or reword them; and which objections are really "a novelist shouldn't be doing philosophy" in disguise, and how to defuse that framing early.

No citations, no page numbers, no bibliography, no verbatim quotations from the texts — I can't check any of it and I don't want invented references. If you need to point me at a passage, describe it loosely enough that I can go find it myself.
