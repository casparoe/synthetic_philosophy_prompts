I have been reading a trade-press book on the neuroscience of consciousness, and one of its chapters opens with the following compressed account of a distinction that keeps turning up in other places I read. Since I cannot tell how much of the passage to trust, I would like a careful audit. It runs like this:

'Ned Block argued that the single word "consciousness" quietly covers two very different things. Access consciousness is information that is poised for use: available to reasoning, to the guidance of action, and to verbal report. Phenomenal consciousness is the raw feel of the experience itself, the intrinsic, ineffable, private quality of what it is like to see red. Block's insight was that the two can come apart, and they demonstrably do. In Sperling's classic partial-report experiments, people briefly shown a grid of letters consciously experience every one of them, but only a handful survive into access before the rest fade; experience overflows what the mind can use. Global workspace theory, the most influential neuroscientific account, says that a content becomes conscious when it is amplified and broadcast from a frontoparietal network to the rest of the brain. But broadcasting just is availability for report and reasoning, which is access on Block's own definition. So the theory cannot be a theory of experience at all: whatever it explains, the feel of seeing is left untouched. Blindsight patients, who can describe and point to things they sincerely deny seeing, are the mirror image of the phenomenon — full access in the absence of any experience.'

The chapter then concludes that this is why the leading scientific theory of consciousness is beside the point, and it treats the matter as closed rather than disputed.

Could you work through this claim by claim and tell me what a researcher in the field would accept, what they would regard as a defensible simplification, and what they would reject outright? There are a few specific places where I suspect the passage is sliding, though I cannot say where:

- Are intrinsic, ineffable, and private part of how Block characterized phenomenal consciousness, or has the passage folded a further doctrine about qualia into the definition of the term?
- Does the partial-report experiment establish what it is said to establish, or is there a step between the data and the claim that people consciously experience all the letters? If there is such a step, do specialists regard it as licensed, and what do the main objectors say about it?
- Does it follow from the fact that the global workspace theory is couched in terms of amplification, availability, and broadcasting that the theory is not about experience? Or does that conclusion depend on premises the passage never states — and what do the theory's own proponents take themselves to be explaining? Is the theory the passage describes the same thing as the theory the neuroimaging work is usually taken to support?
- Is blindsight correctly described as the mirror image, that is, as access consciousness without phenomenal consciousness? The passage says these patients can describe and point to what they deny seeing; I am unsure whether that description is accurate, and further unsure whether, even if it were, it would amount to access consciousness in the technical sense the first sentence defined.
- The word "access" seems to do double duty here, sometimes as Block's term of art and sometimes as an ordinary word. Where does the switch happen, and does anything in the argument depend on it?

And then the larger question: does the chapter's conclusion survive once the passage is corrected? If it does not, what is the actual state of the disagreement — is there a coherent open question about how access and phenomenal consciousness are related, or about what the neuroscientific evidence could settle between the rival answers? Are there researchers who think the distinction itself fails to carve consciousness at any joint and would reject the whole framing rather than the details of this passage? I am after the conceptual audit, not a list of sources, so please argue from the ideas themselves.
