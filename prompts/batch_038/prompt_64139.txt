Retired three years ago after 38 years in physical chemistry, and I've gone and fallen into philosophy headfirst, which my wife finds hilarious. Right now I'm stuck on this Davidson business — radical interpretation, the principle of charity. I came at it through Quine's translator-in-the-jungle setup, which I think is a cousin of Davidson's version, and I built myself an argument to check I'd actually understood the idea rather than just nodding along. Problem: the argument seems to prove something I know from personal experience is false, and I cant find the leak. Here it is, numbered the way I'd write a proof.

1. A radical interpreter starts with nothing but the observable stuff: which situations a speaker utters a sentence in, and whether they hold it true.
2. To pin down what the sentence means they have to settle what belief it expresses, and the only handle on that is those situations.
3. But those are the same situations the interpreter is standing in and looking at. So the only assignment actually available is: the speaker holds the sentence true in just those situations where the interpreter's own matching belief is true.
4. So the beliefs that come out at the end are largely true, and largely the same as ours. That's not a lucky result, it's baked into the procedure.
5. And there's no second route in — no independent way of getting at what they meant apart from the procedure that just produced it.
6. So systematic, massive error isn't attributable to anyone we can interpret. Anything we can even state as a disagreement is a small local patch against a big background of assumed agreement.
7. Which applies to interpreting anyone, including us reading past scientists, and including future people reading us.
8. So it can't turn out that a whole framework — phlogiston, caloric, the luminiferous ether — was false about nearly everything. At most it was off at the edges.
9. But that's simply not what happens. In my own field I watched two well-established pictures get replaced inside my working lifetime, and nobody at the time would have said they were only wrong at the edges.
10. So one of 1 through 9 is false, and I want to know which.

Where I'd like to land: I'd rather lose (5) or (8) than (9), because (9) is my own career and I don't want to be told I'm misremembering it. I know that's not a reason tho.

One thing I tried on my own. I thought maybe charity is like fixing a gauge — you adopt a convention so you can describe the situation at all, and the real content is whatever stays put when you change conventions. That felt good for about ten minutes, then I noticed a gauge choice is supposed to leave the physics untouched, whereas here the choice seems to determine which beliefs come out true, so I'm not sure it's the same kind of move at all. Tell me whether the analogy can be repaired or whether it's just me being an experimentalist about everything.

So: which step do you reject, why did it look unobjectionable when I wrote it down, and what does rejecting it cost? If there's more than one candidate, price them against each other rather than listing them. And if you think (9) is the weak link after all, say it straight and tell me what I'm allowed to say about phlogiston.
