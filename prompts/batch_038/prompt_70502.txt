My flatmate's a condensed-matter PhD and she walked me into this over dinner and I still can't find the way out.

(1) If a higher-level property has lots of distinct lower-level realizers, it isn't identical to any single one of them, so it doesn't reduce.
(2) Phonon is like that: bosonic normal modes in a sodium lattice, in diamond, in superfluid helium, in neutron-star crust — completely different dispersion relations underneath each time.
(3) So is temperature. Mean kinetic energy works for an ideal gas, but a spin system can have a negative absolute temperature, and in a plasma the electrons and ions have seperate temperatures, so "the temperature of the thing" isn't one micro-state.
(4) And you can't rescue it by identifying the property with the disjunction of all the realizers, because a disjunction isn't a property: it never shows up in a law and it doesn't explain why the instances resemble each other.
(5) Generalise and every property worth having in a special science comes out multiply realizable — gene, memory, superconductivity, recession.
(6) So none of them is identical to any physical property.
(7) But a property that isn't identical to a physical property is either something over and above the physical or it does no causal work.
(8) So either physics leaves out most of the world, or the special sciences are inert bookkeeping.

She's perfectly happy with the first horn for phonons. I'm not, because the same route makes being a table, and being a twenty-pound note, extra ingredients in the universe. Where does it break? I'd rather give up (7) or (8) than (2) and (3), but tell me what each option actually costs me.
