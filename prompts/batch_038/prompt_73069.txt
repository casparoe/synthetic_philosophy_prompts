I write a weekly ideas column and about once a quarter I get roped into running a training session for the newsroom's new hires — mostly general-assignment reporters, one or two off the data desk, nobody's done philosophy since high school and a couple of them are openly suspicious that philosophy is a waste of column inches. Forty-five minutes, a whiteboard, no slides (something about the borrowed room and the projector, dont ask).

Topic this time: how to tell when two sources who look like they're disagreeing are actually just using the same word two different ways. I care because I've killed two stories this year on exactly that hill. One of them had two researchers apparently at war over whether something counted as an addiction, and it turned out each was using addiction in a way that ruled out the other's usage, both usages perfectly defensible, and neither one would say so on the record because both thought conceding it meant losing.

What I want from you is not the explanation — I can write the explanation in my sleep. I want the plan for the room, because if I just start talking I'll blow it.

So:

The order. What has to be in their heads before the next piece can go in, and why that piece and not another one. I can already tell that if I open with the funny examples they'll file this under party tricks about dictionaries and never reach the part that actually changes how they report, so I need to know what the part that changes how they report depends on.

The one case that carries the whole session. Not my addiction story, I'm sick of it and its got newsroom gossip attached. It has to be something they can argue about out loud, and it has to do three jobs at once: show a dispute that looks substantive and dissolves once you pull the contested word out of both sides' sentences, show a dispute that looks like pure word-play and refuses to dissolve, and survive the smart-aleck in the back row going so what, they're both right, that's not a disagreement at all. Also tell me what it must NOT be — no trolleys, no ship of Theseus, this room has heard both.

Where the genuinely hard bit sits. The version where the fight isn't over a technical term but over whether something exists at all — is there such a thing as a race, is a corporation a thing, do numbers exist — plus the move that says those are verbal disputes too, and the pushback that some ways of using the words carve up the world better than others and that's precisely what a reporter should be chasing. I need to know whether a room of reporters can hold that inside 45 minutes or whether I should cut it and say I'm cutting it, and if I keep it, where in the sequence it goes so it doesn't retroactively turn everything before it into semantics.

The misconceptions — the reporter-specific ones, not the philosophy-undergrad ones. My guesses: this is just semantics therefore not a story; get them to define their terms on the record and the dispute evaporates; whichever usage matches the dictionary wins; and the nastiest one, if it's verbal then nobody's wrong so there's nothing to report. Tell me which of these arrives first and where in the sequence I have to head each one off, because if one lands early I'll be fighting it for the rest of the session.

The closing check. One question I can put to the room, answerable in a sentence or two, that tells me whether the idea landed or whether they've just learned to say a phrase.

And what I should refuse to get into at this level, out loud, so the refusal looks like a choice rather than a hole. My philosophy contact kept throwing quantifier variance at me and I have a feeling that if I say it in the room I'll get asked something I can't answer.

No names and no readings, please — this room is not going to go and read anything, and I don't want to sound like I'm lecturing. Just the plan, and the reasoning behind the order.
