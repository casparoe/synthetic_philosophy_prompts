I am seventeen, in my last year of high school, and I sit on our school's peer accountability board: a student panel that hears cases in which one student alleges that another has breached a community standard — copying lab data, damaging someone's property, engineering another student's exclusion from a team. Roughly once a term, the student who has been reported does not deny the allegation but turns it back on the person bringing it. "You did the same thing in ninth grade." "You're the one who gained the starting spot from it." "You've said publicly that you don't think this rule should exist." Our faculty adviser tells us to set such replies aside as beside the point, and about half the board agrees; the other half thinks they can be decisive. Before I take a side in that argument, I want to know what should be demanded of any account of standing to blame — a specification I could hold proposals against, including proposals nobody has written yet.

So please set out the desiderata for a theory of standing to blame: the phenomena it must explain, the verdicts it must deliver in particular cases, the distinctions it must not collapse, and the ordinary facts about blame it must remain consistent with. Each requirement should come with the case or consideration that motivates it, and a sentence on what goes wrong for a theory that fails it. I am not asking for a survey of the debate, and I cannot check references, so please describe positions by their content rather than by author or title, and leave out bibliographies and reading lists entirely.

Among the cases I have made up for myself, please tell me which belong in the data set a theory has to fit and which I should discard as confused. They are deliberately not the standard illustrations:

- Priya falsified a results table in ninth grade, was caught, served the consequence, wrote the reflection, and has not done it since. In eleventh grade she reports another student for exactly that. Her target says she has forfeited any right to speak. Has she — and if not, what restored it, and on what date?
- Nadia never broke the rule in question, but she knowingly kept the benefit her rival's breach handed her, and she is now the loudest voice condemning the rival.
- Marcus is entirely entitled to object but cannot bring himself to, so he drafts the objection and has a classmate with no history of the offense deliver it verbatim. Can standing be handed over like that, and if so, does the deliverer acquire it or merely lend a mouth?
- The same Priya also thinks badly of herself in private. Does whatever would disqualify her from addressing another disqualify her from addressing herself, and if not, what is the difference made of?
- A student who believes our phone policy is unjust and says so at every assembly nevertheless reports someone under it. Their target says: you do not even accept the standard you are invoking.
- Two students did the same thing; one did it once four years ago, one does it weekly. Does the theory have room for that difference, or only for a yes/no?

Also tell me which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really the preferences of one camp — for instance, whether a theory must explain the *feeling* of force behind "you did it too" or only its normative status; whether it must leave the accused's culpability completely untouched; whether standing has to come in degrees; whether it must be a property of a person or can be a property of a particular act of blaming in a particular relationship. If some subset of the desiderata turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable, say so explicitly and give the argument, because that result would matter to me more than any of the individual items. And identify the single requirement that does the most work in eliminating candidate accounts.

One further wrinkle. My purpose is practical, and adequacy depends on purpose: whatever account I end up defending has to be translatable into a rule a panel of sixteen-year-olds can apply in public, without interrogating anyone's private moral history or grading their character. That seems to add demands of its own — publicity, operability, some limit on how much of a person's past is admissible. Please say plainly whether those belong on the list as genuine desiderata for a theory of standing, or whether they are only constraints on my institution and would be a category error to build into the theory itself.

Finish, briefly and only if the list permits it, with a paragraph each on how the main available explanations fare against it: the view that keys lost standing to the blamer's own wrongdoing, the view that keys it to the blamer's failure to hold herself to the standard she invokes, the view that treats blame as a form of address that presupposes some relationship, and the view that there is no such thing as standing and that the intuition is an artifact. Keep this short — the criteria are what I actually need.
