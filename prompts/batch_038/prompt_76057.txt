I've spent the better part of a decade on the infinite-ethics cases — the ones in which a universe contains infinitely many lives, so total welfare is already infinite and any finite good you do appears to contribute exactly nothing — and I've become convinced that the standard imagery (an unbounded pile, an endlessly expanding future) is what keeps the discussion stuck. The sharper picture is a conditionally convergent series, where the same terms, merely reordered, will sum to whatever you please. I would like a sestina that cashes this out. Constraints: keep the traditional rotation of end-words exactly, so that a reader who knows the form can verify it stanza by stanza; if you can manage it, take *one*, *more*, *still*, *order*, *sum*, and *enough* as your six, on the principle that each should be able to mean both a quantity and a life; and let the permutation carry the argument, so that every stanza is the same six things counted in a new sequence and coming to a different total. In the closing tercet, where two end-words must share each line, I want the poem to attempt a rule for counting that does not depend on the order — and to let the reader watch that attempt cost it something it had wanted to keep. Name no theorem and no philosopher. Begin your reply with the poem and nothing else: no preface, no commentary, no notes on your choices.
