I'm a Conservative rabbi in a Rust Belt city of roughly two hundred thousand people, and on the fourth of next month I've been asked to give both the invocation and the benediction at the swearing-in of our new mayor, a former public defender who will hold the ceremony in the atrium of the courthouse — bad acoustics, hard floors, three hundred folding chairs, a live-stream with a chat I have been advised not to read. Before I tell you what I need written, let me say where I'm coming from, because the speech I want has a philosophical load-bearing wall in it and I would much rather state the wall than have you guess at it. I came to political philosophy the embarrassing way: through fifteen years of rationalist blogs and forums, arguing about whether honest agents with shared evidence converge (Aumann's agreement theorem was briefly my religion), and about the ways political identity corrupts cognition. That world has a folk theory of religious reasons in public argument, which is roughly that they are inadmissible — you may not cite your scripture at someone whose compliance you are demanding, because it is not evidence for them, and the whole game is to trade in considerations everyone can check. When I finally read Rawls properly, in my forties, I found the folk theory is an exclusivist caricature of him. What I want to work with is the much more permissive structure of his late essay on public reason revisited, as I understand it: the duty of civility binds citizens and officials when they debate and decide constitutional essentials and matters of basic justice in what he calls the public political forum — legislatures, executives, courts, campaigns — whereas the background culture of churches, synagogues, universities, associations, and the press is unrestricted and is where comprehensive doctrines are supposed to live and argue freely; comprehensive doctrines may be introduced into public political discussion at any time, on the proviso that in due course proper public reasons, drawn from a reasonable political conception of justice, are also presented, sufficient to support whatever the comprehensive doctrine was brought in to support; the proviso says nothing about order, so the public reasons may come before or after the religious appeal, and the reasonable sequencing differs for a voter, a candidate, a judge, and a clergyman who has been handed a podium for four minutes; and a citizen who offers their own doctrine while also supplying public reasons is, on Rawls's account, strengthening the ideal rather than insulting it. As I recall — and I flag that this is memory, not a citation — he thought the abolitionists and the civil rights preachers did not contravene the ideal at all, which is a considerable thing for a theorist of public reason to concede about people who argued almost entirely from comprehensive doctrine.

The complication is local and personal. The mayor's first initiative is an ordinance guaranteeing legal counsel to tenants facing eviction, paid for out of a modest transfer fee, and it is contested in the ordinary way: the landlords' association says it is a subsidy to bad faith and a taking from people who did nothing, the tenants' union says an eviction hearing without a lawyer is a formality with a witness, and the city attorney has questions about the fee. Two weeks ago a political philosopher at the law school four blocks from my shul — we were in the same graduate cohort before one of us went to yeshiva and the other one didn't — published a short piece in the local paper arguing that when a cleric occupies the official podium at an official transition of power, on the eve of an official decision about basic justice, the cleric is exercising political power on grounds that fellow citizens cannot reasonably be expected to share, and that a liberal city should stop inviting us. He did not name me, but he did name my synagogue's denomination and the date of the ceremony, and my phone has been busy ever since. So the room will contain, at minimum: the mayor's family, flown in from a parish three states away; the outgoing administration; about forty people from the tenants' union who have heard me speak at their meetings; the landlords' association's counsel; a scattering of law-school types, including him, who know exactly what a proviso is and will be scoring my sentences in real time; three local reporters; and my own congregants, among whom are at least two who would regard any concession to secular reasoning as a public embarrassment of the Torah, and at least two who would regard any invocation of it as proof that I have finally gone native. I do not want a speech that picks a side in that room. I want a speech that is visibly, structurally compliant with the proviso in a way the philosopher cannot honestly deny, without ever sounding like compliance.

Here is what I need from you. Write the invocation-and-benediction as a single continuous piece of about four minutes spoken aloud — call it five hundred to six hundred words — followed by a separate craft note of no more than three hundred words. The constraints, all of which are load-bearing, are these. First, it must be an actual prayer: there must be sustained second-person address to God, in the idiom of my own tradition rather than of generic American civil religion, and it must be theologically committed rather than a set of uplift sentences with a deity attached as decoration. Second, the vocabulary of the theory must never surface in the speech itself: the words "public reason," "comprehensive doctrine," "overlapping consensus," "proviso," and "civility" may not appear, and no sentence may read as a seminar contribution; the idea has to be enacted by the arrangement of the material, not announced. Third, the speech must itself discharge the proviso rather than merely promise that someone else will discharge it later: somewhere in those four minutes, in the plain language of the atrium, I have to say what I take the political justification for the contested ordinance to be — the reasons a fellow citizen who finds my God unconvincing could weigh and reject on the merits — and to say it with enough specificity that it is arguable rather than a platitude about compassion, since a reason nobody could disagree with is not doing any work. Fourth, the temporal structure of "in due course" should be audible: the piece should make room for the fact that a prayer and a deliberation have different clocks, that what is owed now at a podium is not the same as what is owed in six weeks in a council chamber, and that some of the reasons are mine to give and some are the city's to work out without me. Fifth, it must not contain the move that only the faithful can see the reason, and it must not contain an apology for my presence there; both are easy traps and both would be dishonest. Sixth, please do not put any verse or liturgical text in quotation marks and do not attach chapter-and-verse attributions; if you gesture at a scriptural image or a rabbinic idea, name it and paraphrase it, because I would rather cut a line than correct a fabricated citation from a podium. In the craft note, tell me which single sentence you intend as the one my philosopher colleague would have to concede satisfies the proviso and which single sentence you intend as the one my two most traditional congregants would recognize as genuinely addressed to God and not to the room; tell me whether you placed the public reasons before or after the religious appeal and why that ordering suits a clergyman rather than a legislator; and give me the strongest objection he could still make afterward, in his own terms, plus my best honest reply to it.
