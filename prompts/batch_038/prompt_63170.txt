I sit on an electoral-law review commission and I am drafting the section of our report on the five percent legal threshold. Our statute has three features I cannot get to cohere: a party must clear five percent of the national vote to win any list seats; a party registered as representing a recognised national minority is exempt from the threshold altogether; and a party that falls short nationally can still enter the legislature if it wins three single-member constituencies outright. Meanwhile the country next door elects its legislature by first-past-the-post, has no threshold of any kind on its books, and routinely hands a party an eighth of the national vote in exchange for a single seat — and nobody there calls that a threshold or expects it to be justified.

What conception of representation and of political equality does our threshold, together with those two exemptions, presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, not a list of pros and cons: what must be true about the unit being represented — voters, votes, opinions, or groups — for a numerical cutoff on group size to look like a requirement of fairness rather than an arbitrary exclusion; what the minority exemption reveals about which groups the polity treats as already existing rather than as brought into being by the ballot; and why geographic concentration is accepted as grounds for waiving the cutoff while ideological concentration is not. Then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely sits comfortably with several rival theories, say whether the exemptions contradict the governability rationale the threshold is officially given, describe what the system would have to become under the strictest reading of "every voter counts equally," and tell me what the contrast with our neighbour implies about whether an effective threshold stands in need of the same justification as a formal one.
