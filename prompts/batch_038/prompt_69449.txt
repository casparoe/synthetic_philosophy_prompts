ok so i'm a junior and i co-run this lunchtime club at my school, unofficially called "arguments and pizza" (the pizza part is load bearing, not kidding). we get 15-20 people, mostly sophomores and juniors, but realistically a third of them are there for the food and would rather be scrolling. we get 40 minutes in a regular classroom with a whiteboard. the projector bulb has been dead since september and nobody has filed the form to fix it, so assume no screen and no video. phones are supposed to go in the caddy by the door and about half of them do. i can print maybe 30 pages total and i have a stack of index cards and sharpies.

the situation: in two weeks our student council votes on whether to kill the freshman side-door rule. at my school freshmen are not allowed to use the main staircase or sit on the stone steps out front — they come in the gym-side door and eat in the little cafeteria off it. everybody says it's because a kid fell down those stairs in the 80s and it's basically a memorial thing. our advisor actually dug through old handbooks and found the rule already in there in the 60s, and no record of any accident. but also: the little cafeteria is where the ESL teacher runs her lunch tutoring table, and the only reason it has any students at all is that freshmen are guaranteed to be in that room every day. abolish the rule and the tutoring table probably quietly dies and nobody would connect the two for a year.

two more like it, if you need them. the bell rings four minutes early on wednesdays, which everyone says is because we used to share buses with the tech school across town — true, except that route ended years ago, and nothing depends on the four minutes anymore. and then there's the summer research program nominations, where the council president and the two debate captains automatically take three of the five slots; the stated reason is that they're the most committed students, the real history is that it started as a way to hand slots to alumni families and the merit story got stapled on later, and it still does exactly that job today.

my half-formed idea is to run the session on those three but disguised — hand them out as traditions belonging to some made-up school two states over, let the club judge them cold, and at some point reveal the mirror. problems i can already see: some kid will recognize their own school in minute three, the reveal might land as a gotcha, and if it works too well my club looks like its running a covert campaign for the council vote, which i promised my advisor we arent. my co-president is on the council and she's voting to abolish, and honestly i lean her way — i do not want the session to end up as "so we should keep it."

what i want from you is the actual plan for the 40 minutes, not an essay about the ideas. specifically:

- which of the three carries the activity, and whether to use all of them or add a fourth as a decoy (and if so, what kind — i'm out of ideas)
- what the kids actually *do*. my instinct is they have to commit to keep-or-abolish before they're given anything, and then information arrives in stages — but i don't want the stages to just be me reading cards at them. one thing i keep coming back to: each group gets a limited number of questions they can "ask" (index cards, whatever), and some of the questions have no answer because the answer is genuinely gone. make that mechanic work, with a cost, so they feel that they have to choose what to investigate and that they might run out before they know enough
- rules, roles, materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline that includes them eating
- the debrief: the exact questions in order, where the disguised-school reveal goes, and where i finally say the names (chesterton's fence, the burke thing) — they should not hear either name until the end
- what predictably breaks the first time i run it and how to handle it live. i'm bracing for: the pizza kids checking out, and one kid who watches a lot of philosophy youtube who will say some version of "this is just the appeal to tradition fallacy and status quo bias — we don't need a reason to tear it down, they need a reason to keep it," which is honestly a good point and i don't have an answer for it. the session shouldn't fake an answer, but it also shouldn't just fold
- the overcorrection risk: half the room walking out thinking "never change anything." i'd rather they walk out able to tell apart the three cases above, which are not the same case
- and be straight with me about what this activity can't reproduce. i think it's that my cards have an answer key and real life doesn't, but tell me if there's something bigger i'm missing

no assigned reading, nobody would do it, and please don't open with a lecture i have to give first.
