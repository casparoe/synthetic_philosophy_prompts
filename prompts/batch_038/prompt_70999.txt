Everyone knows the three-way split in Cold War origins historiography: the orthodox line that Soviet consolidation of Eastern Europe drove the confrontation, the revisionist inversion in the sixties putting the weight on American economic expansionism and atomic diplomacy, and the post-revisionist synthesis from the seventies onward that spreads responsibility across mutual misperception and security-dilemma dynamics, then got a second wind when the Russian archives briefly opened in the nineties. What strikes me is that seventy-plus years on, nobody has actually moved anybody: the same three positions get restated with new evidence that each side absorbs without flinching.

My working theory, which I want you to pressure-test rather than flatter, is that the impasse isn't about evidence at all. It's that 'who started it' is doing the work of three different questions at once: a sequence question about who did what first, a counterfactual question about whose choices the outcome was most sensitive to, and a blame question about whose conduct violated expectations that were reasonable for them to hold. Each camp answers one of the three and thinks it has answered all three, so the arguments never actually meet.

Is that right, or is there a genuine factual or methodological disagreement underneath that this diagnosis papers over? I want the diagnosis argued from what each camp actually says, not a tour of the camps. Then tell me what would count as progress on your account — a distinction, new archival material, a change of question, or nothing — and be honest about whether the whole thing might be unresolvable by design, i.e. wheter the question is ill-posed rather than just hard.
