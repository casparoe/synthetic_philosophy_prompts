I am a high school junior writing a research paper for my honors history seminar. My thesis is that the Munich analogy — the move from the failure of appeasement in 1938 to the conclusion that concessions to an aggressor's limited demands are always both futile and discrediting — usually functions to end an argument rather than to open one. My teacher makes the same objection every year: students like me list the episodes in which the analogy misfired and quietly omit the ones in which it appeared to work, and any set of real cases can be redescribed as cherry-picking. He is right that I cannot answer him with more real history. So I want to answer him with a designed case instead.

Please construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

Give me two fictional crises, Crisis A and Crisis B, each described in exactly one paragraph.

- Both must share the same surface skeleton: a mid-sized power makes a territorial demand on a weaker neighbour, framed as the protection of a co-ethnic minority in a border province; a distant great power holds a security commitment it is not plainly obliged to honour; there is a deadline of a few weeks and an offer of a summit.
- The details that normally make an analyst think of 1938 must be present in both, to the same degree: recent remilitarisation, withdrawal from a treaty, a weak and divided government in the target state, and public invocation of historic right by the demanding power's leader.
- In Crisis A the analogical move yields a sound recommendation. In Crisis B the identical move yields a recommendation that is disastrous.
- The difference between the two must be traceable to a single feature. That feature must be of a kind visible from public information both governments actually had at the time — the economic strain on the demanding power, where the minority population is physically concentrated, the composition and logistics of its armed forces, what its leader has said he would and would not accept — and not a fact about private intentions, secret documents, or anything only knowable afterward.
- Tell the two crises in the same voice, with the same quantity of detail, and with equally unfamiliar names, geographies and technologies, so that nothing smuggles in a real-world association. Do not thinly disguise an actual event: if I can guess which crisis you mean, the case fails, because then I have simply re-imported the cherry-picking.
- No science fiction, no nuclear exchange, no omniscient narrator, no perfectly rational actors.

After the two paragraphs, please do the following.

1. State what the analogy recommends in each case, and what the best available course actually is, showing that in Crisis B the two come apart. The disaster in B must follow from facts stated in the paragraph, so that a reader who sets the analogy aside can see it coming on the page; if the verdicts are asserted rather than generated, or if I could swap the labels A and B without any sentence needing to change, the design has failed and I would rather you say so.
2. Name the single feature that does the work, and explain the mechanism by which it flips the verdict — including why the analogy actively discourages an analyst from noticing it, rather than merely failing to point at it.
3. Run the removal test. What happens to Crisis B if that feature is deleted, or replaced by its counterpart from Crisis A? Does B collapse back into A, or does it merely become harder to classify? If the latter, tell me, because that weakens my paper.
4. Give the strongest reply available to a defender of analogical reasoning — roughly, that an analogy is only a prompt to ask the right questions, so the failure belongs to the analyst who stopped asking — and tell me whether that reply genuinely rescues the practice or merely relocates the problem into a step nobody performs.
5. Close with one or two blunt sentences on what my thesis actually gains from a case like this, and on the obvious objection that a made-up crisis tells us nothing about real statecraft. I would rather concede that limitation in my own words than be ambushed by it in class.

Please keep the whole response under about a thousand words.
