so i co-mod a mid-size politics subreddit (~180k people) and for about two weeks now the mod meta thread has been eating itself over a proposed rule change: one of the mods wants us to stop removing posts that accuse commentators or users of arguing in bad faith, on the grounds that our current policy is too permissive and lets people say "this guy knows exactly what he's doing, he's lying" and get applause for it. my co-mod — smart guy, i like him, we've run the sub together for three years — keeps ending the argument by pasting in "never attribute to malice that which is adequately explained by stupidity," usually under a post about a pundit who got caught quote-mining a study, and once under a thread about a state legislature whose stated rationale for a bill was demonstrably false at the time of the vote. Then he always makes the same second move: since they're not malicious, we should be arguing with the claim and not the motive, and anyone who raises motive is poisoning the well and should get their post removed. Someone shot back with the parody version, "any sufficiently advanced incompetence is indistinguishable from malice," and he said that was just a joke doing no work, which i think is wrong but again i cant say why.

Can you lay out the different precise theses that one sentence could actually be standing for? i mean like: is it a statistical claim about the base rates of human motivation, a norm of charitable interpretation when reading other people, a rule about where the burden of proof sits, a principle about blame and punishment, a forecasting heuristic about what happens next, or some kind of parsimony claim about explanations (it's named after a razor, after all, and i don't know what the razor is supposed to be shaving off). For each reading tell me what kind of claim it is, whether it's a platitude, whether it's substantive but defensible, whether it's flat false, and — this is the part i care most about — whether it actually earns him the second step, the move from "not malicious" to "talking about motive is illegitimate." i have a strong feeling those come apart and that only some of the readings get him there, and that the readings that get him there are the least plausible ones.

two things the menu should really engage with, because that's where our thread got stuck. first, malice and stupidity dont look exhaustive: there's the Frankfurt case where the speaker isn't tracking whether any of it is true and so isn't lying at all but isn't confused either, and there's the self-deceived person who'd pass a lie detector and sincerely report their own reasons. does the razor have a slot for those, or does it just sort them into one of its two bins by force, which would make "adequately explained by stupidity" true by stipulation? second, a pundit and a legislature aren't individuals in a vacuum: the pundit's income depends on keeping an audience activated, and there's a whole feedback apparatus rewarding the pattern, so you can get reliably bad-faith output from people who each feel perfectly sincere and would be insulted by the accusation. if the structure does the work, does the stupidity clause ever get to fire, or is the razor only usable at the level of individual psychology and simply breaks when you point it at institutions? tell me which readings survive that pressure and which collapse. then finish with (a) the one or two readings i should just concede to my co-mod because he's right about them, (b) a rewritten one-liner that keeps whatever's defensible without smuggling in the extra work he's using it for, and (c) a short account of what evidence would actually settle whether a given arguer is in bad faith, since that's the question the rule change hinges on and nobody in three hundred comments has tried to answer it.
