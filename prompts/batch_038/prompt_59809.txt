I run a small political-theory podcast (40 minutes, no ads, roughly 900 listeners) and next week's guest is a theorist whose recent book argues we should hold binding referendums far more often than we do — not as a populist gesture but on explicitly epistemic grounds. Her core move is a Condorcet-jury-theorem one: a large electorate voting a binary question is more likely to land on the right answer than a few hundred legislators are, and she leans on that constantly. She's smart and very likeable and has a habit of answering a hard question with a charming story about some Swiss commune, which eats five minutes and walks her back to safe ground. Act as an expert on democratic theory and the epistemic-democracy literature, think carefully about where that literature genuinely presses on a view like hers, and draft me ten interview questions.

Before the questions, give me three or four sentences sketching what you take the real pressure points to be and why — I want to see the reasoning, but keep it short. Then the questions. Ground rules: don't ask her to restate the thesis, and no "what got you interested in this?" filler. I want at least one question on each of the following. (a) Independence: if she also thinks deliberation is part of what makes referendum outcomes good, then deliberation correlates voters' errors, and correlated errors are exactly what wrecks the jury-theorem argument — I want that tension put to her directly, not as a gotcha but as a fork she has to pick a side of. (b) Competence above a half: what the argument needs is voters being better than chance at getting it right, and what happens to the whole edifice if for many referendum questions there's arguably no fact of the matter for a jury to track. (c) Framing and the "which question is actually being asked" problem — the gap between the words on the ballot and the mandate a government claims afterwards, including the case where a win gets cashed out in a much more specific form than anyone voted on. (d) Turnout and quorums: does her view commit her to thresholds, and what does an organised boycott of the vote do to the epistemic story. (e) Intensity and logrolling: a binary vote can't register how much anyone cares, and legislatures have a mechanism for trading that off which referendums structurally lack — is that mechanism corruption or is it information. (f) Re-running the question: is a second referendum on the same issue a second roll of the dice, or the epistemically better one now that voters have seen the consequences.

For every question, write the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likley to give — as an actual sentence I can say out loud, not a description of one — and add a single line saying which questions you expect her to pivot away from and roughly what the pivot will sound like. Sequence them so the early ones are ones she can handle well and the heat builds through the middle. End on one question about what observation or empirical result would count against her view, phrased so a generality won't get her off the hook. Keep it usable: i have to read these off a piece of paper in a room with bad acoustics, so short sentences, and no jargon that the audience would need her to translate.
