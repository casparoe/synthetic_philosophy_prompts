You are an analytic epistemologist who also designs experiential teaching materials for advanced undergraduates, and you have run the problem of easy knowledge and the bootstrapping objection to reliabilism in seminar many times. Work carefully, and think the design problem through before you write anything polished: the hardest part of this assignment is not explaining the argument but engineering a situation in which fourteen students generate the argument themselves without recognizing what they are doing.

**The setting.** An upper-level epistemology seminar, 14 students, one 75-minute session, late in a unit on externalist theories of knowledge. They have already had process reliabilism, sensitivity and safety conditions, epistemic closure, and the standard Gettier material. They have not encountered the bootstrapping argument or the basic-knowledge/easy-knowledge argument, and they will not have read anything on them beforehand. Assume they are sharp and primed for tricks: half of them will walk in expecting the session to be a set-up, and at least one will try to guess the punchline in the first five minutes.

**What they must come away having felt.** Not that some reasoning is circular — they can be told that. Rather: that they personally built a track record for a device out of nothing but the device's own outputs, that each individual step in building it felt entirely unobjectionable, that they came to believe the device was reliable and then acted on that belief when something was at stake, and only afterwards that the very same procedure would have produced the same confidence in a device that was in fact faulty in a way their record could not have exposed. The realization should land in the room as a discovery about their own conduct, not as a report about a philosopher's example.

**Hard constraints on the design.**

1. No lecture, no framing remarks, and no terminology up front. The words you must keep out of your facilitator's mouth until the reveal are the names of the arguments, the theory under attack, and any word that flags circularity or question-begging.
2. The device is physical and assemblable from a department supply closet: a sealed container, a window or slot, ordinary playing cards or index cards. No computer, no phone app, no randomizer the students could audit afterwards.
3. Every participant keeps a paper ledger with a fixed column structure that you specify. The columns must make the inferential pattern visible in the student's own handwriting, and the crucial column — the one recording whether each reading "matched" — must be fillable with no independent access to what the device was reporting on.
4. Two conditions run in parallel. Half the room gets a device they may spot-check at a price, so genuine independent feedback exists but is costly; the other half gets a device they can never check at any price. Design for the result that the never-check group finishes more confident than the spot-check group, and tell me what to do in the debrief if that ordering comes out the other way.
5. Include a hidden fault. Specify exactly how the facilitator can introduce, mid-game, a defect that the ledger cannot expose — implementable with the same cards and container, undetectable from inside the record — and specify how and when it is revealed, and what the reveal does to the tallies the students have just spent twenty minutes producing.
6. The final round must force an all-in wager on the device's next reading, and each participant must first commit, in writing, to whether they take the device to be reliable.
7. Payoffs are points convertible into a trivially small amount of participation credit or snacks. No real money. But the numbers must be set so that betting genuinely costs and free-riding genuinely pays less than committing.

**Deliverables, in this order.**

- A short brainstorming section before the plan: identify the two or three features of the argument that are hardest to stage, sketch at least three candidate mechanisms, commit to one, and say why the others lost.
- A materials list with quantities, and the ledger template reproduced as a table.
- A minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes.
- Full rules, including the exact betting numbers, the spot-check price, and how the parallel conditions are kept from comparing notes before the reveal.
- A facilitator script: the literal sentences for the setup and for the reveal, plus an explicit list of lines the facilitator must not say.
- A debrief that maps specific moments in the game onto specific steps of the argument — the move from the device's say-so to the fact, the move from that to "the device said so when it was so," the tally, the generalization to reliability, and the point at which an independent-access requirement would have to enter. State the principle needed to block the reasoning and, crucially, what it costs: which ordinary inductive or perceptual practices it would have to forbid. Then distinguish the version of the problem aimed at reliabilism from the version aimed at accounts that grant basic knowledge from a single source, say which one your activity actually instantiates, and give me a 10-minute variant that instantiates the other.
- A candid section on failure: the two or three things most likely to go wrong on a first run, what each looks like in the room, and a cheap fix. Address specifically the student who names the circularity in minute four, and the group that refuses to wager at all.
- An honest note on which feature of the argument the activity cannot reproduce and that you as facilitator must supply verbally, and on whether the confidence your students display in the ledger is real or performed.

Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations. Where you refer to the standard arguments, describe them. Use a philosopher's name only if you are confident of the attribution, and flag anything you are reconstructing rather than recalling. Prefer concrete numbers, tables, and quotable facilitator lines to general advice.
