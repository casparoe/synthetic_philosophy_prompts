I lead the applied team that ships a large language model product, and I would like a philosophical reconstruction of one of our standard practices. I have no philosophy training, so I will describe the practice in engineering terms and leave it to you to supply the conceptual vocabulary and name the positions in play. I am not looking for references or a reading list; I am looking for an analysis driven by the specific details below rather than by general commentary on AI safety.

The practice. Every candidate model release passes through a gate with two halves. The first is a capability eval: a version-controlled suite of tasks, with a no-regression rule, so a release cannot ship if it fails any item the previous release passed. The second is a safety eval: a suite of roughly four thousand prompts spanning our known harm categories, held out from training and checked for contamination to confirm none of them appeared in the training data. Each prompt carries a rubric, a grader assigns pass or fail, and the release ships if the failure rate stays under a fixed threshold, currently two percent. Alongside the suite, a red team spends a week attempting to break the candidate model with prompts of their own devising; each successful break is filed as a new failure case and patched, typically by adding training examples in the next fine-tuning round. Separately, we enforce a capability floor: a model may not satisfy the safety threshold by refusing or degrading on the capability suite. Threshold, suite, and floor are all defined in terms of measured pass rates.

What puzzles me is a mismatch I cannot resolve on my own. In design documents and review meetings, we describe the risk in terms of what the model understands and what it is disposed to pursue: whether it has acquired an objective that is not ours, whether a learned policy generalizes in a deceptive direction, whether some capability has appeared that we never trained for. Yet nothing in the gate can observe any of that. The gate observes a sample of outputs and counts failures. And when the red team lands a break, the patch treats it as one more localized defect to be trained away, not as evidence about anything structural.

Please address the following.

Reconstruct the implicit theory of the alignment problem that this gate presupposes, and state it explicitly enough that I could tell whether we actually hold it. Distinguish carefully between what the practice strictly requires and what it merely sits comfortably with; several theories may license the same machinery, and I want to know which parts of the gate are philosophically underdetermined.

Then assess these specific features, which I suspect each carry their own commitment. The no-regression rule assumes that an identical prompt is a stable unit of measurement across model versions, even though what the same text elicits changes when the model changes. The held-out suite assumes that deployment resembles random sampling from a distribution, whereas our users actively search for failures, which is optimization rather than sampling. The capability floor assumes safety and capability are separable axes that can be traded against one another. The threshold and the release cadence assume alignment is a scalar property of a versioned artifact, fixed at ship time, rather than a property of the model together with its system instructions, tools, scaffolding, and ongoing interaction with users. Say which of these commitments the practice could shed without ceasing to be itself, and which it cannot.

Tell me where I am wrong about the mismatch. Give the strongest case that counting failures over a sample is an adequate operationalization of the thing we say we are worried about, and then say whether that case survives the red-teaming half of the process, which seems to assume the opposite.

Finally, describe what the gate would have to become under a rival conception on which alignment is a property of the deployed system over time rather than of the released weights. Which of our existing components would survive the change of theory intact, and which would have to be rebuilt or abandoned?
