So, context, sorry in advance. I write novels — domestic ones, lots of people being quietly unfair to each other in kitchens — and my little brother Ari is getting married to Tess on June 14th and I've been handed the toast. About four minutes, call it 600-700 words. There will be roughly 130 people in the room and they are two entirely different species: Ari's whole department plus a scattering of visiting economists, i.e. actual bargaining theorists who have opinions about axioms, and then our family from outside Toledo plus about thirty of Tess's coworkers from the pediatric ICU, who mostly believe Ari "does something with computers." Both groups have to feel like the speech was for them. I should also be honest that I'm going to fictionalize this toast into the last chapter of the book I'm working on, so it can't be speech-y filler; it needs to hold up as prose when a stranger reads it cold.

The reason this isn't just a Hallmark card assignment: Ari has spent his career on the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution, and specifically on why he thinks Nash's independence-of-irrelevant-alternatives axiom is a mistake. His whole objection is that widening the set of things two people could agree to should be allowed to move the deal even when the extra options never get chosen — if making a third option available raises one person's best conceivable outcome, that person's claim on the agreement goes up. So his picture of a marriage is not slicing a fixed pie. It's: you locate where each of you would end up if you walked away, you locate each person's ideal — the best they could possibly do given the other's cooperation — you draw the straight line between the walkaway point and the ideal point, and the deal is wherever that line hits the edge of what's actually attainable. And the part that got me at one in the morning in a hotel lobby, the part I want the whole thing built out of, is what that line means: along it, both people get the same *fraction* of what they were each reaching for. Not the same amount. If your ideal sits much farther out than hers, you concede much more in absolute terms and it is still exactly fair by that measure. Build the toast on that. I'd also love one quiet wink for the front tables at the rival view — the egalitarian solution, which equalizes absolute gains instead of proportions and which Ari has argued against in print — that the economists will catch and nobody else will notice. There will also be at least one dyed-in-the-wool Nash-solution partisan at the head table, so please don't accidentally assert something he'd have to correct from his seat.

Hard constraints. One: Tess told me, very sweetly and very firmly, that she does not want to be "the other variable in his model," and she's right, so the speech cannot be a four-minute tour of Ari's axioms with her standing there holding a bouquet. Her own ideal point needs to be a real thing in the room — she's spent eleven years in an ICU and she has a completely different, much more concrete sense of what a good life looks like, and the symmetry requirement is the mathematical version of the point that you can't relabel the two people and get a different answer. Two: give the disagreement point its due without being morbid — the whole apparatus is undefined until someone answers honestly what happens if this doesn't work, and a good marriage seems to involve both people keeping that number in view and refusing to let it slide into either bravado or dread. Three: no equations, no Greek letters, no jargon that isn't paid for with a laugh first; don't say optimal unironically anywhere; make it funny in the first ninety seconds and soft at the end. If the affine-invariance idea shows up at all — that it doesn't matter what units the two of you measure your happiness in, so long as you're consistent — it has to arrive as a sentence about Toledo and about night shifts, not as a property of a solution concept. And don't end on the model. End on them.
