I've been going back and forth on the Boltzmann brain thing for a couple weeks now (I'm auditing a philosophy-of-cosmology seminar and it came up twice) and I think I've landed somewhere. The view I want to defend is roughly this: the BB argument is a genuine reductio, not a curiosity. If a cosmological model predicts that observers who are locally indistinguishable from us are overwhelmingly outnumbered by ones who fluctuated out of a thermal bath with fake memories of a past that never happened, then that model isn't just improbable on our data — it's unacceptable, full stop, because it can't be both true and justifiably believed. And I want to hold that this has real empirical teeth: it's supposed to discriminate among live models, e.g. by ruling out an eternal de Sitter phase with a stable vacuum, and favoring ones where the vacuum decays fast enough (I think the usual figure is a decay timescale shorter than something like twenty billion years, so that nucleation never gets a chance to accumulate), or where the dark energy is quintessence that eventually dies away rather than a true cosmological constant. I also don't think the "but we'd notice we're BBs" intuition does any work — noticing is exactly what a BB with a fake past would do.

So: what would count against this position? I want the stuff that should actually move me, sorted by how decisive it is, not a general survey of the literature. Specifically I'm wondering about the load-bearing assumptions and which of them could go. (a) The semiclassical estimate of the nucleation rate — is it even coherent to talk about fluctuations in a vacuum state that is stationary and in equilibrium? I have a vague memory that Carroll himself argues something like this about empty de Sitter space, which would make the reductio partly self-consuming: the same physics that kills the BBs might kill the argument for rejecting anything. (b) Whether a fluctuation-observer has beliefs at all, or whether "locally identical to an ordinary observer" is doing quiet a lot of work. (c) The self-locating step: going from "most observers are BBs" to "I am probably one" needs a measure over observers, and if the measure is up for grabs, does the reductio survive? (d) The reflexive worry — if my reasoning is undermined by the conclusion, isn't the arguement that got me there undermined too, and is that fatal or just a price to pay? (e) The empirical-leverage claim. Here's the datum that bugs me most: our best-fit cosmology has a positive cosmological constant and an asymptotically de Sitter future, which on the face of it means infinite BB nucleation, and yet basically nobody treats that as a reason to reject it. Is that evidence the argument has no teeth, or just evidence cosmologists haven't taken it seriously? Also worth asking whether things like the reverse-decay-path idea (a BB doesn't have to pop into existence all at once, it can assemble as a time-reversed decay sequence) strengthen or weaken my case.

Give me the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which ones a committed defender of this view would themselves concede as counting against it versus which ones they'd swat away, and why. I'd also like an honest verdict on whether the position has been insulated into unfalsifiability — since it functions as an a priori constraint on theory choice rather than a prediction, maybe it can't lose, and maybe that's fine, but I want to know what it risks if anything. And tell me which of the escape routes I listed are actually live given what we currently think the universe is like, versus which are just logically possible. No need for a reference list or citations, I'm not writing a paper, I just want the reasoning laid out.
