Twenty years ago my mother signed a document refusing certain kinds of life-prolonging treatment in the event of advanced dementia, and last month it stopped being hypothetical. She is now in that condition. She is not suffering. She likes the light in the kitchen in the afternoon, she likes being read to aloud, she likes her sons, though she no longer reliably knows which is which. My family is split cleanly down the middle about whether the document still governs, and each half thinks the other is proposing an obscenity. I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, so I have been reading around the problem on my own, and my conclusion is that the two positions cannot both be stated in prose without each one sounding like it has already answered the other. That is why I am asking for a poem rather than an argument.

Here is what I understand the two claims to be, and I want both of them held open rather than adjudicated. On one side: a life is a single whole, authored from the front, and the person who is now in the chair has lost the capacity to author or revise it; to set the directive aside is to let the most recent afternoon veto everything she ever judged about how her life should go. On the other side: whatever the paper says, the being now present is content, has not withdrawn the refusal, and cannot withdraw it, and is being acted upon on behalf of someone who merely shares her name; if the connections that made her the signer have dissolved, the signer's authority dissolved with them.

There are two further things I want the poem to gesture at without arguing. First, the ordinary force of an advance refusal seems to depend on the fact that the person who made it could always take it back; here the capacity to take it back is precisely what is missing, so the document's authority appears to rest on an inability, and I cannot tell whether that is what makes it binding or what makes it void. Second, it is possible that the woman who signed was describing a life she had never entered, and was simply wrong about what it feels like from the inside.

The form. Twenty-four lines, in two halves of twelve, separated by a single blank line, with no headings. The first half is the earlier self, in the register of the document itself: declarative, first person, the grammar of conditions and instructions. The second half is her afternoon now, and it is under a hard constraint — every word in the second half must have already appeared in the first. No new vocabulary enters the poem after the break. Within that constraint, each word must be doing different work than it did before the break: the same lexicon, no longer carrying the same commitments. In addition, each line of the second half should be shorter than the line directly above it in the first half; if that turns the thing into a puzzle instead of a poem, loosen it and tell me you did. The last line of the poem must be a line from the first half, repeated exactly, unmarked and unattributed, so that it arrives in her mouth rather than the document's. If you title it, the title must be one word from the first half that means something different by the end.

The test I have in mind: someone who has thought about this debate should be able to point at each half and name the claim it is making, and should still not be able to say which half the poem is on.

Two restrictions. Please do not borrow my own route into the problem — I build deployment systems in which a policy signed by an old release continues to bind a new release that cannot read it — so no commits, keys, signatures, versions, checksums, or rollbacks anywhere in the poem. And no clinical vocabulary, no elegiac tone, no saints and no victims: she should be neither pitiable nor inspiring, and I would rather the word *dignity* did not appear at all.
