Last night a friend and I argued for three hours about a historical analogy, and I should like a second opinion before I commit anything further to writing.

I am one of four editors of a report on how far historical precedent can bear on reasoning about transformative artificial intelligence. The member who chairs the group — call her Nora; she is a historian of technology — has over two years ruled on which candidate precedents are admissible as evidence in the report. Our charter's inclusion test is one sentence, and it is the source of the trouble: an analogue is admissible only if it isolates a decision rather than a trend — identifiable people choosing under uncertainty about consequences, and a surviving record of what they took themselves to be doing.

Nora's record. Admissible: European movable-type printing; the electrification of factories and households; surgical anaesthesia; the oral contraceptive pill; the private motor car as a consumer purchase. Inadmissible: nuclear weapons, save for one narrow question about a handful of decision-makers making irreversible commitments under mutual suspicion — she calls it the small-room problem and allows the analogy there and nowhere else; the Industrial Revolution, rejected because it is a period rather than a technology; the Green Revolution, of which she said only, "That one was done to people"; the standardisation of railway time, which she has never explained and which still puzzles me, since it looks for all the world like a member of the printing-and-telegraph family; and the highway system, which she separates sharply from the motor car itself. Her asides, which may or may not be evidence: of the pill, "Nobody had to be persuaded"; of electrification, "It took forty years, and everyone agrees that is the interesting part"; of printing, "It is the only one that carries its own argument with it."

The case before us next week is mine. I have proposed containerised freight — the boxed, standardised handling of cargo that spread from the late 1950s — as the best available analogue for a technology that dissolves a category of work while its gains arrive in prices rather than in wages, and that yields nothing until a standard has been negotiated and physical infrastructure rebuilt around it. Nora would not be drawn on her vote, saying she has not read the chapter and will not prejudge it. But she made two remarks I cannot place. First: "It is the nearest thing we have to a case where every number moved in the same direction and nobody who was affected by it could see any of them." Second, when I objected that the dockers who lost the work never chose the box, she said, "The dockers were not the practice; the practice was moving goods." I cannot tell whether either was a concession or a trap, and she declined to say more about railway time.

What I should like is this. Reconstruct at least two distinct rules that account for the whole of Nora's record — every acceptance and every rejection, including the two she has scoped and the one she has never explained — and that nonetheless diverge on containerisation. For each rule, state what it predicts about my case and what she would say in justifying the vote, and consider whether a third possibility is likelier still: that she admits it for one specified question and excludes it for the rest, as she did with nuclear weapons. Then tell me which reading you would bet on, and why, given last night's remarks. Then name the further candidate precedent, not yet before the group, that would most cleanly tell the rules apart, and explain why the cases already decided cannot do that work. Finally, sit in the chair's seat: if we wish to be consistent with our own charter and our own nine verdicts, what should we decide about containerisation, which existing precedent is the closest analogue to it, and — if the record genuinely underdetermines the answer — what principle would we be adopting by deciding either way?

Please write in American English: my own drafting habits are British and the other three editors are not. I am not after references, a bibliography, or a reading list; I want the reasoning, and I would rather you were forthright about which of my readings you think is mistaken.
