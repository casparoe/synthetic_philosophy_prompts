I teach an MSc option that I have rather grandiosely titled 'Pests and Persons: The Moral Status of Invertebrates', and this year I am co-teaching it with an entomologist and a vector-control officer seconded from the regional public-health authority. The module closes with a one-day workshop, and the room is always an awkward mixture: philosophy postgraduates, people whose working lives consist of killing insects at industrial scale, and two or three conservation biologists who regard the entire conversation about invertebrate welfare as a distraction from the collapse in insect biomass. Last year's session collapsed into cross-talk, for an instructive reason. A student who doubted that any arthropod was sentient and a student who thought all lethal control should cease turned out to endorse almost identical policies; meanwhile two students who both described themselves as insect welfarists disagreed about practically everything. What I want this year is a decision tree that sorts the room by what its members would actually decide, and I want it built from the logical space first, so that the positions fall out of the combinations of answers rather than being read off a list of camps.

Please write a decision tree of eight to ten nodes leading to twelve to fourteen leaves. Every node must be answerable without knowing which leaf it leads to, and as far as possible each node should be a verdict on a concrete case rather than a thesis about minds — nothing of the form 'do you think insects matter?' Build it around cases like these, and add others if you think they will do more work: a municipal authority aerially spraying an adulticide over a city after an outbreak of a mosquito-borne virus, knowing that the target population will rebound within weeks so that the spraying must be repeated, and that the drift kills a great many pollinators; a stored-grain warehouse choosing between fumigation, which kills everything quickly, and pheromone-based mating disruption plus sanitation, which is slower, dearer, and prevents reproduction rather than killing adults; a sterile-insect programme that mass-rears millions of flies, sterilises the males, and releases them so that wild matings produce no viable offspring, and whose welfare ledger includes the crowded conditions of the rearing facility and the eggs that never develop; a gene drive intended to suppress or eliminate a vector species altogether; a subsistence farmer facing a locust swarm whose choice is between the harvest and the sprayer; bed bugs in social housing, where the options are whole-room heat treatment, repeated insecticide applications, or tolerating the bites, the insomnia and the stigma; a householder dealing with ants in a kitchen, grubs in a lawn, or wood-boring beetles in the timbers of an old building, where nothing but convenience or tidiness is at stake; and the general background of crop production, where tillage kills soil invertebrates, no-till spares them but depends on herbicide that removes the weed habitat insects feed on, and harvesting kills a great deal either way. I want the tree to turn on the distinctions that actually separate people here rather than the familiar ones, so please make sure these axes appear somewhere in the branching: what decision rule one adopts under uncertainty about sentience (dismissing, precautionary, or credence-weighted expected value), and whether that rule is independent of what one believes; whether the harm that matters is death or suffering, since this determines whether a painless method discharges the duty or merely changes its shape; whether insect interests are discounted per capita in virtue of psychological simplicity, or count equally per unit of suffering; whether for a species that replaces any killed individual many times over the killing wrongs the individual at all, or is offset by its successors, and whether bringing farmed insects into existence can count as a benefit that offsets their slaughter; whether the identity of the agent — state, corporation, farmer, householder — changes what is permitted rather than merely how the reasons weigh; whether there is a duty to innovate, that is to develop and adopt less harmful methods at substantial cost, as distinct from a duty to pick the least harmful method currently on the shelf; and whether ecological value, including species, food webs and pollination services, constrains or competes, and in particular whether insect decline is itself a tragedy that makes population reduction impermissible even where individual welfare would improve.

Two ground rules before the format. First, no bibliography and no reading list: I want the architecture, not the scholarship, and this is going into a printed handout for a mixed room. Where a leaf has an established name, use it; where it does not, coin one and tell me you have coined it. Second, write in British English throughout, and gloss any entomological or public-health term on first use, since the philosophers in the room will not know it. Please structure the response in exactly these sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths: 'The Axes' (about 300 words), setting out the questions that generate the space and the options at each, before any tree is drawn; 'The Tree' (about 700 words), giving each node in order with its answer options and one line on what each answer commits the respondent to downstream; 'The Leaves' (about 600 words), one entry per leaf giving the name, the strongest one-sentence formulation of the position, its most awkward commitment, and which of the cases above it handles worst; 'Empty and Overcrowded Cells' (about 250 words), identifying combinations of answers that no one occupies and diagnosing for each whether it is incoherent, merely unattractive, or simply unexplored, and noting any region where several distinct positions have collapsed onto one leaf because my nodes lack resolution; 'What the Tree Cannot Separate' (about 200 words), naming the pairs of leaves that give identical verdicts on every case I listed and saying whether their difference is nonetheless substantive; and 'Confidence' (about 150 words), in which you tell me how confident you are in the structural claims you have made — in particular which of the axes you think are genuinely independent and which are entangled — and single out the one joint in the tree you are least sure of and why. Do not soften any leaf into a strawman, including the ones you clearly find unpersuasive; the point is that the entomologist and the abolitionist should each recognise themselves in their leaf and find it stated better than they could state it themselves.
