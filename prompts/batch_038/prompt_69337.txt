I'm doing an MA in philosophy of biology and this week our seminar finally gets to naturalising mental content — teleosemantics, indicators, all of it — and I've put this of until the last minute, which is my own fault. The format is that we're each handed a case and have to say what our supervisor would rule, which sounds easy except she's never actually stated a principle anywhere, she's just accumulated verdicts over the years in papers and in seminar, and half of them are only written up in my notes from last term. We meet Thursdays at three, in a room that is somehow always freezing, and there's a synthetic biology group coming in who've been given a slot to pitch a construct they want to build. So I need to be ready for it.

Her verdicts so far, as best I've reconstructed them: the honeybee waggle dance has genuine content, and she likes stressing that the dance is about a food source that nobody in the hive is currently looking at. The frog's retinal bug-detector has content, but the content is something like small-dark-thing-moving-over-there rather than fly — she's quite happy that a frog snapping at a ball bearing isn't making a mistake, because flies and small dark moving things have never come apart in its pond. A domestic thermostat has no content of its own; she called it "borrowed aboutness", the dial is about the temperature only because we made it so. The Venus flytrap does have content, grudgingly, "the cheapest content in nature" — two trigger hairs touched within a few seconds is about something. E. coli running and tumbling up a glucose gradient has none: "irritability, not aboutness", nothing in the bacterium stands for the sugar separately from the flagella just getting on with it. A pigeon that spent four months in an operant box learning to peck at pictures containing water does have content, and she was firm that four months of reinforcement is plenty, you don't need deep time. And an old robot vacuum's bumper and cliff sensors have none, engineered, so whatever aboutness they've got is ours.

Now the construct. They splice into a fruit fly a gene for an olfactory receptor taken from a moth species, so that one neuron in the fly's antenna now fires when that moth's pheromone is in the air; and with a second construct they wire that neuron into the fly's existing sugar-feeding circuit, so the fly flies upwind and extends its proboscis at the pheromone exactly as it would at a drop of sucrose. There are no moths anywhere in the facility, none ever have been. The pheromone is puffed in by the experimenters and every puff is followed within about a minute by sugar. The line has been bred with both constructs in place for forty-odd generations, all of it indoors. So: does that neuron's firing have content, and if it does, what is it about — the pheromone, moths, sugar, or the arrival of a reward? Which of her seven cases is the closest precedent, the pigeon or the robot vacuum or the frog, and does picking one actually decide anything? What I really want is the two or three rules that fit all seven verdicts simultaneously — bear in mind the bacterium and the thermostat are awkward for the obvious candidates — laid out so I can see exactly where they part company on the fly, then your best guess at her answer with the reasons for it, and finally one further case, ideally one I could describe to her on Thursday in a sentence, that would force the surviving rules apart. My worry is that at least two of them agree on everything she's ever said and that the disagreement is therefore idle, and I'd like to know whether that worry is right or whether I've just not thought of the discriminating example. Please don't give me a bibliography, the library copy is on loan to somebody else and I can't get hold of anything before Thursday — work it all from the verdicts.
