I retired three years back after 38 years in a lab doing trace analysis of river sediments — mostly mass spec, mostly waiting around for the instrument to stabilise, which turns out to be a fine way to acquire a lot of loose thoughts and no discipline. Since then I've been reading philosophy properly for the first time, badly and out of order, and there's a theme I keep coming back to that I'd like you to write me a story about rather than an essay, because every essay I read on it turns into a seminar. The theme: two people who don't share a moral outlook can still both come out ahead by compromising, for the same dull reason countries gain from trading wheat for cloth — because each cares a lot more about one issue than about the other, so swapping concessions isn't splitting the difference, it's a genuine bargain with something in it for both sides. But there's a second thought pushing the other way, which is that some things are supposed to be off the table entirely, and whoever trades them has done something to himself and not only to the thing. And a third, which is the one I actually can't make sense of: after a compromise that was by everyone's accounting the right call, people still behave as though they owe something — an apology, a reparation, keeping the stand of trees fenced off forever — as if a bargain that left both sides better off had left a stain on it anyway.

Here's the story I want. An inland valley, one family, the father has just died and left the land to two of his children with instructions that are ambigious on purpose, while the third sibling gets the house, the money, and no say at all. One of the two land-owning siblings thinks the valley is a working asset and that its highest use is a solar array that would power a town downstream and pay for a clinic the county refuses to fund; the other thinks the stand of old trees at the head of the valley is not a thing anyone may put a price on, full stop, and would sooner see the whole family poor than see it graded. Neither is a caricature, neither is confused about their own values, and their values cannot be converted into each other's units — that's the whole point, please don't let one of them be secretly persuadable. They make a bargain. Then a fourth party turns up, a developer or a utility, offering cash that dwarfs both plans, and the two of them have to work out whether what they struck was a compromise or just a slow sale with extra steps. End it on the decision, or on the moment right before it — no tidy resolution, no wise outsider, no dying-parent narration from beyond the grave.

Format, if you don't mind: hard cap of 1000 words, no epigraph, no title page, no dedication. And before the story, give me exactly one sentence saying which of the two value systems you think the story sides with — or whether you think it refuses to side at all, and if so why that isn't just a dodge. That sentence is the nearest thing to a bottom line a piece of fiction can have and I'd rather see it up front than have to guess; then the story, then stop, don't explain your choices afterwards.
