I'm a second-year graduate student, and my department's medieval philosophy reading group has just spent three weeks on Avicenna's flying man. As I understand the scenario, you are asked to suppose a human being created all at once, fully formed and adult, suspended in air or empty space: his sight is shrouded so that he perceives nothing external, the air around him is calm enough that he feels no perceptible current or pressure on his skin, and his limbs are held apart so that they never come into contact with one another. In that condition, Avicenna says, the man would nonetheless affirm the existence of his own self while affirming nothing about his limbs, his bowels, his heart, his brain, or any external thing; he would not even take his self to have length, breadth, or depth, and if it were possible for him to imagine a hand, he would not imagine it as part of himself or as a condition of his existing. The conclusion he draws is that the self is something other than the body and is apprehended without the body.

One member of the group keeps translating the whole thing into modern terms as a sensory deprivation tank: a person cut off from sight, sound, and touch who, in the resulting quiet, notices that he exists. He thinks that gloss is deflationary. On his telling, the flying man establishes only that self-awareness does not depend on sensory input — a perfectly naturalistic point about attention and introspection, entirely compatible with the self's being the brain — and the further step to an immaterial, non-bodily soul is a non sequitur Avicenna bolts on to give the passage theological work to do. Another member objects that the tank is not even the right modern cousin, and that what Avicenna is really running is a brain-in-a-vat case, which would put the passage in the neighborhood of external-world skepticism rather than philosophy of mind.

Could you audit the tank analogy for me? I would like an explicit mapping — what in the tank case is supposed to play the role of each element of Avicenna's setup (the fresh creation all at once, the still air, the separated limbs, the shrouded sight, the imagined hand that is not taken to be his), and where each of those pairings fails. Then sort the disanalogies into the ones that genuinely threaten his deflationary reading and the ones that are merely decorative. I am especially interested in what the tank picture quietly imports: it seems to hand you a subject who already has a body and a lifetime of memories of having one, along with ongoing interoceptive and vestibular input, and it recasts an ontological claim about what the self is as an epistemic claim about what can be known without evidence — but I would like that worked through rather than asserted. Finally, tell me whether the deflationary conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, and if the tank is the wrong analogy, what a better one would be and what it would have to include in order to carry the conclusion Avicenna actually draws.
