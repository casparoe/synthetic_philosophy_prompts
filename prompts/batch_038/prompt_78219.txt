I teach an upper-level seminar in political philosophy and the philosophy of law — fourteen students, one hundred minutes, weekly. They have already worked through the South African model of amnesty: individual application rather than blanket immunity, amnesty available only on full disclosure of all facts relevant to the act, and only where the act was associated with a political objective and proportionate to it. I want a simulation that makes them feel a structural problem with that design, one the literature tends to assert rather than stage.

The problem I want them to run into has three interlocking parts. First, a commission's power to purchase truth rests on the threat of prosecution, and every amnesty granted removes an instance of that threat from the table; applicants can see the remaining stock of threats shrinking. Second, the commission cannot commit: it must decide each application on its merits against published criteria, so it cannot promise future leniency in exchange for present candour, and an applicant cannot be confident that candour will be repaid. Third, the criteria can only be applied if enough people apply. Disclosure is checked against other people's disclosure, because the commission has no independent evidentiary base of its own; the relevant truth is a complementary good fragmented across applicants, and it is assembled by precisely the mechanism it is consumed by.

Design the session so that students encounter this by playing rather than by being told. Specifically, I need:

1. A fictional post-authoritarian state — invented, and deliberately not South Africa, Argentina, or Rwanda, so that nobody can coast on knowing how it actually turned out — whose amnesty statute reproduces the individualized, disclosure-conditional structure above. Roles: applicants holding private dossiers, commissioners bound by a one-page criteria card, representatives of victims' families with scoring sheets, and a prosecutor's office whose capacity to bring cases declines from round to round.

2. The materials written out in full, ready to print: the commissioners' criteria card, four to six applicant dossiers — each containing some facts nobody else holds, some that corroborate or contradict another dossier, and the name of one person not before the commission — the family scoring sheet, and the track for prosecutorial capacity. Paper and whiteboard only, no real money, no preparation exceeding an hour.

3. A minute-by-minute timeline for the hundred minutes, with the number of hearing rounds and what is announced when.

4. A payoff scheme in which at least one family confronts a genuine, non-dominated choice between securing a conviction and securing the location of a relative's remains, and in which the total truth yield depends on the order in which applications are heard.

5. Nothing that names the mechanism during play, and no distribution of the aggregate structure; the shape of the whole should only become visible in the debrief.

6. A debrief that maps particular moments in the session onto the distinctions at issue: amnesty as exchange versus amnesty as political settlement; individualized versus collective amnesty; the epistemic good of establishing what happened versus the retributive good of punishing those who did it; and the commitment problem peculiar to a body required to be impartial case by case.

7. An honest account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — at least three failures, with the fix for each. I expect commissioners to collapse into blanket grants or blanket refusals, cards to leak, and one articulate student to dominate; tell me what else.

8. A frank statement of which features of the real thing the activity cannot reproduce and that I must supply verbally, including the moral cost of reducing victims' interests to points at all.

9. Your judgment on which single parameter most determines whether the mechanism fires — the degree of overlap between dossiers, the verifiability of disclosure, the rate at which prosecutorial capacity decays, or whether decisions are announced before the next hearing — and how to set it for fourteen students rather than thirty.

10. Finally, the minimal change to the rules that would let the same session carry the affirmative case for conditional amnesty — the truths that would otherwise be permanently lost — instead of only the objection to it.

I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or a survey of the literature; I want apparatus I can hand out next Tuesday.
