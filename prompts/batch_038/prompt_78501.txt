Can you write me a short story for me — something like 2000 words — that gets at an idea I keep turning over and cant seem to settle? The main thing I want is for it to actually be a story: the ideas should live in scenes, bodies, shifts, dialogue, and if the abstract words "consciousness" and "unity" never appear as nouns in it, that's better, not worse. No essay with a plot stapled on.

Here's the idea, spelled out so you don't have to go looking for anything:

There's an old line of thought — it's in the Upanishads, and in the twentieth century it was picked up by a physicist who's now more famous for a thought experiment involving a cat than for any of this — that experience is never encountered in the plural. You only ever come across one: your own. So the number of minds in the world isn't eight billion and one, it's one, and the many-ness is appearance, like faces in mirrors. Set up a room full of mirrors and you see your face in all of them, each one looking exactly as real and solid as the one you see when you look directly, and none of them is a copy of the others, because there was only ever one face. The obvious objection is: then why don't I remember your life? The obvious answer is that memories hang off the bodies and brains, not off the subject — the way a single dreamer can play ten characters in a dream and each of them feels private from the inside, or the way someone with a gap in their memory is still, uncontroversially, one person.

I want to add a wrinkle of my own, which is the part I'd most like you to build the story around. A physicist I know once said the real trouble with that objection is that it assumes counting is even a meaningful thing to do here. With electrons, it isn't: two electrons in an atom are not two little beads with labels, and "is this the same electron as the one that was here a moment ago?" has no answer, not a hard-to-find answer but no answer at all. So maybe the correct claim isn't "there is one mind" — that's still a number — but "how many?" is simply not a question that applies. And if that's right, then nobody's private experience could ever be evidence for or against it, because there was never a fact of the matter for the evidence to be about.

Now the story. Roughly:

- The main character is a nurse on a step-down/palliative unit, twelve-hour shifts, and over a few months she starts having what she can only describe as waking up in her patients — not visions, not empathy, but arriving. She has bits of their inner life she shouldn't have: a smell of paraffin and orange peel, a sentence in a dialect she has never heard, the fact that a staircase in some house turns left at the top.
- Handle those details honestly. Most of them turn out to be traceable — a daughter mentioned it at the bedside, it's in the chart, she overheard two porters. One of them isn't traceable at all. Don't let that one item prove anything either way.
- The institution's response should be bureaucratic and humane and quietly insulting: an occupational-health referral, a wellbeing e-learning module, a suggestion that she's been saving up her annual leave for too long. Nobody accuses her of anything. That's what makes it unbearable.
- Give her a physicist in her life — a brother, an ex, a patient's son, whatever works — who delivers the electron point in a few sentences, in his own flat way, and who is not a mouthpiece for the theme. He should also be a bit unbearable himself.
- The last stretch should be practical rather than revelatory: she's on shift and has to decide about a dose that will stop the pain of the person in bed four, and she has to notice whether the two pictures — one subject, or no subject-counting at all, or eight billion sealed-off ones — change a single thing she's about to do with her hands. Whether they don't is the horror or the relief; you pick, and don't explain your pick.

A few constraints: don't name any real philosophers, physicists or scriptures in the story itself; keep the physics talk to a handful of sentences; no "it was all a dream," no deathbed revelation, no scene where two people argue positions at each other for a page and a half. Give her feet that hurt, a car that won't start in the morning, a sandwich she eats standing up. And end on something concrete — a sound, an object, a piece of paperwork — rather than a thought about the meaning of it all.
