Im a retired plant virologist, forty years mostly on viroids — naked circular RNA that infects potatoes and borrows the host's own polymerases, so about as far from self-sustaining as a biological object can get. Which means forty years of being asked at parties whether my organisms are alive, and forty years of saying "depends what you mean," which nobody likes. Since I hung up the pipette Ive been reading the philosophy-of-biology version of that argument and I've become convinced most of it is people defending a boundary that isnt there.

My grandson is an undergrad and has this idea that peer review is a solemn temple. I'd like to disabuse him, and amuse myself. So: write me a parody peer review report, with the full genre trappings — the reviewer's own summary of the manuscript, then Major Comments numbered, then Minor Comments numbered, then the confidential Comments to the Editor, then the recommendation line (accept / minor revision / major revision / reject). Reviewer 2 at an invented journal in origins-of-life research. Around 900 to 1100 words.

The manuscript is invented too, and here's the setup so you have something concrete to chew on. A group has run a droplet system in a turbidostat for several hundred generations. The droplets have a lipid boundary, take up an activated feedstock monomer from the medium, grow, divide once they get too big, carry a template strand that gets copied with errors, shrink in a salt gradient, and their lineages measurably outcompete one another. The authors claim the thing is alive, and their argument is that it ticks every box on the standard list — boundary, metabolism-ish, growth, reproduction, heritable variation, response to stimulus, evolution — plus the working definition the astrobiology crowd uses, which as I recall runs roughly: a self-sustained chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution.

The jokes have to come from the science and the definitional logic being right, not from the reviewer being a fool. He should be competent, fair, genuinely hoping the paper works, and completely deadpan — no winking at the reader. The comedy is that his own rigor keeps flipping the verdict: every criterion he insists on, applied honestly, either lets in something nobody calls alive (a flame, a growing crystal, a self-copying program) or kicks out something everybody does (a mule, a tun-stage tardigrade, a seed bank, a virus, and — as he eventually has to notice — my own viroids).

Things I want him to work hard on: 'self-sustained' — the feedstock is supplied, so is the droplet self-sustained or is it being sustained, and at what point in the chain from the flask back to the sun does self-sufficiency get handed over; the fact that the winning lineage wins by sticking to the reactor wall so it doesn't get washed out, which makes its not obvious what was selected; the droplets "responding to stimuli" by osmosis; and the authors' fallback that if it isn't alive yet, give it more generations — in which case which generation, and how would you tell. Also have him ask, and fail to resolve, which boxes are supposed to be necessary and whether the list is jointly sufficient, so that the authors end up having cited the whole list at him instead of naming the criterion doing the work. And work in the old thermodynamics point about an organism keeping itself away from equilibrium by feeding on free energy — as a paraphrase, never as a quotation.

Ground rules: no bibliography, no real papers, no citations of any kind; anything the reviewer says the authors should have cited is either invented or generic ("the standard review"). Don't put quotation marks around words attributed to any real person — I dont want invented quotes floating around. Authors, journal, and manuscript number all invented. And keep it legible to an undergrad who's never sat through a definitions-of-life workshop, while making someone who has laugh rather than wince.
