My friend and I got into the calculation debate last week and he walked me into something I cant get out of. His argument:

1. Rational economic calculation requires market prices for capital goods, and such prices exist only where independent owners actually exchange those goods.
2. Inside a firm there's no such exchange. Divisions get transfer prices set administratively, and a vertically integrated multinational produces much of its own input, so a lot of its capital goods never get sold to anybody at all.
3. So by (1), rational calculation inside a large firm is impossible.
4. But it obviously isn't. These firms shift capital between divisions, close plants, outlast competitors, and go under when they misjudge -- which is exactly the profit-and-loss test (1) says calculation needs.
5. Therefore (1) is false: calculation doesn't require genuine market prices for capital goods, only something that does the same job.
6. And if internally-set accounting prices can do that job at firm scale, they can do it at any scale. So there is no calculation problem at any size, and the whole Mises-Hayek-Lange business was about bookkeeping conventions rather than anything economic.

(6) is the part I refuse to accept -- it seems to erase the difference between a firm and an economy instead of explaining it, and neither side in the debate ever conceded it. I can't tell whether the trouble is that (2) is just false, or that (3) doesn't follow from (1) and (2), or that (6) overgeneralizes from (5), or some mix. Which step should I reject, why did it look fine going in, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd also like to know whether Hayek's version of the worry -- which I don't think is really about prices -- survives whatever you do to the argument.
