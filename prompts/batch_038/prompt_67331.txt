Third year in a philosophy of math/logic program, and I've sort of wandered into a metaphysics hole I need help climbing out of. I'm drafting the chapter of my dissertation that's supposed to defend a restricted finitism — not about mathematics (I'm happy with Cantor, I think ZFC is fine, I don't want to revise set theory) but about concrete reality: my claim is that no actually infinite collection of concrete particulars can exist. The way I've been cashing it out, partly to dodge the usual complaints that I'm just doing bad intuition-pumping with Hilbert's hotel, is with a structural principle I've been calling the Dedekind constraint: any collection of concrete things must be Dedekind-finite, meaning you cannot pair off the whole collection with a proper part of it. The thought is that this is what's really going wrong in the hotel cases — the manager shuffling everyone from room n to room n+1 to make space for one more guest, and the reverse version where countably many guests check out of the odd-numbered rooms and the hotel is still full — plus the counting-from-eternity cases where an infinite past supposedly has to be traversed. So the principle does the work and the hotels are just illustrations. My supervisor, who works on set theory proper, read the section and said the principle was too weak to get me what I want, and he wouldn't say more than that, which has been driving me crazy for a week.

So: what would count against this view? I want a genuine map of its exposure, ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to separate the things that would actually refute it from the things that just make me squirm. A few places I already suspect it's soft, which you should push on hard rather than politely nod at: (1) does the Dedekind constraint even entail finitude? My recollection is that without the axiom of choice there are models with infinite but Dedekind-finite sets, so a collection of concrete things could be infinite and still satisfy my principle — is that a refutation (my principle licenses the very thing I'm trying to rule out), a loophole I should happily embrace, or a sign that I've stated the principle in the wrong logical setting altogether, given that choice is arguably not a live question about physical collections either way? (2) What about spacetimes in the Malament-Hogarth family that people invoke for relativistic hypercomputation — an observer who takes in the entire infinite history of another worldline in finite proper time? If one of those is physically possible, do I have to deny that, and does my denial amount to just assuming the conclusion? (3) Continua: if spacetime is a continuum, there are already uncountably many concrete points sitting there, and my principle seems to force me into atomism about space and time as a separate substantive commitment I hadn't signed up for. (4) The infinite-past stuff: does the traversal argument survive the standard objection that an infinite past has no first moment, so there's nothing to traverse from, and how much of my hotel reasoning secretly depends on supertasks being impossible — given that Benacerraf's old reply to Thomson and Black is usually read as showing that supertasks aren't ruled out by anything like the arguments people give?

Two more things I want, and please be blunt about both. First, does the view risk anything at all? If any observation — an infinite regress, an endless chain of causes, a cosmological model with infinitely many galaxies — can always be redescribed as finite-but-unbounded, or as an artefact of an idealized mathematical model rather than a concrete collection, then has it been qualified into immunity, and is that a defect for a metaphysical thesis the way it would be for an empirical one? I'd like to know what my view predicts that the actualist-about-concrete-infinity rival doesn't, if anything. Second, tell me which defeaters the defenders themselves would concede as counting against them — I suspect the Craig-style proponents of the kalam premise and the potentialists in the Linnebo vein would not draw the line in the same place, and I want to know whose concession practice I should be imitating and why. I'm presenting this at a work-in-progress session in about two weeks and half the department thinks the whole project is a category error, so anything that helps me say crisply what would change my mind will be worth more to me than more ammunition for the view.
