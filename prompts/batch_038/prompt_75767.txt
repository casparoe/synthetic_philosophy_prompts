I run a small program at a bioethics funder, and I need help building a defensible funding agenda rather than a survey. Our regional health system has begun piloting an artifact we've been calling a *values transcript*: at the point of a dementia diagnosis, and renewed annually for as long as capacity persists, the patient spends six to ten hours in structured recorded conversation with a trained interviewer whose job is to draw out not a checklist of interventions refused or accepted, but the patient's own ranking of goods, her reasons for those rankings, and — this is the part that interests us — her explicit instructions about how the transcript is to be read later. Some patients specify conditions of application ("if I am still visibly glad to hear this music, do not treat my earlier remarks about music as governing"), some name which of their commitments they regard as load-bearing and which as incidental, and some delegate the interpretive question to a particular person rather than to a document. The system also reports two empirically messy facts: transcripts are far more detailed and internally qualified than standard advance directives, and when clips are shown to patients in moderate dementia, some endorse the person on screen, some disown her, and a great many are simply indifferent.

Two camps have formed on our review panel. One says the transcript mostly settles things: if we now have unusually good evidence of what the earlier self actually wanted, the long-running dispute over whether her wishes should bind is largely a data problem, and philosophy has little left to do. The other says this conflates two very different kinds of authority — the transcript is an epistemic improvement, telling us better what the earlier self wanted, and it does nothing at all about the normative question of why that should matter to a patient who no longer endorses it, who may not be the same person in the relevant sense, and whose present experience of her own life may be good. I find myself mostly with the second camp, but I suspect the richer artifact creates genuinely new problems that neither camp is describing well: that greater specificity multiplies the ways a transcript can fail to apply to the situation that eventually arises, that a patient's recorded rules for interpreting her own later words may or may not be self-binding, and that endorsement or disowning by someone whose capacity is impaired may carry more or less weight than either camp allows. We have roughly $2.4 million over three years to support conceptual work — philosophy and law, not clinical trials or instrument validation — enough for four to six projects, and we shortlist at the next board meeting.

So: which three open questions should we fund first, in what order, and why? Please treat the ranking as the deliverable and argue for it explicitly, along the dimensions we actually use: how much else turns on the answer, whether the question is tractable within three years by people trained in philosophy rather than in geriatric medicine, whether it is neglected relative to its importance (there is a great deal of writing on precedent autonomy in general and very little on the specific puzzles this artifact creates), and whether a good answer would change what clinicians, courts, or families do on a Tuesday. I want to see the dependency structure: which question, if answered badly, would corrupt the others, and which could safely wait because most positions on it converge on the same practical upshot. Equally important, tell me what we should *not* fund — at least two prominent questions in this debate that you think are overrated or that the transcript renders moot, with reasons. Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I am not asking you to demonstrate command of the literature but to identify where the live conceptual work is, and you should engage with existing positions by describing them rather than by citing them.

Format: begin with a section titled **Bottom line** (about 120 words) giving the three funded questions in order, one sentence each on why. Then **What the transcript changes, and what it does not** (about 250 words), which should sharply separate epistemic from normative authority and say which of our two panel camps is closer to right and where each is wrong. Then **The shortlist, ranked** (about 300 words per question), where each entry states the question precisely enough that a philosopher could begin working on it Monday, explains why it is upstream of other questions rather than parallel to them, says why it is tractable in this timeframe and by this kind of person, describes what a successful result would look like, and notes what would be worth keeping even if the project fails. Then **Deferred and deprioritized** (about 250 words). Finally, **Crux and reconsideration** (about 200 words): identify the single consideration most likely to reorder your list, and state plainly what argument or piece of evidence would change your mind about the ranking, together with the point at which you think your own reasoning here is most likely to fail.
