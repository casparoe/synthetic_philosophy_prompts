I sit on the acquisitions and authentication committee of a museum with oddly mixed holdings: paintings, manuscripts and first editions, eighteenth-century furniture, a wine cellar, and an archive of scores and historical recordings. Every time a disputed object comes before us we rebuild our standard from scratch, and the arguments turn out to be the same ones in different clothes. Before the next meeting I want the specification written down.

Set out the desiderata — what any account of why an original is worth more than an indistinguishable copy has to deliver — and give, for each requirement, the case that motivates it. The list has to cope with at least these: a flawless forgery of a painting, perceptually indiscernible from the original, which seems worthless the moment it is exposed; that same flawless copy signed and dated by the person who actually made it, which now looks like a legitimate achievement; an exact re-performance of a Baroque score, where nobody thinks the copy is second-rate; an artist who re-executes her own famous early canvas decades later, more skillfully; a panel painting whose surviving surface is largely the product of successive restorations, so that the "original" is itself a kind of copy; and the openly labelled reproductions in our furniture and wine holdings, where the market's easy indifference to copies needs explaining rather than being assumed.

Then tell me which requirements are genuinely binding and which are the prejudices of a particular camp smuggled in as common sense, and identify the subset that cannot be jointly satisfied. If the honest conclusion is that nothing can honor both the intuition that aesthetic value is available to perception and the intuition that provenance bears on that value aesthetically and not merely morally, state that as the result rather than papering over it. Finish by naming the single requirement that does the most work in eliminating candidates.
