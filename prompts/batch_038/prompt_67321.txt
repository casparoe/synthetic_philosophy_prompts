I direct the incident investigation program at a mid-sized specialty chemicals plant, and I have become convinced that our reporting procedure rests on assumptions about causation that no one here has ever examined. I would like a rigorous reconstruction of what the procedure presupposes. Please write your response in American English.

Here is the procedure. Any incident producing injury, a release, or damage above a set threshold must be investigated by a team working from a standard form. The form has three causal fields: "root cause," "contributing causes," and "conditions." Exactly one item may be entered as the root cause. Our written guidance instructs investigators that the root cause is the earliest link in the causal chain that was within the organization's control — the first point at which someone could have done something differently — and that everything downstream of it, along with everything that was never controllable at all, belongs in the other two fields. So the weather goes under conditions, the reactivity of the material goes under conditions, the site layout goes under conditions, and what lands in the root cause field is almost always something a person or a department could have done otherwise.

Two further features matter. First, the root cause we identify is nearly always an absence: no written procedure, no task-specific training, no inspection at this interval, no barrier or alarm, no permit requirement. We essentially never write that a pipe corroded, that a gasket failed, or that a vapor ignited; those are recorded as contributing causes or conditions, and the root cause is the missing control that let them matter. Second, every root cause must be paired with a corrective action bearing a named owner and a due date, and that action is later "verified" by an auditor confirming that a document exists, that the training was delivered, or that the procedure was revised — never by any demonstration that the incident would have been prevented had the action already been in place.

Three things puzzle me.

1. The same company runs a confidential near-miss reporting system whose training material insists that accidents are almost never the product of a single cause, that they arise when many latent conditions line up, and that the search for one root cause is precisely the error that ends in blaming an operator. That sits awkwardly beside a form with one mandatory root-cause field.

2. Our "earliest controllable link" rule once produced a strange result. A vapor cloud ignited between two pipe racks; the team traced the chain back to a decision made eleven years earlier, during a capital project, to site the racks closer together than our current standard would allow. That was controllable then, but nothing could be done about the spacing now, and the corrective action we filed was a revision to hot-work permitting plus an additional gas detector — neither of which touches the spacing. The item named as the cause was not the item we intervened on, and the intervention we did make was available at a dozen other points in the chain.

3. The root cause field is never left blank. In six years of files, some variant of inadequate procedure, inadequate training, or inadequate supervision has always been available and has always been used. If a field can be filled by the same small family of answers on every occasion, I am not certain it is reporting a fact about the world.

Some of my colleagues say the point of a root cause is that it is the factor such that, had it been different, the incident would not have occurred; others say the point is that it is the factor we could have changed. In the second puzzle those two tests plainly came apart, and the form did not register the difference.

With all that in view, please do the following:

- Reconstruct, as precisely as you can, the implicit account of causation the procedure presupposes. State which claims about causes it cannot do without, as opposed to which it merely happens to fit comfortably.
- Identify the rival accounts of causation the procedure rules out or cannot accommodate, and explain what in the form or the guidance rules them out.
- Say whether the control filter commits us to the thesis that only manipulable factors can be causes, or whether the procedure could function without that thesis — and, if it could, what the filter is then doing.
- Explain how the procedure's consistent preference for absences as causes bears on the reconstruction. My understanding is that omissions and absences are a recognized difficulty for at least one family of accounts of causation, though I could not say which or why.
- Point out where the procedure is philosophically underdetermined: where several incompatible theories would license exactly the same form, the same field structure, and the same filling-in habits.
- Tell me concretely what would have to change — in the form, in the guidance, and in the verification step — if we adopted each of the principal rival accounts in turn, and which of those changes would cost us something we currently value.
- Address the tension in the first puzzle directly: is it a genuine inconsistency in what we presuppose, or can one account make sense of both the single-root-cause form and the many-latent-conditions training?

I am not a philosopher. Define every technical term you introduce, and do not assume I will recognize a name, a label, or an argument that has not been described in this message. Please write throughout in American English.
