I'm a software engineer — I write training loops, schedulers, and cache layers — and I have no formal philosophy training. I'd like the philosophical debate about pure time preference translated into the vocabulary I actually use, as faithfully as you can manage, followed by a residue report telling me what didn't survive the translation.

Here is the debate as I understand it, so you know what has to be carried across. First, the position that pure time preference is irrational: if you hold everything else fixed — no uncertainty about whether the future will arrive at all, no expectation that future people will be richer or poorer, no effect on what you can do now — then discounting a benefit merely because it occurs later is no better a reason than discounting it merely because it occurs far away in space, or because it happens to someone other than you. Frank Ramsey put it sharply in his 1928 paper on how much a nation ought to save, calling the practice of discounting later enjoyments "ethically indefensible" and attributing it to "the weakness of the imagination." (The same Ramsey of Ramsey theory, which is the only reason I'd heard of him.) Second, the technical objection from the other side, as I understand it: in an optimization over an unbounded horizon, if an agent is exactly indifferent between a benefit now and the identical benefit one period later, that indifference can be iterated without limit, so any benefit can be postponed forever and no program is optimal; a strictly positive impatience term is what prevents the paralysis. Third, the empirical layer: human discounting is steeper at short horizons than at long ones, which makes it dynamically inconsistent, and the usual remedy is commitment rather than a change in the rate.

Now the translation. Use only my working vocabulary: the discount factor on a return, the smoothing coefficient of an exponential moving average, TTLs and staleness, deadline and weighted-round-robin scheduling, compounding and the opportunity cost of committed resources, the per-period survival probability of the process, amortization, and immutable logs. Please be honest rather than accommodating: if a claim only translates by quietly changing what it asserts, say so instead of counting it as a fit.

Then the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Specifically: does my vocabulary contain any term for a discount that survives once survival probability, opportunity cost, staleness, and dynamic inconsistency have all been set to zero? If it does not, is that because my vocabulary is missing something real, or because "pure time preference" names nothing once the instrumental reasons are subtracted? Also: my frameworks treat the past as fixed because logs are immutable, not because the past matters less — does that hand me the past/future asymmetry the ethicists want, or something else entirely? And does the verdict "indefensible" have a counterpart at all — a bug, a misconfiguration, a spec violation — or is there simply no normative slot in my language for it to occupy?

One last data point, and I'd like you to say whether it has a counterpart. Three years before the savings paper, in a short essay published only after his death, Ramsey wrote of the world's eventual cooling and end that "its present value at compound discount is almost nothing," adding, "I apply my perspective not merely to space but to time." In my terms that reads as a gap between the discount rate you defend in a design review and the one your code actually runs with. Is that gap the whole of the debate, or is the debate about which of the two rates is the bug?
