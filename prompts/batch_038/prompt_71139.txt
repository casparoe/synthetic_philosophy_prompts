I'm putting together a module for a comparative constitutional law seminar on how courts police the boundaries of the electorate, and I've been reconstructing the reasoning behind one court's franchise decisions over the past dozen years. I'm keeping the jurisdiction deliberately vague: I want the structure of the reasoning, not the authority, so please don't supply real case names, statutes, or citations. Here is the cluster of verdicts I've assembled.

1. Lowering the voting age to sixteen for municipal elections: upheld. The same lowering for national elections: struck down.
2. Municipal voting rights for non-citizen permanent residents with at least eight years of continuous residence: upheld. National voting rights for that same group: struck down.
3. Citizens living abroad: retained on the register for ten years after departure, removed thereafter: upheld.
4. Pre-trial detainees may vote; prisoners serving sentences of under one year may vote; prisoners serving a year or more may not: upheld.
5. Persons placed under full legal guardianship may not vote; persons carrying a psychiatric diagnosis who are not under guardianship may: upheld.
6. A statute conditioning the franchise on passing a civics examination: struck down.
7. Voting made compulsory for citizens but optional for the resident non-citizens enfranchised under (2): upheld.

The next docketed case is the one I've been asked to brief. A citizen emigrated twelve years ago and was removed from the register when her ten-year period under (3) expired. She does not vote in her country of residence, but she has filed domestic tax returns every year on rental income from a property she still owns, both of her children attend school in the country, and she returns several times a year. She challenges the removal on the ground that the cutoff treats her ties as having lapsed when in fact they never did.

What I want is a prediction about the outcome together with the reasoning that generates it. Lay out the two or three principles on which these verdicts could jointly rest — membership, durable subjection to the polity's coercive authority, capacity to form a political judgment, stakeholding or the sharing of burdens, punitive sanction, or something else — and say what each predicts here. Flag which of them the seven clusters already strain: the pairing of (5) with (6) looks to me like the sharpest test of any capacity-based reading, and (3) and (4) are awkward for a pure membership reading. Then tell me which single principle best unifies the whole set, give me your best guess about the ruling and why you prefer it to the alternatives, and name one further hypothetical case — described concretely enough that I could pose it to the seminar — that would cleanly separate the two leading principles. My co-instructor's view is that the court is doing nothing more principled than tracking the constitutional text and the political salience of each affected group, and that hunting for an implicit rule here is a mistake; take that objection seriously rather than waving it away. Finally, if the court rules the way you predict, say what principle it would thereby be committing itself to for future cases, and in particular whether it would be settling on a criterion that could later be turned against the eight-year municipal residents enfranchised under (2).
