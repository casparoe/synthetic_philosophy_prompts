So my brother does research at a mid-size lab and over the holidays we got into a long argument about the competitiveness constraint — the idea that safety work costs you capability and therefore market position, so people who'd gladly pay an alignment tax get pushed into not paying it. He thinks the whole framing is confused and self-serving. I think there's a real principle underneath and I've been trying to pin it down by writing out my gut verdicts on cases. Here they are, roughly in the order I typed them into my notes app:

- Lab A holds a release back three months to run evals, knows a rival will ship something comparable in that window, tells nobody why: admirable.
- Lab B does the exact same three-month hold but puts out a blog post naming the specific eval it failed, so every rival learns what to expect and what to hide: same cost, and now I think it's worse than doing nothing.
- Lab C makes safety improvements to its own model, publishes them openly, rivals free-ride, and this sets C back maybe two months relative to a do-nothing C: unambiguously good, maybe the best case on the list.
- Lab D is certain to lose the race regardless — it's fourth, it's underfunded — and spends 5% of its capability budget on alignment work nobody copies: good, but I can't say why, since it changed nothing about who wins or how the winner behaves.
- Lab E slows down, loses the race, and the winner turns out to be the least careful actor in the field: worst case on the list, worse than B.
- Lab F slows down, loses, and the winner is careful and was going to win anyway: totally fine, arguably praiseworthy.
- Lab G signs a pact with its one main rival to slow down by the same amount and open their eval suites to each other, with a way to check compliance: gold standard. But I notice my enthusiasm here isn't about the tax being small, it's about the pact being checkable.
- Lab H pays a big tax to build a genuinely safer model, ships six months late, and it turns out customers wanted the safer thing so H wins anyway: I don't know what to feel about this one. It's like the tax wasn't a tax.

The pair I can't reconcile is B versus C. Both pay a real cost and both hand something to competitors, and my verdicts are opposite. D versus E also bothers me: D changes nothing and is good, E makes the outcome worse and is awful, but the difference between them looks like pure luck about who's sitting behind you in the standings rather than anything the lab actually did.

One thing I made up to argue about with my brother, feel free to throw it out: I want to seperate the alignment tax, which I'll use for a permanent capability cost that stays paid, from what I'll call the alignment toll, a one-time delay you can sometimes recoup by shipping later at the same quality. My hunch is that my verdicts track the tax/toll distinction plus something about legibility — whether competitors can see the cost and correctly infer something from it — rather than tracking expected effect on the outcome. But that's a hunch, not an argument.

So: is there a principle, or a small set of them, that gets these verdicts, or nearly all? Please answer in these sections, in this order:
