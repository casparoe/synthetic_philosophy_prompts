I am the standards lead in the central communications office of a metropolitan government of roughly nine hundred thousand residents. My two reviewers and I clear about forty items a week before release: press releases, fact sheets, briefing slides, social posts, corrections, and the occasional official statement that ends up attached to a legal record. Almost every one of them is accurate in the ordinary sense — the numbers reconcile with our own data, the quotes are correctly transcribed, the sourcing is documented. The failures we actually have are of a different kind, and our existing accuracy workflow does not catch them. I want to build that second screen on Arendt's account of lying in politics and of the relationship between truth and politics: specifically her distinction between factual truth and rational truth, her claim that factual truth has a coercive, non-negotiable character that makes it unwelcome in a domain built on persuasion and opinion, her treatment of image-making as the characteristic modern political falsehood, and her insistence that the damage of such falsehoods is not that people come to believe any particular lie but that the shared world of settled fact stops being common property.

Three recurring patterns are what I need the procedure to sort:

1. Selective construction. Every figure is correct and citable, but the choice of figures, the baseline, the comparison period, and the ordering are arranged so that a reader arrives at a belief about a contingent matter of fact that our own data do not support.
2. Anticipatory image. A commitment, a council vote, a signed contract, or a target date is expressed in the grammar of an accomplishment, so that the record later says the thing happened on the day it was announced.
3. Quiet revision. An already-released item is corrected or replaced online with no marker, which may improve accuracy in the individual case while making the record whatever the office currently says it is.

Please write the screen my office can run. I want an ordered set of tests, each with a stated pass condition and a stated verdict on failure — release as drafted, release with a named repair, hold for the communications director, or do not release, together with what we say instead in that last case. Give me the escalation triggers separately, since the five-minute first pass cannot carry them. It has to fit on two pages, be runnable by staff who have never read Arendt and will not be reading her, and be usable by a reviewer whose boss wants the item out before the evening news. Say explicitly which of her distinctions you have preserved, which you have dropped as unworkable in a municipal press office, and why, since the translation is the hard part.

Then run it on three items. First: a release announcing a thirty-one percent year-over-year fall in reported street robberies, sourced and arithmetically correct, where the same dataset shows the reporting threshold changed partway through the year and the comparison period contains two months in which the affected district was closed to the public; leadership wants it today and I expect it to clear — tell me plainly if it should not, and tell me whether the obvious repair of adding a qualifying sentence is actually sufficient or is itself a form of the pattern it appears to fix. Second: a social post stating that the city "has fixed" a structurally deficient bridge, where the funding is approved, the contract is signed, and work begins in the spring. Third: a draft that silently replaces a three-week-old fact sheet whose lead figure was wrong by a factor of ten; I regard this as unambiguously good, because the replacement is more accurate, and I want the procedure to overrule me if it disagrees, with the specific marker it requires and the reason that marker is not merely a formality.

Finally, state the limits. Tell me what the screen cannot do: how it should behave on items where experts inside the agency genuinely disagree about the underlying facts, on forecasts, which we publish constantly and which have no settled fact to be faithful to, and on a drafter who believes the image being projected. Mark every point where what looks like a test is in fact a judgment call someone has to own.

Do not offer me a menu of plausible Arendtian readings, or a survey of what she might have said, or a set of options for me to choose among. Take a position: commit to one reading of her account, build the procedure on it, defend that choice against the most obvious rival standard — a well-run verification process, or a simple test of whether a statement would mislead a reasonable reader — and say what my office gives up by adopting yours. Where a test depends on a contested interpretation, flag it in a line and keep the test. Everything the procedure needs must be inside your answer; do not point me to sources, and do not include a bibliography or reading list.
