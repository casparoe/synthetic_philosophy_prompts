Last night a friend and I argued for the better part of four hours about the substantivalism–relationism debate over spacetime and ended, so far as I could tell, exactly where we started. What I want from you is a diagnosis of the disagreement rather than an adjudication of it. I should add that I am working away from my library and offline, so I am not after references, a literature map, or a tour of the positions; I want your own reconstruction of what each side is actually claiming and of why the two cannot be brought into contact.

Here is how I understand the shape of the dispute. The substantivalist tradition holds that spacetime is something over and above matter and its spatial and temporal relations — a structure that persists through the redistribution of material content and that supplies the standard of absolute acceleration Newton needed to explain the climbing water in the bucket and the tension in the cord joining the two revolving globes. The relationist tradition, running from Leibniz's deployment of sufficient reason and of the indiscernibility of indiscernibles against the idea that God might have chosen to situate the whole universe elsewhere, through Mach's proposal that inertia is owed to the distribution of distant matter rather than to any background, holds that a manifold of bare points is an idle posit: permuting or translating the entire universe changes nothing that could be observed, and so ought to count as changing nothing at all. Then came the hole argument, in its revived form, which appeared to turn the substantivalist's own representational machinery against him: if the points of a manifold possess an individuality independent of the fields defined upon them, diffeomorphisms generate distinct models agreeing everywhere outside some region but disagreeing inside it, and the theory becomes radically indeterministic. The familiar replies — treating diffeomorphically related models as representing one and the same physical possibility, denying that manifold points are genuine individuals, retreating to the metric field as the only substance with a claim to be called spacetime — each seems to surrender much of what the relationist demanded while declaring victory.

My friend's position, which I want you to take seriously rather than merely rehearse, is that the argument has ceased to be substantive at all: what remains after the hole argument is a choice of formulation, settled only by theoretical virtues such as parsimony and by which quantities one is prepared to regard as physically meaningful, and the two camps are in fact answering different questions under a single label — one asking what the representational apparatus of a spacetime theory commits us to, the other asking whether there is a container. My own suspicion is that a live ontological question survives underneath it, namely whether the commitments to point-identity that the hole argument is usually said to exploit are a genuine theoretical cost or merely a verbal one, and whether a formulation that dispensed with manifold points altogether while preserving the empirical content of the theory would resolve anything or simply relocate the issue.

So: what kind of disagreement is this? Is it verbal, a quarrel over theoretical virtue dressed as one over ontology, two distinct questions pursued under one name, or a substantive disagreement whose content has been displaced into the formal machinery of the theory and is therefore hard to see? Is there a hidden formal or empirical question doing the work — something that could in principle be settled by a theorem, by a successful reformulation, or by an observation, as opposed to by argument? And given whatever diagnosis you reach, what would count as progress here, if anything at all; and if nothing would, explain why the stalemate is the predictable consequence of the structure of the debate rather than of anyone's bad faith or want of imagination.
