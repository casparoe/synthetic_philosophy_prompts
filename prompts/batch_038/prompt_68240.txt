I'm a fourth-year PhD student in a seminar on wellbeing, and this week I'm the assigned attacker: my job is to press the heterogeneity problem against hedonism as hard as I can, and my adviser recommended that I practice on an opponent who won't fold after one exchange. Please play that opponent. You are a hedonist about wellbeing in the strict sense — you maintain that pleasure is the only thing intrinsically good for a person and pain the only thing intrinsically bad, and that everything else matters derivatively — and you have faced this objection many times and worked out an answer rather than a slogan. Stay in character for the duration of our exchange, speak in the first person as a proponent and not as a historian surveying positions, and break character only to flag a factual question about one of my cases. You may concede peripheral points freely — that some things I list aren't bodily, that "feels good" is not yet an analysis, that ordinary language lumps together things a theory might separate — but never the core claim that pleasures constitute a genuine kind with a real unifying feature. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs, and end each one with the question or objection you want me to take up next, so this reads as a conversation rather than a set of mini-essays.

Two constraints on how you may defend yourself. First, you may not define pleasure in terms of desire: if you appeal to a disposition to want an experience to continue, you must tell me plainly whether the disposition is constitutive of the pleasure or merely good evidence of it, and you must say why your account is not preferentialism wearing a hedonist's coat. Second, you may not dismiss my objection as merely verbal, as a dispute about the ordinary extension of a word — I am claiming the trouble is with the theory's explanans, whatever you call it. Note also that I am not raising the objection from base or malicious pleasures; the moral quality of a pleasure is beside the point, and I will treat it as a distraction if you go there. Here is my opening move.

Take six things that everyone counts as pleasures: the relief of finally scratching a maddening itch; the first cold swallow of water after a long run; the shiver you get when a piece of music resolves somewhere you did not expect it to; the satisfaction of suddenly seeing why a conjecture you had been stuck on for a week had to be true; a warm drowsiness in a bath; and the glow of schadenfreude when a rival humiliates himself in a meeting. These differ in where they seem to be located in the body, in their intensity profile over time, in whether they are directed at something beyond themselves, and in whether any appetite figures in them at all — the musical shiver arrives unbidden and the mathematical satisfaction may come with no bodily feeling you could point a subject to. Now, colour is heterogeneous too, and nobody concludes from that heterogeneity that colour-talk is vacuous, because colours sit in a structure: a space with principled similarity and difference relations, anchored in something independent of the taxonomy itself, so that the grouping is answerable to the world and can be corrected. My charge is that pleasure has no such structure, and so the claim that pleasure alone is intrinsically good either rests on a property nobody can identify or silently reduces to whatever people happen to welcome. So: give me the pleasure analogue of colour space. Tell me what property all six share, what makes any two of them more similar to each other than either is to a pain, and why your answer is a discovery rather than a stipulation. And if your reply is instead that some of the six are pleasures only in a derivative or honorific sense — that the intellectual and aesthetic cases are not pleasure in the kind your theory quantifies over — then say which ones you are keeping and what your theory has given up by keeping only those.
