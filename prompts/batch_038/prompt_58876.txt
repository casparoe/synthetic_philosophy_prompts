A friend of mine thinks the scale argument for insect welfare refutes itself, and I have not been able to find the flaw. Here is her reasoning, set out as neutrally as I can manage.

(1) When we are uncertain whether beings are sentient, the morally relevant quantity is expected sentient welfare: the possible welfare of each being is to be weighted by the probability that it is sentient.

(2) Grant her two empirical stipulations for the sake of argument: that any individual insect has a probability of at least one in a hundred of being sentient, and that the insects alive at any moment outnumber all mammals, birds and fish combined by at least a million to one.

(3) So the expected number of sentient insects exceeds the number of sentient vertebrates by at least four orders of magnitude — and by (1), that is how much more moral weight insects carry than every vertebrate we are confident about.

(4) Grant a third stipulation: that the micro-invertebrates in the top layer of soil — nematodes, rotifers, springtails — outnumber insects by a further factor of at least a hundred, and that each of them is sentient with a probability smaller than an insect's but not a hundred times smaller.

(5) So by the same calculation the expected moral weight of soil micro-fauna exceeds that of the insects.

(6) Nothing in the reasoning from (2) to (5) turns on insects or on soil fauna in particular: for whatever class of organisms we start from, there is a smaller and more numerous class to which the same calculation applies.

(7) So the series has no stopping point, and the expected moral weight of the smallest, most numerous animals swamps that of everything we can actually point to.

(8) Therefore we ought to direct our moral attention to creatures whose sentience nobody claims to have any evidence for, and the claims of dogs, pigs and people are negligible.

She holds that (8) is absurd and that (1) must therefore be false — that ethics should not be multiplying small probabilities by large numbers at all. I want to keep (1), since refusing to weight by probability strikes me as far worse than anything in this argument, and I suspect (8) simply does not follow; but I cannot say where the chain breaks or why it looks so convincing. Please diagnose it: name the step or steps that fail, explain what makes each of them seem legitimate, and price the alternatives — including what I would have to give up if I insisted on keeping (1). Write in British English, and say how confident you are in the diagnosis you settle on, singling out the point in your own reasoning that you are least sure of.
