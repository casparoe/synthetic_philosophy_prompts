So my roommate's a phil grad student and he trapped me with an argument after dinner that I still can't get out of. He claims two-dimensional semantics refutes itself — not that it's incomplete, but that its own machinery deletes the very phenomena it was invented to explain. I want to say he's wrong, but every time I object he points at a step and says "that one's just the framework, you can't have the framework and deny the step." Help.

Here's the setup as he gave it, cleaned up a bit. Every sentence S carries two intensions. Its **primary intension** is a function from worlds *considered as actual* to truth values: you ask, if this world turned out to be the one we live in, would S be true? Its **secondary intension** is a function from worlds *considered as counterfactual* to truth values: you hold fixed how things actually are, then ask whether S would still be true over there. The core 2D thesis is that the secondary intension is determined by the primary intension plus which world is actual. And the two headline readings: S is a priori (for an ideal reasoner) iff its primary intension comes out true at every scenario; S is metaphysically necessary iff its secondary intension comes out true at every world. His argument:

1. S is metaphysically necessary iff S's secondary intension holds at every world; S is a priori iff S's primary intension holds at every scenario.
2. For any S, S's secondary intension is fixed by S's primary intension together with which world is actual.
3. That fixing relation is itself an a priori one. An ideal reasoner who is handed S's primary intension and told which world is actual can read off S's secondary intension by pure reflection, with no further empirical work. Anything less wouldn't be an explanation of modality, just a redescription of it.
4. A priori knowledge of a determining rule, applied to inputs, yields a priori knowledge of the output. You cannot get a posteriori ignorance out of an a priori procedure.
5. So the only inputs available are S's primary intension and which world is actual.
6. But "which world is actual" is not an extra ingredient that only experience could supply. Given S, its primary intension already delivers S's truth value for every candidate for actuality — it's a total function over scenarios — so there is no leftover input for experience to fill in.
7. Hence by (4) every sentence's secondary intension is a priori knowable, and necessity coincides with apriority. There is no necessary a posteriori: not "water is H2O," not Hesperus/Phosphorus.
8. And running the same machinery backwards: wherever a primary intension fails at some scenario, that just is a world where S fails, so S is genuinely contingent. Nothing a posteriori could be necessary, and nothing a priori could be contingent — no "I am here now," no truths delivered by stipulating a name for whoever invented the zipper, no standard-meter-bar cases.
9. So the 2D matrix has content only on the diagonal. The framework that was built to house the necessary a posteriori and the contingent a priori shows there's no such thing as either. Two-dimensionalism is self-defeating: either it explains nothing, or it explains away its own explananda.

He says this is why he's moved to direct-reference views, and that the 2D theorist's usual complaint — that you're conflating worlds-as-actual with worlds-as-counterfactual — misses the point because step (2) is supposed to be exactly the bridge between those two, and (3) is supposed to make the bridge walkable a priori. That last part is what I can't answer.

Honestly I'd much rather keep (1) and (2), since those are the whole framework, and I'd happily throw (4) overboard — it sounds like something he invented at the table. But I worry that's too cheap and that (4) is load-bearing for a reason I'm not seeing.
