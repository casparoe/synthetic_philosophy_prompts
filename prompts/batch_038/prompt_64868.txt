I am a senior in a cognitive science program, and this term's philosophy of mind seminar has just finished its unit on Hofstadter's *I Am a Strange Loop* (2007), in particular the passages where he sets up the "careenium": a brain-like box that, seen from below, is a swarming chaos of tiny fast events, and that, seen from above, is a room in which a few large slow meaningful objects — his simmballs — shove one another around. The seminar question was the predictable one: do the simmballs actually do any shoving, or is all the shoving done by the small events, with the big objects merely a convenient summary of them? I have heard the objection that if the small level already accounts for everything that happens there is nothing left over for the large level to do, and I take that objection seriously. It is not, however, what I am writing about. I am writing about a related but, I think, much more specific pattern that I keep running into and cannot locate in our readings.

Here is the pattern. First instance, from my own work: for two years I have been writing a program that plays a board game badly. Its play is governed by a hand-written routine that assigns a number to each candidate position and picks whichever move maximizes it. When I add one term to that routine — a small bonus for holding the middle of the board — the program's entire style changes. It stops trading on the edges, it begins to over-extend, it loses to an opponent it used to beat. I can predict all of that in advance using only the vocabulary of the routine, and the prediction holds whether the program runs on my laptop, on the department server, or on an emulator of hardware that does not exist yet. Now compare that with what it takes to produce the same change by editing what the program physically is: I would have to retrace how the bonus propagates into several hundred stored values and every branch that reads them, and an edit made at that level without the top-down description either does nothing, or does something I cannot anticipate, or stops the program from running at all. Second instance, and it is not software: the heating system in my building has a dial. I can change the output of the whole plant by moving a small plastic pointer onto a number, and I can predict the consequence — how long the boiler fires, how the rooms respond — without knowing whether the device behind the wall is a bimetallic strip, a thermistor, or a small computer telephoning a server. Swap the mechanism out entirely and the very same intervention, described in the same three words, has the same effect. Yet nothing in the dial-and-number story tells you where the energy comes from; every joule is accounted for by the gas line and the boiler, and if you want the same change without touching the dial you must go work on that machinery, at a cost in effort and precision with no counterpart on the dial side.

So the recurring shape is this. The higher-level variable is a place where the system can genuinely be grabbed: interventions on it are possible, reliable, predictable in advance, and they survive total replacement of the underlying machinery. Meanwhile all of the actual pushing is done from below, and any intervention on the high variable is also, trivially, an intervention on the low ones. The high variable is a locus of control but not a source of force. My two intuitions pull apart accordingly: one says that whatever is not a source of force is not a cause, and the other says that a variable you can reliably set, and whose setting makes a difference you can state beforehand, is a paradigm case of a cause. I have also noticed that the grabbing seems to work only against a restricted menu of interventions — if I allow myself every physically possible intervention on the substrate, the high-level variable loses its special status, and its specialness exists only relative to the subset of interventions available to someone who knows the high-level description and nothing else. Which makes me suspect the handle-ness may be as much a property of my relation to the system as of the system.

What I would like to know is whether this configuration has an established name, in philosophy of mind, in the philosophy of the special sciences, in control theory, or anywhere else. I expect that it does not have a single clean one, so please do not stop at a word. Take the nearest concepts in turn — say where each comes from, what it claims, and exactly how the pattern above differs from it or fails to differ; say which fits best and why; and if none of them fits, coin a term, define it so that it is non-trivially distinct from its neighbors, and give me its edges: what a system must be like for the term to apply, and what a case that looks like the thing but isn't. The label is not the point, and I will not be satisfied with one; the carving is the point. Please also address three cases I cannot place: a spreadsheet, where a cell labeled 'revenue' is transparently identical to a memory location and both levels seem equally easy to edit, so the asymmetry appears to vanish; a listing of my evaluation routine printed on paper, where the exact same high-level description is available and grabs nothing whatever; and a flyball governor on a steam engine, where the variable 'speed' visibly moves the valve, and which strikes me as possibly a handle that is also a source. I am not after a bibliography — names of concepts and positions are enough, and I would much rather you flag uncertainty than invent a reference.
