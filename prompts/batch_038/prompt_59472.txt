so I've got a thing that's been eating at me for weeks and instead of more 2am googling I want to turn it into something I can actually use with other people.

Background: I'm not a philosopher or anything, I fix and resell old stuff — glassware, tools, kitchen things — and I got stuck thinking about what it means to call something fragile, or flammable, or water soluble, when nothing is happening to it. A glass in a box in my garage for ten years. Is it fragile that whole time? And what exactly makes that true, if nothing ever touches it?

Then I started inventing cases and they broke my head.

Case one: a genuinely fragile glass, except there's some device sitting next to it that switches off its fragility the instant before anything hits it. So it never breaks. I want to say it's still fragile and the device is just cheating.

Case two: a perfectly dry match that would light, sealed in a jar with no oxygen in it. Strike it all you want inside the jar, nothing happens. Honestly I'm not sure this one is the same kind of problem as case one — it feels like maybe the striking isn't really the full "stimulus" without oxygen, rather than something interfering with the match. But I can't say what the difference is.

Case three: a solid chunk of steel that isn't fragile at all, but there's a device/wizard such that if you hit it, it would shatter. Would-break-if-struck is true of it, and yet I definitely don't want to call it fragile.

So the easy "fragile just means would break if struck" story is dead, but I don't know what's supposed to replace it, and I'm pretty sure there's jargon for case one and case three that I never learned.

What I want out of you: a decision tree / sorting quiz that I can run on the people in my little online group — all laypeople, zero philosophy training — that works out which theory of dispositions they actually hold, from what they say about cases rather than from what label they like. Ground rules:

- every branch node has to be a concrete case or a concrete claim someone can answer without already knowing where it leads. no "do you believe in irreducible causal powers" style questions.
- build it from the logical space first, then drop the actual positions in. I want to see the questions and the combinations of answers they permit before I see any names.
- leaves I'm insisting on, plus whatever else you think belongs: the plain would-break-if-struck view; the same thing with an "all else being equal" or "under ideal conditions" patch bolted on; the view that powers are real and irreducible properties; the view that disposition talk is just how we describe the underlying categorical stuff plus the laws; the view that everything is dispositional all the way down with nothing categorical at the bottom; and the deflationary view that it's all useful fiction.
- for each leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment.
- flag any two leaves your tree genuinely can't separate, and say what extra question would tell them apart — or say plainly that nothing would.
- mark the empty cells: answer combinations that are logically available but nobody actually occupies. for each, say whether its uninhabited because the view is incoherent, because its unattractive, or just because nobody's looked there.

and I want you to commit, not hand me the map and then shrug. at the end: tell me which leaf you'd actually sign up for and why; tell me which of my three cases above I've got wrong and what the right verdict on it is (including whether case two is really a different animal from case one); and tell me which leaf my own answers put me in. my answers, for the record: ten-years-in-a-box glass is fragile the whole time; case one glass is still fragile; case two match is still flammable; case three steel is NOT fragile; and I think something can have a disposition it never once manifests, in fact most dispositions never manifest.

be as confident as you can honestly be, and if there's a part of this where you're less sure than you sound, point at that part specifically instead of hedging the whole thing.
