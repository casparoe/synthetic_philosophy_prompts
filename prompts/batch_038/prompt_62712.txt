I would like a case analysis that runs in both directions: a real episode examined through the frameworks currently on offer for how a vastly more capable artificial system ought to communicate with people who cannot verify what it says, and then those frameworks examined for what the episode does to them.

Begin with the frameworks. In discussions of how a superintelligent system should address human interlocutors given that it may be extremely persuasive, at least five distinguishable positions circulate: (i) a strict veracity constraint, on which the system may assert only what it takes to be true and may not exploit the audience's cognitive biases, attentional limits, or deference to authority, even when doing so would produce better beliefs or better outcomes; (ii) graduated disclosure, on which what the system reveals may be sequenced or withheld according to the consequences of revelation, including consequences for third parties; (iii) benign steering, on which framing, emphasis, and simplification are permissible — indeed obligatory — insofar as they move the audience toward what the system judges to be correct, and the loss of the audience's independent judgment is a price worth paying; (iv) adversarial verification, on which the audience's inability to check the system's claims is solved not by educating the audience but by exposing those claims to an opposing party of comparable capability, so that truth-tracking is a property of the process rather than of the listener; and (v) process legibility, on which the audience need not be able to evaluate the conclusion but must be able to evaluate whether the reasoning was honestly produced, making auditability of method the central communicative virtue.

Now the episode. In January 1946 the U.S. Secretary of State's Committee on Atomic Energy was formed under Dean Acheson, with James Conant, Vannevar Bush, John McCloy, and General Leslie Groves among its members. On 23 January it appointed a Board of Consultants — David Lilienthal as chairman, with Chester Barnard, J. Robert Oppenheimer, Charles A. Thomas, and Harry A. Winne — which did the substantive work, the report being written largely by Oppenheimer. The Board's central claim was negative before it was positive: that a regime of inspection and policing layered over sovereign national programs could not work, because the thing to be controlled was not a device but a chain of industrial capabilities. Its remedy was an international Atomic Development Authority owning and operating the dangerous parts of that chain, from the uranium and thorium mines through the production of fissile material, licensing peaceful uses and denying weapons-usable material to everyone, including the United States, which would relinquish its monopoly by stages. The Board also reasoned explicitly about the informational consequences of talking at all: its transmittal letter of 16 March 1946 divides what may be disclosed into successive stages and estimates that revealing the first tranche to an industrially capable nation might shorten that nation's path to a bomb "by as much as a year." The report was transmitted on 16 March and released in full to the public on 28 March, rather than remaining internal advice. In June, President Truman sent Bernard Baruch to carry the proposal to the United Nations; Baruch added punitive sanctions for violations, insisted that those sanctions be immune from Security Council veto, and maintained that the United States would retain its arsenal until it was satisfied that the control machinery worked. Acheson and Lilienthal both rejected those amendments. The Soviet counterproposal, presented by Andrei Gromyko on 19 June, called for outlawing atomic weapons and destroying existing stockpiles within three months while preserving the veto, and it died there.

What I want from you is the mapping, done carefully and then tested.

First: which of the five positions actually describes what the Board of Consultants was doing? The Board possessed knowledge no member of its audience could independently assess, spoke with the authority of the people who had built the weapon, and recommended giving away the American monopoly to a public and a Congress predisposed against it. Say what each position prescribes about the decision to publish the report in full rather than let it travel through internal channels — was that an instance of the veracity constraint refusing to shape belief by concealment, or a sophisticated steering operation that used expert authority to fix public opinion before interested parties could revise the substance? Distinguish these two readings by what would have to be true for each.

Second: treat the staged-disclosure reasoning as the most interesting object in the case. Note that the stages are gated not by the audience's capacity to understand but by the strategic effect of understanding on a rival state. Ask whether any of the five positions has room for that motive, or whether it reveals a sixth consideration — the externality of communication on parties who are not in the conversation — that the AI-facing frameworks systematically omit because they model a system, a listener, and nobody else.

Third: examine adversarial verification where it actually operated. Baruch was the relay, an eminent intermediary who could not evaluate the technical substance and who altered it; the Soviets and their delegation were the adversarial checkers, and congressional and press criticism was a further one. Did adversarial scrutiny improve the epistemic quality of what survived, as the debate position predicts, or merely expose a conflict of interests that no amount of argument-discipline could have resolved? Be precise about which of the plan's components the adversary attacked on technical grounds and which it attacked on political ones.

Fourth: state where the analogy fails, and do not paper over it. The Board commanded no enforcement power, had no continuing relationship with its audience, could be overruled by a financier it did not select, and its superior knowledge concerned a physical technology rather than the listeners' own values or interests. Identify which features of the episode are artifacts of 1946 geopolitics and which are structural features of any asymmetric-competence communication problem, so that I can tell what genuinely transfers to the case of a persuasive superintelligent system.

Close with a verdict: is this episode a confirmation of one of the positions, an anomaly for all of them, or a case none of them anticipated? Say which position, had it governed the Board's conduct, would most plausibly have changed the outcome — and where the theories are simply silent rather than determinate, mark that silence explicitly instead of forcing an answer. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want the reasoning, not the sourcing. If you are unsure of any detail of the episode beyond what I have supplied, flag the uncertainty rather than inventing a fact to fill the gap.
