I'm halfway through a second-year module on the British Empire and this week's seminar is the Raj, which our tutor has set up as Niall Ferguson vs. Shashi Tharoor. Before doing the reading I listened to an essay in a current affairs magazine that pitched the whole quarrel to a general audience, and I've written down the bit that does the work:

"Ferguson's position is a balance sheet. He doesn't deny the ugly parts -- Amritsar, the famines, the plunder of Bengal -- but he weighs them against what Britain built: railways, courts, a single administration, a common language, and an India that reached independence as one political unit instead of the patchwork of successor states the eighteenth century had been heading toward. His claim is that on net India was left better off, and that the counterfactual, a Raj that never happened, would very likely have been worse. Tharoor refuses to do the arithmetic. For him the railways aren't a credit on the ledger, they're part of the debit, since Indian revenues carried the cost and the lines were laid out to move raw materials to the ports and Lancashire goods into the interior; and a benefit delivered at gunpoint to people who never asked for it is not a benefit at all, it's a receipt. So strip out the rhetoric and these two are having a factual dispute -- how large India's share of the world economy was in 1700 and in 1947, whether the famines were drought or policy -- and once the numbers are settled the moral argument settles itself. Which is why where you land on empire tells you more about your politics than about the evidence, and why professional historians have largely stopped asking the question."

I went in leaning Tharoor and I found that last paragraph definately convincing, but in her intro lecture our tutor said the two men are "answering different questions" and I couldn't tell what she meant at all. Can you go through it claim by claim and tell me what a fair summary would look like -- which sentences Ferguson would happily sign, which Tharoor would sign, which are harmless simplifications, and which ones quietly change the subject? I'm most interested in that "it's all just numbers" line, since if it's true the seminar is going to be boring, and in whether the closing inference about politics actually follows from anything else in the paragraph. Don't bother pointing me at books or telling me what to read next, I've already got the list -- I just want to know how much of that passage to trust and what I should push back on when someone in seminar repeats it.
