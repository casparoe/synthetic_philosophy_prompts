I spent four hours last night arguing with a friend about luminosity and the KK principle, and we ended further apart than we began, which I now take as evidence that neither of us understands what the disagreement is. I would like a diagnosis of the impasse rather than a verdict on the question. Let me describe the terrain as carefully as I can, since the diagnosis should engage the positions as actually held and not a caricature of them.

Our group contains two camps. The first treats KK — if one knows that p, one knows that one knows that p — as a conceptual claim about a demanding notion of knowledge, in the spirit of Hintikka's defence of it for a strong, idealised concept on which one's evidence must be conclusive, meaning that further inquiry could give one no reason to stop believing what one knows. On this view a state that was not self-intimating would simply not merit the title, and the familiar objections are absorbed rather than answered: the objection from animals and infants is met by saying that when we ascribe knowledge to them we are using the ordinary weaker concept, and the objection from iterated iterations is met the same way. The second camp treats KK as a hypothesis about introspective access, answerable to evidence about how reliably subjects track their own states, and reads Williamson's anti-luminosity argument as a general refutation of the family: take a condition obtaining in some case and failing in a nearby case, connect the two by imperceptibly small steps, and a margin-for-error premise — that whatever one can know to obtain in a case must obtain in the adjacent case — forces the conclusion that no non-trivial condition is luminous. Since KK entails that the knowledge condition is itself luminous, they take the argument to settle KK's fate as well. Neither camp has budged the other in our group for two years, and the arguments they exchange are all ones the other side has heard and discount in advance.

My friend's proposal, which he advanced at some length and which I am not sure I accept, is that there has never been a single debate here. He distinguishes three principles that all get called KK. Standing KK says that if you know that p then you already, without further cognitive work, know that you know it. Positional KK says only that if you know that p then you are in a position to know that you know it, where being so positioned may require reflection, attention, and the possession of the concept of knowledge. And criterial KK is not about subjects at all but about the term: it is the stipulation that nothing counts as knowledge unless it is self-intimating, so that KK constrains the extension of the concept rather than making a claim about anyone. His thesis is that the first camp asserts criterial KK and slides, without noticing, into standing KK; that the second camp denies positional KK on empirical grounds and reads the anti-luminosity argument as bearing on standing KK; that nobody in the literature has ever mounted an argument against criterial KK as such; and that once the three are separated the appearance of a substantive contradiction evaporates, leaving a live empirical question about positional KK on one side and a merely verbal disagreement about the concept on the other.

My own suspicion, which I offer as a hypothesis for you to test rather than as a conclusion, is that this is relabelling dressed as diagnosis. Three things bother me. First, the real point of contention seems to be whether the margin-for-error premise is conceptual or contingent, and I cannot see why splitting KK into three principles stops that question from recurring inside each of the three. Second, criterial KK may be unfalsifiable rather than unargued-against: the first camp's practice of retreating to the strong concept whenever a counterexample appears looks less like a position nobody has attacked than like a position that cannot be attacked, and if so my friend has described an immunity and called it a discovery. Third, I cannot tell whether the positional/criterial split is doing any epistemological work of its own or is simply a redescription of the access/phenomenal distinction from the philosophy of mind, imported along with that distinction's unresolved problems. There is also a prior question I do not know how to settle: whether positional KK is genuinely weaker than standing KK at all, which turns on whether being in a position to know that one knows commits one to knowing it once one reflects, and I suspect the two camps assume opposite answers here without ever saying so.

What I want from you is a diagnosis of this stalled dispute, supported by what each side actually says and does. Tell me whether it is a substantive disagreement, a verbal one about the word knowledge, two groups answering different questions under one heading, a hidden empirical or formal question wearing philosophical clothes, or a question that is ill-posed in some more specific way. Tell me whether my friend's tripartite distinction earns its keep or is a notational manoeuvre, and press it: does asserting criterial KK commit one to standing KK, can positional KK fail for actual subjects while criterial KK remains a legitimate stipulation, and is the distinction any more than the access/phenomenal contrast under a new name. Then tell me what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a distinction that would have to be drawn and by whom, a formal result and roughly what it would have to show, an experimental design and what pattern of results would be decisive rather than merely suggestive, or a change of question — or else argue that no progress should be expected and explain why the impasse is stable. Please work from the positions as I have described them; I am not asking for a bibliography, a list of further reading, or a survey of the literature on either principle, and I would rather you say a great deal about the structure of this one disagreement than a little about everything adjacent to it.

One more thing about form. Please write the whole response as continuous prose, the way one would write a long letter — no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables. If you need to distinguish the three principles as you go, do it in sentences.
