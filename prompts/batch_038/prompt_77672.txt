I am a second-year PhD student in epistemology, and in six weeks I defend a prospectus chapter that proposes my own account of how empirical warrant is structured — specifically, an account meant to survive the family of objections that go by the names of the bootstrapping problem and the problem of easy knowledge. My problem is not that I have too little to read; it is that every paper I read evaluates rivals by a different set of standards, so the dialectic looks like a shouting match about what the target even is. One camp treats it as decisive that a view preserves closure under known entailment; another treats it as decisive that the bootstrapper's reasoning feels like a cheat; a third thinks the entire issue is a parlor trick involving instruments and dissolves once you stop modeling warrant on thermometers. Before I commit to a view, I want the specification itself: what any theory of the structure of empirical warrant has to deliver in order to count as an adequate response to this problem. So please act as a senior epistemologist who has spent a career on the architecture of justification and who is scrupulous about separating real theoretical requirements from the local prejudices of particular camps, and think carefully before you start — I would rather you be slow and exact than comprehensive and glib. Begin with a short, unfiltered brainstorming pass: list every candidate requirement that occurs to you, then prune and merge, and tell me explicitly which candidates you dropped and why they were not genuine desiderata at all.

The deliverable is the requirements list. For each entry, give me the requirement; the case, argument, or phenomenon that motivates it (spelled out concretely, not gestured at); a verdict on whether it is non-negotiable for any adequate theory or instead a preference that only some camps are entitled to impose; and, where relevant, what it costs a theory to satisfy it. I want the full range: the verdicts a theory must return on particular reasoning patterns, the distinctions it must not collapse (for instance, between a belief's being warranted and its warrant's being transmitted through an inference; between a source's being reliable and a subject's being in a position to know that it is; between warrant for a conclusion and warrant for a claim about the warrant), the structural constraints it must respect about closure and about basing, and the background results and stable intuitions it must remain consistent with, including the anti-skeptical ones. Here is my own draft list, which I suspect is a mixture of genuine requirements and camp propaganda, and part of what I want from you is the sorting: (1) the theory must preserve closure under known entailment, or explain precisely why not; (2) it must entail that the bootstrapper does not come to know that her instrument is reliable by bootstrapping; (3) it must not generate skepticism about ordinary perceptual knowledge; (4) it must allow that we can sometimes come to know that a source is reliable by checking it; (5) it must treat the problem as a problem specifically for externalist or reliabilist accounts; (6) it must explain why the bootstrapper's reasoning strikes us as defective rather than merely explaining why it is defective; (7) it must not allow warrant to be generated from nothing — no free epistemic lunch; (8) it must handle the situation in which the instrument is in fact unreliable, and not just the situation in which it is reliable; and (9) it must be statable without taking sides between internalism and externalism. Tell me which of these are real, which are redundant with one another, which are question-begging, and which are simply false.

Two things matter more to me than anything else in your answer. First, the impossible subset: I have a strong suspicion that some cluster of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied — that closure, the premise knowledge the bootstrapper seems to have, the diagnosis that her inference fails to transmit, and the prohibition on perceptual skepticism cannot all be kept — and if so the list is itself a result rather than a shopping catalogue. State the unsatisfiable cluster as precisely as you can, identify which single requirement is the cheapest to abandon and which is the most expensive, and say what a theorist is left with after that trade. Second, the discriminating requirement: tell me which one desideratum does the most work in ruling out otherwise attractive theories, and demonstrate that by running it against the cases below. I constructed these myself while reading, and I have not found them discussed, so please treat them as raw material rather than as something you should recognize — say what verdicts change, if any, and whether an account that handles the textbook case still handles these. (a) Chained case: a subject bootstraps the reliability of gauge one in the usual way, and then, without ever checking gauge two directly, reasons that gauge two is reliable because gauge one says so and gauge one is reliable; the chaining is repeated a third and fourth time. Does whatever diagnosis condemns the first inference condemn the later ones with the same force, or does the defect attenuate, accumulate, or change kind? (b) Retrospective case: the subject takes several hundred readings and forms no beliefs about reliability at the time, but months later reconstructs the whole sequence from memory in one sitting and concludes that the instrument is reliable. If the diagnosis is about the structure of the evidence, the verdict should be unchanged; if it is about the reasoning process, it should not be. (c) Benign narrowing case: the subject already knows her vision is broadly reliable and uses it to establish the much more specific claim that her vision is reliable at distinguishing shades of blue in daylight. Almost everyone finds this acceptable, so a theory that condemns all reasoning from a source to claims about that source is over-general — and a theory that permits it owes an account of where the line falls. (d) Generalization case: the subject reasons by modus ponens to the conclusion that modus ponens is a reliable rule, and similarly for memory, testimony, and induction. Any diagnosis that depends on features special to instruments and readings cannot reach these, and I want to know whether that generality is a genuine desideratum or an optional ambition. (e) Hostile case: the instrument is in fact unreliable, but the subject has no way to tell and the bootstrapping produces a confident, coherent, wholly unsupported endorsement of it — what does the theory owe us here beyond the word lucky? Finally, close with a brief scorecard — no more than a few lines each — for the main families of response, described by their theoretical shape rather than by who holds them: a reliabilist externalism, a safety- or sensitivity-based externalism, an internalist basic-knowledge or dogmatist view, a warrant-transmission-failure view, and a view that simply denies closure. I do not want citations, a bibliography, quotations, or any exegesis of particular authors' texts; you have no way to check those and I would rather you reason than guess. A table is fine for the requirements themselves, provided the motivating case for each is written out in prose, and please flag every point at which your own classification of a requirement as non-negotiable reflects a substantive theoretical commitment of yours rather than a neutral judgment.
