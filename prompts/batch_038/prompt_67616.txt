In three weeks I deliver the closing talk at a two-day interdisciplinary workshop on metacognition, and I know this room well enough to dread the question period more than the talk. I want a Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of my view.

The thesis I am defending is that introspective reports about one's own current attitudes are not attempts to detect an independently settled inner fact, and that evaluating them by detection standards — does the report match the state? — mischaracterizes the mechanism. On my account, such reports are commitments produced by the same interpretive machinery we deploy on other people, running on a richer and largely non-conceptual sensory feed, in the lineage of interpretivism about self-knowledge, but with a twist: their success condition is predictive rather than veridical. The criterion I propose, which is my own coinage and not a name for anything already in the literature, I call asymmetric predictive access: a subject's report about an attitude should predict that subject's own subsequent attitude-revealing choices better than a well-informed observer's report about that same subject predicts them, including in cases where the two reports disagree and where both are false about the underlying state. I have pilot data on this from a preference-reversal task, and I argue that the criterion dissolves the force of the classic confabulation, misattribution, and choice-blindness results without conceding introspective pessimism, because those results are failures of the detection model rather than of the mechanism being described. My argument runs: interpretation all the way down; constitution rather than correspondence for dispositional attitudes; prediction as the only measurable proxy for either; and therefore first-person authority as a predictive asymmetry, not an epistemic privilege.

The room will be about twenty people. Most are experimental psychologists who run confidence-rating paradigms and think about metacognitive sensitivity in terms of meta-d′ and the M-ratio; they will hear predictive success and immediately hear calibration measured sloppily. Two are philosophers of self-knowledge who are introspective pessimists of the Schwitzgebel stripe and will suspect I have renamed the problem rather than solved it. One is a psychiatrist working on loss of insight in psychosis and on anosognosia, for whom the decisive cases are ones where a patient's self-model is flagrantly false yet powerfully predictive of the patient's own behavior. Two work on calibrated confidence in machine learning models and have no patience for first-personal vocabulary. Nobody present is a constitutivist about self-knowledge, and nobody is a phenomenologist. I want the objections this audience will actually raise, in their vocabularies — not the ones I would get from a rationalist forum or a continental philosophy seminar.

Give me exactly eight numbered entries, ordered from most damaging to least, where damage is a function of both how likely the objection is to be voiced and how hard it is to answer in the two minutes I will have. Each entry should identify which subgroup it comes from, state the objection the way that subgroup would state it, give my best compressed reply in a sentence or two, and then flag by name the point at which that reply is soft. Keep every entry to four sentences or fewer. No introduction, no concluding paragraph, no summary of the state of play.

Rank the objection I am most hoping nobody raises where it actually belongs in the ordering rather than appending it at the end.

Two labels, and only these two, should appear inside the entries themselves rather than anywhere else: mark the two objections I ought to concede explicitly in my opening five minutes, and mark the single entry whose reply has to land if the view is to survive the session. Be committed throughout: tell me flatly which concessions to make and which ground to hold, commit to a ranking rather than noting that reasonable people would order these differently, and give me a straight verdict, without hedging, on whether I can in fact answer the objection I hope to dodge.
