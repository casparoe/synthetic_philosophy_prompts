Context first, sorry: I'm a novelist, not a philosopher. Third book, literary-ish, small advance, whatever. Two of my mains are Helen, a retired ethics lecturer who spent her career on Kant, and her son Tomas, who runs a distressed-debt fund and is convinced his mother's entire moral framework is a fraud with better manners. There's a long kitchen-table scene at Christmas where he tries to demolish the universalizability test and she has to actually beat him — not with a speech about feelings, with the argument. I've had one passage pinned above my desk for four months and I still don't fully get it, so here's my paraphrase and then a pile of questions.

The passage: Groundwork, second section, the last of the four examples Kant runs through after stating the universal law formula — the one about beneficence. A man in flourishing circumstances sees others struggling with want and could easily help them, but tells himself it's no concern of his, each person can be as happy as heaven pleases or as he can manage for himself; he'll take nothing from anyone and envy no one, he simply won't contribute to anyone's welfare or assist them in need. Kant then says that if this way of thinking became a universal law of nature the human race would subsist quite well, and very possibly better than in a world where everyone talks about sympathy and benevolence and even practises it now and then, while cheating whenever they can get away with it. But he goes on: although such a universal law of nature is possible, it is impossible to will that it should hold everywhere as a law of nature, because a will that resolved on this would conflict with itself — cases might often arise in which he'd need the love and sympathy of others, and by the very law of nature springing from his own will he'd have cut himself off from all hope of the aid he wants.

(I'm paraphrasing rather than typing it out of the old Abbott translation because I'd mangle the wording and you'd spend the whole answer correcting me instead of answering.)

What I need:

1. That remark about the universalized world possibly being a *better* world has been driving me crazy. Every pop explination I've read says the test goes: imagine everyone doing this, then ask whether you could live in that world or whether it would be worse. But Kant says the universalized indifference world might be better than the alternative, and he still condemns the maxim. So what is the test actually doing? If it's not tracking how the world turns out, what does it mean to say he cannot will the maxim as universal law, and why is that a failure rather than just a taste?

2. Is the conflict in the will a genuine second test with its own logic, or a soft fallback for maxims where the conception test doesn't fire? I'm asking because Helen switches mid-scene between the false-promise case and this one, and I need to know whether that switch is legitimate, and what the difference between perfect and imperfect duty buys her when she makes it.

3. Tomas's move — this is my own dialogue so attack it freely: "You can't will it. Fine. That just means willing it would leave you worse off later, when you need a hand. That's a calculation about your own happiness, which is exactly the conditional, if-you-want-the-end reasoning Kant spends the first section throwing out. You've smuggled prudence in the back door and dressed it as duty." Is that a fair hit? What is the strongest reply Helen can make that doesn't appeal to consequences, to her sentiments, or to how she'd like to be treated? I want the real reply, not a dodge — and if there isn't one, I want to know that, because it changes the ending.

4. Granularity. Tomas doesn't say he never helps anyone. The maxim he reads out of his notebook is narrower: when someone's ruin was foreseeable to them and they had the means to avoid it, I will not contribute to their relief, though I will honour every contract I've signed. Does a maxim tuned that finely just sail through? If it does, is that a bug in the test or a feature, and what does Helen say — is there anything in the passage about how maxims get described that she can use, or is she stuck conceding it?

5. The hermit problem, which is where I think the scene actually turns. Tomas has arranged things so he will never need anyone's goodwill: retainers on lawyers, concierge medicine, salaried staff, enough money to buy every kind of help he could ever want. So the prong about cases arising in which he'd need the love and sympathy of others just doesn't bite on him. Does the test only work on the vulnerable? If it does, is that fatal, or is something left standing once you delete the vulnerability premise — and if so, what exactly is the thing that's left?

Plain English where you can, but don't sand off the technical distinctions, because the whole point of Helen is that she's precise and the whole point of Tomas is that he's clever and wrong, and I need to know exaclty where the line between those two falls.
