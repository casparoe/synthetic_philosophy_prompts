I am a staff software engineer at a payments company of roughly nine hundred engineers, with no philosophy training of any kind. I came to this material last winter through the Dennett and Caruso debate volume Just Deserts: Debating Free Will (Polity, 2021), which I picked up after an argument with a colleague about whether one of our engineers should have been placed on a performance improvement plan for causing an outage. In three weeks I present a proposed rewrite of our incident accountability policy to our engineering practices council, and the rewrite is explicitly grounded in the free-will skeptic side of that debate rather than in the usual pragmatic case for blameless postmortems. The thesis is this: because no one deserves blame in the basic-desert sense, the residual retributive elements of our current process — naming the causal engineer in the review document, the default that whoever broke something owns the fix, and the practice of converting an incident into a performance action — should be removed and replaced by a forward-looking framework with three tracks: containment (restricting access or pulling someone from the on-call rotation when a pattern of behaviour poses a live risk), remediation (fixing the conditions that made the failure possible — tooling gaps, rotation load, incentive misalignment, deadline pressure), and restoration (reintegration and repair of team trust), with the severity of any containment measure limited by the least restrictive means adequate to the residual risk rather than by how culpable the person appears. I will also argue that the metaphysical premise is load-bearing rather than decorative: the pragmatic case for blameless postmortems is already accepted here and, on its own, is perfectly compatible with keeping every practice I want to eliminate, since better data is a reason to write the review kindly, not a reason to stop issuing performance actions. The worked example will be our Q3 payments outage, where an engineer bypassed a canary gate that had flagged a four percent error spike because a marketing launch was scheduled that evening and he was on his eleventh consecutive on-call shift; the bypass required a single flag, was logged but unwatched, and the outage ran forty-seven minutes and cost about 1.9 million dollars. He was named in the review document and put on a performance improvement plan.

The council has twelve members and no philosophers. Four are engineering directors who think in terms of throughput metrics, attrition, and how they will justify a policy change upward to their own leadership. Three are incident-command and SRE leads who are ruthlessly pragmatic and care only about whether a change reduces repeat incidents; they already run blameless postmortems and consider themselves advanced on this question, so they will be irritated by the suggestion that our process is still retributive. Two are People partners who worry about due process, precedent, legal exposure, and their duty of care toward the engineers who follow process and absorb other people's sloppiness. One is a staff security engineer who is a hard-liner about insider risk and will insist that the actor who deliberately exfiltrates source code is a different animal from the exhausted engineer at the canary gate. One is an outside human-factors consultant who has read enough compatibilism to be dangerous and is likely to press something close to Dennett's line from the book: that holding people responsible is not about inflicting what they deserve but about addressing them as agents competent to respond to reasons, and that a policy which refuses to address anyone as an agent will erode the very responsiveness the process depends on. The room as a whole is broadly hostile to metaphysics and will not tolerate anything that sounds like an argument about determinism.

Please prepare my question-and-answer sheet: the eight to ten objections I will actually get from this specific audience — not the ones a philosophy seminar would raise, and not the straw man that free-will skepticism means abolishing all consequences — ordered by how much damage each would do to the proposal, and each phrased in the register of the person most likely to raise it (the director talking about incentives and the message the change sends, the SRE lead talking about who skips the change gate at two in the morning once the gate has no teeth, the People partner talking about the engineer who did everything right, the security engineer talking about the deliberate insider, the consultant talking about competence and reason-responsiveness). For each objection, give me a reply I can deliver in roughly two minutes in plain language without sounding as though I am reciting a paper, plus an honest note on where that reply is soft, what it costs me, and which of my own claims it quietly gives up. I would particularly like the soft spots called out on proportionality: if culpability is not what limits containment, what is, and what prevents my least-restrictive-means rule from authorizing something harsher than the performance improvement plan I am objecting to — including the case of a second bypass by the same engineer next quarter, where the director will ask what changes for that person under my policy. There is also one line of attack I anticipate and cannot currently answer: that once desert is removed I am left with ordinary consequentialism about sanctions, and a consistent consequentialist about sanctions would sometimes make an example of one engineer to deter fifty, so that my dignity and proportionality constraints are doing the work that desert used to do. If you can give me a usable answer, include it; if you cannot, say so plainly, because I would rather walk into the room knowing where the hole is. Finally, tell me what to concede in the first five minutes so that it cannot be extracted from me later, which single reply has to land for the proposal to survive this room, and the question I am hoping nobody asks. I do not want citations or a reading list — please do not invent references — but if you rely on a distinction the room will not recognize, give me one sentence of plain gloss I can say out loud.
