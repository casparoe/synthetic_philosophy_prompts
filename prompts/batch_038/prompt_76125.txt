I'm a psychometrician, and I'd like you to act as a philosopher of social science who is genuinely fluent in formal measurement theory — not someone who uses the phrase "latent variable" as decorative vocabulary, but someone who could sit down and write out an identification argument, and who also knows the moral-psychological and metaethical debate I'm about to describe from the inside. Please think carefully before answering; this is a hard translation and I would rather have a shorter, more precise answer than a comprehensive and hand-wavy one. Do not give me a reading list or citations — I cannot chase them and I care about the arguments, not their provenance. Refer to positions by their content.

Here is the situation that produced the question. For most of the last decade I have built and validated self-report instruments: item response models, confirmatory factor analyses, and a great deal of differential item functioning work, because several of our scales get administered across quite different populations and every paper we write has to argue about measurement invariance. I have had one undergraduate ethics course and no formal philosophy since. At a recent workshop I got into a long argument with a moral psychologist about her de-biasing pipeline. She had collected judgments about cases involving physical harm, purity violations, and victims who were either described statistically or presented as a single named individual with a photograph, and she treated the inflation in condemnation attached to the named victims as an artifact to be corrected for before the data could be said to reveal anything about people's real values. My objection was that I could not see, from inside her analysis, what licensed that move: for all her model showed, that inflation might be contamination of a latent trait, or it might be part of what the trait consists in. She said that this was a very old argument, that moral psychologists and moral philosophers had been having it for decades under the general heading of when a response is a bias and when it is a preference or a value, and that I would recognize it if I read it in their vocabulary. I tried, and everything I read I had to translate back into mine, badly, in my head, and I have not been able to let the argument go since.

So this is what I want. Take the substantive positions that have been defended on the question of when a moral response is a distortion to be subtracted and when it is the thing one is measuring, and restate them, one at a time, in the vocabulary I actually use: latent traits and item response functions, identification, local independence, construct validity, measurement invariance and DIF, method factors and nuisance parameters, response styles, purification procedures, model fit and misfit. At minimum I want three families covered. First, the deflationary view, on which disgust-driven condemnation, in-group favoritism, and the extra weight given to vivid, named, nearby victims are artifacts of human psychology that a competent theory should detect and correct for, and where the interesting sub-disagreement is about what the correction is anchored to. Second, the view on which such responses are not distortions at all but morally informative — on which partiality toward one's own, or a visceral reaction to a particular kind of act, is constitutive of the value rather than a smear on the measurement of it. Third, the view on which preferences formed under deprivation, coercion, or long-standing oppression fail to be the person's preferences in the relevant sense, even when the person sincerely endorses them, because they were produced by conditions the person would disavow. For each family, tell me what carries over cleanly into a model I could actually write down; what carries over only if I quietly reinterpret something; and what has no expression whatsoever in this framework. Be honest about the direction of the translation — do not solve my problem by importing psychometric assumptions and then presenting them back to me as what the philosophers meant.

Two further things. Before you give me the translations, spend a paragraph or so sketching the map of the debate as you understand it, and say which of these positions you find strongest and which weakest and why; I want to see your reasoning before your conclusions. And when you get to the residue report, address a specific suspicion of mine: the identification problem I keep running into in my own work, where you must assume some items are unbiased before you can estimate the trait, yet you need an estimate of the trait to determine which items are biased, so you iterate and hope the purification converges — is that the same circularity the philosophers are circling around, or only a superficially similar one? Finally, give me a verdict rather than a shrug: does the untranslatable remainder point to something my framework structurally lacks, or does it suggest that the philosophical disagreement is less substantive than it looks, amounting to a disguised modeling choice that my vocabulary would have forced into the open?
