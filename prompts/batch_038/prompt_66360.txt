THE DRAFT: Why the Past Is Behind Us

1. Run a film of a wine glass falling off a table backwards and any viewer will tell you within a second that something is wrong. The shards leap upward, reassemble, and settle neatly onto the tabletop. Nothing like that ever happens. Yet if you could watch the same event at the level of individual atoms, you would be unable to say which way the film was running. Almost every law in the physicist's toolkit — Newton's, Maxwell's, Schrödinger's, Einstein's field equations — works equally well forwards and backwards. Reverse every velocity and let the system evolve, and what you get is a movie of the original movie, played in reverse, that is just as legal as the first.

2. There is one exception, and it is usually skipped, so I want to dwell on it. Neutral kaons, and later B mesons, were found to decay in ways that distinguish matter from antimatter. Given that the joint operation of charge conjugation, parity reversal and time reversal is an exact symmetry of nature, that observed asymmetry implies a genuine violation of time-reversal invariance in the weak interaction. The microscopic laws are therefore not perfectly reversible after all. It is tempting, and I think correct, to see here the seed of the thermodynamic arrow: a small arrow buried in the deepest layer of physics, which everything we experience as the flow of time is that arrow amplified.

3. Boltzmann's move was to stop asking what a system must do and start asking what it will almost certainly do. Take a macrostate — a specification in terms of temperature, pressure, volume — and define its entropy as a measure of how many distinct microscopic arrangements of particles are compatible with it. The second law then ceases to be a prohibition and becomes an overwhelming improbability. There is no law against the air in a room collecting in one corner; there are simply incomparably more arrangements in which it does not. Boltzmann's H-theorem seemed to do better than this, deriving the monotonic rise of entropy directly from the reversible equations of motion of colliding particles, which would make irreversibility a theorem about mechanics rather than a contingent fact about the world.

4. Two contemporaries objected, and both objections are still live. Loschmidt noted that if you take any permitted history of a gas and reverse every velocity, you obtain another permitted history in which entropy decreases; so any derivation of monotonic increase must have smuggled the direction of time in somewhere. It had. Boltzmann's assumption that colliding particles are uncorrelated before they collide but not after is itself time-asymmetric, and it, not the mechanics, is doing the work. Zermelo noted that Poincaré's recurrence theorem says a bounded mechanical system returns arbitrarily close to its starting state given enough time, so entropy must eventually fall. Boltzmann's reply was that the recurrence time exceeds any number worth writing down. For the universe as a whole the reply is less comfortable, since a gravitating, expanding cosmos may not meet the hypotheses of the recurrence theorem at all, and I am not convinced that an objection can be answered by observing that the timescale is long.

5. The current consensus — if that word can be used of something this unsettled — is that the arrow is not in the laws at all. It is in the boundary condition. David Albert, in Time and Chance, calls this the Past Hypothesis: the universe began in a state of fantastically low entropy, and everything we call irreversibility is the shadow that condition casts forward. Add the Past Hypothesis to the microscopic dynamics and to a statistical postulate assigning equal weight to every microstate compatible with a given macrostate, and the second law becomes a theorem rather than a hope. Entropy rises toward the future because, given where we started, that is the only direction in which it can rise.

6. This is where the usual illustrations break down. Entropy is introduced to students as disorder, and disorder is illustrated as a gas spreading evenly through a box, that being its highest-entropy arrangement. But once gravity matters, uniformity is not equilibrium; clumping is. A smooth cloud of self-gravitating gas is in a low-entropy state, and the same matter collapsed into a black hole carries so much entropy that black holes dominate the entropy budget of the observable universe by many orders of magnitude. Penrose's Weyl curvature hypothesis is an attempt to state the Past Hypothesis geometrically: what was constrained at the beginning was the Weyl curvature, the free tidal part of the gravitational field as opposed to the part fixed locally by matter, and it was vanishingly small even though the matter was already in thermal equilibrium at a uniform temperature. Hot, dense and smooth: that combination is the low-entropy condition we have been looking for. The improbability Penrose attaches to it is usually written with an exponent containing an exponent.

7. Inflation is sometimes offered instead, as a brief episode of accelerated expansion that smoothed the universe out. But smoothness is not the achievement here, since smoothness is precisely the special condition requiring explanation. Inflation takes a patch that is already improbable and makes it more so. Whatever it explains about large-scale structure, it relocates the problem of the arrow rather than dissolving it.

8. Boltzmann himself proposed a more radical fix: the cosmos has been in thermal equilibrium forever, and we inhabit a rare fluctuation out of it. The difficulty with this, appreciated only much later, is that small fluctuations are vastly more probable than large ones, so a universe in eternal equilibrium should contain incomparably more disembodied brains flickering into existence for an instant, furnished with false memories of a past, than embodied observers embedded in a thirteen-billion-year history. We have no reason to believe we are such brains, and every reason to distrust the memories of anyone who is, so the fluctuation picture refutes itself. This is why the Past Hypothesis tends to be presented not as a fortunate accident but as something closer to a law.

9. The last asymmetry is the closest to home: we remember the past and not the future, and we act on the future and not on the past. Records turn out to be thermodynamic objects. To leave a trace — a memory, a photograph, a groove in a record — one system must become correlated with another and then be unable to spontaneously decorrelate, which in practice requires dissipation. Any creature that makes records at all will find that its records are of the lower-entropy direction. The psychological arrow is therefore not an extra ingredient of the world; it is the thermodynamic arrow seen from inside. Something similar may hold for radiation. Accelerated charges emit outgoing spherical waves and are never seen absorbing converging waves from infinity, and in the 1940s Wheeler and Feynman tried to derive that asymmetry from cosmological conditions on the surrounding absorbers rather than from the electrodynamics itself.

10. There is a family of proposals that goes a different way. Barbour, Koslowski and Mercati argue that in a purely relational, gravitating universe with no fixed background, typical solutions pass through a unique moment of minimum size and complexity — a Janus point — and then expand and complexify in both time directions away from it. On each side, subsystems form and their internal entropies increase, pointing away from the Janus point. Observers on either side call their own direction the future. On this picture there is no low-entropy boundary condition to postulate and nothing improbable about our situation: the arrow is two-headed, and we live on one of the heads. This strikes me as the most attractive option available and the one I understand least.

11. So the answer to the child's question — why can't we unscramble the egg — is not that the laws forbid it. The laws permit it, and would permit almost anything. The answer is that the egg was scrambled out of something far more extraordinary than the egg: a beginning so special that everything since has been downhill from it, in the strict statistical sense of downhill. We do not live in a universe that runs forward. We live at the trailing edge of an improbability that has not finished unwinding.
