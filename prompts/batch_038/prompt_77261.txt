Okay so I'm a novelist, not a philosopher, and I've been living inside a book for two years now about a county board trying to decide whether to tear up a rule nobody in the town can explain anymore — a nineteenth century ordinance that every house in the old district has to be brick, no exceptions, and the arguement has been going so long that the people having it have forgotten what started it. To research it I read a lot of Burke and his modern admirers, and my friend R., who teaches political theory and is a genuine Burkean, has taken to stopping me mid-sentence with Chesterton's fence: there's a fence across the road, the reformer says it's useless, and the reply is that until you can say what it was put there for you don't get to remove it. He uses it to license a fairly strong conclusion — that the burden of proof always and automatically sits on the person who wants to change something, and that this is the default position of rationality rather than a conservative tic. He also extends it to cases where the practice demonstrably never had a builder at all, and that's where I start to lose him.

Here's my problem, and I want you to think it through carefully rather than give me a standard both-sides summary. Ive started keeping a private list of fence-types because I suspect one image is doing at least three different jobs, and I've come up with two of my own that I don't think the image covers. First, what I've been calling sediment: practices nobody ever built, that accreted out of accidents and lock-in — the paragraph indent, the shape of the QWERTY keyboard, why a chapter is the length it is, and arguably the brick ordinance itself if it began as one builder's cheap choice that everyone else copied. With sediment there's no builder's reason to recover, only a history, so the instruction to go away and think until you can see the use of it has no object. Second, what I've been calling the second fence: the original reason expired eighty years ago but the practice is now held in place by a current party who benefits from it, and when the reformer objects, the defender answers by pointing at that present function — which is not the reason the thing exists. Do those carve at joints, or can the fence image absorb both just by adding epicycles?

So: act as a professional philosopher who works on tradition, precedent, and social epistemology. Brainstorm first — sketch four or five candidate mappings of the analogy before you commit to one — then give me the correspondence table explicitly: what plays the road, the fence, the builder, the builder's purpose, the reformer's ignorance, the removal, and the reversibility of removal. Then the disanalogies, but sorted into the ones that actually bite against R.'s burden-shifting conclusion and the ones that are merely decorative; I don't want a list where "fences are made of wood and customs aren't" sits next to something load-bearing. Then tell me what the image quietly imports that practices lack — my own suspicion is that it assumes reasons are always good reasons, that a practice has one why instead of a chain of them, and that maintaining a thing is costless in the way that letting a fence stand is costless. Then the verdict: does the burden-shifting conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, and does Burke's own argument survive with it, or has "Chesterton's fence" been fused to Burke by slogan-convenience when it's actually a different argument with different commitments? If there's a better image, name it — my candidates are a load-bearing wall in a house whose blueprints are lost, a footpath worn across a field, a word's usage in a language nobody legislates, and a reef — and say which carries the Burkean point best, which R. should be using instead, or invent a better one than all four. No citations, no reading list, please, I can't chase them down and I don't want them; just argue it out.
