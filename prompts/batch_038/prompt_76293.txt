My supervisor's been running a confirmation-theory reading group for years and he's developed this habit of handing down verdicts on cases people bring him, almost like case law. Here's the record so far, all variations on the raven thing.

- A red apple sitting on the table: it does confirm "all ravens are black", but he says anyone who cites it in a paper deserves what they get. Size of the bump: a hair's breadth, and he won't be pushed on how small.
- The warehouse scan — a team that logs species and colour for every object on the shelves and ends up with forty thousand non-black non-ravens: confirms, and he was quite firm that you can't wave the forty thousand away; piled up they're a real, if tiny, increment to the posterior.
- The rigged survey — a team that deliberately sampled only mammals and duly found no black mammals: no evidence whatsoever, because the sample was chosen so that a falsifier couldn't turn up in it.
- The box you're told in advance contains either a raven or a shoe, and its a shoe: also nothing. Knowing the alternatives beforehand kills it, he said.
- The case where the right background knowledge makes spotting a black raven slightly *bad* news for the universal: he accepted it happily and used it to make his pet point that confirmation is always relative to what you already knew.
- Grue emeralds: real problem, but a different one — about which predicates we're licensed to project, not about which observations count as evidence. He refuses to let the two get mixed up.

Next week's case is mine, and I want to predict his ruling before the session so I can argue with him properly instead of being ambushed. Our lab has a species classifier. We point it at a big unlabelled archive of field photos and it flags 2% of them as possibly-raven.

- Audit A: a human checks every single flagged photo, and finds that every actual raven in there is black. (Some flagged things turn out to be black birds that aren't ravens; those get tossed.)
- Audit B: a human checks a random 1% sample of the *un*flagged photos and finds no ravens in it at all, black or otherwise.

Which of A and B does he say confirms "all ravens are black", and by how much? My gut says he'll favour B and treat A as compromised by the flagging step, but I can equally see him saying A is the only one that's about ravens at all and B is just noise about the classifier. I can't work out which.
