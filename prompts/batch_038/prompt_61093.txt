I am in the Upper Sixth and I run a small decision-theory reading group at my school. Over the summer my sister, who is in her second year reading Philosophy, Politics and Economics, worked through a batch of problems with me and gave a verdict on each. I wrote them down at the time because I intend to present them next week; unfortunately the case I most want her opinion on is one she has never seen, and she is travelling without reliable internet until after the presentation. I should therefore like to work out what she would say.

Her verdicts, in my phrasing rather than hers:

1. The familiar problem: a predictor of unblemished record has put £1,000,000 in a closed box if and only if it predicted I would take that box alone, and nothing if it predicted I would take both; a second, transparent box visibly holds £1,000. The problem says nothing whatever about how the predictor produces its predictions. She says: take the closed box alone.
2. The same, except that I am told the predictor works by running a perfect simulation of my reasoning and forecasting that I shall do whatever the simulation does. She says: still take the closed box alone.
3. The same sums, but there is no simulation. The forecast was made by a firm of actuaries using only aggregate statistics about people of my age, income and postcode. Their record is 90 per cent — good, but nowhere near perfect. She says: take both boxes.
4. A one-off prisoner's dilemma against an exact copy of myself, which I am assured reasons as I do. She says: cooperate.
5. A one-off prisoner's dilemma against a stranger who knows nothing about me and whom I shall never meet again. She says: defect.
6. A gene that causes both a certain cancer and a strong urge to smoke, where the smoking is itself harmless; the gene is described as 90 per cent reliable in producing each effect. She says: smoke.
7. The familiar problem again, except that both boxes are transparent and I can see the £1,000,000 sitting in the closed one. She says: take both.

The case she has not seen, which I want to put to the group, deliberately crosses two of her earlier ones. The predictor is once more a perfect simulation of my reasoning — the same scanning technology as in 2 — but its designers, anxious about litigation, have wired in a randomiser that overrides the simulation's verdict on one run in ten. Its overall record is therefore 90 per cent, precisely as in 3. The closed box holds £1,000,000 if and only if the noisy simulation says I shall take it alone; the transparent box holds £1,000.

I want a prediction of her verdict, but I am more interested in the structure beneath it. Please:

(a) Set out the candidate rules that account for all seven verdicts at once. I can see one that turns on how the forecast is produced and one that turns on how reliable the forecaster is, and I suspect there are others I have not thought of. My group knows the labels causal decision theory and evidential decision theory, and one member insists on a newer third family, but I should rather you derived the rules from her record than matched them to names you have met before.

(b) Say what each surviving rule predicts for the noisy-simulation case, and identify which of her seven earlier verdicts each rule would treat as the closest analogue of it.

(c) Give me your single best guess, with the reasons for preferring it to the others — and be blunt about whether the seven verdicts genuinely underdetermine the eighth, or whether I have simply failed to see the one rule she is running all along. If some of her verdicts sit awkwardly together, tell me which and why.

(d) Name one further case, describable in a paragraph, that would most cleanly separate the rules that survive your prediction, and say briefly why that particular case does the separating work.

If the record does underdetermine the new case, please also tell me what I would be committing my group to by presenting your prediction as the likely one, since I shall have to defend the choice in the room.
