You are a legal philosopher whose specialty is constitutional theory and the logic of rule-change — the problem space running from Hart's distinction between continuing and self-embracing sovereignty, through Ross's puzzle about amendment clauses, Suber's work on self-amendment, and the American debate about amendment power outside Article V. I want you to think carefully and analytically rather than historically: assume I already know what those authors conclude, and give me your own reconstruction of an impasse I am living inside.

**The situation.** I chair the governance committee of a forty-year-old, member-governed international research-data consortium. It is not a state: no courts, no police, no legislature, roughly two hundred institutional members across thirty countries, a secretariat, an auditor, and a founding Charter that has been amended eleven times. Article IX of the Charter provides, in the relevant part: "This Charter may be amended only by the unanimous written consent of all Founding Members. No amendment shall alter this Article." There were nine Founding Members in 1984. Two institutions have since dissolved with no successor, and one state entity was reconstituted under a different name; the secretariat treats three surviving bodies as "all the Founding Members." Because unanimity has become practically unattainable, a working group has proposed a two-step route: first, secure the unanimous written consent of the three survivors to delete the second sentence of Article IX; second, at the next Assembly, replace the voting rules by the two-thirds majority that the disentrenched Article IX would then permit.

Note what I take to be the real difficulty, so your diagnosis does not miss it: the first step is prohibited on the face of the second sentence, since deleting that sentence is itself an alteration of Article IX. Nobody on the committee thinks the text authorizes the maneuver. The dispute is about whether the text's prohibition is the last word on the matter.

**The three hardened positions.** After four meetings, nothing has moved, and each faction now thinks the others are making a category error.

- **A (the validity purists).** Step one is void, not merely imprudent. A rule of change cannot authorize its own replacement, because the authority of any amended clause would have to derive from the very clause it abolishes; once the entrenchment is genuinely absolute, the only honest route out is a break — the members reconstitute the consortium under a fresh instrument, and thereafter validity flows from the new acceptance rather than from Article IX. They also hold that "all the Founding Members" cannot silently shrink to three, so the premise of step one is already false.
- **B (the constituent-power camp).** Entrenchment binds constituted organs, not the body whose power constituted them. Article IX allocates a procedure; it does not exhaust the amendment power of the membership, which was never surrendered and cannot be. Acting together — the surviving Founding Members, or the Assembly as the whole membership — the members retain a self-embracing capacity to alter even the amending article. On this view the two-step is not a trick but the ordinary exercise of a residual power the text presupposes.
- **C (the acceptance camp).** Both A and B are wasting the committee's time. There is no fact about validity independent of what this organization's officials actually accept afterward: the secretariat, the auditor, the Assembly chair, the national funders who certify our budgets. Run the two-step, see whether anyone treats the resulting Charter as authoritative, and the question settles itself. The doctrinal argument is post-hoc rationalization of a political preference about how hard change should be.

One further datum that puzzles me. Our reading group plays a Nomic-style rules game — a game whose rules exist to be amended by the players, including the rule governing amendment — and the same three-way split appears there, among the same people, with no officials, no funders, no auditors and no legal system anywhere in sight. If the disagreement reproduces under those conditions, then C's "go look at what officials accept" cannot be the real fault line either.

**My hypotheses, offered to be tested rather than assumed.**

1. A, B and C are answering three distinct questions under the single heading "can we do this?": a question about the derivation of validity within the system, a question about the locus of residual constituent power, and a question about acceptance and prediction. No argument touches because none is addressed to the question the others are asking.
2. A and B agree on every fact and on the entire text. They differ only in a picture of authority — a chain-of-delegation picture on which every power must be traceable to a prior conferral, against a residual-power picture on which the constituting body's competence is never spent — and neither picture can be defended from inside the Charter. If so, the disagreement is bedrock, not verbal and not empirical.
3. The Ross-style paradox is an artifact of description. It arises only if one treats validity as a property conferred once and for all, and only if one collapses the difference between "a rule can be changed" and "a rule can authorize its own change."

**What I want from you.**

- A diagnosis of the structure of this disagreement: verbal, substantive, a splitting of questions, a clash of evidential standards, a clash of pictures, or an ill-posed question. Build the diagnosis out of what each faction actually does with the same Article IX, and tell me explicitly which of my three hypotheses survive contact with the details and which do not.
- Whether A and B genuinely disagree, and if they do, whether anything short of adopting one picture of authority could decide between them.
- Whether C is right that the issue collapses into an empirical prediction. If the doctrinal talk is not idle, say precisely what normative or practical work it is doing that the prediction cannot do — and use the Nomic case as a test of your answer, since that is where C seems weakest.
- Whether there is a real logical paradox here or only an artifact, and if real, what the paradox does and does not rule out. In particular: does it rule out the two-step, or only one description of the two-step?
- Whether the fact that this is a private, member-governed body rather than a state changes the analysis, or whether that difference is quietly doing work in the stalemate — for instance by removing the institution that would normally settle which picture of authority is operative.
- Whether the basic-structure line of cases in Indian constitutional law and the American argument about amendment outside Article V are the same puzzle, or different puzzles that rhyme. Tell me frankly where the analogy misleads a committee like mine.
- What would count as progress at the next meeting: a distinction, a re-description of the proposal, a piece of evidence about acceptance, a change of question, or a decision procedure that lets the committee act without resolving the theory. Give me something I can actually put on the table, and state what each faction would have to concede for it to be accepted.
- Finally, an honest judgment about whether progress should be expected at all, or whether this is the kind of disagreement that terminates in a decision rather than an argument — and if so, what makes one decision procedurally legitimate rather than merely successful.
