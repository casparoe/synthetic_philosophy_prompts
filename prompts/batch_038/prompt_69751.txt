My talk argues that the standard 'victors' justice' complaint about Nuremberg aims at the wrong target, and I need to be ready for a room that will not enjoy being told this. In a fortnight I give the keynote at a one-day workshop on the legal legacies of the post-war trials, hosted by a law school centre for international criminal justice. The audience is about sixty: mostly postgraduate students and early-career lawyers who intend to work at the ICC, the residual mechanisms or in atrocity-prevention advocacy, and who need the 1945–46 tribunal to be a respectable ancestor rather than an embarrassment; two legal historians of the trials; a retired appellate prosecutor from the ad hoc tribunals; and one or two legal philosophers who know Hart's account of international law as a regime of primary rules lacking secondary rules of change and adjudication.

My thesis, in outline. First: the familiar retroactivity objection — nullum crimen sine lege, the London Agreement of August 1945 criminalising crimes against peace after the event — is the weakest form of the complaint, because the answerable version of it concedes too much and the strong version is available only to someone who thinks the defendants could not have known their conduct was criminal. Second: the damaging objection is about pedigree, not chronology. Four states made an instrument that created criminal norms for, and constituted a tribunal over, nationals of a state that was no party to it and had no government capable of consenting — the pacta tertiis problem later codified in the Vienna Convention. Third: the stock reply, that the Berlin Declaration of June 1945 vested supreme authority over Germany in those four powers, concedes the point rather than answering it, since it locates the tribunal's authority in the victors' assumption of sovereignty. Fourth: the structure did not disappear with Rome; it migrated to Security Council referrals of non-party states and to the current push for a special tribunal on aggression against Ukraine, where the accused state cannot consent.

Prepare my Q&A sheet: eight or nine objections this audience will actually press, ordered by the damage they would do, phrased in the register each group uses — the activists' worry that I am handing comfort to trial-denialism and to Justice Pal's dissent, the historians' insistence on the live alternative of summary executions in 1945, the ex-prosecutor's catalogue of what the tribunal got procedurally right. For each, a two-minute reply, what I should concede in the first sentence, and where my reply is softest. Finish with the question I am hoping nobody asks.
