For several years I sat in the seminar of a philosopher I will call R., who has since retired, and I have kept her verdicts on a running set of cases about whether it was permissible for someone to hold a belief. I am now trying to reconstruct the principle she was operating with, and I need your help adjudicating a case she never addressed.

Her verdicts, as I recorded them at the time (her stated reasons are my paraphrase of what she said in the room, not quotations):

1. A housing administrator signed off on a dormitory's electrical inspection because he did not want to delay move-in, having pushed aside doubts raised by a maintenance worker. The wiring was in fact fine. Verdict: culpable. She stressed that the wrongness lay entirely in how he arrived at the belief, and that the fortunate outcome was irrelevant.
2. A husband believed his wife's denials of an affair despite messages, receipts, and a friend's eyewitness account. Verdict: culpable, and she treated this as the paradigm case, noting that the belief also kept the marriage functioning for two more years.
3. A woman whose adult son disappeared in a river believed he was still alive after the official search was called off, and that belief financed her own continued searching. There was no body. Verdict: not culpable. She distinguished case 2 on the grounds that this woman's evidence was thin rather than contrary.
4. A graduate student believed his manuscript would be accepted and worked through the night revising it; it was accepted. Verdict: not culpable. R. remarked that the student's belief was about his own effort and was corrected daily by the work itself.
5. A physician told a dying patient that she believed he would beat the disease, while knowing the statistics. Verdict: R. split it. The avowal was defensible; the belief was not. She insisted that what one may say or do in a role is a separate question from what one may believe.
6. A juror on a deadlocked panel voted to acquit because a conviction would ruin a life and the evidence was evenly balanced, and afterward told reporters he knew the defendant was innocent. Verdict: culpable for the statement to the press, not for the vote. A decision under compulsion is not a belief, she said, but describing a decision as knowledge is.
7. A village elder believed the river ice would bear the weight of a winter crossing and told the others to go. Several drowned. Verdict: culpable, emphatically. She made the point that when others act on your belief, the duty to inquire is stricter, not weaker: the gravity of the option raises the evidential bar rather than lowering it.
8. A coach believed his unfancied team would win the regional title and said so constantly; the team did. Verdict: not culpable. She noted two features: the proposition was one whose truth his confidence helped produce, and its effects ran only through players who had signed up to be coached.

The case she never ruled on, and the one I want decided: a regional public-health director faces a collapse in childhood vaccination coverage. Her own analysts have surfaced a small, statistically ambiguous safety signal from a newly licensed vaccine that they cannot yet attribute either to the product or to reporting artifacts. Measles transmission has resumed. The surveillance system that could resolve the ambiguity is funded out of the immunization program's own budget, so if coverage falls below the threshold the data that would settle her doubt will never be gathered. She concludes, privately and in a press briefing, that the vaccine is safe for the region's children, and she withholds the ambiguous signal from the briefing on the reasoning that disclosure would tip coverage below the threshold. Parents vaccinate on the strength of her statement. Two of her analysts ask to have their dissent minuted; she refuses.

I want the following, in this order:

- The candidate rules that jointly fit all eight verdicts. I expect proportionality to evidence, something in the family of James's genuine option, a rule keyed to whether a belief helps make itself true, and a rule keyed to whether the believer stays exposed to correction, but do not assume that is the right decomposition; if one of these cannot be made to fit cases 3, 7, and 8 simultaneously, say so and replace it.
- What each surviving rule predicts for the director, and where two of them come apart most sharply.
- A single committed prediction of R.'s verdict, with the precedent I have listed that is the closest analog and an account of why it is closer than the others. Do not give me a balanced survey or a menu with a shrug at the end: pick a verdict, say which rule she is running, and defend the attribution against the best rival reading. If you think the track record genuinely underdetermines her answer, say so explicitly and then still commit, explaining what would have to be true of her for your prediction to be wrong.
- One further case, described in enough detail to be adjudicated, that would discriminate between the two rules you take most seriously, together with what each would say about it.

Please write the response in American English. I am not looking for a summary of the debate between Clifford and James and their commentators; assume I know it. What I need is a reading of this particular track record and a decision about this particular case.
