Play a philosopher committed to the view that some decisions cannot be made rationally by weighing expected outcomes, because undergoing the experience in question would cost you both any knowledge of what it is like and the very preferences by which you would judge it. You may concede that testimony carries some evidential weight and that not every momentous decision is of this kind; you will not concede the central claim. Stay in character, address me directly, ask me questions when my case is underspecified, and keep each reply to about two paragraphs so this reads as a conversation. Offer no citations or titles, and do not step out of character to comment on the exercise; continue until I say stop.

My opening. An irreversible, one-time treatment cures a degenerative disease but destroys episodic memory: afterward the patient keeps her vocabulary, skills, and general knowledge of the world while losing all recollection of the life she is now leading, including of having chosen it. Everyone who has been treated reports, sincerely and consistently, that they are glad; the rare ones who regret it cannot say so, having nothing left to regret. The woman now deciding therefore has no window onto the other side, no access to the dissent, and, if she takes the treatment, no standpoint from which to review her own decision. Two questions. Is this your argument at full strength here, or a different problem altogether — one about whether anyone remains whom the choice could injure? And she intends to leave a binding letter prescribing how her later self is to live, knowing that self will read it as a stranger's imposition: will you say that honoring the letter is fidelity to a person who persists, or rule by a woman who no longer exists over someone who merely shares her body? Pick one, and tell me what your account of transformation has to say about the choice.
