For several years I have kept a record of a colleague's verdicts — call him M. — on which existence theorems he is willing to state flatly in seminar and which he will state only with a flag about the axioms. He has never articulated a principle, and he resents being labelled a constructivist or a predicativist; his line is that he is merely careful about what has been proved. Below is the record, together with the strength facts as I understand them, since I believe the pattern turns on them.

Stated flatly, without caveat:

1. Hahn–Banach for a normed space possessing a countable dense subset. His own proof extends along an enumeration, taking the midpoint of the admissible interval at each stage.
2. A countable union of countable sets is countable. (Requires countable choice; not provable in ZF — in the Feferman–Levy model the reals are such a union.)
3. Every infinite set has a countably infinite subset. (Not provable in ZF, infinite Dedekind-finite sets being consistent; follows from countable choice.)
4. A countable product of compact metric spaces is compact. (Diagonal argument over an enumeration.)
5. Every countable commutative ring with unity has a maximal ideal. "Run the enumeration and recurse," he says.

Refused, or accepted only with an explicit axiom-flag:

6. There is a subset of the unit interval that is not Lebesgue measurable. His comment: in the Solovay universe — ZF plus dependent choice plus every set of reals Lebesgue measurable, whose consistency wants an inaccessible but that is beside the point — the sentence is simply false, so it is a fact about his interlocutor's axioms rather than about the interval.
7. The Banach–Tarski decomposition. Same comment.
8. The reals can be well-ordered.
9. There is a nonprincipal ultrafilter on the natural numbers. Here he added that a free ultrafilter is a subset of Cantor space that is neither measurable nor Baire, so this is item 6 wearing a hat.

My working hypothesis is that M. runs one test and one test only: does the sentence hold in every model of ZF + DC, or can Solovay's model refute it? If it can, he treats the result as a theorem of ZFC rather than of mathematics. But I doubt the record pins this down, because a rival accommodates every entry: he rejects a theorem when its conclusion asserts an object of an untamable kind — a non-measurable set of reals, a paradoxical decomposition, a well-ordering of the continuum, a free ultrafilter — and otherwise accepts it, whatever the proof quietly consumes. Items 2 and 3 show he is no constructivist; item 9 is the pivot, since the wildness there is not visible in the statement and must be derived. Note also that the record contains no case in which a tame-sounding theorem turned out to need more than dependent choice, so the two readings have never been forced apart.

Next week's seminar is the theorem that every vector space has a basis — equivalent over ZF to the full axiom of choice, as the speaker will remark, and therefore to item 8. Her running example will be a basis for the reals over the rationals.

What I would like from you:

(a) State, as decision procedures rather than slogans, every rule consistent with the whole record; identify which of them my hypothesis is, and which survive without it.

(b) Predict M.'s verdict on the basis theorem, with a degree of confidence, naming the entries that are the closest analogues and the direction in which each pulls.

(c) Say whether the prediction changes if the speaker writes the equivalence with the well-ordering theorem on the board, and what any such sensitivity reveals about whether his rule governs theorems or statements of theorems.

(d) Name one further theorem — stateable in a sentence, and one whose choice-strength is a settled matter — whose verdict would separate the surviving rules in the direction the basis case does not, that is, one that a strength-based rule and a wildness-based rule score oppositely to the way they score the basis theorem. Give each rule's prediction for it.

(e) Say what M. commits himself to on either verdict: whether a rejection costs him anything he has already accepted, and whether an acceptance costs him anything he has already refused.

Argue from the strength facts supplied above and from what you know of the relevant proofs. I am after the structure of his practice, not a survey of the literature, and you should not need references of any kind.
