I lead the platform group at a mid-size software company. We maintain roughly forty shared libraries and internal services, and we enforce a house dependency policy that has been inherited rather than argued for. Its rules, stated as plainly as I can:

1. Every package carries a three-part version number. The last part increments for internal corrections that leave the package's stated interface alone; the middle part increments when functionality is added without breaking that interface; the first part increments only when a change breaks it.
2. Every package must publish an explicit statement of its interface — the functions, arguments, return types, and error conditions it guarantees. Anything not in that statement is, by policy, not guaranteed.
3. Consumers must pin the first number exactly and are allowed to let the other two float, so that corrective and additive releases arrive automatically.
4. A lockfile in each repository records the precise resolved version of every package, direct and transitive, and is regenerated only when a human deliberately asks for it.
5. Our house rule when a floating update breaks someone's build and the published interface statement did not change: the defect is in the producer's version number, not in the consumer's code. The consumer files a bug, pins back, and moves on.

(The version-number convention is essentially the one the wider ecosystem uses; I mention it only so you don't have to guess at rule 1.)

The policy generates recurring disputes that it cannot seem to settle. A "corrective" release reorders the keys in a map it serializes, or changes the rounding of a figure it computes, and a downstream service's output shifts; the producer points out that its interface statement says nothing about ordering or precision. Another library has always silently coerced malformed input into a default value; a correction makes it raise instead, and a consumer that had been relying on the coercion for years loses production traffic — again, the coercion was never documented. A third case involves a change two levels down in the transitive dependency tree, where neither the immediate producer nor the consumer wrote any of the altered code.

I want an account of what this policy takes change to be. Specifically:

Reconstruct the implicit theory as precisely as you can. What does it assume about the relation between a published statement of an interface and the package's actual behavior? What does it assume about how far the consequences of a modification can propagate, and about who is in a position to know them in advance? What does it assume about what remains the same across a release — is that assumed to be statable, and by whom?

Then separate what the policy strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with. I suspect several quite different accounts could license these same five rules — for instance, one on which the version number is a promise made between cooperating parties, another on which it reports a fact about the world, another on which it functions as an allocation of liability. Say which of these the practice actually requires, and where it is genuinely underdetermined.

Next, tell me which rival views of change and of systems the policy rules out or cannot accommodate — including any view on which "breaking" is a relation between a modification and a particular consumer's use of it rather than a property of the modification itself, and any view on which a system's behavior belongs to the whole configuration and cannot be distributed across its parts.

Assess the policy for internal coherence. Rule 1 tells consumers to trust a declaration about what has changed; rule 4 tells them to trust nothing and to freeze the entire world instead. Do these express contradictory assumptions, or does one repair the other? And is rule 5 coherent at all, if what a consumer depends on outruns what any producer can state?

Finally, take the rival seriously: suppose compatibility cannot be declared by producers but only discovered by consumers, and suppose that what a change leaves untouched is indefinitely large and never fully enumerable in advance. What would our artifacts, our build pipeline, our release cadence, and our organization have to become? What would that cost, what would it lose that the current policy preserves, and is there a defensible middle position rather than a choice between the two extremes?
