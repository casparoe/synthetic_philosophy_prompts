I have been asked to deliver the tribute at a farewell dinner next month for a philosopher of mind who is retiring after thirty-six years on the faculty. The dinner is held in her own building, and she has submitted, characteristically, a page of notes on what she does and does not want said.

Her view, so that the speech can get it right. She came up in the higher-order tradition and spent her career defending a particular version of it:

- First-order perceptual and affective states are never conscious in their own right. Nothing is like anything for a subject in virtue of a state's intrinsic properties or the sophistication of its information processing; a state is conscious only insofar as the subject has a suitable higher-order representation of being in it.
- Those higher-order representations are themselves unconscious. She rejected every proposal to halt the regress with an inner sense that is itself phenomenally lit up, and she was equally firm that the only phenomenological data her theory owes an account of are facts about how things seem to the subject.
- Her signature move, and the one her former students will be listening for: against the objection from empty higher-order thoughts — the case in which the higher-order state misfires and represents a first-order state that is not there — she held that experience is constituted by the representation rather than matched to it. A misfired higher-order state does not produce a false appearance of an experience; it produces a real one, and there is no further fact about what the subject would have been having in its absence.
- The upshot, on which she built her reputation for provocation: consciousness is cheap in some places and expensive in others. She was generous about fish, cephalopods, and modestly self-monitoring machines, and entirely unmoved by how complex a system's first-order processing might be.

The room will hold roughly seventy people. A third are philosophers of mind, including four of her former students who now hold faculty positions and who will register instantly if the summary above is garbled. Another third are from the cognitive neuroscience wing of the building; they have spent a decade running pre-registered adversarial collaborations against her theory and regard it as unfalsifiable in principle, and two of them are seated at her table. The remainder are family and friends who have never read a philosophy paper: her husband, her two adult children, and her younger brother, a hospice chaplain, who has told me he will be listening for whether anything I say could be repeated at a bedside.

Her notes, which I intend to honor:

1. The word qualia is banned outright. She thinks it teaches ordinary listeners to treat experiences as objects one possesses, and she would rather the speech say nothing than say it in that vocabulary.
2. No inner-theatre imagery of any kind: no lights coming on or going out, no one home, no screen, no spectator, no show.
3. No analogies that make consciousness a secretion or an excretion of the brain. She holds that they explain nothing and license the wrong metaphysics.
4. One joke, and it must be a joke about the theory rather than about her lateness or her marginalia. It has to work for the neuroscientists and for her husband, who has never read her.
5. She knows that on her own account a person under heavy sedation at the end of life may go on processing while the higher-order states lapse, and she has said plainly that she would not want it reported of her that she was still in there. She does not want the speech to lie about this, and she does not want it to make a room full of grieving people say it aloud either. She wants the difficulty carried, not dissolved.
6. The final two minutes must be acceptable to the neuroscientists, to her students, and to her brother without any of them accepting her theory — a claim about her rather than about consciousness, and one that is true on every account of consciousness represented in that building.

Please write the tribute. It should run about six minutes when spoken, which is to say roughly eight hundred to nine hundred words; it should be warm rather than solemn; and where it is technical it must be exact. I specifically need one passage, early enough that the non-specialists are still following, that renders the constitutive move from the third bullet in language a hospice chaplain can follow and a philosopher of mind would not wince at. The genuine difficulty, and where I would concentrate your effort, is satisfying the fourth, fifth, and sixth notes at once: a joke that turns on misfired higher-order representations, a refusal to reassure the room with a claim her own theory denies, and a peroration that is neutral between her view and its rivals are pulling in three different directions. Do not append an explanation of how the constraints were met; give me only the speech, with, if you wish, brief bracketed stage directions for pauses.
