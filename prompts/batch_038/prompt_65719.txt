For three years, six of us have kept a shared spreadsheet of predictions. Anyone may add a question with a yes-or-no answer — whether the bridge reopens by spring, whether a given film takes the prize, whether it rains on the day of the picnic — and anyone may post a number between 0 and 1 against it. When the outcome comes in we record it as 1 or 0, and each of us is scored on the average of the squared gaps between the numbers we posted and the outcomes, taken over just the questions we posted on. The lowest average at the end of the year buys the whisky, and the rest of us get nothing.

Two of the others have lately taken to advising me on how to post, and their advice points in opposite directions.

One says I should never post above 0.9 or below 0.1, however sure I feel. His reasoning is that sooner or later I will be flatly wrong about something I was certain of, and a single bad squared gap can undo a season of sensible middling numbers, so it is better to be a little off on everything than badly off on one thing.

The other says I should never post at all unless I am sure. Her reasoning is that the average is taken only over the questions I bothered to answer, so every question I leave alone is a question I cannot be wrong about, and whoever posts on fifty questions has handed themselves fifty ways to lose.

Here is what troubles me. If I followed both, I would end up with very few numbers on record, all of them timid, all of them on questions I hardly care about — none of them the number I actually believed — and I would win. It seems absurd that a game whose whole point is getting things right should be won by the person with the least on the record and the least at stake, and yet I cannot locate the bad step in either argument. Which of them is right, if either, and is there something we should change about the spreadsheet itself?

Please answer with exactly five numbered points, each of one or two sentences, and with no introduction or conclusion.
