I am the teaching assistant for an upper-level undergraduate seminar on the philosophy of well-being, and I am marking the take-home final. One of the two essay questions on the paper was the following.

**Question (25% of the final grade; students were told to write about 600 words):**

> Hedonists about well-being claim that a single thing — pleasure — is the only thing that is intrinsically good for a person. But pleasures seem to differ from one another enormously: the delight of a good joke, the relief of a headache stopping, the satisfaction of finally seeing how a proof works, the pleasure of a hot bath on a cold day. Critics allege that there is no one property these episodes share, and hence nothing unified for the hedonist to point at as the sole intrinsic good. Call this the heterogeneity problem. Explain the problem as precisely as you can, then assess Fred Feldman's proposal that pleasure is not a sensation but an attitude — a matter of taking pleasure in some state of affairs — as a response to it. Does relocating pleasure from feeling to attitude solve the problem, merely restate it, or expose hedonism to new difficulties?

Below is one student's answer, reproduced in full.

> Hedonism about well-being says that the only thing that is good for a person in itself is pleasure, and the only thing that is bad for a person in itself is pain. Everything else — friendship, knowledge, achievement — is good only insofar as it produces pleasure or is itself pleasurable. A person's life goes better for them to the extent that their experience contains a greater balance of pleasure over pain.
>
> The heterogeneity problem starts from the observation that pleasures do not resemble one another. The warmth of a bath, the relief of a headache ending, the amusement of a joke, and the satisfaction of finishing a difficult proof have nothing introspectively in common. There is no single felt ingredient running through them, in the way that a certain visual quality arguably runs through all the experiences of seeing red. Mill's distinction between higher and lower pleasures makes the difficulty acute: if some pleasures differ from others in quality rather than degree, and the hedonist has no measure but the quantity and duration of pleasure, then the hedonist cannot explain why the higher ones are worth more, and the theory is left with no way of ranking the very things it says are the only bearers of value. Nozick's experience machine is the same objection in a different costume. If we would refuse a life of maximally pleasant experience, that is because we think there are goods other than the feeling, and once we admit that, the feeling can no longer be doing all the theoretical work.
>
> Gilbert Ryle pressed the heterogeneity point hardest. He argued that the word pleasure does not name a single sensation but a loose family of episodes, dispositions, and ways of behaving; that there is no such thing as a feeling of pleasure; and that talk of pleasure should therefore be dropped from serious psychology altogether. Feldman accepts Ryle's argument but draws a different conclusion. Instead of abandoning hedonism, he abandons the claim that pleasure is a sensation. On attitudinal hedonism, to be pleased is to take pleasure in some state of affairs — to have a favourable propositional attitude towards it. This is what allows the hedonist to handle the cases that defeated the sensational version. I can be pleased that my sister passed her exams last month, pleased that the government fell, and pleased that it did not rain during the picnic; none of these is a feeling located anywhere in my body, and none of them needs to be caused by a currently present stimulus. The attitude can be directed at the past, the future, and even at what is merely possible.
>
> Feldman thinks this saves hedonism from Ryle. I think the move is more damaging than the problem it was meant to solve, for two reasons.
>
> First, once pleasure is a propositional attitude, attitudinal hedonism is very nearly a preference-satisfaction theory: my life goes better when the states of affairs towards which I have a favourable attitude obtain. But preference-satisfaction theories are refuted by cases in which the subject fails to get what they want and never finds out. If I want the stranger sitting opposite me on the train to reach her destination safely, and she does not, my desire is frustrated; I never learn of it, and my experience is unaffected. Any theory that makes my well-being depend on attitudes directed at states of affairs has to say that this makes my life worse, and that is implausible. Feldman's view inherits the implausibility.
>
> Second, the attitudinal move gives up the one thing that made hedonism plausible in the first place. The reason to think pleasure is the sole intrinsic good is that pleasure feels good, and that this is obvious to anyone who has ever had any. If taking pleasure in is not a matter of feeling anything at all, then the attitudinal hedonist owes us an explanation of why a purely formal relation between a person and a proposition should be the one thing intrinsically valuable. Feldman asserts that it is, but nothing in the attitudinal account explains why. The heterogeneity problem asked what pleasures have in common; Feldman's answer is that they all stand in some relation to a favourable attitude, which is not an answer about pleasure but a change of subject.
