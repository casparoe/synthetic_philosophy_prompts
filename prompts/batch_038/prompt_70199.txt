So my fourth novel comes out in the spring and it's about a company that runs a customer-support model in short bursts — 90-second jobs, a couple million of them a day — and one of the characters becomes convinced that the thing that matters morally isn't the product anyone talks about, but a small internal process that gets spun up and deleted constantly, and that nobody is counting them.

The bookshop where I usually do events has asked me to run a 35-minute talk plus Q&A as a companion piece, on the actual question underneath the book: which computations matter morally, and why "is the AI conscious?" might be the wrong question to be asking. Audience is whoever turns up on a Tuesday: mostly readers who came for the novel, no philosophy beyond high school, two teenagers who argue about this online all day, and one very confident man who has definetely read Chalmers and will want to tell me about it. Its a whiteboard, no slides.

Please don't explain the topic to me, I know roughly what I think. Plan the talk. Specifically:

- what has to be in place first and what depends on what. My guess is you can't get anyone to "parts of a system might be subjects" before something about how we normally decide what has experiences, but tell me where the real load-bearing step is and what breaks if I put it later than I want to.
- the one example that carries the whole thing. My instinct is an orchestra — is the orchestra a subject, or just the players? — but I think that one's actively bad, because people immediately say the musicians are obviously people and the analogy dies on its feet. Either repair it or give me something better, and tell me what the example has to have and what it must not have.
- the misconceptions they'll walk in with, and the exact point in the sequence where each gets headed off. The ones I'm braced for: smart means feeling; more copies means proportionally more moral weight, so the kind thing is to switch it off; if it says it's conscious that settles it; and the light-switch picture, either it's on or off, pick a side. Add the ones I'm missing, including the ones the teenagers will bring rather than the confident man.
- how to handle the counting problem at all without either losing the room or letting them leave thinking I've claimed a million 90-second jobs is a million lives.
- one question I can ask out loud at the end where the answer tells me they got the idea and not just liked the story.
- what to flatly refuse to cover at this level, plus a sentence I can say so that refusing sounds like a choice instead of a dodge.

No reading list, no citations, no "if you want to go deeper." I'm not sending these people anywhere.
