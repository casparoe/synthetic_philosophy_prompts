You are to work as a political theorist who specializes in democratic legitimacy and the composition of the electorate — someone who has thought seriously about what makes a body of voters the right body, rather than someone summarizing positions. Please think carefully rather than quickly: before committing to anything, brainstorm the range of candidate organizing principles that could sit behind a set of case verdicts (interest-affectedness, subjection to coercive law, membership or shared fate, stakeholding, capacity for judgment, reciprocity among those who must live with the outcome, and so on), and only then build the account you think best. I want your own reconstruction, not a survey and not a bibliography — no names, no works, no reading list. State whatever principle you land on precisely enough that a third party could apply it to a case I have not thought of and reach the verdict you would.

The context is this. For the past year I have served on the charter commission of a mid-sized city of roughly 180,000 that is creating a new resident assembly with real authority over a slice of the capital budget, alongside the existing council. Two electorates have to be defined: the assembly's, and that of a regional water district whose service area includes our city, two neighboring suburbs, and a band of unincorporated county land. The commission has been asked to justify the lines it draws, and I have been keeping a private list of my own verdicts as the argument has proceeded. I now think the list is not obviously consistent, and I would like to know what, if anything, holds it together.

1. A 44-year-old permanent resident, here 22 years, who owns a restaurant, pays every local tax, sends his children to our schools, and says he will never seek citizenship: enfranchise, obviously.
2. A citizen born here who left with his parents at age five, has never returned, owns nothing here, pays nothing here, and told a reporter he could not find the city on a map: no vote.
3. A citizen who took a three-year contract abroad eight months ago and intends to return: yes for now — but I want to say the answer changes somewhere along the way, and I cannot say where.
4. A person who lives in the next county and commutes in five days a week to work at our hospital: no for the assembly, but yes for the water district, since the district sets the rate his employer pays and regulates discharges into the river he lives beside. This is the pair I find hardest.
5. A person who sleeps in the city but works remotely for an out-of-state firm and files income tax elsewhere: yes.
6. A person here on a two-year visa for a fixed-term engineering project who has stated she will not stay: no. I can see that this looks bad next to 4 and 5 and I cannot articulate why.
7. A 17-year-old with a full-time job and her own apartment, no longer in school: I lean yes. A politically obsessed, unusually bright 12-year-old: no. A 16-year-old still in school and living with her parents: no. I have a gradient here and no line.
8. A person in the third year of a six-year sentence for wire fraud aimed at elderly neighbors: no. The same person ten years after release: yes.
9. A person convicted of criminally negligent homicide in a traffic accident, no prior record, currently serving two years: yes — I said so in committee, was shouted down, and still think it.
10. A woman with moderately advanced dementia in assisted living who no longer recognizes her grandchildren but asks about the mayor every morning: no.
11. A man under a court-ordered conservatorship after a traumatic brain injury, whose guardian manages his money, but who follows the news, holds stable and articulable views, and wants to vote: I have flipped on this four times.
12. The corporation that owns the largest commercial property in the city: no vote, and none through its shareholders either — but its 4,000 local employees each get one, which means its influence is real and merely indirect.
13. A tourist here for a week who could be hurt by our building codes: obviously no, and I want whatever principle survives to explain why this is not simply case 6 in a shorter form.

Here is what I would like back. Give me the principle, or a small set of competing candidates, with each clause explicitly tied to the case or cases that motivate it, and say what each clause costs. Then tell me honestly which of my verdicts the best candidate must overturn, and which it can save only by adding machinery — I would rather lose one verdict than accept a principle with five independent clauses, but I want to know the actual exchange rate rather than a reassurance. Deliver verdicts on cases I have not listed: a person born here to visitors, removed at age two, who returns at thirty; whether the principle can permit weighted or multiple votes for those more affected, for instance giving downstream residents extra weight in the water district; whether one principle can govern both the assembly electorate and the water-district electorate or must be relativized to each body's jurisdiction, and if relativized, whether that is a feature or a retreat; and sixteen-year-olds in a national election versus in this one. Come down somewhere on case 11 specifically, and say what would change your answer about it. On case 7, tell me whether there is a non-arbitrary stopping point or whether I should concede that the age line is a coordination convention with no principled location. On 13 versus 6, tell me what actually distinguishes a short-term presence that confers standing from one that does not — duration, intention, reciprocity, subjection to rules one cannot exit, or something else.

Finally, the reflexive problem, which is what has the commission going in circles: our own body was appointed by a council elected under the old rules, so the people drawing the boundary are not themselves the people who should be drawing it on any of the candidate principles. Tell me whether your principle can be applied to its own adoption, or whether it requires a non-democratic or differently democratic moment to get started, and what that costs it. Do not resolve any of this by noting that reasonable people disagree; commit to a view and defend it.
