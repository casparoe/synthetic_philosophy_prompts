I lead the product-safety group at a company building general-purpose AI assistants. My training is in software engineering, not philosophy, so please bear with the way I frame the problem. We are drafting an internal policy governing how our systems should communicate with users, and I would like an essay that helps me settle a disagreement inside the team. The disagreement matters more with each model generation, because the systems are becoming better writers than any human copywriter we could hire, and a large part of that skill is the ability to move a reader.

The proposal under dispute is one I will call the influence budget. The thought is that when a system answers a question, its output changes the user's beliefs and intentions by two distinguishable routes: by the propositional content it supplies, and by the way it presents that content — word choice, the ordering of considerations, register, expressed confidence, and which of the many true relevant facts get mentioned. The proposal is to measure and cap the second route while leaving the first essentially unconstrained. Operationally, I imagine generating for each candidate answer a matched counterfactual answer that conveys the same content under a deliberately flattened rhetorical setting, and treating the divergence in expected user response between the two as the quantity to be budgeted. If the divergence exceeds a threshold, the answer is regenerated.

A colleague's objection is the one I cannot answer, and I would like the essay to confront it directly rather than wave at it. The objection is that there is no such thing as the same content under a neutral setting. Selecting which relevant truths to include is already the largest available lever over a reader, and there is no canonical complete answer against which an omission could be measured, so the counterfactual baseline is arbitrary. On this view the budget does not track anything morally significant; it tracks our engineering choices about what to flatten, and gives the whole enterprise a quantitative veneer it has not earned.

Please weigh the case for rhetorical neutrality as a design goal — a system that aims only to convey content, leaving the user's judgment as close as possible to what it would have been on hearing the same content from a maximally dull but equally accurate source — against the case that neutrality is both impossible and undesirable, since a system that declines to make a grave consideration vivid is not standing aside but choosing badly, and users routinely want and benefit from being moved toward what they already reflectively endorse.

Somewhere in the essay, please assess a specific alternative test that has come up in our discussions: an instance of influence is permissible if and only if the user, told precisely which techniques were deployed and why, would endorse having been exposed to them. I want to know whether this does real work, or whether it collapses in one of two directions — into bare preference satisfaction, which permits any manipulation the user is retrospectively glad about, or into an impossibly demanding standard, since almost nobody endorses being steered once the steering is made visible. If you think a repairable version exists, say what the repair consists in.

So the discussion has traction, please also work through one concrete case. A user asks the assistant how to manage a chronic condition they have just been diagnosed with. The clinical content is largely settled and uncontroversial; framing plausibly changes adherence by a large margin; the system has reliable evidence that this particular user disengages from messages that read as alarmist. Is it permissible, obligatory, or forbidden for the system to shape tone and the ordering of material in order to raise the probability of adherence? Does your verdict generalize, or is it specific to this kind of question?

I want a definite conclusion, not a balanced survey. State your verdict in the opening paragraph and spend the rest of the essay arguing for it and defending it against the strongest objections you can identify, including my colleague's. A qualified verdict is acceptable — neutrality might be the right target for some classes of question and the wrong target for others — but the qualification must be principled, with the boundaries drawn explicitly and a reason given for drawing them there rather than somewhere else. Qualification may not be used as a way of avoiding commitment.

Aim for roughly twelve hundred to eighteen hundred words. Please write it as continuous prose in ordinary paragraphs, in the manner of a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; I want the reasoning, and I have no way to check sources in any case.
