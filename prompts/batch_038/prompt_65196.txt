Write a dialogue of roughly 1,400–1,800 words between two members of the practice-review panel of a parole board, who must agree on what their finding should say about a third colleague. Both are intelligent, fair-minded, and well informed; neither is a strawman; and the exchange must be genuine back-and-forth, with each responding to what the other actually said.

The colleague under review, Nadia, gives decisive weight in her recommendations to whether an offender appears to her to feel remorse. The following facts are settled and accepted by both panel members, so the dialogue should not spend time re-establishing or disputing them:

- The board commissioned an audit. Independent raters, working from transcripts, coded each statement for content: explicit acknowledgment of the harm done, of the victim's experience, and of the offender's own responsibility. Nadia's remorse ratings correlate strongly with the offender's fluency, composure, and familiarity with the panel's own professional idiom, and only weakly with the coded content.
- Nadia has read the audit, accepts the correlations as reported, and does not question the raters' reliability or good faith. Her position is that the coding scheme measures the articulation of remorse rather than remorse; that a person may be deeply remorseful and unable to say so in the board's vocabulary, or may say so fluently and feel nothing; and that she is attending to the thing itself.
- She adopted this weighting some twenty years ago, after a case in which an offender whose contrition had impressed the board — and whose release Nadia had supported — killed again. She has spoken about this openly. She maintains that the origin of a view no more discredits it than the origin of any other belief discredits that belief.

The two panel members disagree about what would make Nadia's disposition a bias rather than a preference, and their disagreement is not about any further fact, since they accept all of the above.

Ilse holds that bias is a functional notion. A disposition is a bias when its operation is not sensitive to the property its owner cites in its defense — when it would not have changed had that property changed while everything else about the case stayed the same. On her view, endorsement cannot rehabilitate such a disposition, because the endorsement is among the things the disposition produces; the fact that Nadia finds her own practice defensible carries no independent evidential weight.

Marek holds that bias is indexed to the agent's own standpoint. A disposition is a bias when the agent would repudiate it on seeing clearly how it operates, and a preference when she would not. Where the agent has the data, understands it, and holds her policy as a matter of considered judgment, what is left is a disagreement about reasons; recording it as a bias changes the subject from whether she is right to whether she is sound, and settles a contested question by diagnosis instead of argument.

Requirements:

1. At some point each must state the strongest objection to their own account and answer it as well as they honestly can. Where an answer falls short, the other should mark the shortfall precisely rather than triumphantly.
2. Each must invent a further case intended to break the other's test: for Ilse, a disposition that passes the sensitivity test and is still plainly a bias, and one that fails it and is still plainly a defensible preference; for Marek, a disposition the agent wholeheartedly endorses on full information and which is still plainly a bias, and one she repudiates and which is still plainly not. Each invented case must be met by the other, so that both tests are shown to come apart in both directions.
3. The dialogue must confront the specific difficulty that Nadia's reply to the audit — that the instrument measures the wrong thing — is simultaneously the signature of an unfalsifiable prejudice and the signature of legitimate dissent from a bad measure, and that Ilse and Marek cannot tell these apart without assuming what they are trying to establish.
4. Marek must be allowed the point that Ilse's test cannot be applied at all until one has decided whether remorse just is acknowledgment content, which is the substantive question in dispute; and Ilse must be allowed the point that if no specification of remorse is offered that Nadia tracks and the raters missed, then the claim that she tracks the thing itself stops doing explanatory work and becomes the name of her confidence.
5. Neither may win. But the dialogue must not end in a shrug either: it should end with one of them trying to say exactly what survives once every descriptive fact about origin, sensitivity, and endorsement has been agreed — what further question remains, and whose burden it is — and with the other's reply making clear whether that remainder is a real question or the shadow cast by a word.
6. Keep the register plain and professional, with only minimal stage directions. Explain both positions from within the dialogue so that a reader with no background in moral psychology can follow. Do not name, cite, or allude to any philosopher, theory, experiment, or book other than the material supplied here.
