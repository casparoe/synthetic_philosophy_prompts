For roughly a decade, a handful of research groups have run recurring large-N surveys of machine-learning researchers and adjacent experts, designed to elicit probability distributions over when transformative AI will arrive and how quickly it will then improve. The instrument has a recognizable shape. Respondents are asked for the year by which unaided machines will be able to accomplish essentially every economically valuable task better and more cheaply than human workers, reported as quantiles: some chance of that year being early, a median, and a late bound. They are then asked a second, conditional question about the speed of what follows: given that this threshold has been crossed, what is the probability that the climb from broadly human-level performance to vastly superhuman performance takes only months rather than years or decades. Results are published as aggregate distributions, and the median date together with the share of respondents expecting a fast transition gets quoted in policy writing and in public arguments about how much time we have.

The same field, meanwhile, reports its actual progress almost entirely in disaggregated form: hundreds of separate benchmarks, scored per task, with routine acknowledgment that frontier systems are jagged — decisively superhuman at some things, worse than a novice at others — and with explicit resistance, from many of the same researchers, to any single scalar measure of a model's intelligence.

I want the implicit theory of the survey instrument, not a defense or a critique of its forecasts. Reconstruct what the elicitation format presupposes: what it assumes about intelligence being a magnitude that systems possess more or less of; about the transition being a datable event rather than a process with no beginning and no end; about performance being comparable across domains through one ordering that collapses quality and cost; and about whether the speed of what comes after the threshold is a property of the system or a property of the world it is deployed into — complementarities, supply chains, adoption lags, regulation. Say which rival pictures the instrument cannot even express, and therefore quietly rules out, and whether the conditional fast-versus-slow question smuggles in a mechanism such as recursive self-improvement that the threshold question itself does not require. Then take up the tension with disaggregated benchmarking: is the survey flatly inconsistent with the community's own measurement practice, or is there a view of intelligence on which both are legitimate? Finally, describe what an instrument built on the rival picture would actually ask, what it would have to give up that the current one delivers, and whether anything recognizable as a takeoff-speed forecast survives the swap.

Be strict about separating what the practice genuinely requires from what merely sits comfortably with it; where several theories would license the same question wording, say so instead of manufacturing a single metaphysic to fit my framing, and mark clearly which parts of your reconstruction are your own inference rather than anything the designers of such surveys have said. And if you think the premise is confused — that an elicitation instrument is not the kind of thing that presupposes a substantive theory of intelligence, or that I have mischaracterized how these surveys work — say that plainly and explain why, rather than politely reconstructing something to satisfy the request.
