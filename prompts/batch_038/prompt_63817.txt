I spent most of a five-hour train ride from Chicago to Minneapolis arguing with an old friend of mine, who did demand forecasting for a regional grocery distributor for six years before starting a philosophy PhD program, and I have not been able to dislodge his conclusion since. He is unusual in that he knows the logistics side of the claim firsthand — he has actually written the reorder-point rules for poultry and produce — and he insists that this is precisely what makes him a skeptic about individual efficacy rather than a sentimentalist about it. What follows is my reconstruction of the argument he gave me, cleaned up considerably; he says the reconstruction is fair, which I take as no guarantee, since he also says the parts I find most objectionable are the parts he considers merely descriptive.

The argument, as I have it:

(1) An act is wrong only if the world goes worse — in fact or in expectation — because of it.
(2) Any act that contributes to a collective harm does so through one of two kinds of causal structure: either the structure is lumpy, so that outcomes change only when an aggregate crosses a discrete trigger, or it is continuous, so that contributions are diluted into a well-mixed pool.
(3) If the structure is lumpy, an individual contribution falls below every trigger. In real supply chains, inventory buffers, order minima, and batch processing absorb the marginal unit entirely: the probability that my purchase is the one that moves an order is not small but zero, and the same goes for my ballot, since the chance of its being decisive is not merely tiny but swamped by the fact that the polls, the machines, and the tabulation are not sensitive to it at the level of one.
(4) If the structure is continuous, my contribution makes an increment smaller than any quantity that could register as harm. There is no fact of the matter about a difference below the resolution at which the world keeps score, so the increment is not a small wrong-making feature but no feature at all.
(5) Therefore, on either structure, my flight, my steak, and my abstention each leave the world exactly as good as it would otherwise have been.
(6) The reasoning is fully general. It applies not to me only but to every individual act inside any aggregate: the plant manager who authorizes one more shipment, the minister who signs off on one more permit, the driver who takes one more trip. Nothing anyone does individually is ever assessable as making a collective harm worse.
(7) The familiar escapes fail. Appeals to expected harm, complicity, participation in a group agency, integrity, or to what would happen if everyone did it either smuggle back in exactly the efficacy claims that (3) and (4) have just ruled out, or they locate wrongness in features of the agent — intentions, association, character — that would equally condemn acts that are harmless and even beneficial.
(8) So no individual contribution to a collective harm is wrong.

Two things about the argument bother me, and they pull in opposite directions, which is why I cannot settle it myself. I distrust (4) and could not get him to say what it means: it sounds like the claim that a difference too small to be noticed is not a difference, and when I pressed him with the case of a thousand torturers, each of whom raises a victim's pain by an amount too slight for the victim to detect, he said the case was different because pain is not buffered and dismissed it as a change of subject. But I also cannot attack (3), because (3) rests on empirical claims about buffers, batch ordering, and dilution that he knows far better than I do and that I am in no position to dispute from an armchair; if the probability really is zero rather than one-in-ten-thousand, the expected-harm reply I wanted to reach for seems to have nothing to grip. I should add that I am not neutral: I want very much to keep (1), I want to keep some purchase on the thought that there is something to be said against my taking the flight, and I find (6) plainly unacceptable, especially as it applies to the manager and the minister rather than to me. When I asked him where the harm comes from if no single act contributes any of it, he said the question was ill-posed, and I could not tell whether that was a good answer or a dodge.

Could you tell me where this argument goes wrong, if it does? Specifically: whether (2) sets up a genuine dilemma or a false one, and whether a single case can be lumpy in one respect and continuous in another so that (3) and (4) are not the exhaustive alternatives they claim to be; what (4) can coherently mean, and what it costs to read it as identifying imperceptibility with nonexistence; whether (3) and (4) can both be true of the same phenomenon, given that the aggregate harm in question obviously occurs; whether (7) argues against the non-consequentialist replies or simply assumes (1) against them; and whether (6) is best read as a reductio of the whole argument or as a bullet my friend is entitled to bite. If you conclude that the argument is valid and that some premise has to go, tell me which one I should give up and price each candidate — including (1), which I would rather keep, and (3), which I would rather not have to contest on empirical grounds. Please write in American English.
