My younger brother died in March, and in the months since I have been unable to let go of a single question, which I have gradually realized is not a private fixation but the center of a real metaphysical dispute: whether the past still exists, or whether what has happened is simply gone and only our talk of it remains. I teach physics at a secondary school, so I have some idea where the physics bears on the question; what I lack is any feel for the philosophical argument, and the little I have read stops at the slogans. I have been invited to contribute a piece of philosophical fiction to a small review that runs one story per issue, and I would like to submit something that treats the dispute seriously without degenerating into a dialogue in disguise. Please write that story for me.

Length: roughly 1,800 to 2,500 words. Setting, characters, and voice are yours to choose. What I would ask is that the story be built around three particular pressure points, which I will state as plainly as I can, and that each of them be carried by something concrete a reader can picture — an object, a document, a machine, a procedure someone has to go through — rather than by conversation alone.

First: if only present things exist, there is nothing in the world for a claim about the past to correspond to. A well-known reply is that such claims are made true by exhaustive descriptions of how things were — maximal, mutually incompatible sets of propositions, exactly one of which holds, and which one holds shifts as the world shifts. The difficulty, as I understand it, is that descriptions of this kind are abstract and unchanging, and it is obscure what could single one of them out as the one that obtains, or how a body of descriptions could be answerable to a man who is nowhere. I would like the story to contain something playing that role — a register, perhaps, kept with excessive care — and for a character to discover that it can be altered in a way that leaves the present detectably identical to what it would otherwise have been.

Second: two observers in relative motion disagree about which distant events are happening now, and neither of them is wrong; the disagreement grows with the separation between the events and with the relative speed. If the present is an objective feature of the world rather than a manner of speaking, something ought to select one of those incompatible accounts over the others. Please let this bear on a character practically or personally — a decision that must be made about what is happening somewhere far away at this very moment — and not merely theoretically.

Third: there is a middle view on which everything, past and future alike, exists, and yet there is in addition a privileged present that sweeps across the whole of it, so that only one region of the whole is now. Its critics say the sweeping does no work: it explains nothing that the static picture did not already contain, and if it genuinely moves, it seems to require a second time in which its rate could be measured. There is a further wrinkle I find appealing — the privileged present need not be a knife-edge instant but might have some thickness, in which case one must ask whether its edges are sharp, and if they are not, whether that unsharpness lies in the world or only in us. I would like a character to need this view to be true, and to be given a reason for doubting it that is not an intellectual one.

A few constraints. No character should utter the words presentism, eternalism, or block universe, and no one should explain the metaphysics to anyone else. The story should have a plot containing at least one irreversible event, and it should not resolve the dispute: I have no wish to be flattered into thinking the question has the answer I would prefer. What I want is to come away feeling, distinctly and separately, what each of the three points costs.
