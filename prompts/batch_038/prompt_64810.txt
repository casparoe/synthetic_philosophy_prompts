I am in my first year of a philosophy PhD and this term I am auditing the graduate seminar on formal epistemology; my qualifying paper is supposed to concern bounded knowledge — the worry that the standard possible-worlds treatment of belief renders every believer logically omniscient. Alongside the seminar I co-run a reading group with two people from the computer science department who work on epistemic logic for multi-agent systems, and they keep pressing me on what the philosophical payoff of any particular fix is supposed to be, which is a question I have not been able to answer. I have come to suspect that the reason is that I have been surveying candidate repairs — awareness operators, impossible worlds, resource-bounded models, syntactic or quotational treatments of belief contents, and the more deflationary line that simply denies closure outright — without ever having written down what I am entitled to demand of any of them. So before I commit to defending one, I would like the specification: the list of requirements that an adequate theory of non-omniscient knowledge must meet, with the phenomenon, datum, or case standing behind each one.

Here is my provisional list, which I expect is both incomplete and badly organized, with several items that turn out to be the same requirement in disguise and at least one that I now think is a piece of technical housekeeping I have mistaken for data. (i) Belief or knowledge must not be closed under consequence in general, since otherwise the theory is refuted by the obvious fact that we fail to know the remote consequences of what we know. (ii) Yet the theory must not simply abolish closure, because it has to explain how deduction can extend knowledge without any new empirical input, including the ordinary case in which someone is shown a proof and thereby comes to know something they did not know before. (iii) The theory must grade consequences by difficulty in a principled rather than an ad hoc way: getting conjunction-elimination and other trivial moves right at one end and deep mathematical consequences right at the other, and getting the direction right on asymmetries such as the fact that an agent may fail to know the conjunction of many things each of which they know, while easily knowing that a conjunction entails each conjunct. My supervisor pointed out that any measure based on the length of the shortest derivation looks ill-defined, since the shortest proof of a given theorem depends on the formal system one chooses, and stronger systems can compress proofs by unbounded factors; I would like this handled explicitly rather than waved away. (iv) Contents must be individuated finely enough to keep logically equivalent beliefs apart — otherwise the account collapses back into the possible-worlds picture it was meant to replace — but not so finely that obviously valid everyday inferences cease to be valid. (v) No primitive, unexplained parameter doing the work: if the theory posits an awareness relation or a resource bound as a basic ingredient, I want to know whether that explains the limits on knowledge or merely encodes them. (vi) The account must survive iteration and social embedding: bounded agents must be able to model other bounded agents, and the theory should be able to distinguish what a group would know if its members pooled their information from what each of them knows, and from what everybody knows. (vii) It should say something about the cases in which rational agents appear to hold inconsistent belief sets, and about the failures of self-knowledge in which an agent does not know what they believe. (viii) It must be able to state what the agent is missing when the agent cannot represent the consequence it fails to see — that is, ignorance must not come out inexplicable or vacuous. (ix) The bounds must not be fixed by a single global constant, since the theory has to accommodate agents considerably better at logic than its own authors.

What I want back is this. State each requirement precisely enough that one could tell whether a given theory satisfies it, and give the motivating case in a sentence or two. Then sort the list into genuine desiderata — things a theory owes an explanation of because they are pre-theoretic data about our epistemic practice or about rationality — and camp preferences, requirements that only look like data because one research programme has made them load-bearing; my own hunch is that insistence on retaining a possible-worlds semantics, and insistence on a sound and complete axiomatisation, belong in the second bucket, while the demand to explain how deduction extends knowledge belongs in the first, but I want the sorting argued rather than assumed. Next, and this is the part I most want, tell me whether some subset of the requirements is jointly unsatisfiable: I have a suspicion of a trilemma involving contents individuated finely enough to separate logically equivalent beliefs, a notion of closure that is genuinely available to the agent rather than merely defined over the theory, and the prohibition on a primitive awareness or resource parameter — if that suspicion is wrong, tell me where the real tension sits instead, since if the list is unsatisfiable then the list is itself the result. Finally, identify which single requirement does the most to eliminate candidates, and why. A brief closing scorecard is welcome — one line per approach, a table is fine — but keep the weight on the criteria. Please do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; I know the literature roughly and I am not asking you to reconstruct it, so motivate every requirement with a case rather than with a reference.
