I am the clinical safety lead at a company that installs passive sensing hubs in the homes of older adults living alone — motion sensors, door contacts, thermostat and lighting state, appliance use. Our product now adapts the home on the basis of inferred preferences: it learns when a resident likes the hallway lit, what temperature she settles on, when she takes her tea, and then changes the defaults without asking. Our machine learning team frames this as inverse reinforcement learning — the log is a demonstration, the resident's preferences are the reward being recovered — and files each proposed change with me as a ticket. I decide roughly forty of these a week, and I get about twenty minutes per ticket, as do the two colleagues who cover for me. None of us is a modeller; I am a nurse. The errors are not symmetric: a wrongly dimmed hallway at night is a fall, and a wrongly overridden preference is the reason families withdraw consent and the reason a resident stops trusting her own house.

What I need is a written decision procedure, not a discussion of the issues. It must be runnable by a non-specialist in twenty minutes, and it must survive being read aloud, gate by gate, to an adult daughter who has complained that we changed her mother's habits.

Please organize the answer as follows.

**1. The gates.** Give me an ordered sequence of gates a ticket must pass before we act. For each gate, state: the question the reviewer asks, in plain words she could actually say aloud; what counts as a sufficient answer; what kind of evidence could never satisfy the gate no matter how much of it we accumulate; the outcome if it fails (reject, defer to the panel, act only after asking the resident, act); and whether the gate is settled by fact or is a question of values that the procedure has no business pretending to answer. Where the ordering matters — where a later gate is wasted effort if an earlier one has already failed — say so.

**2. The things that burn us.** The procedure must handle these explicitly, and each of them should be traceable to a gate:

- Our ML lead tells me that many different reward functions are consistent with the same observed behaviour, so a good model fit is not by itself evidence about the preference. If that is right, what is a reviewer supposed to do with it, given that she cannot inspect a posterior or run the model? I want a plain-language test she can apply to a ticket, not a lecture on identifiability.
- Behaviour under constraints. A resident who never raises the set point above 17°C may dislike heat, may be frugal, may lack the dexterity to work the panel, or may be reading a room that is genuinely colder than the one the sensor sits in. Tell me how to separate preference from budget, ability, availability, and misperception using only what a passive log contains, and what to do when the log cannot separate them.
- Contaminated history. Every log we have is about six weeks long, and the hub changed its own defaults partway through most of them. Later behaviour is therefore partly our output reflected back at us. Specify what a reviewer may infer from post-intervention data and what she may not, and whether a clean pre-intervention segment of two weeks is enough to carry a claim.
- Conflicting channels. Our current model treats a resident's verbal report as just another observation channel with its own likelihood, weighted by how accurate her reports have been in the past. I want your assessment of whether that is legitimate, or whether it silently converts a question about whose preferences we ought to serve into an estimation problem. If it is illegitimate, say what the reviewer should do instead when the log and the resident disagree, and whether the answer changes when the disagreement is about a comfort setting versus a safety-relevant one.
- Change versus drift. Some residents' preferences genuinely shift; in other tickets our estimate is simply moving as noise accumulates. The reviewer cannot tell these apart from inside a twenty-minute window. Say what the procedure should do given that it cannot resolve the difference.
- Residents who cannot endorse. For a resident with moderate dementia, the behaviour may express a preference she would not affirm at her clearest, and she may be unable to answer a question at all. Mark precisely where the procedure stops being about evidence and becomes a decision the panel owns, and what the reviewer writes in the ledger at that point.

**3. The constraints to design around.** One-minute-resolution passive sensing; no video or audio, ever; six weeks of history per resident when a ticket opens; at most one clarifying question put to any resident in a week, because asking more is intrusive and several residents are aphasic; no ability to run controlled experiments in the home; a monthly panel of three — me, an ethicist, an engineer — that can grant exceptions; and every claim we act on must be recorded in a ledger in a single sentence a family member could read without feeling deceived. If your procedure needs something this list does not provide, say so rather than assuming it.

**4. Three tickets.** Run the procedure on each, gate by gate, showing where it passes and where it fails, and ending in the ledger sentence we would write or the refusal we would record.

- (a) An 84-year-old resident. Every evening between 20:00 and 22:00 she lowers the living-room set point to 16°C, and it stays there until about 06:00. The intake nurse recorded that she likes the house warm, around 21. Nothing else in the log is unusual; she moves freely and there have been no night-time incidents. The model has inferred a strong preference for cool nights and wants 16°C made the overnight default.
- (b) A resident who gets out of bed between 02:00 and 03:30 on roughly four nights out of six weeks; the kitchen light and kettle activate each time; no falls. The model reads this as a preference for a night drink and wants to pre-warm the hallway light and hold the kettle ready at 02:00. Her daughter writes that her mother has been up in the night since her husband died, and asks us not to make it easier.
- (c) A feature the product team wants shipped this quarter: automatic dimming, in which the hub lowers lighting to whatever level residents settle on after a few weeks. On our usual measures this is the strongest ticket we have — pooled across nine hundred homes, tight posteriors, engagement up after rollout, and residents almost never revert the setting by hand. I expect this one to pass.

**5. The limits.** Close by stating what the procedure cannot do: the classes of ticket it will systematically get wrong and in which direction; which of the distinctions above I have to insist survive into a non-specialist's hands because the reasoning collapses without them, and which she can safely be allowed to drop; and where the twenty-minute budget forces shortcuts you expect to produce errors, so that I can decide whether to spend money on the review instead. Wherever you have made a judgment call and dressed it as procedure, label it as such.
