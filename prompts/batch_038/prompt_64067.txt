I am preparing a seminar handout on the limits of explanatory reasoning, and I need a case engineered to a fairly exacting specification. What the handout is meant to show — and this fixes what will count as cheating — is that two familiar moves fail at once: inference to the best explanation cannot be defended by pointing out that the investigators were merely unlucky in their lot, and the standard Bayesian answer to that complaint, namely reserving prior probability for a catch-all, does not by itself restore truth-tracking. A case that delivers these verdicts by gerrymandering the numbers, or by announcing the verdicts instead of letting them fall out of the stated criteria and the stated probabilities, fails.

**Setting.** A routine diagnostic investigation in an ordinary professional context — an engineering failure analysis, a contamination or outbreak investigation, a crop or livestock die-off, a quality-control mystery. Not a courtroom, not a homicide, not a scientific revolution; no exotic instrumentation and no exotic physics. Two or three named investigators. The evidence should consist of three to five concrete, checkable observations, listed explicitly. The scenario itself should run to no more than two paragraphs; the analysis that follows may be as long as it needs to be.

**The repertoire.** The investigators entertain exactly three candidate explanations, H1–H3. All three are consistent with every observation, and all three are ones a competent practitioner in the field would think of early. State them the way one would state them to a colleague, not as formal schemas.

**Specification 1 — the loveliest hypothesis is false.** Fix your explanatory criteria before applying them; at a minimum they should include consistency with the data, unifying scope, parsimony of mechanism, and freedom from ad hoc auxiliary assumptions. H1 must win decisively on those criteria, and the ranking must be shown criterion by criterion. H1 is nevertheless false.

**Specification 2 — the truth lies outside the repertoire.** The actual explanation, H\*, is one no investigator in the case has formulated: not considered and rejected, not tacitly assumed, not recoverable from anything they say. It must be invisible because of how the problem has been framed — because the investigators are thinking in terms of the equipment rather than the maintenance regime, say, or of the pathogen rather than the chemistry — not because it is far-fetched. Once stated, H\* should look obvious and slightly embarrassing. H\* must also be more mundane than H1, never more exotic, and it must involve no hidden agent, no conspiracy, and no coincidence the reader would call contrived.

**Specification 3 — the catch-all loses.** Add a fourth hypothesis, H0: the cause lies outside H1–H3. Give H0 a generous prior, in the region of 0.25, so that the retort "you simply assigned the unknown no probability" is unavailable. Then assign rough likelihoods for each observation under each of H0, H1, H2, and H3, show the arithmetic, and demonstrate that H1's posterior exceeds H0's. The reason must be structural rather than numerical: H0 commits to nothing specific, so it must spread its probability across the open space of unknown alternatives, and that diffuseness is precisely what sinks it. Report the posterior for H0 and say what would have to be true of H0 for it to win.

**Specification 4 — enlarging the lot splits the two rules.** Now suppose H\* is articulated, assigned a defensible prior, and the calculation re-run. Bayesian conditionalization must shift substantially toward H\*. Inference to the best explanation, applying the very criteria fixed in Specification 1 and no others, must still rank H1 first, because H1 remains the lovelier story: one mechanism where H\* needs two, or a unified account where H\* needs an untidy conjunction of independently common factors. Confront the obvious technical difficulty rather than sliding past it — H\* is a conjunction, and conjunctions pay a prior penalty — and explain why its prior is nonetheless respectable given the base rates in your setting.

**Deliverables, in this order.** (a) The scenario. (b) The criteria, applied, with the loveliness ranking. (c) The probability table for the four-hypothesis calculation, arithmetic shown. (d) The recalculation with H\* included, and the two divergent verdicts. (e) A short statement of the single feature of the case that does the work, together with two removal tests: what happens to the result if the catch-all is permitted to be articulated into specific alternatives from the outset, and what happens if one of the observations is deleted so that H1 no longer unifies as much. (f) An honest verdict on your own construction: does it indict inference to the best explanation as a rule, or only the investigators' repertoire, and what would have to be true for it to indict the rule. (g) The strongest reply available to each of the following, and which of the three you judge most damaging to your case: a defender of inference to the best explanation who insists that loveliness, properly specified, would have preferred H\*; a Bayesian who objects that H0 is not a hypothesis at all and therefore cannot bear a likelihood; and a proponent of the bad-lot objection, in van Fraassen's line, who claims your case proves more than you intended.

Flag any step that rests on a contestable assumption rather than quietly choosing numbers that suit you. Do not draw on a real historical episode, and do not supply references or a reading list.
