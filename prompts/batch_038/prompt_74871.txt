I am a novelist at work on my third book, and I have run into a problem that is not really about prose. The central character, Elias Vane, is a retired proof theorist who spent forty years on consistency proofs and came out of it convinced that his subject had delivered a verdict about minds that the rest of the world had failed to notice. There is a set piece in which he lectures a robotics institute, and a later scene in which he sets out the same thought privately, much more carefully, to a graduate student who does not follow him. I sent the manuscript to two friends: Jonas, who works on ordinal analysis and spends his days with exactly the theorems Vane invokes, and Naomi, who works in philosophy of cognitive science and has written on machine models of reasoning. I asked only whether Vane sounds like a man who understands his own field. They both said no, and then disagreed at length about why, and about how much of what he says can be rescued. I have reconstructed their exchange below as faithfully as I can from my notes; neither of them knew the other's moves in advance, and I have kept the order they came in.

For the record, the claim as Vane states it in the lecture is this: "For any consistent formal system rich enough to contain arithmetic, there is a sentence the system can neither prove nor refute, and yet I can see that it is true. No system, then, contains everything I can see; and were my own reasoning the output of a single such system, I would be incapable of recognising precisely that sentence. I am capable of it. I am therefore not such a system." In the private scene he withdraws the last two sentences and offers instead what he calls the honest form: either the mind is not equivalent to any such system, or it is equivalent to one whose consistency I cannot know from where I stand — and either way the strong claim that I am a machine whose correctness I could in principle certify to myself is false.

The exchange ran as follows. (1) Jonas: the lecture version is wrong twice over. Gödel supplies a sentence undecidable in one specified system, not a sentence that no formal system can prove; that very sentence is provable in the system augmented by its own consistency statement, and there are consistent complete theories of arithmetic that decide everything. So "there are truths no formal system can prove" is not a loose formulation of a theorem, it is the negation of one. And even the local point gets nowhere: to convert unprovability into truth Vane must know which system he is and know it consistent, and if he does not know his own system he cannot even write down the sentence he claims to see. (2) Naomi: the first half of that refutes a slogan, not an argument, and Jonas knows it. The real argument is a scheme quantified over systems — for every consistent recursively axiomatised F, whoever knows F sound can see its Gödel sentence — and its natural output is the disjunction Vane states privately. Both of its horns are damaging to the version of mechanism worth attacking, the one that says we are equivalent to an algorithm whose correctness we could in principle certify. So the lecture is bad rhetoric and the private scene is defensible, which is exactly the contrast the novel is built on. (3) Jonas: the disjunction is too cheap to count as a discovery. Every consistent system fails to prove its own consistency, so "either I am not a formal system or I cannot see that I am" follows from nothing distinctive about consciousness; it is close to a tautology about any reasoner you like, including the ones we build. Worse, the human side is doing unearned work: seeing the Gödel sentence requires a reflection principle for F, and a machine equipped with the same reflection principle, or an oracle, or an iterated consistency extension, sees it just as well. The argument never excludes machines; it excludes machines held fixed at F. (4) Naomi: being held fixed is the whole of the target. Mechanism says the mind is identical to one particular algorithm, not to an infinite ascending chain of ever stronger ones. If, each time I nominate F, you respond by nominating F plus reflection, then the machine on the table has changed and nothing has been answered — the infinite regress is your objection restated, not a reply to it. And note the asymmetry: you hand the human being her reflection step without asking what entitles her to it, while demanding that the machine be furnished one before it may take the same step. (5) Jonas: the asymmetry is deserved, because humans are demonstrably not sound. We assert falsehoods, contradict ourselves, and revise; the diagonal step needs the reasoner's consistency, or better her soundness, to turn unprovability into truth. A program that emits contradictions is broken. A person who does is ordinary. So the premise that flatters us is the one that fails, and the only consistent reading idealises the human being out of the picture. (6) Naomi: idealisation is legitimate provided it is applied evenly. We already idealise the machine — unbounded patience, no hardware failure, no bit rot — and then refuse the human being the corresponding idealisation of consistency. If Gödelian arguments collapse because people contradict themselves, then computational models of cognition collapse because Turing machines never crash. Moreover, the transfer claim I actually care about needs no mind at all: no sufficiently strong formal theory can define its own truth predicate or establish its own consistency, and that is a real constraint on any fixed framework, including frameworks in law or in a science elaborate enough to encode its own syntax. (7) Jonas: which concedes the point. Those theorems are about systems with a designated proof predicate, a specified language, and derivability conditions; they say nothing about legal codes, theologies, or novels, none of which has a recursively enumerable consequence relation, an arithmetisation of its own syntax, or an agreed notion of consistency. What travels is a structural pattern — self-reference plus a fixed standard of proof — and far fewer domains have both than people assume. If Vane slides from "no fixed formal system contains its own truth predicate" to "there is more to me than any formal system," he is not reporting a result, he is committing a category error that a genuine proof theorist would spot in himself. (8) Naomi: I grant the slide is invalid; I deny that the novel needs it. Vane holds the disjunctive claim in private and performs the slogan in public, and the distance between the two is the most accurate thing in the book about how working logicians talk about their own theorems away from the blackboard.

Would you act as referee here? For each of the eight moves, give me a rating on a scale running from clearly mistaken through a draw to clearly right, and say specifically which sub-claim earns the rating, since several moves contain two or three distinct assertions and I suspect they do not all fare alike — in particular, please tell me wherever one of them has misstated a theorem, and give the correct statement rather than the correction. Flag any move that reaches a defensible conclusion by a route that will not bear weight. Then tell me what neither of them addressed: I have a suspicion that at least three things went untouched, among them which of the two incompleteness theorems is being leaned on at each stage, whether consistency and soundness are being traded silently, and which notion of mechanism — output, process, or knowability — the argument is actually aimed at. Finally, and on the strength of the adjudication rather than as a separate question, tell me which formulation of the thesis I can put in Vane's mouth without making a man who knows his subject look like a fraud: the lecture version, the disjunctive version, or something purely analogical — and whether, if I keep the lecture version, Jonas's seventh move is enough to make it a fatal error in the character or merely an error he could plausibly commit. I should add that I want no citations, no reading list, and no bibliography; I am after your judgment on the arguments as they were actually made.
