I'm a program officer at a small family foundation — about $8m a year, mostly public health and goverance work in three countries — and I make something like 40 grant decisions a year with one junior analyst and no evaluation capacity beyond reading proposals and calling the occasional academic.

Six months ago one of our two board members went down a rabbit hole on "consequentialist cluelessness": the argument that the indirect, knock-on effects of basically anything we fund are unknowable, and could easily be larger than the direct effects, so we can never actually tell whether a grant did good. He now stalls or blocks proposals on the grounds that we can't rule out the long-run stuff. The other board member's counter-proposal is a flat rule: if we can't estimate an indirect effect, assume the good and bad knock-ons cancel out and decide purely on the direct, measurable effects. I've been asked to write something the board can actually adopt, and honestly I dont know whether either position is coherent, or whether they're both half-right.

So: can you write me a decision procedure I can run on a proposal in about 45 minutes, that my analyst could also run without any philosophy background — the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where the procedure stops with a no, and where it explicitly hands the call up to the board because it's a values question and not a fact question? It has to say what to do with indirect effects we're confident exist but can't size at all, and it has to take a position on the "assume they cancel" rule — either build it in with the conditions under which it's legitimate, or replace it with something better and tell me why mine is wrong. Flag the steps that are really judgment calls in procedural costume.

Then run it on three live ones: (1) a radio campaign supporting a deworming program — direct effects estimable from the literature, plus a plausible but unquantifiable effect on clinic attendance and on local trust in health workers; (2) a grant to train and place 30 junior technocrats inside a finance ministry, where the direct benefit is small and the entire argument rests on who those people become and what they later decide; (3) a renewal for a bednet distribution partner that's clearly strong per dollar but whose country program may be crowding out a domestic manufacturer. I expect one of these to come out in a direction I won't like — I'd rather find out now than in front of the board.

And finish with what the procedure can't do: the places where it's going to hand me confident-looking outputs that are just my priors with a checklist wrapped around them.
