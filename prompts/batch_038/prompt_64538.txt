I've been sitting on this for a week now and I still can't get out of it. I'm auditing a philosophy-of-mind reading group (mostly grad students, plus one who's training to be a clinical psych, which is how self-deception ended up on the agenda) and somebody brought an argument that strict self-deception is impossible. The trouble is that it seems to work right up until the last few steps, and then it drags along a conclusion nobody in the room wants. Here's my reconstruction:

1. Deception succeeds only if the deceiver knows the truth and the victim comes to believe the falsehood.
2. You can't just choose your beliefs; the only way to get yourself to believe something is to manipulate your own inputs — what evidence you gather, what you dwell on, what you keep out of sight.
3. So if self-deception happens, it happens by one person steering their own attention away from something they know.
4. Steering attention away from a consideration requires you to keep flagging that consideration as the one to avoid: you have to recognize it every time it surfaces and recognize it as a threat to the belief you're trying to install.
5. But a consideration is only a threat if it's true, so flagging it as a threat is taking it to be true, and in the normal case taking something to be true just is believing it.
6. So the self-deceiver believes p the whole time; the belief that not-p never gets installed, she just talks and acts as if it had.
7. Hence strict self-deception is impossible, and every case we call it is really motivated pretending or avowal without belief.
8. Notice, though, that (4) and (5) never mention the self or self-deception — they're general claims about attention and evidence-management. So the same reasoning shows that nobody can ever change what they believe by managing their own attention: the positive-thinking exercises, the deliberately-not-dwelling-on-a-suspicion, the trying to talk yourself into optimism before a job interview. All of it would be impossible, not merely ineffective.
9. That's obviously false, since people reshape their beliefs by choosing what to think about all the time.
10. Therefore (2) is wrong, and belief is under voluntary control after all.

The group just accepted (10) and moved on, which struck me as a bizarre place to land: we started out asking whether people can fool themselves and ended up holding that you can believe at will, which if anything makes self-deception easier rather than impossible. I suspect something goes wrong between (7) and (8), or maybe further back at (5), but I can't name it, and the person who brought the argument says any diagnosis I offer either begs the question against (5) or leaves self-deception mysterious all over again. I'd like to keep (2) if I possibly can — I really don't want to give up the idea that belief isn't the kind of thing you can settle by deciding. I'm also unsure whether this is supposed to be a reductio of self-deception or a reductio of doxastic involuntarism, because it reads like its trying to be both at once.

So: which step do you reject, and what does rejecting it cost? Please also say what the main positions in the neighborhood — the partitioning response that splits the mind into semi-autonomous subsystems, the Mele-style deflationary accounts that drop any requirement of an intention to deceive oneself, and the bad-faith tradition — each have to pay in order to reject the step you pick, since they don't all reject the same one. Write in American English. And at the end, tell me what would change your mind: which argument or consideration would push you to a different step, and where you think your own diagnosis is most likely to fail.
