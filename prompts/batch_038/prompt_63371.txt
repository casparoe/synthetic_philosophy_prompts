I work as a policy analyst for a regional planning authority in a part of Europe whose population has been shrinking for most of this century's first quarter: three of our eleven primary schools are slated for consolidation, the maternity ward at the district hospital has been downgraded to an outpatient clinic, and the regional assembly has just commissioned a framing paper on whether we should treat population size as a policy target in its own right. I have been asked to draft that paper, and while preparing it a colleague directed me to the longest of Hume's political essays, "Of the Populousness of Ancient Nations," which first appeared in the Political Discourses of 1752 and was later folded into the collected Essays. Two things in it struck me as directly relevant to my assignment. First, Hume treats a comparison of population levels between ages and states as carrying weighty consequences, in effect settling questions about the whole quality of a people's government, manners, and constitution — populousness functions for him as a diagnostic sign of good rule. Second, his causal mechanism is an economic-constraint theory of fertility: as he puts it, "almost every man who thinks he can maintain a family will have one," and whatever restraints exist must therefore arise from difficulties in people's situation that a wise legislature ought to observe and remove; he points to colonies and new settlements, where provision for a family is easy, as places where mankind multiplies fastest, and he treats the much-vaunted populousness of antiquity with considerable evidential suspicion, since we have no reliable counts and are asked to infer from scattered mentions of armies, censuses, and grain supplies.

The difficulty for my paper is that the modern data pattern seems to run his indicator backwards. In the societies that score best on exactly the dimensions Hume associated with good government — low violence, long life expectancy, historically low child mortality, secure property, functioning courts, high self-reported life satisfaction — the fertility rate is lowest, often well below the level needed for replacement, and has been for a decade or more. The proximate mechanism is not starvation or infant death but postponement and abandonment of family formation by people who, when surveyed, still say they would prefer two children; the costs they cite are housing, the career penalties of parenthood, and the general expense of raising a child to the standard now considered adequate. Public cash transfers and subsidized childcare have moved these rates by only modest amounts. I would like you to reconstruct, as carefully as you can, what Hume would make of this. In particular, please work through three candidate verdicts and tell me which he would most plausibly adopt, or whether some fourth is likelier: (a) the indicator survives and the surface reading of "good government" is what fails — low fertility reveals a hidden deficiency in these societies, perhaps in how the means of family formation are distributed, or in the way refinement raises the perceived cost of children; (b) the indicator was true only under pre-modern conditions, because the link between well-being and fertility was mediated by the absence of reliable contraception and by the economic value of children, and should therefore be retired rather than applied to us; (c) the indicator survives but points the other way, so that voluntary restraint is itself evidence of the security and liberty Hume prized, and a slowly declining population may be a symptom of good government rather than of decay.

Two further questions should be addressed alongside that choice. First, would Hume's own methodological skepticism about registers and counts extend to our statistics — leading him to distrust survey measures of desired family size, self-reported happiness, and even the fertility figures themselves, in a way that cuts against the technocratic use of his indicator by people like me? Second, does the "wise legislature" clause commit him to endorsing the modern pronatalist toolkit of subsidies, housing policy, and parental leave, or would he regard that toolkit as futile project-mongering, given how much of his explanation rests on general manners and the ordinary conditions of life rather than on particular enactments? I would also like a clear answer to the underlying question my paper has to face: for Hume, is a large population good in itself, or only as evidence of, and instrument to, the happiness of the people — and if the latter, what would he say to an assembly debating whether to spend money to make its region bigger? Please conclude with the single most uncomfortable question you think Hume would put to our policymakers. Since I cannot check sources during the drafting period, do not give me a bibliography, citations, or page references, and do not put any words in his mouth beyond what you are confident he actually argued; wherever your reconstruction goes past the texts, say so explicitly and label it as a plausible extension rather than a documented view.
