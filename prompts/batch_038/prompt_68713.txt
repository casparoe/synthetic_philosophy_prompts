In the small hours of August 1, 1966, Charles Whitman stabbed his mother and his wife to death, having first typed a note asking that his brain be examined once he was dead; he then shot fourteen people in and around the University of Texas Main Building over the next ninety-odd minutes before police killed him. The autopsy found a glioblastoma pressing on tissue near his amygdala, and experts have argued ever since about whether it had anything to do with what he did. In the months beforehand he had complained to a campus counsellor of violent impulses he could neither explain nor control.

Take that episode through the Dennett–Caruso dispute over just deserts, organized around a distinction I will stipulate: a theory of responsibility is tumor-sensitive if a lesion like Whitman's changes what the theory says about the agent, and tumor-invariant if a lesion can only change what the theory says about our response. My suspicion is that Caruso's skepticism is tumor-invariant and Dennett's compatibilism tumor-sensitive, and that here the sensitivity is a liability rather than a virtue. Test it.
