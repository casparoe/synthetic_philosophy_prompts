I am in the final third of my third novel, and one of its two narratives belongs to a philosopher in her late sixties, Rosalind Fenn, who for twenty years has run the same graduate seminar around a single set of records from a fertility clinic in the north of England: a boy conceived there in 1994 from a donated vial, born the following spring, and a standing list of stipulated variations on his conception. My editor thinks the seminar chapters read like a casebook and wants them thinned; I think they are the spine of the book, but my own philosophy stopped at one survey course thirty years ago, and I cannot afford to have Fenn rule inconsistently, because the retired professor in my reading group will notice long before any reviewer does. So I want the last chapter checked against the ones already written.

Here is what Fenn has said so far, chapter by chapter. (1) The same egg and the same sperm, but the embryo carried to term by another woman: the child is Elias; the womb, she says, is a place and not a parent. (2) The same egg and sperm, but the embryo frozen for four years, so that Elias is born in 1999 rather than 1995: still Elias. (3) At the eight-cell stage one blastomere is drawn off for testing before transfer, and in a second version two are: still Elias on both counts; she remarks that an embryo is a process, and that processes outlive the loss of their parts. (4) The same egg, fertilised by a different sperm from the same donor vial: not Elias — his brother, another person entirely. (5) The same sperm, and a second egg retrieved from his mother in the same cycle: not Elias — his sister. (6) The same egg and sperm, but the egg exposed in the laboratory to a mutagen, so that the resulting child carries one novel point mutation: still Elias; she offers no general principle here, only that this makes no difference. (7) The union is achieved by injecting the sperm into the egg rather than by conventional insemination, everything else being as it actually was: still Elias. (8) The embryo splits at the two-cell stage and two boys are born: neither of them is Elias, and she takes care to add that this is not a case of our merely being ignorant which of the two he is. (9) She is asked whether Elias could have come from a sperm and an egg differing from the actual ones by degrees — a molecule at a time, until nothing recognisable remains — and she refuses the question, saying she would rather admit that there is no line she can draw than invent one. Throughout, she insists that her question is numerical: not what Elias would have been like, but which of these children would have been him.

The closing chapter turns on a second set of records that surfaces late, from the same cycle. Everything in it is as it actually was — the same egg, the same mother, the same womb, the same morning, the same vial — except that the sperm which fertilised the egg was a different spermatozoon from that vial, one which, and here Fenn imposes a stipulation a geneticist friend of mine assures me is wildly improbable, carried a haploid complement identical base for base to the complement of the sperm actually used. One child is born. There is no twin, no rival, no second claimant anywhere in the story. Is this child Elias? What I need from you is a prediction of Fenn's verdict, with the degree of confidence you would be willing to defend in front of a professional; a laying out of the candidate rules her nine verdicts are jointly consistent with — I expect at least three, and I want each stated in its own terms rather than under somebody's name, though if a rule has a familiar label you may as well attach it — together with what each of those rules says about this child; an identification of which earlier verdict is the nearest neighbour to the new case, and a candid view of whether it is near enough to settle the matter or only appears to be; a statement of what Fenn thereby commits herself to and what she has to abandon, whichever way she rules; and, if the records genuinely leave her verdict underdetermined, the one further variation — describable in a paragraph, and one I could plausibly write into a chapter — that would most cleanly separate the rules that survive your analysis. Please reason from the verdicts I have given you rather than sending me after the literature: I am not looking for references, and my character's coherence matters to me far more than her pedigree.
