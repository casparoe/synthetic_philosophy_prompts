So I'm two things at once and it's making my life weird: I'm a nurse-informaticist at a mid-size hospital, and I'm finishing a part-time MA in philosophy. For the past year I've been the note-taker on our Serious Event Review Committee, and one member — I'll call her Dr. A, she's a bioethicist — votes against the committee's "contributing factors" list almost every single time, and her dissents always turn on whether the thing someone *didn't* do actually caused the harm, versus just being a condition the harm happened in. I've been keeping a private log of her verdicts, partly because I'm building a chapter on causation by ommission around them and partly because next Thursday she rules on a case I'm standing right in the middle of, and I'd like to know what's coming before I open my mouth.

Her record so far, stripped down to the shape of it:

- ICU nurse, on duty, skips a scheduled neuro check; patient herniates; a check would have caught it with two hours to spare. **Caused.** Her gloss was about it being her job.
- Off-duty nurse in the cafeteria, ninety seconds away, code called over the PA and nobody goes; patient dies. **Didn't cause.** Gloss: she wasn't on the hook.
- Night watchman paid to press a dead-man's pedal every ten minutes, dozes off, the alarm never reaches the police, warehouse gets emptied. **Caused.**
- Attending doesn't order a CT that would have shown the bleed — but the scanner had been down all week and the only transfer ambulance was out of service, so on the committee's own reconstruction, ordering it changes nothing. **Didn't cause.** Gloss: no difference, no cause.
- Hiker, no relationship to anyone, no role, is the only person within an hour of a stranger arresting on a trail and has a working sat phone; doesn't use it. **Caused** — and she added that the law is right not to make her liable, but that's a separate question.
- Two lifeguards on the same stand, both on their phones, drowning in plain sight, either one alone would have reached him. **Caused**, both of them, and she split the causal share 50/50 instead of letting them cancel out.
- Surgeon at the end of her shift declines a bedside consult and signs off to a colleague six minutes away who would have saved the patient; patient dies inside those six minutes. **Didn't cause.** Something about the duty having already moved.
- Asked to compare two technician cases: one leaves a valve open overnight and coolant takes the floor, the other never opens the valve that would have fed a reaction and the batch is lost. **First caused, second is "a background condition, not a cause."**

Here's Thursday's. Last year I pitched and wrote the spec for a "quiet handover" pilot: twenty minutes at shift change where unit phones go into a charging cradle at the station so incoming and outgoing nurses can actually talk face to face. Burnout thing, very popular, ran eleven months without a hiccup. On the night in question a heparin pump occludes during the window and its alarm routes to a phone sitting in the cradle. The bay's nurse is awake, trained, fifteen feet away, and her role description names her as the one who answers infusion alarms — except the handover was live and the outgoing nurse hadn't signed the bay off, so between the two of them the rota is genuinely ambiguous for those twenty minutes. The occlusion goes unnoticed, the graft clots, patient dies. Two complications that I think matter: the vascular note says the graft was already failing and would have thrombosed on its own within the hour, so the most honest reconstruction is that the alarm buys her forty minutes rather than a life; and the cradle isn't anybody's omission on the unit at all, it's the direct product of a policy someone affirmatively wrote, which was me.

What will she say — caused, didn't cause, or caused-something-narrower — and give me a rough probability on each, not just a hunch. Then do the harder part: lay out at least three candidate rules that could generate her entire log (duty-tracking, difference-making with some restriction on which contrasts count, the no-absences view where only her responsibility talk sounds causal, the ongoing-threat-versus-missing-ingredient split, the handover rule, whatever else fits), say for each one which lines of the log strain it and which it eats for breakfast, and then say what each predicts for Thursday. I especially want to know whether the forty-minutes detail flips her: does hastening count as causing the death, or only as causing a death-earlier-than-it-would-have-been, and does that distinction survive contact with her graft-case verdict? And design me one further case — the single one I could hand her next month — that would best separate the live candidate rules, with what each verdict would tell me. Last thing, and please be blunt: my own preferred reading is that hastening is causing and that the cradle was an act upstream which left her omission as the only candidate, and I'm the guy who wrote the cradle policy, so tell me if that's motivated reasoning dressed up as analysis.
