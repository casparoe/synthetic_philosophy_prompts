I work as a docent at a natural history museum, and last month we hosted an evening talk on the Anthropocene. Afterwards two visitors who'd both clearly done some reading got into it by the coffee urn and I couldnt referee it — I mostly just stood there nodding. Marta is an environmental historian; Dev works in a sediment core lab and dated things for a living. Here's roughly how it went, in order:

(1) Marta opened by saying the start date has to be 1610 — the so-called Orbis spike, that small dip in CO2 visible in the Antarctic ice cores that comes from the collapse of Indigenous populations in the Americas after 1492 and the forest regrowing on abandoned farmland. Her claim was that this is the first moment human activity leaves a signal you can see in the atmosphere globally, and that it dates the epoch to the Columbian Exchange rather than to steam engines. (2) Dev said no, a boundary needs a sharp, globally synchronous marker you can actually find in a core, and the 1610 dip is tiny and smeared across decades by the ice record — you couldn't point to it in a lake sediment or a coral band. The real marker, he said, is fallout: plutonium and the bomb-produced carbon-14 from thermonuclear testing, which appears in 1950s layers all over the planet to within a year or two, which is why the working group landed on Crawford Lake in Ontario and a start around 1950. (3) Marta's reply was that his criterion isn't neutral, it's political: if the Anthropocene begins wherever the marker is sharpest, then it begins with the Bomb and the postwar boom, and four centuries of colonial land seizure, plantation slavery and extractivism get absorbed into the background as mere prehistory. She added that the sharpness of the plutonium signal is partly an artifact of the fact that we've been monitoring everything closely since 1945 — we find mid-century markers easily because that's where our instruments and our archives are. (4) Dev said a boundary can't be chosen for its moral resonance, stratigraphy isn't a memorial, and that mid-century isn't only where the instruments are: population, GDP, fertilizer use, dam building, concrete and plastic output and river sediment flux all bend upward together there, so the marker matches the story instead of preceding it. (5) Marta then half-conceded, sort of — she said maybe choosing a single year at all is a category mistake, that the Anthropocene has several onsets (agriculture, 1492, coal, 1945) and that forcing a plural, historical concept through a golden spike is what's actually broken, which she thinks was vindicated in 2024 when the stratigraphers voted the epoch proposal down. (6) Dev agreed it got voted down but said she was drawing the wrong lesson from it: the objections were mostly technical, the record is too short and the boundary too recent to make a proper epoch, not an admission that dates are plural. He also said other boundaries cut through transitions that took thousands of years and still get a single line, that the line goes where the marker is rather than where the story begins, and that if the concept really does have multiple onsets then it's a historical and Earth-system term that geologists should just let go of — which is more or less what happened.

Can you go through the six moves one at a time and rate each from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification per move rather than a general verdict? Then tell me who came out ahead overall and on what specific point. And I want you to take a real position on the thing that bugged me most, which is move (3): is the requirement of a sharp, globally synchronous marker a neutral technical standard that any date has to satisfy, or does it already embed a substantive choice about which human activities get to count as the beginning of the epoch — and if it's the latter, does that make Dev's argument wrong or just incomplete? Finally, flag anything both of them were assuming that neither one questioned.
