My friend and I spent most of a Saturday arguing about whether criminal bans on incitement to hatred, of the sort found in several European jurisdictions, are compatible with a decent liberal democracy, and I would like a diagnosis of the argument rather than a verdict. What struck me afterward is how little of it was empirical. We agreed on nearly all the facts: that hateful expression does real damage to its targets, that counterspeech reaches a far smaller audience than the speech it answers, that such offences are enforced unevenly and sometimes against minority speakers and dissenters, and that in the American system private platforms now suppress a good deal of speech the state itself could not. Neither of us budged.

My working hypothesis is that the impasse is not about harm, nor about how much weight dignity deserves, but about the structure of the right: whether free speech is a side-constraint that only concrete, imminent danger can dislodge, or a prima facie entitlement that must be weighed against the equal standing of citizens whenever it corrodes it — with a further disagreement about who carries the burden under uncertainty. Is that right, or is there a hidden factual question about institutional reliability doing the work, or two distinct questions travelling under one label? And on whatever diagnosis you reach, say what would count as progress: a distinction, an empirical result, a reformulation of the question, or nothing at all.
