I retired four years ago after a career in condensed matter physics, and I have taken up philosophy with the seriousness I once reserved for journal club. The question I cannot settle is whether objects endure — are wholly present at each moment of their history — or perdure — have temporal parts as they have spatial parts. Rather than argue it, I have written down my verdicts on concrete cases, arrived at independently, and I should like to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

1. A bar magnet cut in two: I say two magnets result, each with its own poles, and I do not think this shows anything about magnets being "really" collections of halves. I record it because I want to be clear that my resistance in case 5 is not a general refusal to take objects apart.

2. A brass rod from my old laboratory, heated until it glowed and left to cool: the same rod throughout, hot earlier and cold later, and I have no wish to say that the hot thing and the cold thing are two objects. When I say it was hot, I mean the rod was hot, not a piece of it.

3. A cast bronze figure melted down and recast as a bell: the figure is gone and the bronze remains, even though while the figure existed it and the bronze shared every one of their parts.

4. An electron: what my discipline gives me is a worldline, and an instant is a limit of shrinking intervals rather than a place at which anything could be wholly present. For fundamental matter I have no intuition of wholly present objects at all.

5. My own body, whose atoms are replaced continuously: I insist that I am wholly here, and that it is I, not a slice of me, who is writing this sentence.

6. Two universes, each containing nothing but a disc of perfectly uniform density, in one of which the disc spins and in the other of which it is at rest. I take these to be genuinely different, and the difference to be a fact about the disc itself. Yet if the world is a block and objects are aggregates of instantaneous slices, the two blocks seem to contain the same slices stacked in the same way, and I cannot see where the difference is supposed to live.

7. Were my two cerebral hemispheres each transplanted into a body, I should say two people resulted, neither of them me; and I cannot identify any present fact that would make it me rather than the other.

The nearest I have come to a rule of my own is this: an object is wholly present at a time if a complete inventory of what exists at that time settles which object it is and what it is like. That rule appears to collapse case 6, which is part of why I distrust it.

So: state precisely the principle, or the short list of candidate principles, that best captures these seven verdicts, telling me for each clause which verdict it exists to secure. Then say what each candidate implies for three cases I have not raised — a photon, whose worldline admits no proper time at all; a standing wave in a cavity, which is the same wave cycle after cycle although nothing travels along it; and a crystal dissolved and recrystallised from the same solution. Finally, tell me plainly which of my verdicts, if any, no candidate can keep, and why the cheaper principle is worth the loss.
