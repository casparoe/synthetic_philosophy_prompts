Last night a friend and I argued for two hours about whether money ought to rust. He is taken with a scheme traceable to a German merchant-economist of the early twentieth century, under which currency carries a standing holding cost — notes must be periodically stamped, or, in the modern version, a central bank digital currency is programmed to expire — so that money cannot be hoarded and is compelled to keep circulating. I claimed Berkeley had already arrived at the same place in The Querist, since he treats money as a token or counter whose entire worth lies in what it records and conveys of the power to command the labour of others, and never as a thing worth possessing in itself; a perishing currency would therefore have delighted him. My friend replied that Berkeley would have detested it, because a decaying token cannot serve as a store of value, and a note people are forced to spend is a tax on thrift rather than an instrument of industry.

Which of us has Berkeley right? Begin by stating his monetary theory as precisely as you can from The Querist and the surrounding Irish pamphlet debate about a national bank of issue — and please keep it clear of the idealism he is actually known for, which I suspect is what usually contaminates these readings. Then give me your judgement on how he would have answered the demurrage proposal: what in it he would have endorsed, what he would have resisted, and how far his answer would turn on matters that have nothing to do with the nature of money at all, such as Irish conditions in the 1730s, the absence of any domestic bank of issue, and his concern with whether industry or luxury is the point of a nation's wealth.

Finally, tell me whether the disagreement between my friend and me is genuinely a disagreement about Berkeley, or whether we are each smuggling in our own priors about inflation and thrift and reading him as the umpire.
