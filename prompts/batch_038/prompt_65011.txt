I cover science-and-ideas stuff for a general-audience magazine, and I'm working on a piece about fatalism — specifically why the old Death in Damascus story still gets dragged into technical arguments about rational choice forty-something years on. I've got the argumentative core drafted and I want a reality check before it goes to an editor, because I have a nagging feeling there's a hole in it and I cant find where. The draft is below, condensed hard from the real thing, so forgive the jumps.

The marketplace story everyone knows: Death looks startled to run into a man in one city, the man panics and rides through the night to another, and Death is waiting there — he was startled because the appointment was written for the second city all along. Decision theorists turned this into a puzzle with money in it: you're in Damascus, Death tells you the appointment is tomorrow in Damascus, and you can pay a fortune for a ticket to Aleppo. If Death never misses and knows what you'll do, then conditionalizing on either act gives you death with probability 1, so the ticket is just money thrown away. My claim in the piece is that this is two different puzzles wearing one costume, and that most of the interesting debate is about the wrong one. Version A, the decree: the appointment is fixed, full stop, and your deliberation has nothing whatsoever to do with it. There is no dilemma here, only bad news; the rational move is to keep your money and drink something nice. Version B, the prediction: the appointment is fixed *because* Death predicted your choice, and his predictions land 99 times out of 100 but not 100. Now the ticket to Aleppo genuinely raises your chance of surviving, because the only way you die in Aleppo is if he guessed you'd go to Aleppo. So the two versions come apart exactly where decision theory is supposed to bite — in A there's no act whose evidential bearing differs from its causal effect, in B there is — and when people say the story shows some decision theory recommending a dominated action, or sending an agent into an endless loop of reconsidering, I think they've quietly assumed B while narrating A. The paradoxical flavor comes from the switch, not from either version on its own.

Two things I'd like from you. Is the A/B split actually load-bearing, or does it collapse — for instance, if Death is a perfect predictor rather than a 99% one, isn't B just A with extra steps, since infallible prediction makes your act good evidence without making it a cause? And does my Version B arithmetic hold up, or am I wrong that fleeing improves your odds once the predictor is merely very good rather than flawless? Tell me too if there's an obvious third version I'm missing — something about randomizing by coin flip, or about whether you're even permitted to treat your own upcoming choice as evidence about the world. Format: open with a two-sentence verdict on whether the argument holds, holds with repairs, or doesn't hold at all. Then no more than six numbered comments, and keep the whole thing under 600 words. Don't give me a reading list or names of papers — I can't chase references on deadline, I just want to know where the reasoning breaks.
