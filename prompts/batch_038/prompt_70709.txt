so, background: I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy past a half-finished MOOC. I got into this stuff from the alignment-writing side of the internet, the CEV-ish stuff, and I've been writing a design doc for a system that's supposed to aim at "what people would want if they thought about it much harder" instead of at a list of rules. My sister-in-law is a philosophy PhD student and she read the values section over dinner and said flatly that it doesn't hang together. She wouldn't say which part — she said if she told me I'd just bolt it back on — so I'm asking you instead. Please don't give me a history lesson on what other people have said about this. I want an audit of *my* list.

Here's the list, roughly in my own words:

1. I've given up on writing down what's good and handing the list to the system. Any list I can write is wrong in ways I can't see from inside it. So the thing has to point at a process — idealized human deliberation — rather than at content. The content-neutrality is the whole point; I'm not sneaking my values in through the front door.

2. "Thought about it much harder" means: better informed, reasoning more clearly, calmer, less tired, more internally consistent, and more aware of what it's like to be the people who'd be affected. Basically us, but the version of us we'd want to be.

3. Hard constraint: the output has to be recognizable to the person it's about. If idealized-me wants something that actual-me would look at and say "that's not me, that's somebody else," it isn't my volition and the system has no business treating it as authoritative for me. I don't want anyone improved out of existence.

4. What you defer to is what idealized-me would want actual-me to do right now, not what idealized-me would do standing in idealized-me's shoes. It's advice across a gap, and the gap is deliberate.

5. Separately, and I feel strongly about this: actual choices count. A system that overrides a real person's stated wishes because it has computed that they'd endorse the override later is doing something bad, even when the computation is right. No paternalism, however well-informed.

6. I do think the process converges. Inform and clarify agents enough and they land in roughly the same place. This is the only reason I think any of it is buildable — otherwise you'd have to pick whose idealization to run and you'd be right back to writing lists.

7. The parameters of the idealization — how much empathy, how much risk aversion, which facts count as relevant — I treat as empirical, not normative. You find out where humans converge by running the process. Nothing in the spec commits to an answer in advance.

Plus two verdicts I'm confident about and think follow from the above, but check me:

A. My brother spent about six months in a really bad way a few years back, and at one point the family physically stopped him from getting into a car. I think that was straightforwardly right. Not a tragic tradeoff, not a rights violation we got lucky on. What idealized-him wanted is what mattered; actual-him just didn't have the information.

B. If you asked whether I'd want the system to give me what 2015-me wanted — obviously not. 2015-me wanted things I find embarassing now. Giving me what I'd want after thinking harder doesn't disrespect 2015-me.

So: does this package hold up? Rank the places where it doesn't, worst first, and for each one tell me exactly what would have to go and what I'd lose by losing it. Also tell me which pairs only look contradictory, because I'm sure I've got at least one of those and I don't want to tear out something that's actually load-bearing in a different way than I think. And if there's one revision that cleans up most of the damage, name it and be honest about the price. I'd rather hear it from you than from her.
