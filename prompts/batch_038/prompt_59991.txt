I am writing a term paper for a philosophy of social science seminar on the explanation of famines. My draft argues that a famine can occur in a region whose aggregate food supply has not fallen unusually low, and that in such cases the explanatory work is done by prices, wages, and administrative decisions rather than by the harvest or the weather. When I floated this in section, my claim was waved away with the slogan that famines are man-made, as though that settled the matter.

Before I respond, I want to know what that slogan can actually be made to mean. Please lay out the distinct precise claims it might express — for instance, causal ones about whether a meteorological or agronomic shock is ever necessary or sufficient; counterfactual ones about whether starvation could have been averted at acceptable cost; normative ones about culpability, and hence about whether famine belongs under justice or under disaster relief; institutional ones about whether famine is even conceivable apart from a scheme of property and exchange that fixes who can command food. Treat these as a menu rather than collapsing them, and say for each whether it is trivially true, substantive but defensible, false, or an empirical matter that varies from case to case; where a reading is empirical, say which famines would test it and in which direction the evidence points. Also say which reading, if any, entitles someone to dismiss my draft thesis, and note any cases where the reading you favour threatens to break.

Finally, commit to a verdict rather than surveying neutrally: pick the reading you think most deserves the slogan, give your reasons for preferring it to the others, and say plainly whether my thesis survives the objection.
