Ok, this is a weird request but bear with me. I'm not an academic — I fix commercial HVAC systems for a living — but for four years I've sat on the grant panel of a small community foundation in my town. We hand out about $250k a year across maybe a dozen local programs: a food pantry, a literacy tutoring thing, a mobile dental van, a program that puts grab bars and ramps in the houses of older people so they don't fall, that kind of stuff. There's seven of us — a retired teacher, two nurses, a lawyer, a pastor, me, and a woman who runs a thrift store — and every single year the same fight happens. Somebody says "just split it evenly, that's fair," somebody else says "no, put it where it does the most good," and then we burn two hours arguing about which of those is even the right question and finish by voting on a spreadsheet nobody trusts.

A friend who does a bit of economics sent me a writeup of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem and I've been chewing on it for weeks. My understanding is probably crude, so correct me where I'm wrong: if a group's ranking of gambles or prospects is rational in the expected-utility sense, and the group always goes along with a prospect every member prefers, then the group's evaluation has to come out as a weighted sum of the members' individual wellbeing. So the math sort of forces you into something utilitarian-shaped — but it doesn't tell you what the weights are. That last part is what grabbed me, because it looks like it separates the bit of our annual argument that's actually forced from the bit that's a value judgment we keep dressing up as arithmetic.

So: act as a professional philosopher who really knows both the formal social choice side of this and how volunteer committees actually behave, and turn that result into a decision procedure my panel could run in one evening, by people with no math past high school. I want steps in order, what each step asks a panelist to do, and what counts as a good enough answer to move on. I want the places where the procedure just stops and outputs a no. And I want the places where it has to hand the decision back to a human because it's values rather than calculation — my guess is that's the weights and the question of how you get everyone's wellbeing onto one common scale, but tell me if I'm missing others. Please start by brainstorming out loud a bit before you write anything, because I want to see which parts of the theorem have to survive the translation into layperson steps and which can be dropped without the whole thing becoming a lie.

Two things I've half-thought through myself and want dealt with head on, not waved at. First, the theorem seems to need everybody agreeing on the probabilities, and my panel absolutely does not agree on them — we argue about how likely a program is to work, and about the base rates. Worse, I have this creeping suspicion that when people disagree about the odds, a unanimous ranking stops telling you anything about wellbeing at all, like when two of us both rank a proposal first for opposite reasons: I think it'll succeed, you think it'll fail but at least it's cheap, so we "agree" and nothing was learned. Does the whole thing collapse there, or is there some repair a panel can actually perform? Second, one of our nurses insists we should rank by what happens in the real world after the money is spent, not by expected value beforehand — she says a proposal can look better on paper and be worse in every single branch of how it might turn out. I think the phrase I read was "ex post" something. How does the procedure handle a case where those two rankings disagree, and which one does it tell us to write down?
