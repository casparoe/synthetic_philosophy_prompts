For the past eight years I have sat on the cross-disciplinary renewal panel of a mid-sized public research funder. Our written criteria adopt the vocabulary of Lakatos's methodology of scientific research programmes — hard core, protective belt, positive and negative heuristic, progressive and degenerating problem-shifts — and every decision we publish carries a short rationale paragraph written in that vocabulary. I am interested not in what Lakatos said but in what our own record commits us to. Our published rationales, in chronological order:

1. A deep-crust abiotic-hydrocarbon programme. Two successive reformulations of its core mechanism each predicted an isotopic signature in cratonic fluids that nobody had looked for, and both signatures were later measured by independent groups. We ruled the problem-shift progressive and renewed in full, saying the decisive feature was that the new content had been risked in advance rather than fitted afterwards.

2. A diathesis–cortisol programme in clinical psychology. Over some fifteen years every disconfirming finding had been met with a new subtype, a new moderator, or a redefinition of the measure, and nothing had been predicted in advance in the last decade. No rival framework existed that could accommodate even the programme's central data. We ruled it degenerating and said so explicitly, yet renewed it at reduced scale, on the ground that it is not rational to abandon a degenerating programme when no progressive rival is available and the field would be left with nothing.

3. An interface-photonics programme, six years old, one problem-shift to date, no prediction tested, no failure recorded. We declined to appraise it, holding the record too short, and funded it on the specificity of its positive heuristic: we could see which problems it intended to attack next, and we said that this was itself a sign of health.

4. A metapopulation-persistence programme in ecology. It had issued a stream of bold novel predictions, four of which were tested and all four failed; each failure produced a specific revision carrying content beyond the immediate repair, and two of those revisions had since been confirmed in systems they were not designed for. We ruled it progressive on balance, writing that a failed prediction counts against a programme only when the repair is contentless.

5. In developmental biology, a fifteen-year-old rival absorbed the three central successes of a degenerating incumbent by re-deriving them from a different core, and issued one novel prediction that had not yet been tested. We funded the transfer, saying that excess empirical content plus the ability to account for the predecessor's successes was sufficient, and that waiting for the rival's own confirmation would demand a certainty the methodology does not require.

6. A programme in a branch of continuum mechanics that had not changed in twelve years: nothing refuted, hard core untouched, protective belt unaltered, the published work re-deriving known results more elegantly. No rival existed. We withdrew funding, writing that a programme which has stopped generating new problems is degenerating even where it is nowhere contradicted, and that the absence of a rival is not a reason to keep paying for one.

7. A psychometric programme whose auxiliary hypotheses were each confirmed but each had been tuned to a dataset already in hand, such that nothing in it implied any measurement not already made. We ruled it degenerating despite its predictive accuracy, writing that accuracy purchased on known data is not progress.

In three weeks we decide between two programmes in the same field, and we may fund both, one, or neither. The incumbent, an allostatic-load programme, is about thirty-five years old. Over the last decade every anomaly has been answered by adding a moderator or rescaling a parameter, none of it risked in advance; nothing has been refuted and its fit to existing data is excellent; three months ago a senior group in the programme derived from its core, plus a new belt hypothesis, a specific prediction about the time course of a marker under a stressor regime nobody has measured, testable in roughly three years at considerable cost; and our reviewers note that its belt hypotheses have become modular, each adjustable without the adjustment propagating to the others, so that it is hard to say which observation could still reach the core. The rival, a network-dynamics programme, is fourteen years old. It has re-derived the incumbent's three central successes more parsimoniously from a different core; it issued two novel predictions, one of which was tested and failed; the failure was repaired by positing an unmeasured latent coupling term that has not been independently tested, but that would also account for a long-standing anomaly in an adjacent field — an anomaly that is well documented yet has never been derived from any framework; and the rival's practitioners cannot say what experiment would measure the coupling term directly, its only empirical consequences running through those two anomalies. These descriptions are complete for present purposes.

Please do the following.

First, reconstruct our implicit rule-set: which candidate principles are consistent with all seven rationales, and where the rationales are in genuine tension with one another. I have in mind the pair 2 and 6, but tell me if you find fault lines elsewhere.

Second, for each candidate principle, state the ruling it predicts for the new case, separately for the incumbent and the rival, and show the reasoning that gets you there rather than the conclusion alone.

Third, give your single best guess at what this panel will actually decide, name the closest precedent in our record, and say what makes it closest — including which features of the new case the analogy strains over.

Fourth, since we must write a rationale paragraph whichever way we go, state what principle we would be adopting with each available ruling, and which future cases each ruling would make harder for us to decide consistently.

Fifth, identify the one further fact about either programme — or the one hypothetical eighth case put to us — that would most sharply discriminate between the candidate rules, and say what each rule would then say about it. If a fact is missing that you think would matter more than any of these, say so there instead.

Do not lecture me on Lakatos's texts, and do not cite or reconstruct passages from them. If you think one of our seven rationales departs from his own doctrine, flag it in a single line at the end and move on: our record, not the corpus, is the object of interpretation here.

Finally, an epistemic request. Tell me what would change your mind about your prediction of our decision — which consideration, if I supplied it, would move you to a different verdict — and tell me where you think your reconstruction of the track record is most likely to have gone wrong, including whether you are reading more structure into seven rulings than seven rulings can bear.
