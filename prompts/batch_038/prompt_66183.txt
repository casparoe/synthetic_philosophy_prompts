A colleague of mine is putting together a workshop on work and automation, and she has asked me to draft the opening case study so that the economists and the philosophers in the room are arguing about the same object. I have chosen the Luddite disturbances of 1811 to 1813, because the received treatment of them is exactly what I want to put under pressure. The received treatment runs as follows: the stockingers and croppers objected to machinery that displaced their labor; classical political economy already had an answer to that objection, which became canonical when Ricardo reversed himself and added the chapter on machinery to his third edition in 1821; on that answer, displacement is transitional, cheaper goods raise real incomes, demand shifts to other employments, and the compensation arrives, if not for the individual knitter then for the aggregate of labor. On this story Luddism is a mistake about the incidence of a benefit, and its moral interest is limited to the fact that the mistake was made by people who were being starved while they made it. I would like the case study to test that story rather than retell it.

The facts I intend to rely on are the well-known ones, and I will not ask you to supply anything beyond them: the movement began among the Nottinghamshire framework knitters in the autumn of 1811 and spread in 1812 to the West Riding shearmen and croppers, whose finishing work was being undercut by gig mills and shearing frames; the knitters were not factory hands but outworkers, many of them hiring or owning the frames they broke, and the breaking was targeted rather than indiscriminate, directed at the wide frames turning out cheap shoddy hose and at the masters employing unapprenticed labor outside the customary rules of the trade; the participants wrote in a legal and quasi-judicial register, styled themselves followers of a fictitious General Ludd, and combined the breaking with petitions, meetings, and appeals to older regulations of the trade whose statutory repeal had left them without a forum; Parliament made frame-breaking a capital offence early in 1812, Byron spoke against the bill in the Lords, and on one common reckoning more troops were stationed in the affected districts that spring than Wellington had under him in the Peninsula; the York assizes of January 1813 ended in executions and transportations; within a few decades "Luddite" had become, and remains, a term of abuse for resistance to technical improvement. Please do not lean on any detail more specific than these, and if a further historical claim is doing real work in your analysis, say explicitly that it is doing work you have not established.

The instrument I want to use on the episode is a distinction I have been developing, and which I am stipulating for present purposes rather than borrowing from anyone. Call a harm of automation *displacement* when the worker's labor is no longer wanted: the job goes, the question becomes what other jobs exist and at what wage, and the whole issue is one of the allocation of a diminished demand for that person's hours. Call a harm *debasement* when the work survives but the terms that made it worth doing do not: the worker keeps the task while losing the skill that made the task his, loses control of the pace and of the standard of what counts as well-made work, loses an income that would support a household at the customary level of decency, and loses the standing that came from belonging to a craft whose rules bound master and man alike. On this account displacement is a question about the quantity of a person's labor and debasement a question about the character of it, and the two can come apart in every combination: work can be debased for those who keep it while nobody is displaced at all, and work can be displaced wholesale while what remains is more skilled and better paid than what it replaced.

With that instrument in hand, here is what I want from you, as the core of the case study.

1. Exactly five numbered items, each a sentence or two, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no summary sentence at the end of the fifth.
2. The first item should say, with reasons drawn from the episode itself, which of the two harms the Luddites were primarily resisting, and should identify the strongest evidence on the other side of that question rather than only your own.
3. The second should say what your answer does to the compensation apparatus: if the harm was debasement, does the classical reply miss the objection, answer a question nobody asked, or still dispose of it, since a degraded but employed knitter is not obviously worse off than an unemployed one?
4. The third should turn the instrument on itself and say whether the distinction survives contact with 1811, or whether it dissolves there, for instance because both names pick out one harm seen from different angles, because the knitters' real grievance was a legal and jurisdictional one about who had authority to fix the terms of the trade, or because the frame-breaking was in substance collective bargaining by riot in a year of dearth, closed export markets, and war, with no claim about the meaning of work anywhere in it.
5. The fourth and fifth should carry the verdict: whether you think the distinction earns its keep in the present debate over automation, or whether it is a presentiment we already capture better with other vocabulary such as task substitution, deskilling, and bargaining power, and what single case the distinction would have to be applied to next in order to tell.

Two further things. Take a position and give your reasons; I am not interested in a survey of what one might say, and where you are genuinely unsure I would rather see one clause of hedging attached to a definite claim than a paragraph of options. Do not sentimentalize the knitters — the analysis should be as willing to conclude that they were wrong about their own case as that the political economists were wrong about theirs — and please do not attribute any specific view to any named historian or supply a bibliography, which I will assemble myself.
