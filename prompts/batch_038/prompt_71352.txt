I am a graduate student in a history department, and after a work-in-progress session last week two of our faculty got into a long methodological argument that neither would close. I was taking notes and I still cannot tell who came out ahead, so I would like a careful referee rather than a summary of the issues.

The trigger was a draft chapter about a port public-health officer in the 1910s who designed a system of medical inspection of arriving immigrants and published tables ranking nationalities by what he called innate fitness. The question was whether the chapter is entitled to characterize his work as racial screening and to call him a racist.

Marcus opened (1) that this is presentism: the officer's category was medical fitness, not race in our sense, the moral weight the word racist now carries is a later acquisition, and applying it turns the chapter into a morality play that forecloses the real question, which is why his contemporaries found his statistics credible. Ines replied (2) that he wrote that certain nationalities were innately less capable, which is his claim and not our vocabulary draped over it, so withholding the label in the name of accuracy amounts to laundering it. Marcus (3): translation is exactly the difficulty — innate national capacity belonged to a hereditarian framework shared by the whole profession, including the officer's sharpest critics, so if the term fits him it fits everyone, and a term that discriminates nothing in a context tells us nothing about the person. Ines (4): a concept can be universally held and still be true of particular people — heretic applied to nearly everyone in a confessional age and still picked out who was who — and if a view's being unexceptionable at the time is a reason we cannot call it wrong, then moral vocabulary applies only to the living. Marcus (5): the alternative is not that, it is an order of work — describe first in his terms so we know what he was doing, appraise afterwards in ours and mark it as ours — whereas her chapter does both at once, and the reader cannot tell which sentences are which. Ines (6): that separation is the very presentism he thinks he is avoiding, since deciding what counts as neutral description already takes a side; one cannot characterize the inspection regime without settling whether its categories were medical or political, and that is settled from where we stand, so there is no unmarked description, only marked and unmarked. Marcus (7): if every description is evaluatively loaded, then the rule about judging by the standards of their own time is empty, and we have lost the only check on writing history as self-congratulation. Ines (8): we have not lost the check, we have relocated it — the discipline is not stepping outside our vocabulary but declaring which of our terms are load-bearing and why.

Please work through moves 1 to 8 in order and say who is right at each, and where a move is only partly right, say which part survives and which does not. Then answer two further questions: does Ines's move 8 actually meet the objection Marcus raises in move 7, or does it merely restate her position in the face of it? And what are both of them missing — is there anything about the question of escaping the standards of one's own time that their framing does not permit either side to raise?
