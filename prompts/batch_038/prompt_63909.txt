My mother has a will and also an advance directive she wrote about fifteen years ago, in her early sixties, when she was completely healthy. The directive says that if she ever reaches a state of severe dementia where she no longer recognizes her children and can't hold a conversation, she does not want to be kept alive by a feeding tube, and she asks that we not spend money on treatments meant to extend her life in that condition. She's now 81, in that condition pretty much exactly as she described it, and the directive is being followed. But here's the thing that got me thinking: my mother today seems, most of the time, mildly content. She likes ice cream, she likes sitting in the sun, she laughs at things on TV. She has no idea the document exists and if you showed it to her she wouldn't understand what it was. Two of the nurses at the facility have said privately that they think following the directive is a kind of killing, and one of my brothers agrees.

So I want to understand what my family and those nurses are actually disagreeing about, philosophically. What theory of persons, and of whose life is being protected, does the practice of honoring an old advance directive presuppose? Under what account of personal identity or of self-ownership does it make sense for the woman who was 62 and articulate to still be in charge, and why do we let her rather than the 81-year-old who is here now and has some interest in continuing? Walk me through the rival views that would license a different practice — the one where you weigh only how the patient is doing now, and the one where you weigh only what she asked for — and say which one our system has actually adopted, since as far as I can tell it does both depending on the day and the paperwork.

Two things I specifically want you to dig into. First, is there an asymmetry in how we treat these documents, and if so what does it presuppose? If my mother had written at 62 that once she could no longer make decisions she should be given ice cream every day and taken outside in the sun for as long as possible and never deprived of any pleasant thing, nobody would call that binding in the same way — we'd say she can't command her future caretakers to spend resources on pleasures for someone who can't ask for them, or maybe we'd say she can't know what she'd want. But refusing treatment we do treat as binding. Why is the refusal special? Is that distinction principled, or is it just a habit that happens to feel solemn because it's about death?

Second, tell me what would have to change in the actual practice — the forms, the conversations, the legal standard, who gets to decide — if we replaced the presupposed view with the strongest alternative. I mean genuinely, what would a hospital or care home operating under that other theory look like on a Tuesday afternoon, and what would it lose.

I don't need citations or a reading list and please don't invent any. I'd rather you reason it out from the structure of the practice itself. Where the practice is actually compatible with several different theories and doesn't force a choice, say so plainly instead of picking one to make the answer tidier — that's part of what I'm trying to figure out. Also feel free to tell me if you think the nurses are right and I've been comforting myself with a document.
