I teach a 4th-year philosophy of mind module and one of my weeks is on the metaphysics of emotion — the three-way fight between feeling accounts, judgment accounts, and perception-of-value accounts. I'm writing the final now and I want one essay question, 30 marks, answerable in 45 minutes closed book. Problem is, every version I've drafted can be answered by a competent student who writes three tidy paragraphs of book report and then concludes that the truth is probably some kind of hybrid. I want that route closed off.

So I've made up a case and I want the question built on top of it. Roughly:

Nadia has worked alongside the same consultant surgeon for four years. She would testify under oath that he is kind, careful and safe; she has no trauma history, no phobias, a normal startle response, and she insists she is not afraid of him "in any sense whatsoever." But when the two of them get into the lift together, her chest tightens, her palms sweat and she drifts towards the door. It happens only in the lift: she is completely calm meeting him in the corridor, in theatre, on video calls. She reports no experience of him as dangerous or frightening — no image, no thought, nothing that feels like an evaluation. And this is the bit I want the question to lean on: when asked what it's like from the inside, she says she genuinely doesn't know. Asked whether the tightness feels like fear, like anger, or like having had three coffees, she can't say; and she agrees that if you described the bodily pattern to her without telling her it was hers, she couldn't have picked it out as fear. It began about six months ago. Since then she has been given evidence twice, from people she trusts, that there is nothing to be scared of, and the response hasn't budged an inch. She now takes the stairs, and describes that as "just what I do now," without being able to say why she does it.

The question should ask candidates (i) what each of the three accounts has to say Nadia's state is, (ii) whether any of them is actually refuted by the case, and (iii) whether the case is even possible — and whether a theory that rules it out is thereby right or thereby better. Keep philosophers' names out of the printed question; they've all read the usual suspects and I want argument, not a name-checking exercise.

Then give me a model answer of about 700 words and a markscheme totalling 30. For the scheme I want it as a seperate section with five must-hit moves, the marks each one carries, and one line under each saying how candidates typically botch it. The A/B line has to be drawn in terms of moves, not coverage — e.g. an A answer has to notice that judgment views can concede recalcitrance only by giving up something specific about judgment, and that the perceptual view's best resource here (something like modularity or informational encapsulation) is exactly what Nadia's failure to have any phenomenology of danger threatens. A B answer that just says "the illusion analogy handles it" shouldn't get past 22. Also flag any move that should cost marks outright — sliding between irrational and arational, treating the Schachter-style arousal ambiguity as though it settled a constitutive question, that sort of thing. No need to tack on references or further reading.

Start your reply with the question exactly as it would be printed on the paper — no preamble, no commentary on my case, no restating what I asked for. Model answer and marking scheme come after it, in that order.
