I am drafting the ethical framework for a mid-sized European ethnographic museum whose holdings include several thousand contested objects — among them pieces comparable to the Benin Bronzes, taken by British forces in 1897, and architectural fragments comparable to the Parthenon sculptures removed under Lord Elgin in the early nineteenth century. Before engaging with any existing position, I want the specification: what a normative theory of restitution must be able to do to count as adequate for that purpose. Write the desiderata, each with the case or consideration behind it, and ensure the list handles at minimum these: acquisitions that were lawful at the time but concluded under colonial duress; objects looted more than once, where the claimant state is not the polity the object was taken from; claimant communities that want an object reburied, burned, or allowed to decay rather than displayed; documentary evidence produced entirely by the taking party; the demand that a principle be applicable across a whole collection rather than adjudicated case by case; and whether offering a cast, a digital surrogate, or shared custody can discharge the wrong that physical return would.

Separate requirements about title from requirements about access, research, and continued exhibition, and say which of the demands actually pressed in this debate — universal accessible heritage, statutory bars on deaccessioning, deference to a source community's own conception of the object's fate — are genuine desiderata and which are entrenched preferences of one camp. Then identify any subset that cannot be jointly satisfied, since I suspect the real result lies there, and name the single requirement that eliminates the most candidates.
