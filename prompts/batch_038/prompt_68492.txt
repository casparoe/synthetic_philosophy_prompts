Act as a senior political philosopher who works on the ethics of migration and on theories of political legitimacy, and who has sat through more faculty workshops than they can count. Think carefully before answering, and follow the structure I specify at the end.

Next Thursday I present a paper to a joint session of my law school's immigration-law workshop and the university's political theory reading group. My thesis is an asymmetry claim about the right to exclude, which I will call the rate/composition asymmetry:

1. If a political community has any entitlement with respect to borders at all, it is an entitlement over the terms and tempo of association, not over the relative standing of persons. Self-determination is exercised over arrangements; it is not a license to rank.
2. A numerical cap regulates tempo. It is impersonal, it applies identically to everyone in the queue, and the cost it imposes takes the form of delay rather than of judgment.
3. Composition selection — preferences by skill, education, wealth, religion, or national origin — is not a regulation of tempo but a public ranking of persons issued by the polity, and it is exactly what premise 1 removes from the state's competence.
4. Hence a state may cap but may not select. Moreover, the cap is legitimate only where it is set transparently, is revisable, and is paired with a duty to mitigate the conditions that generate the demand to move; and it is defeated outright by claims of necessity.

The audience is specific, and the objections will track the composition of the room. There are (a) two communitarians in the Walzerian tradition who hold that membership and the value of a shared political culture license selection just as much as numbers, and who think my premise 1 is question-begging; (b) a Kantian scholar of cosmopolitan right who maintains that turning a stranger away is permissible only where refusal does not bring about that stranger's ruin, and who will insist that a cap is mass refusal by another name; (c) an empirical migration scholar who studies actual admissions systems and will press feasibility and self-defeat, since points systems, family reunification rules, and asylum triage all look like selection, and since a flat ban on selection may simply push states to cap more harshly; and (d) a postcolonial and critical race theorist who doubts that the right to exclude is a coherent frame in the first place and will argue that a cap is a ranking in disguise.

On point (d): I argued this with a friend for two hours last night, and he belongs squarely in that camp. His parting shot was that I have not located a principled line between regulating tempo and ranking persons, only a line between rankings I find distasteful and rankings that operate through a queue — because a first-come, first-served line sorts people by date of birth, by geographic proximity, and by who can afford a smuggler. Assume that objection lands somewhere in the room.
