Going back through Spinoza's two political treatises, the argument that stays with me is the claim that one man cannot possibly hold the supreme right of a commonwealth, since the only limit of right is power and a single man's power is inadequate to the load — so that what looks like a perfect monarchy works in practice as a hidden aristocracy of counselors, and the more absolutely authority is handed over, the less independent the king becomes and the more he fears his own citizens. I have spent years in rationalist comment threads watching social contract arguments get run in every direction except this one, and I would like to see the Spinozist version carried through to its end.

Write me a resignation letter, about 600 words, from the absolute ruler of an invented seventeenth-century city-republic who has accepted that diagnosis and is stepping down in favor of the multitude, addressed to the council and citizens about to inherit his authority. The humor must come entirely from the political theory being applied correctly, not from anachronism or from the ruler being a fool: he should take calm inventory of what he supposed he could command and could not — decrees obeyed only where they already matched the disposition of the populace, the edict against seditious opinion that produced concealment rather than belief, the outward forms of worship that were genuinely his to regulate and the inward faith he could never reach, the generals and counselors and one particular favorite who turned out to be the real sovereign all along.

Internal consistency is part of the brief. He denies free will and has no use for divine right, natural rights, or consent as a moral bond, so the letter cannot read as repentance or as a conversion to liberalism; his recommendation of democracy should be the Spinozist one — the most natural arrangement, since each person hands his power to the whole of which he remains a part, and since the end of the state is freedom rather than fear — delivered as honest accounting rather than moral discovery. He never winks at the reader and never cites Spinoza by name, and the closing should leave the live question of whether a commonwealth whose sovereign is the multitude is any more stable than the one he is leaving.

Continuous prose only, in the shape of an actual letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold text, no tables.
