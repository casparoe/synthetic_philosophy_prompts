I am setting the final assessment for an upper-level seminar on the temporal structure of conscious experience. Twelve students, all of whom have already taken a philosophy of mind course. The assessment is a 72-hour take-home containing one compulsory question worth 40 marks, to be answered in no more than 1,200 words. The topic is the color phi paradigm and the distinction between Orwellian and Stalinesque accounts of it that Dennett and Kinsbourne draw in "Time and the Observer" (1992). The paper is assigned reading, so the question may assume familiarity with it, but it must also carry enough stimulus material to stand on its own: I do not want anyone's grade to depend on how much detail they happen to remember.

Here is the material I want built into the question paper, which you should rewrite as clean exam prose rather than reproduce as notes.

First, the paradigm. Two spots of light, differing in color and separated by a few degrees, are presented in alternation at an interval on the order of a tenth of a second. Subjects report not two flashes but a single spot moving smoothly from the first position to the second, and they report that it changes color somewhere near the middle of that trajectory — at a time when, physically, only the first spot has been presented.

Second, the two explanations. On the Orwellian account, the subject genuinely experiences the first color persisting along the path, and the record of that experience is subsequently rewritten in light of the second spot; the report is read off a tampered trace. On the Stalinesque account, no such experience ever occurs: the moving-dot content is not assembled until enough information has arrived, the color change is written in before the content becomes conscious, and the report faithfully records what it was like.

Third, the standing objection, which I want stated as the exam's central problem: the two accounts are usually said to be empirically indistinguishable, because their proponents agree about every physical event in the brain and disagree only about whether a given event falls before or after the point at which experience is fixed — and because the only evidence available, the subject's verbal report, is generated downstream of both candidate revisions.

Fourth, and this is my own invention, to be attributed in the paper to a hypothetical collaborator in cognitive neuroscience. She proposes a decoding test: train a multivariate classifier to read out, at each moment, which color is currently represented in color-selective visual cortex; then run the color phi sequence. Her claim is that the two accounts now come apart. If the decoded color at a cued mid-path location is the first color right up to the onset of the second spot and shifts only afterwards, there was an original experience that got updated, and the Orwellian account is confirmed. If nothing about the mid-path location carries the first color at any point, the content there was assembled late with the change already in it, and the Stalinesque account is confirmed. She concludes that the alleged emptiness of the distinction was an artifact of relying on verbal report.

The question must require students to do three things. It must ask them to explain why the indistinguishability claim holds in the standard paradigm, and to show what substantive assumption that explanation already concedes about what fixes the content of an experience at a time. It must ask them to assess the collaborator's proposal: to locate exactly where it breaks down, given that both accounts allow for early neural responses to the first color and differ only about the status of those responses; to say what bridging principle would have to be added to make a decoded state probative of consciousness; and to say what it would cost to adopt one. And it must ask them to state how the two accounts differ in their predictions about what modulates the illusion — the interval between the spots, on the one hand, and manipulations of retention and reporting load after the second spot appears, on the other — and whether that difference could be measured without presupposing an answer to the original question. Finally, they must commit to a verdict on whether the distinction is empty, partly empty, or merely hard to test.

Please deliver four items.

1. The exam question exactly as it would appear on the paper, with the stimulus material integrated and a clear statement of what is being asked and of the word limit and mark allocation.
2. A model answer of roughly 900 to 1,000 words, of a quality I could release to the class after marking. It must actually do the three tasks above rather than survey them, and it must reach and defend a verdict.
3. A marking scheme totaling 40 marks, divided into five or six named moves, with the marks assigned to each and, for each, one sentence identifying the error that most often costs marks at that point. Examples of what I want flagged: students who explain the illusion by saying the brain predicts the second color; students who treat the neural decoding proposal as settling the matter automatically; students who infer from "no fact of the matter" that both accounts are false; students who describe the multiple drafts model at length in place of answering.
4. A single paragraph saying what separates a first-class answer from a competent second, phrased in terms of specific moves rather than general praise.

Do not include a bibliography or a list of further readings, and do not have the model answer cite secondary literature; the students have the assigned paper and that is sufficient. Any additional empirical or philosophical detail the answer needs should be introduced and explained within the answer itself.
