Two-thirds through a novel set in a river port in the 1860s — grain warehouses, immigrant labour, one new railway — and the chapter I'm stuck on I want to break out and try as a standalone short story, partly to find out whether the idea works at all.

The idea: the fire is overdetermined. Not "a lot of things contributed." Four causes, each of which by itself would have burned those warehouses down inside the week, and they have to be genuinely separate chains — not one chain described from far away and then again from very close up. A friend of mine who teaches this stuff read my notes and kept saying each cause on its own isn't enough but the set is more than enough, which is exactly the mess I want the reader to feel rather than be told about.

Shape: a harbour office clerk, the nightwatchman's daughter, spots them one at a time and puts three of them out — a lantern, a grudge, a valve. What her work buys is two hours and a smaller blaze. It doesn't buy the thing not happening, and nobody at the inquest has a form to put that in. End on the inquest: the town needs one cause and one face, it picks her father, and that verdict is the paper every later account of the fire gets written from. Last beat is her understanding why they need that — no speech about it, no forgiving them.

Rules: nobody lies, this isn't Rashomon, all four accounts are true and compatible. No omniscient historian voice, no epigraph, no blaming the town itself as a whole. One cause has to be an omission and one a standing condition rather than an event. ~2500 words, close third. Invent the town, I dont care about the history being exact.
