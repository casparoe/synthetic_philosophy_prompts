I am in the closing weeks of a seminar on population ethics and intergenerational justice, and for our final session each of us has to put our own considered position on the table in writing and then field objections to it. Before I submit mine I would like a rigorous consistency audit rather than encouragement. Here is the package, roughly in the order it developed:

1. I reject the Repugnant Conclusion, and I count that rejection as one of my anchors. A world containing a hundred billion people whose lives are barely worth living is worse than a world containing ten billion people whose lives are excellent, notwithstanding that the first contains vastly more welfare in total.

2. My main way of securing (1) is the average view: what matters is average welfare, so a larger population counts as an improvement only if it raises the average. I find this independently attractive and not merely instrumental, since it seems to explain why nobody is obliged to fill empty continents with contented people.

3. At the same time I hold, and hold strongly, that where the choice is between bringing into existence a group of people whose lives are not worth living and bringing into existence a larger group whose lives are worth living though only barely, the second option cannot be worse than the first. I do not treat this as a fragile intuition. If a view tells me a world would be better with more miserable people in it and fewer marginally contented ones, I regard that as disqualifying rather than as a bullet to be bitten.

4. I accept the asymmetry: there are powerful moral reasons not to create a person who will suffer, and nothing comparable on the other side. Creating a person who will flourish is permissible and may be a lovely thing, but its omission is not a loss, and it is never something one is required to do.

5. I accept the non-identity reasoning. If a policy causes different people to come into existence, and those people have lives worth living, they have not been harmed by that policy, since the only alternative for each of them was never to have existed. I do not think we should wriggle out of this by claiming that identity is vague or contingent in these cases.

6. Nonetheless, I think there is a genuinely moral difference — not aesthetic, and not a matter of impersonal bookkeeping — between a depletion policy that leaves the far future populated by people with merely adequate lives and one that leaves it populated by people who flourish. Something serious is at stake in the second case that is not at stake in the first, and I want to say this without giving up (5).

7. I hold a person-affecting restriction on betterness: outcomes are better or worse only insofar as they are better or worse for people, where I am willing to count among the relevant people those who do not yet exist but will. There is no free-floating value that is good or bad without being good or bad for somebody.

8. I am deeply suspicious of my own intuitions about very large populations and about very low qualities of life, since I cannot genuinely imagine either, and I accept that this suspicion is a serious methodological constraint on the whole subject. Even so, I keep (1) as an anchor intuition rather than discarding it along with the rest.

9. I am unwilling to reach for the technical escape hatches. Views that posit a welfare threshold such that lives above it add value and lives below it subtract value, and views on which the value added by extra lives tails off as the population grows, both strike me as machinery introduced solely to generate the desired verdicts, and I do not want a position whose central claim is motivated by nothing but its consequences.

Please go through this set and tell me: which commitments genuinely conflict with which others, ranked by severity rather than listed flat; which conflicts only look like conflicts and dissolve once the commitments are stated precisely, and why they dissolve; for each real conflict, exactly which commitment must be weakened or dropped, and what holding onto the remainder then costs me elsewhere in the package; and finally which single revision would repair the most damage overall. I have deliberately not told you which of these I feel least attached to, and I would rather you not infer it from the ordering or the tone — treat all nine as equally negotiable and let the audit decide. Where I have misdescribed what a principle commits me to (for instance, if the average view does not in fact deliver what I claim for it in (2), or if (7) can be read in more than one way with different results for (5) and (6)), say so plainly.

Two further requests about how you present this. First, please separate what is settled from what is contested: where people who work on this subject essentially agree that a given implication follows from a given principle, say that; where they disagree about whether there is a tension at all, or about how best to diagnose it, mark the point as live and sketch the main lines of disagreement instead of presenting your preferred diagnosis as the consensus view. Second, do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations — I will fill those in from the seminar packet myself — so please argue in terms of principles, implications, and cases rather than attributions.
