So I've been chewing on Hofstadter's careenium, the thought experiment where you've got a big box full of tiny balls careening around and bouncing off each other, and the walls are wired up to little cameras and flippers so the outside world gets in and the box can act back out. Zoomed right in it's just balls hitting balls, no mystery. Zoom out and some of them clump together and stay clumped, and those clumps — simmballs, he calls them — end up standing for things outside, a red ball out there, a hand, whatever. The question he poses is basically who's shoving whom: the balls, or the clumps? I keep thinking the answer ought to fall out of some general rule about when a high-level pattern deserves causal credit, so I tried to reverse-engineer my own rule from my gut verdicts, and it didn't work. My housemate does a PhD in something to do with causal modelling and keeps telling me to just talk about interventions, which I find unsatisfying but can't argue against properly. I originally got into this arguing about whether a macro in a spreadsheet causes the cells to change.

Here are the verdicts. A market drops 7% in an afternoon on a rumour that turns out to be flatly false: I say without hesitation the rumour caused the crash, and I don't want to hear that the money "really" moved because of the individual trades. A kettle screams when the water boils: I would never say it screamed because it was time for tea; the pressure story is the whole story and the tea adds nothing. Those two feel nothing alike to me and I can't say why. Patient takes a sugar pill believing it's an opioid and the pain relief shows up on a scan: I want to say the belief did the healing. A smoke ring in a wind tunnel: the ring shape is as real and as robust as anything, it survives different smoke and different fans, but if someone said the roundness of the ring pushed the air behind it I'd laugh at them. That's the pair I can't reconcile — smoke ring and placebo are both patterns, both stable across what they're made of, and I give one causal credit and deny it to the other. The phantom traffic jam that keeps crawling backwards down the carriageway for ages after the one brake tap that started it: honestly no idea. I want to say the wave is stopping the cars, but there's no wave, there's just cars, and the original tapper isn't involved any more, so what would even be doing the pushing. And a note I wrote myself a year ago that I actually follow through on: I think the ink caused the action, which seems insane given ink is ink.

My tentative rule, which I'm pretty sure is broken, is that a high-level pattern gets causal credit when the same outcome would still have come about if the pattern were realized differently — swap the balls for something else, keep the clump, keep the result. The smoke ring wrecks it (shape is completely robust, zero credit) and maybe the placebo wrecks it from the other side (the belief is doing real work but seems locked to this particular brain). So: is there a principle that gets all or most of these? If there is, state it precisely, clause by clause, and say which of my cases each clause is there to handle — I don't want names or reading lists, just the principle and the mechanics of it. If the only rule that saves everything needs a lot of moving parts, tell me what the simpler one costs, i.e. exactly which of my verdicts it forces me to give up, and whether you'd actually take that deal. Then tell me what your principle says about the case I keep going back and forth on: deciding not to send an angry text, where the thought "this'll make it worse" seems to be what stops my thumb, and I can't tell if that's genuine downward shoving or just a story I narrate afterwards. And finally, pick some case I didn't mention that you think my rule should be tested on and give the verdict — I'd especially like to know whether a feature inside some big trained network that reliably tracks a property of its inputs counts as a simmball doing the shoving, or as smoke-ring scenery.
