I am marking the final question on a second-year applied ethics paper. The question is:

*"My decision not to buy a chicken breast, not to take a long-haul flight, or not to vote makes no difference to anyone. Therefore none of these acts can be wrong."* Critically evaluate this argument. (30 marks)

The mark scheme rewards three things: isolating the implicit normative premise on which the inference depends; assessing at least two distinct lines of response to the argument; and distinguishing the structure of the meat case from that of the flying and voting cases.

Here is the candidate's answer.

> The argument contains one explicit premise and one implicit one. The explicit premise is a claim of causal inefficacy: an individual's purchase, journey or ballot is too small to alter the aggregate outcome. The implicit premise is that an act can be wrong only if it makes a difference for the worse to someone. Given both, the conclusion follows validly, so the question is whether the premises survive scrutiny, and in particular whether the implicit one does.
>
> Parfit's example of the harmless torturers bears directly on this. A thousand torturers each press a button that makes a single victim's agony imperceptibly worse, and each can truthfully say that what he does makes no difference the victim could detect. The individual act is therefore causally negligible, and the case vindicates the first premise of the objection at the level of the single act: we cannot resist the argument by insisting that each purchase, or each ballot, in fact shifts the outcome.
>
> The usual reply appeals instead to expected harm. Chickens are reared and slaughtered in batches, and retailers reorder when stocks fall below a certain level, so some purchases do trigger additional slaughter. Any given purchase may or may not be the one that crosses such a threshold; the chance that it does is small but not zero. Expected harm is that probability multiplied by the harm at stake, and since the harm is great, the expected harm of a single purchase is not zero either. In the meat case this reply is decisive, because the thresholds are genuinely sharp, and an act carrying a non-zero expected harm is wrong on any plausible moral view. The first premise is thus simply false.
>
> The objection is nonetheless well founded as a description of a collective action problem. Individual abstention is the free rider's rational choice, since the costs of the harmful practice fall on others whether or not one abstains. On this reading the objection does not really establish that these acts are permissible; it establishes that abstaining from them is individually irrational. This is also why the argument carries over unchanged to voting: a single ballot is almost never decisive, and casting it costs the voter time and effort, so abstention is the rational course.
>
> Finally, the argument is self-defeating. Were everyone to accept it, the harms in question would continue exactly as they are, and an argument whose general acceptance leaves its own subject matter untouched cannot be sound.
