I work as a jury consultant, mostly helping defense attorneys prepare cross-examinations of eyewitnesses, and the line I lean on more than any other is "all observation is theory-laden." I use it to argue that an eyewitness account is never a neutral recording: the witness's expectations shaped what they saw, so the report can't be treated as independent evidence. Opposing counsel recently told me that this is a philosopher's slogan and that it does not mean what I need it to mean, and I could not answer him.

So I would like the claim taken apart. What are the distinct things "observation is theory-laden" could be asserting, running from the near-trivial to the strong? I want them laid out separately rather than blended, and in particular I want to know whether the following are really different claims: that a witness's beliefs change the visual experience itself; that they change only what she attends to and therefore what information reaches vision; that they change only what she later remembers, describes, and reports; that they are built in by past experience rather than operating in the moment; and that the claim is really about the words used to describe an observation rather than about seeing at all.

For each claim, tell me whether it is plausibly true, and separately whether it would do the work I am asking of it in a courtroom. Which ones, if true, would genuinely show that an eyewitness report is weaker evidence than we normally assume, and which would be entirely compatible with witnesses being reliable? I also want to know whether any version of the slogan is self-undermining, in the sense that the experimental findings people cite in its favor would themselves be contaminated by it, and which version the people who repeat the phrase most likely have in mind. If the strong version turns out to be false and the weak versions turn out to be trivial, what is the most I can honestly tell a jury?
