I'm 4th year in moral philosophy, writing my dissertation on standing to blame, and I've hit a wall — partly in the literature but mostly in my own reading group, where the same argument has now gone round three times without anyone moving a millimetre. I want you to act as an expert on blame and moral responsibility (professional-philosopher level, someone who has actually argued about this in seminars) and diagnose the impasse for me rather than try to settle it.

Here's the setup. Everyone in the group agrees on the stock cases: the guy who lectures a colleague about infidelity while sleeping with his own intern has lost something; the tax dodger who moralises about civic duty has a problem. Nobody defends the hypocrite there. Everyone also agrees on the bracketing extremes — the blameless saint obviously has standing, and someone who has literally never done anything wrong doesn't exist. The fight is entirely about what gets lost in the middle, and about whether "standing" names one thing at all.

Three wedges where they split, and I think these are the diagnostic ones:

(1) The reformed hypocrite. Anna used to plagiarise, got caught, genuinely repented, hasn't done it in eight years, and now tells a grad student flatly that plagiarising is wrong and he should withdraw the paper. Half the group says she's fine — she isn't applying a double standard now. Half says she's still doing something objectionable, because she's demanding of him a standard she was happy to exempt herself from when it suited her, so the inequality is historical rather than current. Nobody can say what would settle it.

(2) The candid hypocrite. Ben still cheats on his taxes, and he tells his brother: I do this too, I'm not proud of it, but it's still wrong and you should stop. Most of the group thinks Ben's blame is fine, or at least a lot better than the silent cheater's. Which is weird, if the trouble with hypocrisy is inconsistency between your conduct and your judgment — Ben is perfectly consistent, he just says the quiet part out loud.

(3) The private hypocrite. Clara feels the resentment and the disapproval and never utters a word to anyone. One faction says there's nothing wrong with that whatsoever, because resentment isn't adresssed to anybody and so can't wrong anybody. The other says Clara's attitude is itself distorted or badly grounded, that this is precisely what the standing literature is supposed to be tracking, and that the absence of any victim proves the point was never about harm.

My hypothesis — attack it as much as you defend it — is that this isn't one debate. Roughly: one camp is asking a normative second-personal question (what may you permissibly do to another person when you condemn them, and does doing it while guilty of the same thing wrong them or slight their equal status — the Wallace-ish line, with Cohen's fairness-flavoured version alongside it), while another camp is asking a metaphysical or speech-act question about whether a hypocrite's condemnation can even succeed: does it misfire, so that there's no blame there to be impermissible, or does it land and thereby wrong someone? Different questions, different verdicts on Anna/Ben/Clara, and "standing" is being used both for a right to address and for a capacity to make your blame register. On top of that I suspect a chunk of the group is deploying a term of art borrowed from legal procedure that has no natural job to do inside a reactive-attitude picture of blame at all.

There's an empirical wrinkle as well. Years ago I read something in psych science — "Why Do We Hate Hypocrites?" or close to it, I'm fuzzy, and please don't go hunting for references, I don't need any — arguing that ordinary hostility to hypocrites isn't driven by inconsistency as such but by the fact that condemning someone else sends a false signal about your own conduct. The bit that stuck with me was that admitting to *unrelated* misdeals didn't make people like the hypocrite any more, while admitting the very thing you're condemning did. If that's roughly right, the folk intuition the whole debate is trying to systematise may have a source none of the theories posits, which would be one more reason its stuck.

So: what kind of disagreement is this, actually? Verbal, or two or three questions hiding under one word, or a substantive disagreement about what blame is that nobody is stating outright, or bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? Take the wedges seriously — run Anna, Ben and Clara through each candidate diagnosis and show me where the accounts genuinely diverge, because if they return the same verdicts on every case then there's no real disagreement here and I'd rather be told that. Then tell me what would count as progress: a distinction somebody needs to draw, a question that needs replacing, a new kind of case that would break the tie, or a straight argument that nothing will move it and why not.

Think carefully, and start with some brainstorming — lay out the candidate diagnoses and rule some in or out before you commit to one. Prose, not a survey, and no bibliography: I've read the literature, what I can't do is see its shape.
