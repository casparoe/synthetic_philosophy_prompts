A civil court assessing damages for a permanently injured plaintiff estimates her lost future earnings and her remaining lifespan from actuarial tables stratified by sex and, historically in the United States, by race. In toxic-exposure litigation many of the same courts require the plaintiff's experts to show epidemiologically that the exposure more than doubled the background incidence of her disease before a jury may find that the exposure caused her illness in particular. Yet when a plaintiff's evidence is statistical in form and nothing else — the vehicle that struck her could only have come from the company operating four of every five buses on that road — courts hold it insufficient however high the probability. What account of evidence, and of single-case probability, does this combination presuppose? Reconstruct it as explicitly as you can: state the principle of reference-class selection operative in each setting, and say whether a single principle licenses the first two while excluding the third, or whether the practice is simply incoherent. Distinguish what it strictly presupposes — for instance, whether it requires that group frequencies can make a claim about one individual probable, or only that they may fix the size of a remedy once liability is otherwise established — from what merely fits it well, and mark where it is underdetermined, such that a subjectivist, a believer in single-case objective chance, and a theorist who denies that bare statistics can produce knowledge could each endorse most of it. Then tell me which of the three practices would survive replacement by an avowedly Bayesian factfinder who reports nothing but a posterior probability, and what the others would have to become.
