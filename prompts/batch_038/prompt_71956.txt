I supervise supplier due diligence for a mid-sized retailer, and on Wednesday evenings I teach an introductory decision-analysis class to a mixed group of hospital nurses and logistics managers. Both halves of my week keep producing the same configuration, and I have never found a settled way to talk about it. In class I present the classical result usually credited to I. J. Good — that for a Bayesian expected-utility maximiser, information that costs nothing to acquire is never worth less than not acquiring it, since the agent can always take it and then ignore it — and every semester someone asks the predictable question: why, then, do people pay not to know? I usually answer with the voter who doesn't read the policy papers because the chance of swinging the election is negligible, and everyone accepts that this is a different thing, because there the information genuinely costs effort and yields almost no expected benefit. The cases that interest me are not like that at all.

Here are three, roughly in increasing order of how much they trouble me. First, at work: a supplier offers us a better unit price in exchange for a clause under which they warrant certain outcomes at their finished-goods plant but never disclose which subcontractors perform which operations. Our own written policy says that once we hold credible knowledge of a specific abuse at a specific facility we must either remediate it or stop buying from it, and both are expensive. So the cheapest way to stay compliant is to arrange, in advance and contractually, never to acquire the knowledge. Nobody involved thinks this is negligence; it is deliberate, documented, and defended in good faith. Second, from the nurses in my class: a clinician asks a colleague who is reviewing a chart not to relay a particular finding, on the reasoning that once she knows it she is legally obliged to file a report that will, in her judgment, do the patient more harm than the finding will do them good. She would be glad to have the information if she could hold it privately; it is precisely the fact that she cannot that makes it undesirable. Third, and in deliberate contrast, a friend of mine stops opening his brokerage app when markets fall, and says he prefers not to know; but there the cost of knowing seems to be the feeling itself, not any change in what he may or must do. I also know the terms rational ignorance and, from legal contexts, willful blindness, and I know that blinding in a trial is a case where information is excluded in order to make judgment better rather than worse — none of these quite covers the first two.

So: does this have a name, and if it does, is it one name or two? What I actually need is the carving, not the label. Please tell me which established concepts sit nearest to what I have described and exactly how the described thing differs from each of them — in particular whether it is distinct from avoiding upsetting news, from rational ignorance where acquisition is costly, from blinding, and from the legal treatment of deliberate avoidance as equivalent to knowledge, which seems to be a verdict on the avoider rather than a description of the decision. Then tell me which premise of Good's theorem fails in each of my three cases: whether it is that utility turns out to depend on the agent's own belief-state, or that the act of learning changes the decision problem itself by adding duties or removing options rather than merely reshaping probabilities, or that the agent is binding a future self they do not trust — and whether those are three variants of one phenomenon or three phenomena that deserve separate names. If there is no established term for the structure I have in mind, coin one, give me a definition tight enough that I could apply it to a new case without consulting you, and say where its edges fall: does my squeamish investor count, does the culpable corporate version count, and does a person who avoids information because they suspect they would overreact to it count? A bare name will not be any use to me; what I want to end up with is a criterion I can teach in ten minutes.
