I run a Wednesday-evening reading group at a community centre in Leeds — half of them are philosophy-ish types who've done a bit of Parfit, half are people who turn up because there's meditation afterwards and who've never read anything. Next week we finish a run of sessions on anattā and personal identity, and I want to close with a poem rather than another discussion, because the discussion always collapses into somebody saying the self is an illusion and then being told that isn't quite the claim, and we go round in circles for an hour.

So: a pantoum, please, five quatrains, done properly — lines 2 and 4 of each stanza reappearing as lines 1 and 3 of the next, and the final stanza folding back so that the very first line of the poem comes round as the last line. The form is the argument, which is why I want this one rather than free verse. What the transmitted lines should carry is the thought that nothing travels down the sequence except the pattern of transmission itself: the aggregates reassembling, a flame taking from a flame, milk gone to curds to butter to ghee, the chariot that turns out to be only a name we give to an arrangement of parts. If someone who knows the Milindapañha reads it aloud they ought to recognise Nāgasena's moves in it, including the one that matters most for us — that what is reborn, and what inherits the consequences of what was done, is neither the same as the one who acted nor a different one. I'd like that neither-same-nor-other business to to land in the closing stanzas through the recycled lines changing sense in their new positions, not by being stated outright.

One hard constraint, and it's the bit I care most about: every first-person singular in the poem has to be inherited. The word "I" may only appear in a line that has already appeared earlier, so that no self in the poem ever originates — each one arrives already spoken by somebody upstream. Keep it out of greeting-card territory too: no lotuses, no we-are-all-one, no consolation about being a drop returning to the sea. It should be plain enough to read to a room containing both halves of my group, and it shouldn't resolve anything. We ended up genuinely unsure whether the standard reply about moral responsibility — that it's the continuum, not a person, that bears the fruit of the deed — actually answers the objection or just gives it a new name, and the poem should end inside that uncertainty rather than picking a side. Twenty lines, and keep the individual lines fairly short so the repeats don't get ponderous.
