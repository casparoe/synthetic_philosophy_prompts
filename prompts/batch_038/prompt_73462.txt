You are a philosopher who has taught Adam Smith's Theory of Moral Sentiments closely for years and who is used to refereeing interpretive disputes between readers who have both done the reading. I would like you to referee one for me. Please think carefully before answering, and resist the urge to summarize Smith's system for me; I have read the book, and what I need is judgment about a particular argument.

Some background. A friend of ours, Daniel, lost his father about four months ago. At the memorial he was composed, told one wry story about the old man, thanked people, and was back at work within a week. Another friend, Ruth, lost her own father around the same time and has found that she cannot get through a day without the loss intruding. Two members of my reading group, Nadia and Owen, got into a sustained argument about whether Smith's impartial spectator would look on Daniel's composure with approval. Neither had read the other as a strawman, and neither would give ground, so they asked me to settle it; I could not, and I did not signal which way I leaned. Here is the exchange as near as I can reproduce it.

Move 1 (Nadia). Smith's account of propriety puts the burden on the person principally concerned. Because a spectator's feeling is always a copy produced by imagination rather than the thing itself, no bystander can feel another's sorrow as the sufferer feels it, and so the sufferer must lower his passion to a pitch the company can go along with. Self-command in sorrow is precisely what Smith most admires. Daniel did exactly that, and did it thoroughly, and on Smith's account that is propriety achieved rather than a coldness to be explained away.

Move 2 (Owen). That treats the spectator's capacity as a fixed ceiling, but Smith's own psychology points the other way in a whole class of cases. When the object of a sorrow is not continually before the sufferer's mind, the sufferer's own feeling goes dull and intermittent, while a bystander who brings the loss freshly and wholly to mind can feel it more strongly than the sufferer does; Smith finds that disproportion moving rather than discordant. So Daniel's near-calm may not be self-command at all but a dulled sensibility, and Ruth's livelier feeling for a loss that is formally her own and not his looks less like sentimentality than like the very standard Daniel has fallen below.

Move 3 (Nadia). Owen's cases are special: they depend on the sufferer's not knowing, or not attending, and Daniel knew perfectly well and attended constantly. More importantly, he has quietly substituted an actual spectator for the impartial one. Smith is explicit that the standard is not whatever the people in the room happen to feel; it is the verdict of a well-placed and well-informed judge we construct in ourselves by generalizing from a great many real spectator responses. One unusually sensitive friend therefore cannot set the standard. And Nadia added that Smith is alert to grief that is in part performed for its audience, which is a further reason not to treat Ruth's intensity as normative.

Move 4 (Owen). That retreat is what ruins the reading. An ideally placed spectator whose fellow-feeling is capped by no actual bystander's capacity is no longer measuring what can be entered into, and "what can be entered into" was the whole ground of propriety. So Smith is left with two standards and reaches for the inner judge whenever the crowd's verdict is embarrassing. Owen then turned the point on himself: if instead you keep the standard with actual spectators, propriety simply tracks the dullest person present, so a stoic among stoics is automatically proper and a grieving man among insensitive colleagues automatically improper. Either way, he said, Smith cannot deliver what Nadia wants, which is a reason to call Daniel's composure admirable rather than merely well matched to his audience. He also pointed to Smith's own expectation that our concern fades quickly even for enormous calamity and that we then get on with our business, which sits badly with making durable feeling the measure of the proper.

Move 5 (Nadia). The inner judge is not a free-floating ideal feeler; it is an artifact of accumulated experience, inheriting the limits of human fellow-feeling while not being hostage to any particular room. And she said Owen has been arguing about the wrong variable all along: Smith's spectator judges a scene, and the flattening requirement governs how strongly a passion is felt at a moment. Whether a grief has the right shape over four months is a different question, and one Smith never claims his spectator answers.

They both stopped there, and both claimed the same book.

What I want from you is an adjudication, not a survey. Begin with a short paragraph identifying what the dispute actually turns on, and whether Nadia and Owen are genuinely contradicting each other at every move or talking past one another at some of them. Then go through moves 1 to 5 in order and rate each one, on a scale running from clearly mistaken through partly right to clearly right, with a few sentences of justification for each rating; for each move say explicitly whether it is a fair reading of Smith, a misreading, or an interpretive gamble that goes beyond what the text supports. Then give me a verdict on the question they started with: can Smith's framework classify Daniel's composure as admirable rather than as merely well suited to his audience, and if it can, which of the two routes to that conclusion survives the objections raised against it. Finally, tell me what both of them missed, including whether the disagreement they were having is really internal to Smith or is a modern question they imported into him.

Two housekeeping requests. Do not hedge into a conclusion that both sides have something to contribute; commit to your ratings and say where each of them is wrong. And do not send me citations, locators, or a reading list; I have no way to check them and they are not what I am after. If you believe one of them has misremembered or overextended a passage, say so descriptively, in terms of the doctrine, rather than supplying a section number you might be inventing.

Please write your answer as continuous prose, the way you would write a considered letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables. Your ratings should be stated in sentences within the flow of the paragraph about each move.
