Acting as a philosopher of language with a serious working knowledge of the debate over the cognitive significance of identity statements — the Fregean, Millian/direct-reference, structured-proposition, mental-file, and pragmatic-or-interpretivist families of positions — I would like you to work through a set of judgments of mine and tell me what, if anything, they add up to. Please do not open with a summary of the standard puzzle; assume I know it and my students will too. Take the space to work through the cases before you commit to a formulation. I would rather see your reasoning about the cases that resist than receive a polished principle that quietly ignores them.

The context: I am preparing a graduate seminar on propositional attitude ascription, and before I assign anything I want to know whether my own pretheoretic verdicts are consistent and what they are tracking. In what follows, "Delia" is a fully competent adult speaker unless I say otherwise.

1. Delia has read a bundle of private letters signed "Samuel Clemens" and has separately read the newspaper notices of "Mark Twain," and has never connected the two. Told that Mark Twain is Samuel Clemens, she learns something: it is news to her, it revises what she believes about the letters, and she would have been justified in withholding assent beforehand. Verdict: genuinely informative, a posteriori, and true.

2. The same Delia, told that Mark Twain is Mark Twain. Verdict: no information whatever, no possible evidence she could have had beforehand that would have justified doubting it, and an assertion of it in ordinary conversation would be defective without being false.

3. Delia believes that Clemens piloted steamboats and denies that Twain ever went near a river. Verdict: "Delia believes that Samuel Clemens piloted steamboats" is true, and "Delia believes that Mark Twain piloted steamboats" is false. I want to insist that the second report is *literally* false, not merely infelicitous, misleading, or true-on-a-loose-reading. I do not want to buy my semantics with a standing escape clause into pragmatics.

4. Here is the one I cannot square with 3. Delia's colleague Arthur writes anonymous columns under the pseudonym "Cato." Delia keeps two dossiers — one for Arthur, one for Cato — and has no idea they overlap; she thinks Arthur sharp and Cato a fool. Verdict: "Delia believes that Cato is a fool" is true and "Delia believes that Arthur is a fool" is false. But I also think it would be entirely correct, and not a case of loose talk, for me to report her state to a colleague who has never heard of Cato by saying simply "Delia believes Arthur is a fool." Same sentence, same reporter, same subject, same world, same referents — false on the strict reading and correct on the second occasion. And I flatly refuse to make belief-report truth-values relative to the audience's files. I know these three commitments cannot all stand; I want you to tell me which goes and what replacing it costs.

5. Aubert, an astronomer of the 1860s, is wholly committed to Vulcan. Verdict: "Vulcan is Vulcan" is trivial for Aubert in exactly the way 2 is trivial for Delia, even though nothing answers to the name; and "Vulcan is the intra-Mercurial planet posited to explain the anomaly in Mercury's perihelion" is informative for him in exactly the way 1 is informative. So informativeness cannot consist in the two names differing in reference, and cannot require that either name have one. Further verdict: the first sentence is not false for Aubert and the second is (or is truth-valueless), but I do not want the difference in cognitive value between them explained by the difference in truth-value, since Aubert is in no position to detect that difference.

6. A child has been told stories about "Santa Claus" and has separately met "Father Christmas" at a holiday fair, taking them for two characters. Told they are one, she learns something. Verdict: informative, as in 5. But second verdict: unlike 5, I want to say the identity here is *true* — true in the pretense, or true of the fiction. I have no non-question-begging reason for treating the live pretense as truth-apt and the failed theory as not, and I suspect this verdict is infected by my sympathies. Tell me if it has to go.

7. Delia has known for years that Hesperus is Phosphorus. Told "Hesperus is Phosphorus," she learns nothing; told "Hesperus is Hesperus," she learns nothing either. Verdict: for her, now, the two sentences have the same cognitive value, and there is no fact about her that distinguishes them — and yet I want to maintain that the first is necessary and a posteriori and the second necessary and a priori, absolutely rather than relative to her, and that they still express different propositions.

8. The case I keep going back and forth on. My new colleague has a full dossier for the woman she had coffee with in Bologna last spring and no information at all attaching to the name "Ilaria Bezzi." Told that Ilaria Bezzi is the woman she had coffee with in Bologna, she learns something; the case looks like 1. But now take someone who has never been to Bologna and has no dossier for either side, told that Ilaria Bezzi is the poet who abandoned verse at twenty. Is that informative? It cannot be informative by merging files, since there is no file to merge. Yet I am loath to call it trivial in the way "Ilaria Bezzi is Ilaria Bezzi" is trivial, because it is empirical, could have been false, and because if she later acquires the dossier she will want to say either that she learned it then or that she learned it now without realizing it. I most want your principle to adjudicate this one.

My tentative principle, which I offer for testing and repair rather than defense: an identity sentence "a = b" is cognitively non-trivial for S just in case S has two distinct, uncoordinated files anchored to a and to b respectively; and "S believes that a is F" is true just in case S has a file anchored to a whose content includes F-ness, where which files count is fixed by S's own dispositions and not by the reporter's or the audience's. It handles 1, 2, 3, and half of 8; it makes a mess of 4, 5, 6, and 7.

What I want from you:

(a) The principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that a graduate student could apply it to a novel case without coming back to me. Clauses, not slogans — and for each clause, the case from my list that motivates it.

(b) The principle's verdicts on at least three cases of your own construction that I have not listed. Please include one in which the subject has forgotten who the bearer of the name is, and one involving the first person — for instance, telling a woman who does not realize that she is the person being described that Ilaria Bezzi is her.

(c) An explicit statement of which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and which I should abandon. Where a verdict can be preserved only by adding machinery, say what the machinery is, what it costs elsewhere, and whether the simpler principle that overturns the verdict is on balance preferable.

(d) A judgment on my tentative principle: repaired, replaced, or partially retained, and which part does the work.

Throughout, mark clearly the difference between what is settled among people who work on this material and what is genuinely contested. Several of my verdicts are ones a substantial fraction of philosophers of language would reject outright; if so, say which, and say roughly where the fault lines run. Do not dress a disputed position off as the consensus, and do not tell me that everything here is up for grabs when it is not. I want a document I could hand to a skeptic of my own intuitions and that would still be useful.
