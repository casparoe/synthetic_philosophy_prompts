Act as a political theorist who works on sortition and democratic legitimacy — someone who has read both the booster literature and the skeptics and hasn't been sold by either. Think carefully; I want reasoning, not talking points. And no citations, no bibliography, no reading list at the end — I'm offline and I don't want to be guessing at page numbers.

Here's the situation. I'm on a city charter commission and we're about to vote on an amendment that would give a randomly selected citizens' assembly — 60 people drawn from the voter file, paid a stipend, one-year term, opt-out permitted — final authority over council district maps and over a defined slice of the capital budget, with the council able to override only by a two-thirds vote. Half the commission is enthusiastic, half thinks its insane, and I've been handed the job of writing the "principles" section of the staff report.

So before I wade thru any more pro/con material, I want the spec: what does a theory of the political use of lotteries actually have to deliver to count as adequate *for a purpose like ours*? Give me the desiderata.

Open with about six lines of brainstorming on what our purpose constrains. We need a theory good enough to (a) survive a challenge claiming that a body picked by chance isn't the city acting at all, (b) persuade a room of skeptics who already accept jury duty, tie-breaking draws, and draft lotteries without a second thought, and (c) actually pin down our design parameters — how big, how to draw, whether a stipend is enough, one house or a second sorted chamber, whether election keeps any role.

Then the list: somewhere between 12 and 15 requirements, one tight paragraph each, every one with the case or consideration behind it. Tag each as non-negotiable, negotiable, or a prejudice of one camp dressed up as a criterion.

Here's what I already suspect belongs — use these, or tell me why they don't:

- The gradient. Nobody blinks at drawing straws to break a tied local election, or at lotteries for jury service, organ allocation, a military draft, or an oversubscribed program with more applicants than seats. A lottery legislature reads as a scandal. A theory has to explain that gradient rather than treating "lotteries in politics" as a single thing.
- The distinction among at least four uses: selecting decision-makers, allocating a good or a burden, breaking a tie, and sampling a public for deliberation. Which arguments transfer between them and which don't.
- Self-selection. Our draw comes from the voter file with an opt-out, so who actually shows up? What does a theory owe us here — a prediction of the bias, a fix, or an admission that the fix (compulsion, stratified draws, quota replacement) changes the nature of the thing being defended?
- The tension I can't shake. One line of argument says the lottery's political value is precisely that it's blind: nobody earned the seat, so no faction can capture it and no loser can claim they were cheated out of what they deserved. Another says the value is that the body comes out statistically like the city and brings a cognitive diversity that makes it smarter. Can a single theory have both? And what happens to the legitimacy story if our assembly turns out to be no better at the job than 60 people plucked at random off the street — which is more or less what the blindness argument says it should be?
- Power without removal. Our assembly can't be voted out and gets the last word on maps, a power we would never hand to an unelected board of consultants. What makes that acceptable, or does it have to be hedged?
- History. Athens sorted most of its magistracies, its council, and its juries, but elected its generals and its financial officers; the American founders knew that perfectly well and chose election deliberately. A theory that makes that choice look like either a conspiracy or a two-century-long mistake won't survive contact with our skeptics.
- Technical load. Maps involve legal constraints, demographic data, and a pile of expert judgment. If the answer is "the staff and consultants carry them," say what that does to the claim that the people are deciding.
- The loser's side. What do the displaced council member and the neighborhood that lost its seat owe the assembly's decision, and in virtue of what?
- Consistency with what existing mini-publics have actually done — including the Irish citizens' assembly that fed into the abortion referendum — without cherry-picking the successes.

Then tell me: which single requirement does the most work in knocking candidates out; which subset of the list you think can't be jointly satisfied, if any (be decisive, this is the part I most want); and which of my items — or yours — are really just a preference for elections wearing a trench coat.

Finish with a short scorecard, a paragraph each and no more: how the epistemic/cognitive-diversity defense, the equality-and-anti-elite defense, the arbitrariness-or-anti-capture defense, and a mixed design that sorts some things and elects others come out against your list. End with two sentences on whether our amendment as drafted can be defended at all, or whether it needs redesigning to be.

American English please, and keep it tight — I'd rather have 15 sharp requirements than 25 padded ones.
