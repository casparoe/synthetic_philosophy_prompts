For three weeks last term I kept the minutes for a departmental reading group on conditionals, and I would like a careful adjudication of an argument that developed in it between two of the regulars, whom I will call Dev and Marta. Neither conceded anything, the minutes record no resolution, and I was in no position to judge which of them had the better of it. I should say at the outset that I am not asking for references, attributions, or a survey of the literature; I want the arguments they actually made assessed, move by move.

The case the group was working with was constructed by a third member, Pia. A manuscript disappeared from a locked reading room on a Tuesday. Only two people had signed in that day: the conservator and a visiting fellow. The investigators established on the Tuesday itself that the fellow had flown out on Monday evening and had been abroad all day Tuesday; this was not made public until the following week, and no member of the group knew it when the disappearance was first discussed on the Wednesday. On the Friday of that week the conservator admitted taking the manuscript. Everyone also knew from the first day's report that the reading room window had been found unlocked, that the window is on the fourth floor, and that it had nothing to do with how anything left the room.

Five sentences were under discussion:

(S1) If the conservator didn't take it, the fellow did — uttered on the Wednesday.
(S2) If the conservator hadn't taken it, the fellow would have.
(S3) It's not the case that if the conservator didn't take it, the fellow did; he was on a plane — uttered on the Friday.
(S4) If the conservator took the manuscript, the window was unlocked that day.
(S5) If the conservator hadn't taken it, the fellow would have — also uttered on the Wednesday, by the same member who uttered (S1), and accepted by everyone present at the time.

The group's agreed verdicts on the data: (S1) was in order on the Wednesday and out of order as re-uttered on the Friday; (S2) is false; (S3) is an intelligible denial that contradicts what (S1) conveyed; (S4) is somehow bad even though the probability of its consequent given its antecedent is as high as such a probability can be; (S5) was accepted on the Wednesday by exactly the people who now reject (S2). On the Wednesday someone also proposed these credences for the group's use: 0.90 that the manuscript was merely misfiled, 0.06 that the conservator took it, 0.04 that the fellow took it, the three being mutually exclusive and exhaustive. Under that assignment, "Either the manuscript was misfiled or the fellow took it" stands at 0.94, while "If the manuscript wasn't misfiled, the fellow took it" stands, in the sense the group cared about, at 0.4.

Dev and Marta then argued as follows.

1. Dev: The (S1)/(S2) pair illustrates a difference of mood and nothing deeper. Both sentences say that the consequent holds in the antecedent-worlds picked out by a comparison of closeness; they differ only in what the comparison is permitted to hold fixed. For the counterfactual the selection is anchored to the actual course of events, which includes the fellow's flight, so the selected worlds are ones in which the manuscript simply stayed in the room. For the indicative the selection is restricted to worlds compatible with what the speakers are treating as settled, and on the Wednesday the flight was not settled, so the selected worlds are ones in which the fellow is still in the building. One mechanism, two settings, and the settings are independently motivated.

2. Marta: The second setting is not a variant of the first; it is a different kind of thing, and that shows up the moment you ask what fixes an indicative's standing. Acceptability tracks the conditional probability of the consequent on the antecedent, which is exactly what your numbers exhibit: 0.94 for the disjunction, 0.4 for the indicative. Now suppose indicatives have truth conditions, as yours do, and suppose what is normally supposed of truth-conditional claims, that one may assert what one takes to be probably true. An indicative, on any such account, is true in every world in which its antecedent is false, so its probability can never fall below that of the corresponding "either not-A or B." But the gap between those two can be made as wide as you like: take a coin that is very unlikely to be tossed at all, and "either it won't be tossed or it will land heads" becomes arbitrarily close to certain while "if it is tossed, it will land heads" does not budge. So one of your assumptions must go, and the one that must go is that indicatives have truth conditions of that kind.

3. Dev: Your bridge principle — assert what you take to be probably true — is false for truth-conditional claims generally, not just here. "This ticket will lose" is as close to certain as anything short of knowledge and is not assertible on any account of assertion ever given. So the argument cannot single out indicatives. What it shows is that an indicative's assertibility is not a simple function of the probability that it is true, which is precisely what my information-state parameter was introduced to explain.

4. Marta: The lottery case is a threshold artifact — the probability is high but not one, and requiring something short of certainty is not absurd. Mine is structural. The divergence does not consist in falling short of a threshold; it widens without bound while the indicative's standing stays fixed. No threshold story explains that. So the bridge must be refused specifically for indicatives, and the only principled way to refuse it is to deny that they carry world-directed content whose probability assertibility could track.

5. Dev: Then say what they carry, and pay for it. On the Friday, (S3) was received as a flat denial of what (S1) had said on the Wednesday, and nobody in the room had the slightest difficulty with it. If an indicative expresses a conditional confidence rather than a claim, then negating one, disjoining one with an ordinary claim, embedding one under "probably" or under "she said that," and using one as the major premise of a modus ponens each need a separate explanation — and (S3) needs one first of all. Nor can you state your own position without the resources you are refusing, since your thesis is about the acceptability of indicative conditionals, which requires embedding them.

6. Marta: I can give compositional contents that are not sets of worlds: let a sentence denote a constraint on, or an operation upon, information states, and negation, disjunction and embedding are handled by ordinary composition. That is a semantics, just not a classical one, so the charge that I cannot handle (S3) is not made out. And your side has the mirror-image problem, which is worse: inside "probably," or inside "she said that," whose settled information fixes your parameter? Either it is the subject's, in which case your account collapses into mine, or it is the speaker's, in which case reports of what other people said come out systematically wrong.

7. Dev: The parameter is the information state of the conversation, which is public and negotiable — that is why we can say to someone "but you knew he had flown" and be answered. It is no more mysterious than the parameter in "it's raining" or "that might be the postman." And notice where you have arrived: an update semantics assigns each indicative a content fixed by an information state and evaluated by conditionalization. That is my account with the word "truth" withheld. Unless you can name a datum on which the two views genuinely differ, the dispute is verbal — and your motive for withholding the word was the bridge principle, which we have agreed fails for lottery sentences as well.

8. Marta: The views differ on (S4). I explain its badness by holding that to assert an indicative is to present a conditional confidence as resting on some connection the speaker takes to hold between antecedent and consequent; here the confidence is maximal and the connection absent, so the presentation fails. You assign (S4) a truth — the window was unlocked in every world compatible with what was settled — and then need a maxim of relevance to explain why it may not be said. A condition on the attitude is cheaper than a truth plus a repair.

9. Dev: It is the same cost in a different place. Your conditional confidence is maximal too: on your account the probability of the consequent given the antecedent is one, and if assertibility tracked that, (S4) would be as good an assertion as any. So you require the very same connection requirement, only as a condition on which confidences may be presented — a pragmatic constraint under a different label. (S4) is a problem we share, and you have described it as though it were mine alone.

10. Marta: Sharing a problem is not sharing a diagnosis. I can build the connection requirement into what the attitude is. You cannot build it into what the sentence says, because on your account what the sentence says is true.

Pia intervened once and was not answered to her satisfaction. She pointed out that (S5) had been accepted on the Wednesday by the same people who now reject (S2), and asked how either view accommodates that: on Dev's, the counterfactual's truth is fixed by objective similarity, so it was already false on the Wednesday, when it was rationally accepted; on Marta's, the counterfactual is supposed to be the truth-conditional member of the pair, so its acceptability should not have drifted with the group's information in the way (S1)'s did. Dev said a counterfactual can be false and still properly asserted, since we are routinely ignorant of the facts that fix similarity. Marta said this merely showed that the counterfactual's assertibility conditions are also probabilistic, which was her general point about an evidential layer, and had no bearing on whether indicatives have truth conditions. The group moved on.

Please go through the exchange and tell me who is right at each step, not merely who wins overall. Rate each of the ten moves on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two justifying each rating. Then answer these questions directly: does the collapse argument of moves 2 and 4 succeed against the view in move 1, and if it fails, at which premise exactly does it fail, and does Dev's lottery reply in move 3 do the work he needs it to do even if a better reply was available to him? Does Marta's embedding challenge in move 6 land, and is Dev's answer in move 7 about the public, negotiable parameter adequate to it? On (S4), is Dev right in move 9 that the difficulty is symmetric, and does Marta's move 10 identify a real difference between the views or only a relabeling — and does Dev's charge of verbalness in move 7 survive the answer? Finally, say what Pia's datum does to each view, whether either dismissal of it works, and, if the data the group agreed on favor some third position that neither of them articulated, describe it and state what it would cost to adopt.
