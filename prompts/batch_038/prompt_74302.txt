**No Term: Social Sanction and the Missing Discharge**

**§1.** Legal punishment, whatever else is wrong with it, has a shape. It begins, it has a length, and it ends. Legal systems also build explicit machinery for ending the after-effects of the sentence itself: spent convictions, expungement, certificates of rehabilitation, limitations periods. The function of that machinery is not mercy. It's finality. It converts an open-ended liability into a bounded one, and it tells third parties, in a way they're expected to honor, that the account is closed.

Informal social sanction has no counterpart. No body convenes, no charge is framed, no term is set, and no one with standing ever announces that the matter is settled. What we loosely call cancellation is not a sentence. It is closer to a lien that nobody is empowered to release.

**§2.** Almost all criticism of social sanction is proportionality criticism: the punishment exceeds the offence. But that framing presupposes a punishment with a magnitude that could in principle be right-sized. A sanction that never terminates doesn't have a magnitude. Its total cost is the integral of a harm over an unbounded interval, and the integrand can spike again arbitrarily far in the future at the initiative of anyone with a motive and an archive. So the complaint isn't that the sanction is too heavy for the wrong. It's that the sanction isn't the sort of thing that could be correctly weighted at all. A proportionality theorist needs a sentence to weigh, and there isn't one.

Note the asymmetry. A ten-year sentence is severe but bounded and knowable in advance. An unbounded sanction of indeterminate magnitude is worse for the sanctioned party in a respect that has nothing to do with how much they suffer in any given year.

**§3.** Bentham counted the moral sanction — pain dispensed by the displeasure of one's fellows, as distinct from what law dispenses — among the four sources of pain and pleasure governing conduct, and he was clear-eyed that it was powerful and erratic. My claim is narrower than a complaint about erraticness. Erraticness is a complaint about variance. What the moral sanction lacks is a terminal condition. Even a perfectly predictable moral sanction would still be one you could never finish serving, because nothing in its structure specifies what finishing would consist in. Predictability settles the expected cost; it doesn't settle the duration.

**§4.** Bankruptcy discharge is a better model here than criminal punishment, because it concerns debts rather than desert. A discharge is a legal act that (a) issues from an identifiable adjudicator; (b) gathers an estate to be distributed; (c) excludes certain categories of debt that survive it; (d) imposes a stay barring further collection on discharged debts; (e) is conditioned on the debtor's cooperation and good faith; and (f) is recorded publicly so third parties are on notice.

Run social sanction down that list:

(a) No adjudicator. The crowd is not a body and cannot bind non-members. An apology accepted by the people you wronged does not bind the people who were never wronged but disapprove of you anyway.
(b) No estate. There's nothing you can tender that is fungible across audiences. Money, apology, restitution and years of unobtrusive good behaviour each convert only in some jurisdictions of opinion and are worthless in others.
(c) No list of non-dischargeable debts — and this is the interesting failure. In bankruptcy, the list of what cannot be shed is largely what makes discharge tolerable. Social sanction runs the list backwards: because nothing is formally dischargeable, everything is functionally non-dischargeable.
(d) No stay. A fresh pile-on is not double jeopardy, since there was never a first jeopardy that terminated.
(e) No credit for compliance. Cooperation cannot be certified to a court that doesn't exist.
(f) Retention as the default, where law's default for a spent conviction is suppression. This is the one place the analogy points at a real institution: de-indexing and right-to-be-forgotten regimes are the closest existing thing to a social discharge, and they're largely rejected on speech grounds in the US. So the fix can't be borrowed from there.

**§5.** Here's the move I think is most promising and least developed. The criminal model aims at restoration — getting people to think well of you again. That's hopeless and it's also a category error, since you cannot be entitled to anyone's beliefs. The bankruptcy model aims at something weaker and more tractable: discharge doesn't change what any creditor believes about the debtor. It removes their standing to enforce. A creditor may think you're a deadbeat forever. What they lose is the garnishment.

So the reformist target shouldn't be belief, it should be standing. What we'd want is a norm under which, once a settlement has been reached with the parties actually wronged, renewed public sanction of the same conduct is itself sanctionable — a fresh wrong committed by the collector rather than the debtor. That also recasts what apology-and-atonement rituals are for. They aren't persuasion attempts. They're attempts to generate a release that third parties are expected to honour.

**§6.** Objections I can't fully answer.

1. People do move on; attention decays. My reply: decay is a stay anyone can lift, not a release. The difference is whether you hold an entitlement or merely luck. I think the reply holds but it may be carrying more weight than it can bear.
2. Some wrongs should never be dischargeable. Conceded — but that's an argument for a taxonomy, and the absence of a taxonomy is precisely my complaint.
3. The strongest one. Informal sanction is the leverage of people with no access to formal institutions, and discharge machinery has historically been a tool of the powerful: bankruptcy discharge emerged out of creditor order, and expungement is administered by the same state that over-polices. Building release mechanisms risks giving the well-connected a way to make their wrongs vanish while the badly-connected still face permanent ledgers. My reply is that release authority should sit with intermediary bodies — unions, professional associations, employers, schools, platforms — each able to issue something binding inside its own jurisdiction. This is soft. Jurisdictions leak. A release binding on my employer binds nobody with a screenshot.
4. Standing-removal is recursive. Who sanctions the re-collectors? If the answer is the same informal machinery, I've assumed what I need.
