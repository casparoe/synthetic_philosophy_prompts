I retired four years ago after thirty-six years in crop physiology — chiefly cereal phenology and the water relations of wheat under terminal drought — and have taken up philosophy rather in the manner that other men take up gardening, though with considerably more argument. For some eighteen months I have belonged to a reading group in a small cathedral city whose members comprise a retired archaeobotanist, a former district medical officer, a solicitor with an interest in political theory and myself. Our present subject is the transition to agriculture, and we have arrived at an impasse that I now find more interesting than the subject that produced it. One half of the table maintains that farming was a catastrophe: they point to the decline in stature, the rise in enamel hypoplasia, dental caries, spinal and joint pathology visible in post-Neolithic skeletal series; to the replacement of a short and varied working day by a long and monotonous one; to the epidemic load that follows from sedentism, stored grain and commensal animals; and to the near-simultaneous appearance of heritable inequality once surplus can be accumulated and defended. The other half regards this as pastoral romanticism wearing a laboratory coat: the foraging baseline is reconstructed from ethnographic populations driven into marginal refugia and is therefore systematically unrepresentative; the transition was gradual, independently repeated in a dozen centres, and for millennia reversible; inequality and monumental construction are attested among complex hunter-gatherers long before cultivation; and only the food production that followed could have carried the cumulative cultural ratchet from which every comfort the first half now enjoys was manufactured. Both sides are numerate, both cite evidence, and in eighteen months not one point has been conceded.

My working hypothesis — held loosely, and I should be glad to see it demolished — is that we are not disagreeing about the evidence at all, but about an aggregation rule nobody has stated. Last winter I built a modest spreadsheet to test this: per-capita available energy and its variance for a modelled foraging band and a modelled farming village, with a parameter for how the farming population responds to its own success. Whether the second arrangement came out better or worse depended entirely on whether I took the mean, the median, or a variance-penalised measure, and it flipped again the moment I allowed the farming population to grow, since a larger population at somewhat lower average condition may or may not be preferable to a smaller one in better condition. That last point I suspect is where the real bedrock lies, and it seems to me to be a question in population ethics rather than in archaeology. I would like you to consider whether that diagnosis is right, or whether the dispute is better described in some other way: as a genuinely empirical disagreement that remains open only because of taphonomic bias and small samples; as a disguised and possibly insoluble normative question about the value of additional lives; as an ill-posed question, since the word "mistake" seems to require an agent who could have chosen otherwise whereas the process resembles a ratchet with no decision point anywhere in it; or as two distinct questions — was this bad for the people who lived through it, and was it bad that it happened — prosecuted under a single headline.

What I want from you, then, is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on the merits, and it should be argued from what the two sides in this debate actually say, not from a general history of the Neolithic. Please identify which of the candidate accounts above does most of the explanatory work, whether more than one is operative at different stages of the argument, and what the residue is that no amount of new data would touch. Then tell me what, given that diagnosis, would count as progress: a stipulated welfare measure that both sides could accept as a target of dispute; a formal model of the trap whose parameters can be estimated from the record; a reframing of the question; or some conceptual distinction I have not thought of. If your honest conclusion is that no progress should be expected, I should like the argument for that, and an account of what the group ought to do with its remaining evenings. I ask partly because I suspect the same structure recurs wherever a technological change is retrospectively evaluated — one meets it in disputes about automation and about medical enhancement — so a clean diagnosis here would be of more general use than it appears. I should be grateful if the reply were written in British English, ran to something in the region of a thousand words, and confined itself to argument rather than to a reading list, which would be of no use to me.
