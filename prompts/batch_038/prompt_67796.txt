For the last four years I've been organizing in a large hospital system, and this spring I moved onto staff, where part of my job is running "know your rights" sessions for units that keep asking whether they would ever actually be able to strike. In preparing those sessions I had to sit down with our staff attorney and work through two features of federal private-sector labor law, and the more carefully she described them, the less like legal technicalities they felt and the more like a settled argument about what a strike is. The first: an employer may not discharge workers for striking, but it may hire permanent replacements for them, and an economic striker who has been replaced is entitled to be recalled only if and when a vacancy later opens up; a striker whose work stoppage is instead a protest against the employer's own unfair labor practices is entitled to get her job back on making an unconditional offer, even against replacements already hired. That asymmetry descends from a Supreme Court decision of 1938 and is still the operative rule. The second: the same statute protects picketing and other pressure directed at your own employer, but forbids a union from bringing secondary pressure to bear on firms not party to the dispute — the linen supplier, the cafeteria contractor, the hospital's lenders — in order to make the primary employer settle.

I want you to treat these two rules as a practice and tell me what philosophical commitments they presuppose. On the first: what theory of the strike makes the asymmetry intelligible rather than arbitrary? Is the protected case protected because the stoppage is a protest against a wrong, so that the remedy is restoration, while the unprotected case is just a bid for more money and so is really a resignation with a hope attached? Does the doctrine presuppose that a job is something an employer confers and may withdraw, so that "you may not be punished for striking" and "you may lose your job for striking" are not in tension at all? Does it presuppose that the state's business is to remedy wrongdoing and not to balance bargaining power — or, conversely, that it does aim to balance power but only where power has already been abused? On the second: what conception of the firm and of a labor dispute does the neutrality rule require — that a workplace conflict is a discrete bilateral event between two parties and that everyone upstream and downstream is a bystander entitled to be insulated from it? Which rival views does each rule quietly rule out: the idea that a strike is the withdrawal of the cooperation on which the enterprise depends and therefore leaves the strikers with a claim on the job they vacated; the idea that an employer embedded in a supply chain has already drawn its suppliers and customers into the dispute; the idea that the state should stay neutral about the outcome of a power contest rather than channel it into particular forms?

Please distinguish carefully between what these rules strictly presuppose and what they merely sit comfortably with, and flag the places where the practice is underdetermined — where two or three incompatible theories would license exactly the same doctrine, so that pointing at the rule settles nothing. Also tell me whether the two rules are consistent with one another and with the rest of the regime they live in: the duty to bargain in good faith, majority-based exclusive representation, and the union's obligation to represent members who voted against it. Then say what would actually have to change in doctrine and in bargaining practice under one clearly stated rival view — for instance, if striking suspended the contract rather than putting the striker's job at the employer's disposal, or if the relevant unit of analysis were the supply chain rather than the single establishment. Finally, since I'll be arguing about this with people who know the case law better than I do: tell me what would change your mind. Which consideration, counterexample, or alternative reconstruction of the same rules would lead you to a different verdict, and where in the above is your reasoning most likely to break down?
