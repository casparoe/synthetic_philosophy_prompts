I left school at sixteen and have done all my philosophy reading on my own, in the evenings, for about twenty years. A friend who did take the subject at university has offered to spend an evening pulling apart whatever I have managed to convince myself of, and he has asked me to send him my position in writing first so that he is not arguing against a version of it I never held. I would rather be refuted precisely than flattered, so I am looking for an audit rather than an introduction to the topic. My subject is the two different things we do when we say "if".

Here is what I believe, as carefully as I can put it.

1. When someone says "if it is raining, the pavement is wet", the strength to which they are entitled to that is fixed by one thing alone: the strength of their belief that the pavement is wet, on the supposition that it is raining. That is the whole rule for indicative conditionals, as I understand them — my degree of acceptance is my conditional degree of belief, and nothing else enters into it.

2. I nevertheless think such a conditional is the kind of thing that can be true or false. When I assert one I am not reporting on my own state of mind or placing a wager; I am saying something about the weather and the pavement, and it is either right or wrong.

3. I take the rule in (1) to be stable under new information. If I learn something further — that it is Tuesday, that the pavement is covered over — my acceptance of "if it is raining, the pavement is wet" should simply track my new degree of belief in wetness given rain. I do not think there is one class of cases in which the rule holds and another class in which some different measure quietly takes its place.

4. I think "if Oswald did not shoot Kennedy, then somebody else did" and "if Oswald had not shot Kennedy, somebody else would have" are the same conditional in two different coats. The first is what you say while you are unsure what happened; the second is what you say once you are sure. The word "if" is not doing two jobs here — the tense and the mood are doing all the work.

5. For the second sort, where we already know how things went, I think what settles the truth is which way events would most plausibly have run had the antecedent held. I picture a range of ways the world might otherwise have been, some of them nearer to the actual one than others in the respects that matter, and I think the conditional is made true or false at the nearest ones in which the antecedent holds. I admit I cannot say much about how nearness is to be measured.

6. I reject the thin reading on which "if A then B" comes to the same as "either not-A or B". My reason is that this makes every conditional with an improbable antecedent acceptable no matter what: I am very unlikely to strike this match, so on that reading I am licensed to say "if I strike it, it will light" however damp it is. But I have noticed that my own rule has something of the same shape, in that the acceptability of a conditional is left untouched by how improbable its antecedent is, and I cannot tell whether that is a fatal resemblance or no resemblance at all.

So: which of these genuinely pull against one another, and which only appear to? Please rank the real conflicts by severity rather than treating them all as equally grave, and for each one say what I would have to surrender to remove it and what I would lose by that surrender. Then tell me which single revision would repair the most. Where something is only apparently in conflict — I suspect (6), though I may be wrong about that — say so plainly and explain why it dissolves, since I have no reliable way of distinguishing the two situations myself. Please write your answer in British English.
