**The regress as an accounting problem**

1. Standard set-up. You ask me why I believe p, I cite q. Why q? I cite r. The classical picture says there are only three ways this can go: the chain stops at something that needs no further reason, it loops back on itself, or it goes on forever. Agrippa's trilemma says all three are bad — stopping points are arbitrary, loops beg the question, and infinite chains can't be got through by a finite mind. So you get scepticism or a bad option.

2. My claim is that the three options aren't really distinguished by the *shape* they give the chain of reasons. They're distinguished by the *cost model* they attach to believing. Let me set up the term I want to use. Call the **dialectical cost** of a belief the work an agent (or a community, or an idealised evaluator) has to do to keep that belief in good standing once it's been challenged. Offering a reason discharges some of that cost, but it also incurs more, because the reason offered is itself challengeable. Justification on this picture is never a stock, only a flow.

3. Read the three positions that way. Foundationalism says some beliefs carry zero cost: they can be held without ever discharging a challenge, and they transmit standing to everything else. The obvious complaint — the one Sellars pressed against the given — is that a zero-cost item which nevertheless does all the transmitting looks like getting warrant for nothing. Coherentism says cost is amortised across the web: no belief is expensive on its own, but the system pays a large fixed cost in maintenance, and the amortisation is only legitimate if the members aren't all simultaneously borrowing from each other. Infinitism says the cost is never discharged, only rolled over: each reason is a refinancing of the previous one. Klein's answer to the finite-mind objection — a reason need only be propositionally available to the subject, not actually believed — comes out on my reading as the claim that debt can be rolled indefinitely without the borrower holding the cash.

4. So the live question is what makes rollover legitimate, and I think the answer is the discount rate. If the value of answering the n-th challenge decays geometrically, then the total outstanding cost is a convergent series, and an infinite chain isn't an infinite debt but a bounded one. Call this **dialectical discounting**: the marginal reason is worth less than the last, because the challenges it answers are increasingly remote from the practice that generated the belief in the first place. Then the disagreement between foundationalism and infinitism is not about whether chains terminate at all. It's about the rate. The foundationalist is someone who thinks the series diverges unless it terminates; the infinitist is someone who thinks it converges without terminating. Coherentism is the claim that it isn't a series but a system of simultaneous equations, where the terms are interdependent and the total is well-defined only if the system has a solution.

5. Three payoffs I want to claim. (a) It moves the debate from a structural question to a quantitative one that is at least in principle answerable by looking at actual justificatory practice — how fast does the force of a challenge decay as you walk away from the belief? — and it allows that the rate differs by subject matter, so infinitism could be right about mathematics and foundationalism right about perception without either being a relabelling of a domain-relative hunch. (b) It explains why the finite-mind objection feels compelling to some people and not others: it silently assumes the agent must actually hold every reason, which is only needed if the discount rate is zero. (c) It takes some of the sting out of the arbitrariness charge against foundationalism. A stopping point isn't arbitrary if the challenges it declines to answer are worth less than the cost of answering them. Basic beliefs aren't cost-free, they're cost-justified.

6. Worries I haven't dealt with, stated so you can attack them. First, discounting seems to work only from the standpoint of an evaluator looking at the whole chain, which suggests it's about propositional justification rather than doxastic, and so may change the subject. Second, BonJour's clairvoyant: Norman's belief is produced by a perfectly reliable faculty and he has no reasons for it at all. Is Norman's cost zero, making him a foundationalist case, or is it merely that nobody has challenged him? My framework looks unable to tell those apart, and that difference is the entire point of internalism. Third, a discount rate that's allowed to vary by domain may just be a fancy way of saying "whichever structural view fits the case wins", which would make the whole thing empty.
