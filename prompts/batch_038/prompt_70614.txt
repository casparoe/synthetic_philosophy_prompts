I'm a second-year philosophy graduate student, and our seminar on the origins of meaning spent three weeks on Lewis's account of convention and Skyrms's signaling games before turning, last Thursday, to the biological literature on animal communication. The session went sideways. One of my classmates, defending the view that a Lewisian equilibrium can't yet be genuine communication because nothing stops a sender from lying when producing the signal is free, closed his remarks with the line "a signal that costs nothing means nothing," and then invoked the handicap principle as the fix — apparently honesty has to be purchased, so meaning requires waste. Our instructor let it stand. I left with the strong intuition that the sentence is doing several different jobs at once and that at most one of them would support his conclusion, but I can't get the readings apart cleanly, and I don't want to walk back into that room next week with a vague objection.

So: take that slogan and lay out the precise claims it could be making, as a menu sorted by strength rather than as a single gloss. For each reading, spell out which notion of cost is in play — average cost versus marginal cost, intrinsic wastage versus foregone opportunity versus an extrinsic penalty imposed by receivers or bystanders, cost borne by the sender versus cost imposed on the system — which game structure it presupposes, in particular whether it is meant for senders and receivers with conflicting interests or with aligned ones, and what "means nothing" is being read as: that the signal is unreliable, that the signaling arrangement is evolutionarily or strategically unstable, or that the signal fails to have content at all. Then give me a verdict on each: platitude, substantive and defensible, contentious, or simply false as stated. I especially want the readings that get strained by the obvious cases — signals whose reliability is physically guaranteed by their production rather than bought, so that low-quality individuals cannot fake them no matter what they would pay; conventional signals held honest by punishment or by reputational consequences rather than by anything expensive about the signal itself; honest communication among close relatives, where shared fitness seems to make truth-telling pay even when it is free; and the general point that what a separating arrangement appears to require is a differential in marginal cost across types, which is perfectly compatible with the signal being nearly free for the best signallers, and equally compatible with a ruinously expensive signal that costs everyone the same and therefore separates nobody. Tell me, in particular, whether the Zahavi-style claim that honesty demands genuine waste and the Grafen-style claim that honesty demands differential marginal cost actually come apart, which of the two my classmate was smuggling in, and whether the stronger one is either defensible or needed for anything he wanted to say.

Finally, be explicit about which reading, if any, would license the use the slogan was put to. My suspicion is that none of them does, because in a cooperative Lewisian game lying is not even a temptation for the sender, so the whole handicap apparatus has no job to perform there — but I want that argued rather than asserted, and I want to know whether there is a defensible core he could retreat to. One candidate I've half-formed and would like pressure-tested: the real worry about Lewisian games is not that free signals invite deception but that many equally good equilibria are available, and it is unclear what fixes which signal ends up meaning which state; if cost could be recruited to break that tie, would that be a cost claim at all, or a salience claim wearing a costume? Say what you think my classmate most likely meant, given how he used the line, and how I might reply in a few sentences without simply trading slogans back at him. Please don't hand me a reading list or citations — I want the analysis, the rankings, and the verdicts, in prose I could actually use.
