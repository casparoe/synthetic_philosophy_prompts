Been reading the rationalist blogs for something like fifteen years now — Overcoming Bias back in the day, then LessWrong, then whatever the current successor venue is this month — and I've finally tried to write down what I actually believe about minds and understanding, mostly in the Dennettian competence-without-comprehension vein. Before I post it somewhere and get torn apart, I want a consistency check.

Play the role of a professional philosopher of mind / cognitive scientist who has actually thought hard about Darwin's and Turing's strange inversions of reasoning rather than just name-dropping them at parties. Think carefully about this one. Before you write the audit, brainstorm out loud for a bit — sketch the dependency structure between my commitments, which ones are load-bearing for which others, where the same word is doing two different jobs. Then do the audit. I can't look anything up, so no reading list, no citations, no suggested papers — just your own reasoning.

Here's the package, numbered so you can point at things:

1. The inversion is real and general. Comprehension gets built out of sheer competence, bottom-up, by processes that don't understand the thing they're building. Evolution did it, and the Turing move showed you could do it with symbol-shuffling too. So I'm a functionalist: what understanding is, is a certain kind of organization, not a special ingredient you have to add.

2. Understanding isn't a on/off thing. It's graded, and it comes in domains — a thing can genuinely understand tides and completely fail to understand people.

3. But I do think there's a real difference between a system that merely emits the right behavior and one that genuinely understands, and I think the difference is whether the system tracks its own reasons. To understand X you have to be able to say why you did or believed what you did about X, and to say it correctly, not just plausibly. Call this the reason-report requirement. I know that sounds introspection-y, but I mean it as a capacity, not as a private glow.

4. Case verdict: a pure search-and-evaluate chess engine that outplays every human does not understand chess. It can't tell you why it played what it played. I feel really solid about this one, and I've argued it a lot online.

5. Case verdict: an octopus that opens a puzzle box it's never seen before, in a lab, with no tradition to copy from, does understand the box. It obviously can't report anything, but I don't think that counts against it, because there's no language in the loop on either side — nobody's asking and nothing's answering.

6. Humans confabulate constantly. People will confidently tell you why they picked something when the actual cause was something else entirely, and that doesn't make them non-understanders. If anything the confabulating is evidence the reason-tracking machinery is running — it's building a story, it just sometimes builds the wrong one.

7. When behavior and self-report disagree, I trust behavior. Self-report is private and basically impossible to check and trivial to fake; behavior is public and repeatable. Methodologically I want a theory of mind an ethologist could apply to an animal she'll never be able to interview.

8. On LLMs: I think a big model probably does genuinely understand a fair amount of what it talks about — including chess, from nothing but game scores — even though essentially everything it says about its own internal reasoning is confabulation, and it says so with total confidence.

9. A thought experiment I made up myself (it's not from anywhere, don't go looking for it, and don't tell me it's a famous one): a billion tiny reflex agents, each one a lookup table from a local pattern to a small motor twitch, none of them modelling anything, and no central module you could point to and say "that's the one that knows." Wired together, the aggregate reliably herds cattle, opens the right gates, walks them to market, negotiates a passable deal, and answers typed questions about cattle in fluent English. My intuition is that the whole understands cattle and no part of it does, which I take to be a nice confirmation of 1 and 2. But here's the thing I can't get past: the aggregate can answer "why did you open that gate," and that answer comes out of exactly the same reflex soup as the gate-opening did. So I have no idea whether the answer is tracking the reasons or is just the soup re-running and narrating.
