I co-teach a grad seminar with an economic historian — call her M — on contingency, the whole for-want-of-a-nail business. We invented a standing exercise: each week we hand the room a counterfactual about a made-up region (made up on purpose, so nobody can win by already knowing the historiography), and M gives her ruling cold, before anyone's allowed to argue. Five years of these, so her rulings are basically a dataset at this point.

Her vocabulary is fixed. She says "same road," "different road, same town," or "different town."

1. The admiral who wins the sea battle that lets an invader land dies of a fever two years earlier → same road. The landing happens inside a decade, same coast, same people.
2. The monk who owns the only copy of a certain treatise burns it for warmth one winter → different town. The argument isn't made again for four centries, and nothing else in the record picks it up.
3. The queen bears a son → different road, same town. Different dynasty, identical tax machinery, identical wars.
4. A late frost ruins the harvest three years before the revolt → same road. Revolt inside five years; only the trigger changes.
5. The surveyor's chain runs short and the boundary falls along the next ridge → different town. The line stays put forever, and two hundred years on the two sides of the valley have different official languages.
6. The plague never arrives → same road. Wages rise anyway, fifty years later, by another route.

Next week's case is the one I can't call. A standards conference in the region, 1870s, two telegraph encoding schemes on the table, no third in the wings. As far as anyone in the room can tell they're equivalent for the traffic the region will actually have; nobody can say which would diffuse faster, and nobody tries. The chair — a hobbyist with no stake — breaks a tied vote for the one that's cheaper to train operators on. Within fifteen years every line runs it and the rival's users are absorbed. Forty years on, the region has one integrated market and its neighbour doesn't, and there's a live argument in my seminar about how much of that sits on the chair's vote. The ruling I want from her is on that: same single market, same lead over the neighbour, or not.

My hunch, which I'd like you to try to break: she's running a redundancy count — how many independent routes there are to the outcome — and "different town" only comes out when there's exactly one. 2 and 6 fit that beautifully. But 5 is the problem, and 3 is the problem in the other direction. In 5 there were a dozen equally defensible ridgelines, so the type *a boundary gets drawn hereabouts* is about as redundantly caused as anything could be, and she still says the token difference is permanent; in 3 the swap is just as arbitrary and she says it washes out. So whatever she's tracking, it isn't merely the number of routes. Maybe it's whether the difference amplifies and freezes, maybe it's whether the alternatives are interchangeable with respect to what the downstream processes are actually sensitive to — I can't tell which, and on the telegraph case I'm not sure its the same question.

There's also a physics student in the seminar who says the whole exercise is a shrug, because nearby trajectories in a system like that separate exponentially, so "would it have happened anyway" has no determinate answer. M's reply every single time: that's not a ruling, that's a shrug. I'm not convinced she earns that briskness. I'd like to know whether her rulings are covertly distribution-level claims — the shape of what could happen is stable even when the token isn't — and if so, which of the six gives that away.

So: what does she rule on the telegraph case, in her own vocabulary, with the one-line reason she'd give? Lay out the candidate rules that fit all six, say what each predicts here, and point out which of the six is already doing discriminating work that I've missed. Tell me where my redundancy hunch is wrong, or defend it if it isn't. Then design one more scenario — same invented region, cheap to pose, a paragraph — whose ruling would separate your top two candidates, and say what each of them predicts for it. And be blunt at the end: if the telegraph case really is underdetermined by her record, tell me what principle she'd be committing herself to under each possible ruling, because I'd rather walk into the room with that than pretend this is a lookup.
