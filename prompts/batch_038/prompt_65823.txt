I'm a novelist — literary historical fiction, mid-list, one book every four years if the gods are kind. I'm three years into a novel set in the decades of Britain that later grew the Arthur material, and my problem isn't the usual one (was he real, was he Artorius Castus, were there Sarmatian cavalry, all that) — it's that I keep having to decide, detail by detail, roughly two hundred times a draft, how much warrant a given piece of a legend actually carries, so I know whether I can put it in the narration as something that happened, whether it has to live only in a character's mouth as something believed, or whether I'm not allowed to touch it at all. What I want is a procedure I can run on one detail in ten minutes with a coffee going cold next to me, not an essay about the state of the question. No reading list, please — I genuinely can't chase one and I want the method rather than the footnotes; naming the actual early texts where it matters is fine and helpful.

What I think it needs to sort on is how many hands a detail has passed through and what kind of thing tends to survive the passing, so build in both axes, because I fumble them constantly. Transmission: contemporary record / later writer with plausible access to something earlier / later writer who is visibly making things up / silence. And kind of detail: my understanding is that place names, objects, topography, the shape of a landscape survive oral transmission in ways that motives, speeches, causes and personal character simply don't — there's a reason people get excited about a piece of armour in epic that matches an artefact type far older than the poem, and a reason that excitement is usually overdone. It also has to catch the trap I fall into most, which is treating a poem as evidence for the world it describes rather than for the world that composed it. Give me named tiers, what each tier licenses me to do in prose, what to do when two layers flatly contradict each other, and what to do with pure silence, which is most of my material and which I dont have a good instinct for yet. Mark loudly the points where the procedure is really just my taste wearing a lab coat and I have to decide as a novelist rather then as a historian. One extra thing: I've been spooked by how easily this genre of book gets cited by cranks, so the procedure should flag when a detail is one that pseudohistory has already colonised, and tell me what an author's note can honestly claim without overclaiming and without pretending the novel is a documentary.

Then run it on three cases and don't soften the third. One: a hero in the old northern British praise poetry — the Gododdin — gets measured against Arthur in passing, in a stanza that may or may not be an interpolation, in a poem dated anywhere from the seventh century to the eleventh and surviving in a thirteenth-century manuscript. What tier is that, and what can I actually do with it on the page? Two: a Hittite treaty with the ruler of a western Anatolian place-name that looks a lot like Troy, whose own name looks suspiciously like the second name the epic gives its prince. I want the procedure to spell out exactly why a name-match is not an event-match, and then say what, if anything, that document licenses a novelist to write. Three: the whole romance apparatus — Round Table, Lancelot, Grail, sword in the stone. I know roughly what the answer is going to be and I want it said bluntly, because I have sixty-odd pages already built on treating one of those as recovered tradition instead of as a late invention, and if the procedure says I have to dismantle them I'd much rather hear it this month than in a year. Finally: tell me how confident you are in the tiers themselves, which single step in the procedure you'd bet least on, and where you suspect you're importing a scholarly consensus that's really about Arthur specifically and stretching it over legends in general. I want the self-doubt attached to the deliverable, not buried in it.
