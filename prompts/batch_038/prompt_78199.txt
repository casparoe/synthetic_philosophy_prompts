Last night a friend and I argued until nearly two in the morning about how governments appraise long-run climate policy, and I have come away suspecting that we were talking past each other. I would like a careful analysis of what the practice in question actually commits us to, so that I can tell which of us was making a philosophical point and which was merely making a technical one.

The practice. Britain's central guidance on project appraisal — the Treasury's Green Book — tells departments not to discount the far future at a single constant rate. As I understand it, analysts are directed to use a rate of roughly 3.5 percent per year for effects falling in the first thirty years, then to step that rate down as the horizon lengthens, with a floor somewhere near 1 percent for effects more than three centuries out. The precise figures are beside the point for what I want from you; the shape is what matters. Two features are essential: the further out a cost or benefit falls, the lower the annual rate applied to it, and the rate applied to a given future year differs depending on the year in which the appraisal is performed. The same schedule governs flood defenses, nuclear waste management, and carbon abatement — programs whose benefits land mostly in the centuries where the low rates apply. The stated justification is epistemic rather than ethical: because we cannot know how fast the economy will grow over three hundred years, a declining schedule is said to be the appropriate certainty-equivalent way to discount under that uncertainty.

My friend's position was that this is a disguised ethical compromise — a split-the-difference between market-anchored rates and a near-zero pure rate of time preference, smuggled into a technical annex so that nobody has to defend it; and further, that the whole apparatus presupposes continued growth, since the low rates are only reached by averaging over a distribution of growth paths, which is exactly what a degrowth policy denies. My position was that the schedule is a neutral response to genuine uncertainty, that it presupposes nothing about whether the future will be richer, and that accusing it of concealing an ethical choice confuses a correction for risk with a value judgment.

Please write an answer that does the following. Reconstruct the implicit theory of intergenerational justice and value that the declining schedule presupposes, stating explicitly what it assumes about the moral standing of future persons, about whether environmental and market goods are commensurable in money, and about whether future people will be better off. Then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits well with: is it licensed equally by a near-zero pure rate of time preference, by uncertainty about long-run growth, and by a deliberate compromise between descriptive and prescriptive rates, or are some of those mutually exclusive? Assess the internal coherence of the practice, in particular the time-inconsistency of applying different rates to the same future year depending on the appraisal date, and the tension between granting future welfare equal standing while discounting future consumption. Say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — where several incompatible theories would license it identically. Finally, tell me what would have to change in appraisal practice, and in which projects get funded, if the schedule were replaced by a single very low constant rate or by a single market-anchored constant rate, and what each replacement would presuppose that the schedule does not. Conclude by adjudicating my friend's growth objection: does a declining schedule genuinely avoid assuming growth, or does it assume it in the distribution over growth paths?

You do not need to cite sources or supply a reading list; I am interested in the argument itself. Please write in American English.
