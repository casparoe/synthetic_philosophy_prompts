I spent thirty-four years doing enzyme kinetics on cold-water marine animals — mostly invertebrates and a few fish — and I retired three years ago, at which point I discovered that I actually like reading philosophy a lot more than I ever expected to. My wife finds this hilarious. I've also, somewhat against my better judgement, ended up on the scientific advisory board of a smallish curated protein-function database (two institutes fund most of it; we meet by video four times a year and argue about semantics). Everyone else on the board is a bioinformatician or a structural biologist, and I'm the only one who's read any of the philosophy of biology, which has made me, by default, the person who writes up our policy notes. I've been working through the old dispute about what a biological function even is — the selected-effect / etiological line (Wright and the people who followed him) versus the causal-role line (Cummins and his followers), plus the modern "biofunctions" literature that tries to broker a peace — and I'll be honest, the etiological view struck me as absurd when I first hit it, because at the bench "what's the function of this protein" always meant "what does it do in this tissue right now," full stop. Now I'm less sure. Anyway.

Here's the mess I need help with. We have a pending annotation. In a squid relative, the lens expresses truly enormous amounts of a protein that is, to a first approximation, a bog-standard glutathione S-transferase — a detox enzyme — at something like forty times its level anywhere else in the animal. The evidence for a *refractive* role is good and getting better: the purified protein is extremely soluble and doesn't scatter light, it measurably raises the refractive index of a reconstituted lens-like solution, the transcriptome work shows the upregulation is lens-restricted, and there's a naturally occuring allele that makes the protein less soluble which tracks with lens clouding in wild-caught animals. So it does something, in the tissue, and it does it well. The problem is the history. Comparative work suggests the lens over-expression is young — the closest relatives express the same gene at low levels in the lens with no visual specialisation worth mentioning, the duplication-and-recruitment looks like it happened after this lineage split, and nobody can find a selection signature on the regulatory changes that drove it up. The ancestral job, detoxification, is unambiguously old and shared. So on a causal-role reading we should absolutely annotate "contributes to lens transparency / refractive index" as a function of this protein; on a strict selected-effect reading we arguably can't yet, because there's no evidence anything was selected for that role. One of my curators wants to annotate it, one wants to hold it pending phylogenetic work that may never arrive, and a third wants to invent a new annotation flag meaning "causally contributes, history unknown," which half the board hates because it will leak into everything. And the stakes aren't academic: our annotations feed a downstream pipeline that helps people call variants, so if we bless the refractive role as a function, a variant that wrecks solubility while leaving the detox chemistry untouched starts getting flagged as damaging, and if we don't, we're telling users this protein does one thing in a tissue where it plainly does two.

So: could you draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday's meeting? Roughly eight hundred words, and it needs to land somewhere concrete — a recommendation on the pending annotation (annotate, don't, or annotate with some specific qualification) *and* a general decision rule the curators can actually apply to the next ten cases without phoning me, including the case where the current contribution is real but the selection history is genuinely unknowable, which for non-model organisms is most of them. I want the memo to be honest about which account of function the rule commits us to and to defend that choice in terms a room full of people who don't care about philosophy will accept — consequences for users, consistency with the rest of the database, how expensive the evidence is to gather — rather than by waving at Cummins and Wright. And it has to give the strongest case against whatever you recommend a real paragraph, not a strawman; my own worry is that any history-gated rule is unworkably expensive and quietly biases the database toward the dozen heavily studied species where someone's done the comparative work. Please open with the bottom line — the recommendation itself, in the first sentence — and then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my question back to me, no preamble-y throat clearing about how interesting the issue is.
