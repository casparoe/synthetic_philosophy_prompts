Been arguing with my mate Rob until about two in the morning and I've come away needing a proper prep sheet rather than another row. Rob does advocacy for a basic income charity, which is relevant because next Thursday I'm giving a thirty-minute paper at a policy institute workshop in London to a room of roughly twenty people: mostly labour economists who've costed a universal payment at some point, two more from Rob's end of the world (one of them ex-DWP), a sociologist of work who sits broadly on the anti-work left, and one analytic philosopher who writes on the ethics of automation and is, as far as I can tell, a prioritarian about everything. Nobody in the room is on my side and I'd rather find out what that means in advance. Rob got me with one thing I couldn't answer, something about my proposal creating a second-class labour market that undercuts the bargaining power of the people already doing that work, and I still don't know whether that's actually fatal or just the standard line dressed up to sound fatal.

My thesis, compressed: the central harm of automation isn't lost income, it's lost standing, and standing is a function of being needed by identifiable others rather than by society in general, which is precisely why cash doesn't repair it. So instead of a universal payment I'm defending a publicly funded programme of genuinely needed but low-productivity work — maintenance, care, archives, restoration, land and waterway upkeep. There are three moves the objections have to get past. First, an asymmetry: two people on the same household income, one made redundant and one who left to look after a parent, don't fare the same, and on my account the gap tracks whether anyone in particular depends on you rather than the money. Second, an identifiability condition: the good in question is positional in a sense I try to spell out, it can be held but not bought or transferred, which is why a payment from the Exchequer doesn't count as being needed by anyone. Third, an anti-perfectionist hedge, since I don't want to be accused of moralising the wage: I'm not claiming work is intrinsically valuable or that leisure corrupts, only that there's one specific social good that employment currently happens to monopolise and that transfers leave untouched. I'll spend about five minutes on each and the rest on how the programme would actually be structured and paid for.

What I want is the question period, not a defence. Give me the eight or so objections this particular room will raise — not the ones I'd get from a seminar full of luck egalitarians and definitely not the ones I'd get online — ordered by how much damage they'd do, each one phrased the way the person asking it would phrase it, so the economists' in their own register (elasticities, displacement, deadweight, who the marginal worker is, how you'd evaluate it, and the inevitable "isn't this just a jobs guarantee with a story attached"), the sociologist's in hers (moralising the wage relation, why paid dependence counts as standing and unpaid care doesn't, the refusal of work), and the philosopher's in his (whether standing is a good or a status, whether something positional can be distributed at all, and what separates my claim from a recognition-theoretic one I don't want to be saddled with). For each, tell me whether it has real force, what I should concede in the opening sentence, a reply I can actually deliver in two minutes, and where that reply is soft — I'd rather you tell me the argument is a mess in one place than flatter it in all of them. Then give me the single reply that has to land for the whole thing to survive, and the question I'm dreading; I suspect it's the one about what my account implies for the retired, the disabled and the long-term carer, i.e. that I've built a theory in which my own grandmother is second class, but tell me if there's a worse one I haven't thought of. And please write the whole thing as continuous prose, like a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, I know that's a weird way to give a prep sheet but I'll be reading it on the train to Reading and the listicle look makes my eyes glaze over; start each objection with a fresh paragraph, put the objector's own words first, then run your assessment straight on.
