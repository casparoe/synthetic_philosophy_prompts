I'm a senior in an epistemology seminar on the value of knowledge, and our section has split into two camps that have spent six weeks talking past one another. Our professor has asked the seminar to produce in writing what a good-faith adversarial collaboration between the two camps ought to yield, and I have been assigned the drafting. Here is the document I need.

The two camps. Camp A, the dissolvers, accept the swamping argument in its usual form: once a belief is true, the reliability of the process that produced it adds no further value, in the way that a good cup of coffee makes the dependability of the machine that brewed it beside the point. They therefore think the project of locating some extra value in knowledge over mere true belief is misconceived — either knowledge really is no more valuable than true belief, or the question is malformed because it presupposes that knowledge is a composite whose value should be assembled from the values of truth, belief, and a reliable source. Camp B, the knowledge-first camp, hold that knowing is a state in its own right rather than a composite put together out of belief, truth, and something further, and that the swamping argument therefore begs the question at its first step, since it assumes value flows only from parts to whole — which is precisely what is in dispute. On their view, the distinctive value of knowledge shows up in the work that knowing does: as a reason for acting, as a license to assert, and as what makes someone a trustworthy informant.

I contributed two cases to the seminar and I want them built into the document rather than waved away. First, Larissa Redux: Bea and Cal both drive a visitor to Larissa and both arrive. Bea has driven the route for years and can explain the road network; Cal was handed the route by a stranger at a rest stop and has no idea whether the stranger knows anything at all. Now add a stipulation meant to switch off the familiar conditional-probability reply: the visitor never returns to the region, neither driver ever takes that road again, and nothing either learned on the trip can raise the probability of any of their future true beliefs. Second, Bad Tether: Dot drives the same route and arrives, having memorized the sequence of turns correctly and unshakably, but her confident account of why the route works is false — she believes it follows a river that is not there, and she would give bad advice about every possible detour. She has a tether, but it is tethered to something untrue.

Please draft the joint statement. I want exactly five numbered claims that both camps could sign, each one or two sentences and none of them vacuous; at least one should concern what the Meno's own tethering answer does and does not accomplish, at least one should concern the second-order version of the problem — why knowledge should be worth more than reliably produced true belief, not merely more than true belief — and at least one should be something both camps accept about my one-shot stipulation in Larissa Redux.

Then a disagreements table with exactly five rows and exactly these columns: the disagreement in neutral terms; how Camp A states its own side in wording its members would sign; how Camp B states its own side likewise; the case, argument, or test both agree bears on it, with Bad Tether appearing in at least one row; and each side's prediction about that test, recorded in advance. Every row must be a genuine crux, meaning that settling it would move at least one camp, and in the prediction column each camp should also say whether a falsified prediction would oblige it to give ground and, if not, what would. If you conclude that any row is wholly or partly a dispute about words, mark it as such in the row and say in one sentence which part is verbal.

No introduction, no conclusion, and no commentary on the exercise — just the five numbered claims and the table.
