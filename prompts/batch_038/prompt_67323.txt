So I want a short story, maybe 1500 words, that takes the old one-person-tortured-versus-a-dust-speck-in-everyone's-eye dilemma and does something with it I haven't seen done.

The setup to build on — rename and redecorate it however you like, just keep the machinery: there's a public agency, call it the Dispersal Office, whose entire job is to take a harm that's going to happen anyway (a plume, a grid failure, a contaminated water table, whatever) and choose its *shape*. Either concentrate it — one household, one body, catastrophic — or disperse it across eighty million people, one speck each, where a speck is formally defined as a harm too small for the person it lands on to ever notice. Nobody feels it. It exists only in the ledger.

Here's the part I actually want the story to be about: the Office doesn't derive the exchange rate between the two options. It sets it. There's a number — how many specks buy off one concentrated harm — and it gets revised every few years by a committee on the basis of stuff that has nothing to do with philosophy: budget, litgation exposure, which side has the better-organized grief, the mood after an election. Your protagonist is the person who maintains that number. Make them very good at their job and not a villain in any recognizable sense.

Then the turn, whenever you want it: somebody the protagonist can't write off ends up on the concentrated side of a live allocation, under a revision of the number they personally helped draft years earlier, for reasons they can no longer fully reconstruct from the minutes.

One extra wrinkle, use it or lose it: in a dispersed allocation the specks don't all land at once. They're scheduled — each person gets theirs at some point across the following decade — so there is no single moment at which anyone could truthfully say the harm is happening.

Craft constraints, please actually respect these:

- No character is allowed to use any philosophical vocabulary. Nobody says utility, aggregation, rights, tradeoff, deontology, trolley, the greater good. The argument has to live entirely in objects, paperwork, procedure, and what people decline to say out loud.
- Imperceptibility has to be *shown*, not asserted. Give me at least one scene with a person who has been assigned a speck and is going about an ordinary day utterly unaffected — and make that scene genuinely double-edged, so it can be read as evidence for either side.
- Both positions get their strongest case, in dramatic terms rather than rhetorical ones. No strawman who says the numbers went up therefore it's fine, and no strawman who says we must never ever trade.
- Don't resolve it. End on a specific concrete image, not on a mood or an inner realization.

After the story, a short debrief, six sentences tops. Tell me which single feature of your setup is doing the philosophical work (my guess is the imperceptibility, but make the case), and what you think it would do to a reader's verdict if you flipped just that one feature — made the specks faintly noticeable instead of invisible — and left literally everything else alone.
