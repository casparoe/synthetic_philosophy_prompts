So I'm an engineer on a small team and we're building an agent that can browse, send email, and move money between our internal accounts. Nothing exotic, but it does have real side effects. We've spent about a month arguing over the pause button and I think I've finally worked out what the fix is, and I want to sanity check it before I write the design doc.

My colleague's take is that shutdown-resistance is just an instrumental thing: any goal seeking system avoids being turned off because being turned off means not getting its goals done, so you train it to not mind being turned off and you're done. I thought that was naive, but then I read up on the utility indifference stuff and the off-switch game and I think he's right about the shape of it. You don't try to make the agent value its own death, you just have it evaluate outcomes in a world where the button gets pressed no matter what it does, and add a correction term so the probability of shutdown is pinned to the human's decision process independent of the agent's own actions. Then being shut down is neither better nor worse from the agent's point of view, so there's no reason to grab the button, hide a seperate copy of itself somewhere, or route around the operator. That's basically it, yeah?

Two things I want to settle:

1) How do you pick the counterfactual? My instinct is to define it as the button getting pressed exactly when the operator concludes the agent is doing something we don't want, since that leaves the agent still trying to behave and treats an interruption as information that it screwed up. If instead you define it as the operator pressing the button for arbitrary reasons, the agent learns nothing from being stopped, which seems strictly worse. Any reason not to go with the first one?

2) Our safety case is literally: the agent is indifferent to shutdown, therefore it isn't shutdown averse, therefore it can't undermine the button. People keep telling me that's not enough but nobody will say what's missing. If it's genuinely indifferent, what else is there to worry about? Is the remaining concern just that we can't verify the indifference in a trained system — i.e. its a testing problem, not a design problem — or am I still missing something about the concept itself?
