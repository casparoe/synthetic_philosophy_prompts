The draft, condensed but keeping my actual claims:

**1. The complaint.** The standard line against revealed preference is that it's empty by construction: utility is defined as whatever choice reveals, and choice is then explained by utility, so the theory is circular and can never fail. You see this everywhere, in philosophy of econ intro material and in general critiques of economics.

**2. My reading of where the content lives.** The charge mislocates the theory's content. The content isn't in the postulated inner state, it's in the restrictions the axioms put on the choice function across varying menus. The weak axiom, roughly: if x is picked when y is available, y is never picked when x is available. That's a claim about observable behaviour and it can come out false. The generalized version used in applied work allows indirect chains but still forbids a chain of revealed preference from returning to an option that was strictly passed over. So the theory is falsifiable in the ordinary sense. The tautology objection confuses the emptiness of a *definition* with the emptiness of *what gets defined*. Compare the complaint that general intelligence is "just whatever IQ tests measure": deflationary-sounding, but the claim that scores load on a single factor is a real structural constraint that the data could fail to satisfy.

**3. Utility as a decoding scheme.** I want to say a utility function isn't a thing an agent has, it's a code we run on an agent's record: a map from observed choices back to a ranking. Then rationality stops being a binary property of a mind and becomes a description-length property of a behaviour. Define an agent's *decoding cost* as the minimum number of distinct utility functions needed to account for everything in the record. Ordinary folk-psychological verdicts fall out: weakness of will, framing effects, hyperbolic discounting are just high-decoding-cost patterns, i.e. cases where one code won't cover the data and we need several charts. That's why we're happy to say someone "has conflicting preferences" without thinking they have two minds.

**4. A case.** Suppose an agent who always orders the second-cheapest bottle on the list. At Chez X the list runs 12, 20, 45 and they take the 20. At Chez Y the list is just 12 and 20 and they take the 12. Tempting to call this menu-dependence and therefore irrational, but I don't think that follows. One and the same decoder generates both picks, and each pick reveals a preference for the runner-up over the alternatives actually on offer. If you insist on a preference over bottles simpliciter, fine — then say the utility function was defined over wine-and-context pairs all along, which is precisely the flexibility critics accuse the theory of lacking.

**5. Sen.** Sen's objection, as I take it, is that the consistency axioms are unmotivated unless you already assume choice displays preference, and that once you admit commitment and social preference, an agent can be perfectly rational while failing the axioms. My draft response: that just shows the axioms aren't definitions of preference, which supports my reading rather than hurting it, since it puts the content in the structural claim. But I half-suspect this leaves my view too thin to do any normative work, and I don't know whether to bite that bullet or add a premise tying decoding cost to something like predictive success.

**6. Flagged worry.** Does the whole thing collapse into instrumentalism about preference talk — "as if" — and if it does, is that a loss?
