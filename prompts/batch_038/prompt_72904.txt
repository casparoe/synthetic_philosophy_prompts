You are a philosopher of medicine and law with clinical ethics committee experience: someone who reads statutes closely but thinks about them in terms of the theories of agency, well-being, personal identity, and evidence they quietly encode. I want you to work carefully and to begin with a private brainstorming pass — sketch, before you commit to any thesis, the several candidate theories that could license each design feature I describe, and note which features are multiply realizable. Then produce the analysis.

Here is the object of study. Two legal architectures for hastening death at a patient's request coexist in the wealthy democracies, and they differ in their machinery in ways I find philosophically revealing rather than merely political.

Architecture A (the United States model, beginning with Oregon's 1994 ballot initiative, now replicated in a dozen-odd states): eligibility is limited to a competent adult with a terminal illness whose death, in the physician's judgment, will occur within roughly six months. The physician may only write a prescription; the patient must physically ingest the medication themselves, and no one may administer it to them. The patient must make two oral requests separated by a statutory waiting period, plus a written request signed before witnesses, at least one of whom is not a relative, an heir, or an employee of the treating facility. Two physicians — the attending and an independent consultant — must each separately confirm the diagnosis, the prognosis, and the patient's capacity; either may refer the patient for a psychological evaluation. Physicians and institutions may decline to participate. Crucially, an advance directive is not honored: a request made while competent cannot be carried out later, when the patient has lost capacity and can no longer self-administer, and psychiatric suffering unaccompanied by a terminal prognosis is outside the statute entirely.

Architecture B (the Benelux model): the physician may administer the drug as well as prescribe it. There is no requirement of terminal illness or of a six-month horizon; the criterion is suffering the patient experiences as unbearable and without prospect of relief, which in principle includes suffering from psychiatric disorder. Advance directives can carry authority, including in cases of advanced dementia where the patient at the time of death no longer understands, endorses, or even recognizes the decision. Cases are reviewed after the fact by regional committees of peers rather than by prosecutors.

Treat my descriptions as stipulated: do not spend any of your response correcting, updating, or caveatting the statutory details, and do not tell me what you think the current law says. Do not produce citations, footnotes, case names, or a reading list — I have no way to check them and do not want guesses. The deliverable is your reconstruction, argued from the features themselves.

What I want:

1. For each of the following features, state what it presupposes — not what it is justified by in public rhetoric, but what view must be true for the feature to be anything other than arbitrary: (a) the self-administration rule; (b) the six-month prognosis requirement; (c) the diachronic request structure of waiting periods and repeated oral requests; (d) the refusal to honor advance directives; (e) the two-physician confirmation requirement; (f) the witness requirement that excludes relatives and heirs; (g) the exclusion of non-terminal psychiatric suffering; (h) the physician's right of conscientious non-participation; (i) after-the-fact peer review rather than prior authorization or prosecution.

2. Distinguish, feature by feature, what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with. Where you think a feature is philosophically underdetermined — licensable by a doing/allowing distinction, by an intention-based view, by a division-of-labor account of professional roles, or by nothing more than legislative compromise — say so plainly and say which of these readings the rest of the statute best discriminates between.

3. Address the consistency question directly. Architecture A seems to me to pull in two directions: the self-administration rule locates moral significance in who performs the final causal act, whereas the advance-directive rule locates it in the authorship of the decision by a presently competent self. A patient with advanced dementia who validly requested death years ago, and who can no longer lift a glass, is excluded by a rule whose rationale seems to be about authorship; and a patient who wrote nothing but manages to swallow is included. Is this a genuine incoherence, a defensible division of labor between what the state can verify and what it cannot, or evidence that the statute has no single implicit theory at all and is best read as a coalition of incompatible commitments? Argue for one answer.

4. Tell me what the epistemology of the two-physician requirement amounts to. It treats agreement between two separately consulted experts as evidence that the prognosis and the capacity judgment are correct. What assumptions about independence, about the reliability of prognostic judgment, and about shared training does that require, and are they plausible? Then say what a jurisdiction that took the unreliability of prognosis seriously would have to change about the eligibility criterion itself.

5. Finally, run a substitution test. Take one of these rival theories — a view on which unbearable suffering alone, however long it lasts, is the sole criterion; a view on which autonomy extends across a whole life so that a prior self can bind a later one; a view on which all intentional hastening of death is killing but proportionate palliative sedation is not — and specify concretely which of the nine features above would have to be deleted, inverted, or supplemented, and which new institutions (assessment bodies, appeal mechanisms, reporting duties, forms of refusal) that theory would require that neither architecture has.

Write in labeled sections, in prose rather than bullet fragments, and let your claims be specific enough to be arguable. I would rather have three sharply defended theses about what these statutes commit us to than a balanced survey of the assisted-dying debate.
