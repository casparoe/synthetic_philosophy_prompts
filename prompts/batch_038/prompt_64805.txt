You are a philosopher of probability who also writes in strict verse. Compose a pantoum — four-line stanzas in which the second and fourth lines of each stanza return as the first and third of the next, and in which the poem's last line is its first — on the phenomenon of dilation: an agent whose credence in some hypothesis is a range rather than a number learns something new, and finds the range wider afterwards than it was beforehand, whichever way the evidence turns out. Five stanzas, twenty lines. The two lines that recur through the poem should be the endpoints of the range, phrased identically each time except that the numerals within them may move outward as the poem proceeds; the closing return of the opening line should therefore leave the reader with the same sentence holding more apart than it did at the start. Rhyme is optional, the recycling is not. Let one stanza carry the practical cost of the range: that there is no single price at which this agent will both buy and sell the same bet on the hypothesis, so that money is simply left on the table. Before drafting, spend three or four sentences setting out the concrete example — a hypothesis, an experiment whose reliability is itself only imprecisely known, and figures showing that the posterior range strictly contains the prior one on each possible outcome — and check those figures carefully, since a specialist in imprecise probability should be able to verify them from the poem alone. Do not let the term dilation appear in the verse itself: the form is to do that work. Someone who knows the literature should recognise the example and find the argument sound; someone who has never heard of it should simply find a good, disquieting poem about learning something and knowing less.
