Ran a seminar on Jain epistemology last Tuesday — anekāntavāda and the syādvāda business — and when a student pushed on whether two religions making flatly contradictory claims could both come out true, I heard myself say 'well, truth is many-sided', and that was that, the line did all the work for the rest of the hour. I want to know exactly what I let myself get away with.

So: take that slogan and lay out the distinct precise claims it could be standing for, from the near-trivial up to the genuinely alarming. I'd want the spread to include the contextualist reading — that a judgement is only true relative to an index, and the Jain lists four parameters: substance, place, time, and form — versus the full-blooded relativist reading where there's simply no unindexed truth to be had; the modest epistemic one about our access being partial versus the ambitious metaphysical one about objects themselves being inexhaustible; and whatever you'd get if someone read it as licensing outright contradictions. Note in each case whether it's a claim about our judgements or a claim about the world, since I suspect a lot of the slides between readings happen right there.

For each, tell me whether it's a platitude nobody would deny, a substantive and defensible thesis, or false — and if it's the last, say where it breaks. Then say which readings would actually have licensed me to wave off the student, and whether the self-application worry (does the slogan apply to itself, and if it does, is it still doing any work?) bites on all of them or only some.

Format: exactly five numbered items, each one or two sentences, giving the reading and the verdict. No introduction, no conclusion, no reading list — just the five, with the fifth telling me which reading I was leaning on and whether it earns its keep.
