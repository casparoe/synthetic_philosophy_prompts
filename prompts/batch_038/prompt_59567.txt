I write about science and ideas for a British weekly, and I am assembling a long read on why time has a direction, prompted by some reporting I did on black-hole thermodynamics and the detection of gravitational waves. In the course of that reporting I have become persuaded by a fairly definite package of claims, and I should like to know how exposed it is before I put my name to it in print. The package has three parts. First, the fundamental dynamical laws need contain no time-asymmetry: nothing in the microscopic equations picks out a direction. Second, the whole of the asymmetry we experience is owed to a single contingent boundary condition — that the early universe was in a state of extraordinarily low entropy — together with a statistical or typicality postulate licensing the move from that condition to claims about how systems behave away from equilibrium. Third, everything else we are inclined to call an arrow is derivative: the asymmetry between memory and anticipation, the fact that we can influence the future but not the past, and the preference of radiation for retarded rather than advanced solutions all fall out of the boundary condition plus the statistics, and require no separate explanation.

What would count against this? Please give me the defeaters ranked, from those that would oblige me to abandon the package to those that would oblige me only to qualify it, and say explicitly for each whether it is a live threat or merely a logical possibility. Several specific pressure points interest me. A rival account that posits a genuinely time-asymmetric fundamental law seems to say the same things about everything we could observe: does that mean my package risks nothing at all, and if so is that a defect in it or an appropriate feature of a view about the global structure of the universe? The claim that the early universe was low-entropy appears to depend on a contested measure over gravitational degrees of freedom, since a smooth, homogeneous young universe counts as special only if clumping counts as high entropy; if that measure is disputed, and if proposals in the spirit of Penrose's Weyl curvature conjecture are needed to make the specialness precise, is the boundary condition a substantive claim about the world or a redescription of the data? The package is usually advertised as blocking the reductio in which observers should expect to be momentary fluctuations rather than embodied creatures with pasts: does it block it, or does it need an extra assumption about our own typicality that is vulnerable to the same worry? If a spontaneous-collapse quantum dynamics, which is explicitly asymmetric in time, were confirmed, would that refute the package or merely relocate the arrow, and could the package absorb it? If the cosmological picture were one in which entropy rises away from a low-entropy state in both temporal directions from some central region — the sort of scenario Sean Carroll and Jennifer Chen have explored — would that be a rival or an instance of my view? And of the derivative arrows, which are genuinely derived: records and memories, the asymmetry of intervention and counterfactual dependence, the radiation asymmetry? Which of them, on the most careful treatments, still require extra assumptions beyond the boundary condition and the statistics? Finally, does the package predict anything its rivals do not?

I should like the answer to distinguish what a committed defender would themselves concede as counting against the view — that the boundary condition is unexplained rather than derived, that the typicality postulate is doing independent work and is not a mere restatement of it, that some of the derivative arrows may not reduce cleanly — from what they would dismiss as a restatement of discomfort rather than an objection. Please be willing to conclude that the package is heavily insulated, and to say whether that insulation is a problem for a position of this kind. For calibration: I am most susceptible to the worry that no observation could discriminate the package from a law-based alternative, and to the gravitational-entropy problem; I am least susceptible to objections amounting to the remark that it is queer for the universe to have begun in so special a state. I can follow an informal argument closely but not a derivation. Please write the response in British English.
