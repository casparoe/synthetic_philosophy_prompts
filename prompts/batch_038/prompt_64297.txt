Since last autumn I have been convening an evening bioethics seminar for clinical staff at the hospital where I work as a pharmacist — eighteen people, mostly ward nurses and pharmacy technicians, three residents, two social workers. We meet for seventy-five minutes every other Wednesday. Nobody reads anything in advance, and I want the session I am planning to stay that way: I am after a runnable activity, not a literature tour, so please do not close with a reading list or a set of references.

I took on this term's topic for reasons I have not fully disclosed to the group. My daughter, who is nine, was assessed last year and is now on a stimulant for ADHD; separately, our paediatrician has raised the possibility that she is short enough to be considered for growth hormone, which American regulators have permitted since 2003 for children whose only finding is that they are extremely short. My spouse and I have spent four months arguing about whether the first decision and the second are the same kind of decision, and I cannot make the argument rest on anything I am satisfied with. I would like my group to run into that problem themselves rather than hear me describe it.

Design a seventy-five-minute, eighteen-participant structured deliberation in which people discover that the therapy/enhancement distinction — or, more precisely, the criterion they themselves wrote for it — does not survive contact with the cases it was written to handle. Requirements:

1. The deciding body must not be an insurer, a hospital formulary, or a public payer. My group will otherwise replay their day jobs and reach for coverage rules they already know. Give me a fictional allocating institution whose structure makes those reflexes useless.
2. In round one the group writes its own one-sentence decision rule, in its own words, and posts it. Subsequent rounds apply it mechanically to case cards. Revision must be permitted but must cost something concrete and visible — retroactive reversal of an earlier approval, forfeiture of tokens, loss of standing with the constituency they represent — so that the ratchet they feel is real rather than rhetorical.
3. Build in two flips. First, a card whose classification changes once the group is told the population prevalence of the trait it addresses, so that a statistical conception of normal function turns an enhancement into a restoration or vice versa. Second, a card that is unambiguously restorative against a species-typical baseline, but where the baseline has drifted, so that nearly everyone now sits below it. Withhold the prevalence information until the rule has already been applied.
4. Include one card drawn from my own family's situation, anonymised: the group classifies a stimulant prescription for a nine-year-old with a confirmed diagnosis without knowing whose child it is, and I reveal it in the debrief. Tell me how likely they are to guess, and what I should do if somebody does mid-round.
5. Give each participant a private stakeholder role carrying one concealed interest, so that how a card gets classified tracks who stands to benefit. The debrief should surface this without my accusing anyone of bias.
6. No real money: specify a token or point scheme whose quantities make the scarcity bite in the third round.

Deliverables: a materials list I can produce on a hospital copier; a minute-by-minute timeline; the exact wording of the round-one instruction and of at least three cards, including both flips; the scoring scheme; a debrief that maps named moments in the play onto the statistical account of normal function, the quality-of-life account, and the objection that enhancement expresses a refusal to treat our endowment as given — described, not cited; the three things that will predictably go wrong with this particular group (I expect a resident to relitigate the clinical evidence, a nurse to raise who decides for a child, and someone to import reimbursement logic from the wards) and how to handle each; and a frank statement of which feature of the debate the activity cannot reproduce and which I will therefore have to supply by hand.
