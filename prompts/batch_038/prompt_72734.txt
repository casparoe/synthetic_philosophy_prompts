I teach philosophy of psychiatry and I've been trying to get straight on Hacking's looping effects, and I've realized my own judgments about which classifications loop and which don't are a mess. Here they are, most of them from arguments I've had with grad students. I want a principle, not a literature review.

1. Multiple personality / DID. Loops, obviously and hard. In the early days the typical patient had one or two alters; a decade or so later people were turning up with dozens, and the alters had pretty much the properties the casebooks said alters have. The description made new people to be.

2. Homosexuality as a diagnostic category. Loops. First there's a name, then there's a community organized under the name, then there's a politics, then there's the declassification, which loops again in the other direction.

3. Left-handedness. Loops, and roughly. A long stretch of forced right-hand schooling, then the pressure lifts and left-handedness surges in the statistics. But notice nobody did this by identifying as a lefty — most of the kids probably didn't know the category applied to them. The loop ran through the enforcement, not through anyone's self-conception.

4. Type A personality. Doesn't loop, which surprises people when I say it. Everybody in the eighties knew the label, half of them applied it to themselves cheerfully at dinner parties, and still nothing came of it: no stable population reorganized around the concept, no shift in who got heart disease, and the construct quietly dissolved. Maximum popularity plus maximum self-application, no loop.

5. Charcot's hysterics. Loops. Whether or not the patients knew they were performing, the presentations at Salpêtrière came to match the demonstration lectures, and the phenomena tracked the description.

6. Dyslexia. Here I waffle. Extra time changes exam performance, accommodations change what happens in a classroom, so behaviour clearly tracks the label — but I don't think there are more poor readers now than in 1950, and I don't think anyone's inner life got reorganized by being told they're dyslexic. My current line is that it loops in the ledger but not in the person, which I admit is a slogan and not a principle.

7. Actuarial risk scores for reoffending or violence. Doesn't loop. The scored person usually never sees the score; the judge and the parole board see it, their decisions change, the prison population changes. That's an instrument with downstream effects, not a kind reshaping its own instances.

8. But teacher-expectancy stuff around an ADHD label — same causal shape, the kid never sees the rating scale, the teachers act on it, the kid changes — and I say that one does loop. My students have pointed out, correctly, that 7 and 8 are structurally identical and I'm calling one a loop and the other not.

9. Retroactive reach. A hysteric who died in 1885: when we re-describe her, or when the Foucauldian line says there simply weren't homosexuals before the word made them a species, I feel something looping even though no living person is affected. On the other hand when mineralogists split jade into two minerals, nothing loops. Both are reclassifications of things that can't object to being reclassified.

10. A rare variant that turns up in maybe forty families worldwide, no treatment, no advocacy group, no school consequences. Doesn't loop, and I think the reason is there's nothing to do with the label once you have it.

11. My niece, age nine, tested into her district's gifted program. Within a year she was describing herself as the kind of kid who doesn't have to try, stopped doing homework, and by the retest she no longer qualified. Loops — but note the direction: the looping destroyed the property that earned her the label in the first place. Does a kind count as looping when the loop erases the kind?

My tentative principle is that a kind loops iff (a) it's applied to people who can come to know they're classified, (b) knowing it gives them a reason or a resource or a set of expectations to change in response, and (c) the change feeds back into the extension of the classification. That handles 1, 2, 6-ish, 8, 11 and rules out 4, 7, 10. But it breaks on 3, becuase the left-handed kids often didn't know, and it breaks on 5 unless I claim the hysterics knew, which I'm not willing to claim, and it doesn't explain 9 at all.

So: give me a principle, or a small set of rival candidates, stated precisely enough that I could hand it to an undergrad and get the same verdicts out of them. For each clause, tell me which of my cases is doing the work for it — I want to see whether every clause earns its keep or whether some are just there to save a verdict I should drop. Then run the principle on cases I haven't listed: burnout as an official diagnosis, BMI screening in school, carrier status handed to parents by a newborn screening panel, highly sensitive person as a pop category with a huge audience and zero institutional backing, and a diagnosis that changes only what clinicians do and never reaches the patient. Finally be honest about which of my eleven verdicts a decent principle has to overturn: I would much rather be told to give up 3 or 8 or 9 than be sold a baroque thing with eleven clauses that saves everything and explains nothing. And if your real answer is that looping effects is a family of distinct phenomena that never should have shared a name, say that instead and tell me what the distinct phenomena are and which of my cases belongs to which.

Tell me how confident you are in whatever you land on, and single out the one claim in your answer you're least sure of. No bibliography, please, and no invented references — I want the reasoning.
