Last week a friend of ours described her search for a used car, and two other friends — I will call them Nadia and Reza — spent close to three hours arguing about what her description showed. I sat through the whole thing without taking a side, and I walked home with Reza afterwards and we kept going for another hour, which still did not produce a winner. Both of them have now asked me to get an outside ruling, so I am passing the argument along as faithfully as I can.

The case. Dana had narrowed her search to three cars, A, B and C, and she reported her judgments pair by pair. Set A against B and she takes A, on fuel economy. Set B against C and she takes B, on cargo space. Set C against A and she takes C, on a considerably better reliability record. She said each judgment was sincere, that each was made about the pair in front of her, and that she had no single weighting of the three criteria that generated all three at once. Everyone at the table agreed to stipulate the following: all three cars are equally available to her, she is fully informed about them, fuel economy, cargo space and reliability are the only things she cares about, her pairwise judgments do not drift as she goes around, and each preference is strong enough that she would pay up to $200 to make the swap when only that pair is in front of her.

Reza then ran the pump. Suppose a dealer knows Dana's judgments and that she currently owns A. He offers a swap: A plus $50 for C. She prefers C to A, so she accepts. Then C plus $50 for B; she prefers B to C, so she accepts. Then B plus $50 for A; she prefers A to B, so she accepts. She is back in the car she started with and $150 poorer. He offers to run it again next week.

The argument, in the order I remember it:

1. Nadia: The pump is decisive. Dana ends the week exactly where she began, with less money, and every step was one she would take again knowing what she knew. Someone who will pay to be walked around a loop is paying for nothing, and a judgment that makes you pay for nothing is not tracking anything. So the three pairwise judgments cannot all be genuine preferences: either one is surrendered or the set is reorganized into a single ranking.

2. Reza: The pump indicts her foresight, not her judgments. It only works if she takes each swap as it is presented. Someone who sees the whole sequence at once — and the case says she is fully informed — refuses the first swap, and nothing is lost. So the conclusion is at most that cyclical judgments plus shortsightedness are expensive, not that cyclical judgments are irrational.

3. Nadia: Foresight does not do that work. Look at the last swap: she is holding B, she is offered A for $50, and she prefers A to B — nothing she knows about the two earlier swaps makes that offer any less attractive. Refusing the first swap therefore cannot be a matter of seeing more clearly; it has to be a matter of acting on something other than the pairwise judgment, namely a view about which of the three endings she wants, taken as a whole. Holding such a view and letting it govern is simply having a ranking of A, B and C. You have not rescued the cycle, you have described Dana as overriding it.

4. Reza: Then notice what you have conceded. The requirement now falls on plans: rank the courses of action available to you, pick the best, and stick to it. Dana can satisfy that perfectly while keeping all three pairwise judgments exactly as stated, because on your own account those judgments never determine a choice. Demanding that they be transitive as well is double counting — you already have consistency where it does any work, and you are charging her again for an inconsistency with no consequences.

5. Nadia: An inconsistency with no consequences is not what you have described. Her judgments do have consequences: they cost her $150 a week whenever anyone bothers to sequence her choices. And if the only way to keep them harmless is never to act on them, then they are not preferences but tastes in hypotheticals. That was my conclusion, and you have argued for it.

6. Reza: The consequences are smaller than your example implies. A $50 fee assumes the trip is worth making at all; set the fee at one cent and nobody drives across town for it. Any real pump has to clear the hassle threshold, which caps what can be extracted. What Dana pays is a bounded toll for having context-sensitive tastes, and people with perfectly transitive tastes pay tolls too, every time they buy an option they never exercise.

7. Nadia: Two problems. First, the toll is not bounded: raise the fee to $200 and, by her own report, she still accepts each swap, and the dealer can run the loop as often as she will play. Second, and this is the real point, the size of the loss is under the dealer's control — he chooses which pair she is asked about and when. No ordering of choices can make a transitive agent lose; some orderings make a cyclical one lose. That asymmetry, immunity to how your decisions get sequenced, is exactly what the requirement of transitivity says, and it is not a bookkeeping nicety.

8. Reza: Then you have told me what the requirement is for, and it is not what you began with. Immunity to adversarial scheduling is prudence — a view about how much one should care about being exploited — not a condition on having coherent preferences. Someone who says "yes, walk me around the loop, I would like to drive all three cars" is eccentric or generous, not incoherent. And for all we know Dana enjoys the swaps, in which case the loop is not nothing and the $150 buys something.

9. Nadia: That is a fork, not an escape. If the loop gets her something she wants, then what she prefers is the loop, so her preferences are over sequences — and the requirement applies there instead, since a cycle among sequences can be pumped just as easily. If the loop gets her nothing, she is paying $150 for nothing and we are back where we started. Either way the three pairwise judgments are not doing the work of preferences: in the first branch they are inert, in the second they are ruinous.

10. Reza: That is a redescription. Preferences over sequences and pairwise judgments about outcomes are different objects doing different jobs, and there is no reason both must be transitive; requiring it in two places at once is the double counting all over again. Dana can rank whole plans, choose the best, and stick to it, and still — when someone asks her which of two cars she would rather have — answer cyclically and sincerely, without ever being made worse off for it. If that is not rational, then "rational" has been made to mean something more than not defeating yourself.

I would like a ruling rather than a restatement. Please answer in exactly these four sections, in this order:

**Bottom line** (about 100 words). Who is more right overall, and the single consideration that decides it.

**Move by move** (about 900 words). Take the ten moves in order. Rate each on this scale: +2 clearly right, +1 mostly right, 0 a wash, −1 mostly wrong, −2 clearly wrong. Give the rating, then two to four sentences justifying it, and say explicitly whenever you think a move reaches a true conclusion by a bad route.

**What both of them missed** (about 200 words). The consideration, distinction or assumption that neither side raised which most changes how the argument should have gone. If you think one of my stipulations makes someone's job artificially easy, say so here rather than using it as a reason to withhold a verdict.

**Best repair** (about 150 words). How the side you judge to be losing ought to restate its position so that it survives, or, if it cannot be restated to survive, what it should concede.

Two further requests. First, be committed: give me definite ratings and a definite overall verdict, including on the moves you think are close — call them and explain what tips them, and reserve 0 for a move that genuinely neither helps nor hurts its side. I would rather have a confident ruling I can argue with than a balanced survey. Second, argue from the structure of this case and the moves as I have reported them; I am not looking for a review of who in the literature has held which position.
