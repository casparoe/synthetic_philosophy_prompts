I am a fifth-year doctoral student in philosophy, and in six weeks I am giving the opening remarks at a two-day workshop organised for the retirement of my supervisor: five minutes, no slides, delivered standing at the front of a lecture theatre. She spent thirty-four years on coherence arguments for expected utility maximization — the family of results holding that if your preferences or your betting prices cannot be exploited, then they are representable by, and so in some sense amount to, the maximization of expected utility relative to some probability function and some utility function. About forty people will be in the room. Roughly half are decision theorists and philosophers who could reconstruct those theorems from memory; a quarter come from an applied AI group that has absorbed the term mainly through talks about what advanced systems will and will not be like; the remainder are her husband, her two children, three grandchildren aged nine to sixteen (promised the afternoon off school), and two emeritus faculty who came for the wine. The workshop's theme is narrow and is my problem: the gap between what the theorems establish and what people say they establish. My own view has moved over the last two years and she knows it. I no longer think those arguments license the conclusions I was taught to draw. Specifically: (i) the representation results typically pin down *a* maximizing structure without pinning down the expected-utility *form* of it, so a great deal of behavior we would not want to call expected-utility maximizing counts as coherent; (ii) the vulnerability results depend on assumptions an agent is entitled to decline — accepting every bet offered at its stated price, being unable to foresee the sequence of choices ahead of it, carrying no background risks that the bet would worsen — and once those go, coherence stops being as demanding as advertised; (iii) one respectable route to invulnerability is simply to be imprecise, since an agent with a spread between her buying and selling price for one and the same proposition is not obviously bookable at all, and yet she is precisely not the single-number agent the theorem was supposed to deliver. If any of that distorts the literature, tell me plainly before you draft; I would much rather be corrected here than garble a theorem from a podium in front of forty specialists.

What I want is the speech, and the constraint that makes it more than a formality is this: the remarks should themselves be a coherence argument. Not a speech about coherence arguments — a speech that puts the room through one. It should open by securing agreement to a run of small, individually unobjectionable premises, some of them about her and some about the audience's own habits, and it should proceed so that anyone who has said yes to all of them cannot without embarrassment refuse the conclusion. The conclusion I want is that coherence is a much weaker net than her professional reputation implies and than the AI people in the room assume — and that she spent her career weaving it anyway, for reasons the speech should render admirable rather than mistaken. That final turn is not negotiable. I am not permitted to use a retirement workshop as a hit piece, and I am not willing to say aloud anything I no longer believe. There is also a true device you should build the spine around, because it gives the room something to hold. For twenty-nine years she ran what everyone called the Tuesday envelope: every Tuesday she left a sealed envelope on the common-room table containing a hand-drawn gamble, sometimes a coin, sometimes something stranger, and each of us wrote two numbers on a card — the most we would pay to buy the envelope, the least we would take to sell it. She kept every card in a shoebox under her desk. My card from my first year, which she has since shown me, has a buying price of forty cents and a selling price of three dollars for the identical envelope, and on the back she wrote a single word in pencil: why? She never told me. I asked twice; both times she changed the subject. Eleven years on I think I know several answers and I am not sure which one she wanted. The speech should leave that word standing open in front of the room, with at least two candidate answers developed far enough to be taken seriously — one a criticism of her whole programme, one a vindication of it — arranged so that she can hear both without flinching at either.

Practical requirements. Around 750 words, so that it runs five minutes at an ordinary pace, with two or three marked places where the text tells me to stop and let something land. It has to work aloud: short clauses, no structure that needs re-reading, nothing that depends on a visual or a gesture I would have to be shown. It must not require the audience to have read anything, and it should name at most two people who are dead — if you name living ones they will start wondering about themselves. The nine-year-old should be able to follow the Tuesday envelope thread even if the rest passes over her, and her grandfather, a retired actuary with a low opinion of philosophy, should find at least one passage in it technically respectable. No jokes about Bayesian updating, none about the three doors, and no reference to that prediction-machine problem; this room has heard all of it a hundred times. Warm but unsentimental, and please do not deploy the word rigor as a compliment.
