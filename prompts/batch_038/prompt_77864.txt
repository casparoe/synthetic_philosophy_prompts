ok so my friend Nadia and I argued until like 2am about whether you can ever actually write morality down as a set of principles, and I'm not over it. She's a particularist, hardline: no principle survives all the cases, and worse, the very same feature can count in favor in one situation and against in another, so theres no stable list of reasons with fixed signs. I kept trying to trap her and she just kept handing me cases with her verdicts on them, and honestly the verdicts are what's bugging me, because I think they're more systematic than she wants to admit.

Her verdicts, more or less in her words:

- A doctor lies to a terrified patient about how much a procedure will hurt and it goes way better. Right — and she got mad when I said "so the lie was justified," because on her view the lying wasn't a bad thing that got outweighed, it just isn't bad there at all.
- The same doctor lies about a prognosis to keep a clinical trial's enrollment numbers up. Wrong, flatly.
- A man tells the soldier at the door where the family next door is hiding. Wrong. She says the truth-telling isn't a reason in favor of speaking here, it's nothing, maybe even part of what makes it bad.
- A woman tells her friend honestly that the friend's manuscript isn't any good. Right, even though it wrecks the afternoon.
- A nurse tells a dying man's wife that he was comfortable at the end when he wasn't, there being nothing to gain from the truth now. Right — though she admitted this one felt different from the first, which I jumped on.
- A man keeps a promise to scatter his dead friend's ashes somewhere stupid and expensive, where keeping it makes nobody better off. Right, but only "barely" right, and I shouldn't be proud of him.
- Someone declines to donate a kidney to a stranger and spends the weekend teaching his kid to ride a bike instead. Fine, not a moral failure.
- She let a guy get mugged thirty feet away from her because intervening meant walking into a crowd where she'd probably have been stabbed. Right.
- She also let a guy get his wallet lifted because it meant missing her bus. Wrong, and she was annoyed I even had to ask.

Here's the case I'm bringing her tomorrow. Dawn volunteers at a hospice. Her patient asks every morning whether her son is coming; the son died four months ago and she forgets again by the afternoon. Dawn can either say "he's coming after lunch," which settles her at once so she eats her breakfast and has a decent day, or sit down and tell her the truth again, which means an hour of fresh grief and no breakfast, after which the nurse says she's generally calmer for a while and it costs her nothing in the long run. Nobody else is affected, no slippery slope, the family genuinely doesn't mind either way, and Dawn has tried both approaches with other patients with no bad knock-on effects. So the consequences are nearly a wash, and the only thing really pointing one way is that one option involves a lie and the other doesn't.

Predict her verdict on Dawn. I want the candidate rules her track record actually fits — including the possibility that she's not a particularist at all but a pluralist for whom honesty always counts a little and lying always counts a little, and the possibility that she's running something like a default-and-challenge picture where a feature counts unless you can show why it doesn't in the case at hand. Say what each of those rules predicts about Dawn, which one you'd bet on and why, and then give me an actual scenario (not a description of what a scenario would have to be like) that would seperate them — or tell me straight out if no scenario could, in which case I want to know what her particularism is even claiming. If two of your candidate rules can't be pulled apart by anything she's said so far, say that in the same breath rather than leaving it out.

Answer in American English as exactly five numbered items, a sentence or two each, no intro and no conclusion. Go in this order: her verdict on Dawn; the rules that fit; what each rule says about Dawn; your bet and why; the case that would settle it, or the admission that none would.
