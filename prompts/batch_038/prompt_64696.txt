I sat in on the last two sessions of a history reading group I'm not formally a member of — a retired archivist, a couple of graduate students, a few people who just like the subject — and I ended up taking notes because the argument got good and I want to think about it properly afterwards. The group had been reading selections from Fritz Fischer's 1961 *Griff nach der Weltmacht* (published in English as *Germany's Aims in the First World War*) alongside Christopher Clark's *The Sleepwalkers* (2012), with the question on the table being whether it makes sense to say that Germany was responsible for the First World War, and if so in what sense. Two members, whom I'll call Miriam and Daniel, took opposite poles and went at it for about forty minutes. The rest of us were asked to referee and nobody would commit to a verdict, so I'm handing it to you instead. Please note that I'm reporting this as a bystander: I have deliberately kept my own sympathies out of the transcript, and I'd ask you not to infer them from the fact that I recorded some moves in more detail than others.

Here is the exchange as nearly as I can reconstruct it. **(1) Miriam:** responsibility tracks decisions, not drift; in early July 1914 Berlin gave Vienna unconditional backing and then pressed it to move against Serbia quickly, before the other powers could intervene, and Fischer showed that expansive war aims were already worked out in Germany — the September Programme of September 1914 is a document, not a mood — so Germany is not one culprit among several but the one that chose. **(2) Daniel:** "chose" is doing the smuggling; Clark's point is that all five chancelleries were running the same playbook, a limited war as a diplomatic gamble with a knowingly accepted risk of generalization, and none of them wanted the continental war they got; Russian general mobilization came first, and French encouragement of Russia was no less decisive than German encouragement of Austria; if the same description fits every capital, then "Germany chose" is just a way of saying "I told the story from Berlin." **(3) Miriam:** identical descriptions don't imply identical shares; even granting the shared playbook, responsibility is not split by the number of players but by the difference each player's decision made, and Germany was the one power positioned to restrain Vienna and instead urged speed, so a contribution that was both decisive and avoidable carries more blame than contributions that were neither. **(4) Daniel:** "decisive and avoidable" assumes a counterfactual you cannot run; there is no nearby world in which Berlin says *wait* in the first week of July and the crisis defuses, because the alliance structure, the general staff's plans, and the mobilization timetables were already loaded, and what you're calling Germany's decision is simply the point where a system's constraints become visible inside a single set of minutes — blame that lands on the visible node is an artifact of narrative form, since a story needs a protagonist. **(5) Miriam:** that view is self-defeating; if no actor could have done otherwise, then "Europe went to war" has no agent at all and you've explained away the very thing the question was about — and it can't survive contact with the one place where a state was actually held responsible in writing, Article 231: "The Allied and Associated Governments affirm and Germany accepts the responsibility of Germany and her allies for causing all the loss and damage to which the Allied and Associated Governments and their nationals have been subjected as a consequence of the war imposed upon them by the aggression of Germany and her allies." Germans read that as a moral verdict, which is precisely why it mattered, and if national responsibility is a fiction the clause was not merely harsh but nonsense. **(6) Daniel:** the clause proves my point; it was drafted as a legal warrant for reparations, its authors framed it as causation of loss rather than guilt, and they were reportedly startled by the German reading of it as a confession — the "war guilt clause" is largely a later construction — which shows that "nations are responsible" is not a fact we discover but a rhetorical device that gets bolted onto whatever instrument is handy. **(7) Miriam, briefly:** so is the entire forty years of historiography a verbal dispute? **Daniel, briefly:** no — it's substantive but underdetermined, and the proof is that we agree on essentially every fact in the July Crisis and still cannot agree on the answer.
