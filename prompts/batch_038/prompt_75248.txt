I'm contributing a story to a small annual that pairs original fiction with a short critical apparatus; this year's theme is rigidity, and my assignment has settled on sphexishness — the digger wasp case that Hofstadter and Dennett both pressed into service and that gave the word its currency. A female *Sphex* stings and paralyzes a katydid and hauls it to her burrow by the antennae; at the mouth she sets it down, goes inside to inspect, and comes out to drag it down. Fabre's variants are the part I care about: cut off the prey's antennae and she does not think to take hold of a leg instead, and if an experimenter shifts the prey a few inches while she is inside, she finds it again, hauls it back to the mouth, and inspects again — and again, by some accounts without end. Two things bother me about nearly every literary treatment of this that I've read. First, the loop is almost always motor and transparent — a character keeps pressing the same button, reciting the same apology, driving the same road home — so the reader diagnoses it within a page and settles in to watch. The wasp's loop is more interesting because it is a *checking* routine: the inspection that looks like the very source of her competence is the step that resets the trap. Second, the anti-sphexishness story has itself hardened into a template. It ends either in epiphany (the character sees the repetition and steps out of it, flattering the reader with the thought that stepping out is available on demand) or in catastrophe (the loop kills somebody, retroactively confirming the dread the reader was having). Both endings convert a structural problem into a satisfying narrative gesture, and both are available only from outside the loop, which is exactly the standpoint the story denies its characters. There is also a live empirical objection I'd like the piece to leave room for rather than steamroll: re-inspecting a disturbed nest may be precisely what a well-designed wasp ought to do, the reports of endless repetition come to us secondhand, and some work has found that the animals do vary their behavior after enough iterations. Mindlessness may be in the eye of the describer.

Here is the story I want. Set it on the bridge of a laden bulk carrier being taken up a river channel by an experienced harbor pilot on the morning after a storm that everyone ashore agrees will have moved the shoal. She has run this water a few thousand times and reads it by transits and leading marks, by the colour of the surface, by how the helm answers under her feet; the chart on the table and the channel in her head disagree, and her discrepancy-resolving procedure — check again, allow for squat and for the flood still making, carry on — is the same procedure each time the disagreement resurfaces. Put a mate or an apprentice on the bridge who can see that she has now done this twice, but who has neither the standing nor the vocabulary to say so. Invent the port, the vessel, the weather; keep the technical surface plausible without turning the piece into a manual. The story should run 1,500 to 2,000 words, and it must satisfy the following:

- No robots, no artificial intelligences, no bureaucracy standing in for a mind. This is an expert's rigidity, and the reader must understand — and at least once share — her confidence that she is doing the skilled thing. If the prose signals doom from the first paragraph, it has failed.
- Structural repetition carries the concept. Two to four sentences must recur at least three times in near-identical form, with one small alteration per recurrence — a number, a substituted sensory detail, a clause that changes position — alterations the pilot never registers and the reader cannot miss. Each recurrence must be, from her point of view, the first time.
- Forbidden vocabulary: neither narrator nor any character may use *habit*, *routine*, *pattern*, *loop*, *cycle*, *rigid*, *instinct*, *reflex*, *automatic*, or *muscle memory*, nor any near-synonym, and no character may remark that she is repeating herself.
- At least one recurrence must be locally correct: given what she knows at that moment, doing it again must genuinely be the right call. The reader should be unable to conclude that she was merely mindless.
- No epiphany and no instructive disaster. The cycle ends, or fails to end, from outside — by an intervention whose own unexamined fixity is as visible to the reader as hers — or by the river simply running out. Nobody is granted meta-access: not the pilot, not the mate, not the narrator.

After the story, give me roughly three hundred words of apparatus. Locate the fixed point: at which level does revision stop, and what is the story implicitly claiming must be unrevisable in order for anything to happen at all? State explicitly whether the pilot is sphexish in the wasp's sense or only superficially so, and identify which disanalogy is doing the work — the wasp's cycle is re-triggered by an outsider moving her prey, whereas the pilot's is re-triggered by her own checking subroutine, and I want to know whether you take that to be a difference in kind or merely a difference in where the lever is attached. Then tell me what the piece commits me to on the view that free will is flexibility: if an agent who can always revise one level higher is not thereby free, what further ingredient is the story pointing at, and does it actually supply that ingredient or only gesture toward it? Close by flagging one place where you think you cheated — where the prose asserts the point instead of earning it, or where one of my constraints quietly bent.
