I am in my third year of a philosophy PhD, writing a dissertation chapter on aggregation, and I have hit a wall that I think is diagnostic rather than scholarly. The wall is the separateness-of-persons objection to utilitarianism: the charge, familiar from Rawls and given its most quotable form by Nozick, that utilitarianism extends to the population as a whole a form of reasoning that is legitimate only within a single life, since there is no entity that experiences the aggregate gain while particular individuals bear the losses. Fifty years on, I cannot find a formulation of the objection that my consequentialist interlocutors do not already have a reply to, and the replies and the rejoinders seem to trade places without anyone conceding anything. I want a diagnosis of the structure of this impasse, not an adjudication of it and not a survey of the literature. Please argue from the content of the positions rather than from who said what; I do not need references and would rather you not supply them.

Here is my own working taxonomy, which I would like you to test rather than accept. The objection seems to me to be doing at least three different jobs, and the debate may stall because only one of them is ever in play at a time:

(R1) An axiological job: well-being is a property of lives rather than of populations, so the good to be promoted is not a scalar sum across subjects, and any theory that treats persons as mere locations of value misdescribes the good.

(R2) A prudential-unity job: intrapersonal compensation is legitimate because the very subject who bears a burden is the subject who receives the benefit, and utilitarianism illicitly postulates a subject spanning the population.

(R3) A right-based job: imposing a loss on one person for another's gain is a distinct moral kind of event, to which loss-and-gain arithmetic simply does not apply.

Two observations motivate my hypothesis. First, in my reading group the objectors and the defenders agree on the descriptive facts and on the verdicts in every standard case we try; the disagreement is entirely about what explains those verdicts and about what a moral theory owes us by way of explanation. Second, the best-known defensive reply — Parfit's suggestion that reductionism about personal identity weakens the objection, because the boundaries between lives are less deep than the objection assumes — is a direct attack on R2 and, so far as I can tell, leaves R1 and R3 untouched; yet it is routinely offered and received as though it answered the objection as such. So my hypothesis is that this is not a substantive deadlock at all but an ambiguity-driven one: the same slogan is deployed on three different levels, each side attacks whichever reading is currently being asserted, and no argument ever joins issue with the reading that carries the objector's real weight. I may of course be wrong, and being too charitable to my own field is a live risk.

To test the hypothesis, consider these two cases, which are mine and are designed to dissociate the readings. In SPLIT, a procedure produces two continuing persons from one, each inheriting a disjoint subset of the original's projects, memories and attachments, and each broadly indifferent to the other's fate; one continuer is then made somewhat worse off so that the other is made considerably better off. In GRAFT, two previously unconnected strangers acquire durable memory-sharing and joint planning, such that each treats the other's projects and future as her own, with no biological or ordinary psychological continuity between them; one is then made worse off so that the other is better off. In each case, say what verdict the objector owes, and what an R1, an R2 and an R3 reading respectively predict. The question I care about is whether the verdicts come apart: if the objection's force tracks prudential unity — weakened in GRAFT, strengthened in SPLIT — then R2 is doing the work and Parfit's reply is on target; if the objection fires in both cases because there are still two persons, then prudential unity is inert and the standard replies are answering a question nobody is asking.

Deliver, in roughly 1200 words: (i) your diagnosis, stated as a claim about the kind of disagreement this is — verbal, level-confused, genuinely substantive but hard, two parties answering different questions under one heading, or a question that is ill-posed; (ii) support for it drawn from what objectors and defenders are actually committed to, including whether R1, R2 and R3 are separable or collapse into one another under pressure; (iii) an assessment of my two cases as diagnostic instruments, including any way in which they are ill-designed or fail to pry the readings apart; and (iv) what would count as progress on your diagnosis — a case pair on which the readings yield divergent verdicts, a formal constraint on aggregation rules that would force a choice, a reframing, or an admission that the objection is a framework-level claim that can only be adopted rather than argued for. If your honest conclusion is that no progress should be expected, say so and give the reason, rather than manufacturing a way forward.
