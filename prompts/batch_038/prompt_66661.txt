I am a health economist working in cost-effectiveness analysis for a national health technology assessment body. My day job is to convert clinical trial outputs into QALYs, to work with the EQ-5D value sets my agency has endorsed, and to argue about elicitation instruments — standard gamble, time trade-off, discrete choice experiments, person trade-off — and the disability weights they generate. I also build models with distributional weights, in which gains to people who already command more healthy life expectancy are deliberately discounted. I have read some welfare economics, but I have no real training in the philosophical literature on measurement and aggregation.

I would like the philosophical debate over interpersonal utility comparisons restated in the vocabulary I actually use — value sets, elicitation tasks, invariance classes, the informational content of an index, the weights in an aggregate objective function — rather than in the vocabulary of a welfare economics textbook, which tends to compress the debate into a single remark about cardinal versus ordinal utility. Please translate the following positions, one at a time:

1. Robbins's charge that comparisons of utility between persons are not merely unverifiable but meaningless, together with the ordinalist retreat to Pareto-based social choice and its consequences for what an index is entitled to say.

2. Sen's distinction, which I have never been able to state precisely, between a cardinal utility function and an interpersonal comparability assumption: the claim that fixing an individual's utility up to positive affine transformation licenses no cross-person comparison at all, that comparability is a further assumption amounting to an interpersonal equivalence relation, and that comparing levels across persons is a different and weaker achievement than comparing differences or units across persons.

3. The extended-sympathy or impartial-observer strategy, and specifically the objection that Harsanyi's representation theorem delivers only a weighted sum of individual utilities, leaving the weights arbitrary, so that the theorem is equally consistent with a preference-utilitarian social ordering at unequal weights and does not by itself yield the equal-weights conclusion its defenders draw from it.

4. The prioritarian move: the claim that utilitarianism and prioritarianism can share exactly the same cardinal, interpersonally comparable information and disagree only about how to weight it, and what that implies about whether the live dispute concerns measurement or aggregation.

5. The disagreement over what the measure is a measure of — preference satisfaction, experienced affect, or objective well-being conceived as capability or functioning — and the associated claim that adaptation can make a within-person cardinal measure systematically misleading as a basis for cross-person comparison even when that measure is internally well behaved.

For each of the five, tell me what the claim would be a claim about in my own terms, and be honest in doing it: do not smuggle in the assumptions of my framework and present the result as a translation of theirs. Where a philosophical claim only appears to say something I already say, flag it as a false friend and explain the divergence.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I most want:

- Which claims map cleanly onto distinctions I already draw in practice, and which of my routine methodological arguments is therefore already a disguised philosophical argument.
- Which map only with distortion, and what precisely gets distorted in the fitting.
- Which have no expression whatsoever in cost-effectiveness analysis — no counterpart among value sets, elicitation instruments, invariance requirements, or weighting schemes.

For the third category I want you to take a side rather than merely catalogue: does the residue show that my framework is missing something that bears on the allocation decisions it is used to make, and if so what adding it would look like concretely — a new assumption, a new instrument, a different objective function? Or does it show that the philosophers are marking a distinction without operational content?

Finally, please test one intuition of mine directly, because I suspect it is complacent. My working view is that disability weights are simply interpersonal utility comparison rendered operational, that a country-specific value set is a comparability assumption with survey data behind it, and that Robbins's objection is therefore beside the point for anything I do. Tell me whether that intuition survives contact with the actual arguments, or whether it quietly presupposes something the debate denies — about adaptation, about whether a person trade-off task elicits a judgement about well-being or a judgement about preference, or about whether a ranking of health states across respondents really amounts to an equivalence relation between persons. And where a philosophical objection does bite against my practice, tell me which of my instruments it bites hardest against and why.

I would like the arguments and the mappings themselves, not a survey of the literature and not a reading list; the answer should be usable by someone who will not be looking anything up.
