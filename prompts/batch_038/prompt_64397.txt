In 2019 Paul Christiano introduced the strategy-stealing assumption into debates about advanced AI: for any strategy a misaligned system could use to shape the long-run future, a comparably resourced group of humans directing aligned systems could use an analogous strategy and capture a comparable share of influence. Its defenders treat it as a sane working default that deflates takeover scenarios; its critics say it fails exactly where it matters, pointing to the alignment tax, to humans who cannot comprehend a strategy they cannot interpret, to coordination failure, and to harms that run in only one direction. Both sides know the other's objections, and after years nobody has moved.

The term is borrowed from combinatorial game theory, where strategy-stealing shows that the second player has no winning strategy — but only in a game that is symmetric between the players, cannot end in a draw, and in which an extra move can never hurt. My hypothesis is that the impasse sits on that monotonicity premise, which is precisely what the critics deny, so the two camps are not disagreeing about a single proposition at all.

Test that hypothesis. Is this a failed transfer of a proof schema whose conditions are contested rather than satisfied; a verbal dispute over strategy, analogous, or stealing, given that in the AI case nothing is taken from an opponent and the beneficiary is whoever moves first; a hidden empirical question about comprehension, implementation, and timing; or a disagreement about what kind of thing the assumption is — a premise, a research target, or a prediction about the world? Then say what would count as progress.
