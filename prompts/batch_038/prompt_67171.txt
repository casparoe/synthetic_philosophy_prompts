I would like a piece of literary criticism, not a book report, and I would like to be told where my own reading fails. I come to this out of years on rationalist blogs and forums, so my default expectation is that a text's premises get laid bare and stress-tested rather than that its atmosphere get described to me.

I have been arguing with a friend about Septimus Warren Smith's death in Virginia Woolf's Mrs Dalloway. Her version is the standard one: the novel stages his suicide as a protest against Sir William Bradshaw and the twin principles Woolf personifies as Proportion and Conversion, and Clarissa's private endorsement of it at her party is the book's moral centre. My version is narrower and, I think, harder to hold. Woolf has inadvertently constructed a test case for Hume's essay Of Suicide, and the case breaks the theory at a joint that Hume's three-part schema cannot name.

My reconstruction of the essay, which you should correct wherever I have got it wrong: Hume asks whether suicide offends God, one's neighbour, or oneself. Against the first charge, if Providence governs by general laws then the surgeon who turns aside a fever interferes with the course of nature exactly as much as the man who throws himself from a window; either divine governance reaches human action, in which case the act falls inside it, or it does not, in which case nothing we do disturbs it. Against the second, a man who retires from life renounces every benefit society confers and so does it no injury; at worst he ceases to do good, and the hermit in his cell withdraws just as much. Where a life has become a burden and its owner is no longer of service, Hume thinks society's claim simply lapses. Against the third charge, his central empirical premise is that our horror of death is so strong that only someone for whom life has become unendurable can overcome it, so that the act itself testifies to the balance of the account. It is worth noting that Hume suppressed the essay during his lifetime and it appeared only posthumously, and that suicide remained a felony in England until the Suicide Act 1961 -- which is why Holmes's and Bradshaw's interventions in the novel carry legal and professional authority behind them, not merely medical opinion.

Here are the load-bearing moves in my reading that I want you to attack as much as to develop.

First, I claim Hume's third argument carries an unstated proviso: the loss of usefulness must be nobody's doing. Hume's cases are disease, misfortune, temper. Septimus's uselessness was manufactured by the state that sent him to the war and is then administered by the profession that refuses to leave him alone with it. If that proviso fails, I want to know whether Hume's defence merely stops applying, or whether it actively turns against him -- since a society that produced the incapacity cannot also invoke it as a reason the man may leave, and a man who leaves is escaping a harm rather than renouncing a benefit.

Second, I claim Hume is arguing against the wrong adversaries. His opponents are theodicy, the commonwealth, and prudence: the parson and the magistrate. Woolf's London has replaced both with a specialist who has no theological commitment and needs no statute, only proportion. Does Hume's schema have a slot at all for a third-party claim advanced in the name of the patient's own good by someone with the power to confine him? If not, that is a structural gap rather than a disagreement, and I want it stated that way.

Third, I think Septimus is a counterexample to the inference Hume's empirical premise licenses. He jumps, in part, because Holmes's hand is on the door; nothing suggests a settled judgment that his life was a net burden, and much suggests the opposite, since he repeatedly perceives the world as urgently meaningful. Hume reads the assessment off the act, which makes his premise unfalsifiable and unable to distinguish a considered conclusion from a panic. Is that a fair hit, or am I reading a modern distinction between settled intent and acute crisis back into an eighteenth-century text that has no use for it?

Fourth, Clarissa. She approves the death, but on grounds Hume cannot express: something about an incorruptible centre preserved, and about the act as communication. If that is right, the novel offers a fourth category -- the act as testimony, measured not by usefulness but by what it says -- and I want you to decide whether that is a genuine addition to the debate or an aestheticizing of it, a way of making a death mean something so that the novel need not ask whether it was permitted.

Finally, the honest part. Tell me where this reading reduces Woolf to an illustration of an essay she probably never thought about, where Septimus's death is better explained by the failure of communication or by the war's aftermath than by any question of permissibility, and where my proviso is a patch I invented to save the theory rather than a feature of it. Then tell me what the novel contributes that argument alone does not -- if anything. No bibliography, no reading list; I want the analysis, and I want the places where you disagree with me.
