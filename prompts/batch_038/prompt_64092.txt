I've just come off a four-hour argument with a friend about Piccione and Rubinstein's absent-minded driver — the one who cannot tell his off-ramp home from the rest of the dark highway — and I want an adversarial assessment of the position I spent the evening defending, because I suspect several of my replies were rhetorical rather than probative.

The position, which I'll call the planning-authority view, has three parts. First: in extensive decision problems where the agent's own earlier choices have destroyed the information he would need to locate himself, the normatively authoritative evaluation of his options is the one made at the planning node, when he is fully informed; the objects of evaluation are plans, not momentary acts. Second: the driver's computation at the intersection, the one that seems to tell him to switch, is not a rival verdict but a mistake — he conditionalizes on centered evidence (that he is now at a decision point) which carries no information the plan did not already price in, and he treats a disposition he has already fixed as though it were a live option. Third: consequently the paradox tells us nothing about the foundations of decision theory. It tells us that agents with imperfect recall can be dynamically inconsistent, which is a contingent fact about their cognitive architecture rather than a defect in the theory. The theory's advice is unchanged — adopt the ex ante optimal plan — and the later temptation is simply irrational.

My friend's rival view is that the driver's total evidence at the intersection genuinely is just that he is at a decision point; that conditionalizing on it is proper; that decision theory must therefore be indexed to the agent's epistemic situation at the time of action; and that the ex ante plan has only derivative, instrumental authority, mattering solely insofar as adopting it causally shapes the dispositions the driver will later have. On his telling, my first claim mistakes a causal device for a normative standard.

I want a map of my exposure, not a defense of me.

1. Lay out the cases, arguments, and results that would count against the planning-authority view, and rank them from fatal to merely awkward. For each, say what would have to be true for it to bite, and whether a committed defender has a reply that costs nothing or a reply that concedes real ground.

2. Say where my view stands or falls with independent debates rather than with the driver case itself: with disputes about centered evidence and self-locating belief of the Sleeping Beauty type (on which my second claim looks like a no-new-relevant-evidence stance, and my friend's like an updating stance); with dynamic coherence arguments — Dutch books, money pumps, no-regret considerations — and whether any of them can actually be run against an agent who cannot revise mid-drive; and with the game-theoretic question of what counts as a strategy under imperfect recall, since behavioral randomization at each node and a mixed strategy over complete plans come apart here. If the paradox is partly an artifact of that formal choice, is my third claim too cheap — and can my friend say the artifact is mine?

3. Ask whether my view collapses into a rival. If the three claims amount to treating every multi-time choice problem as a single one-shot decision, is that a substantive thesis or a redescription? Does it collapse into sophisticated choice, into a precommitment-only account, or into a view on which an agent's deliberation at the moment of action is never genuinely action-guiding — and if the last, is that a reductio rather than a cost I can absorb?

4. Assess three pressure points I constructed during the argument, and tell me for each whether it is fatal, awkward, or harmless.

The passenger. The plan is formed at the bar; a wide-awake passenger riding along says plainly, at one decision point, that this is the ramp. If my view says the plan still governs, it is absurd; if it says reliable new information restores local authority, then my second claim cannot be about information as such, and I owe a principled line between evidence the driver may use and evidence he may not. Give me the line, or tell me there isn't one.

Partial recall. My variant: the road has five decision points, indistinguishable to the driver, exactly one of which — the third — is the ramp. Exiting at the ramp gets him home, payoff 4; exiting at any other point deposits him on a service road, payoff 1; continuing past the fifth point ends in a dead end, payoff 0. Since the points are indistinguishable, the only deterministic plans are always-exit, yielding 1, and always-continue, yielding 0, so the ex ante optimum is a behavioral strategy: exit with some probability at each point. Now interpolate memory: with probability r the driver at a decision point correctly recalls how many points he has already passed; with probability 1−r he recalls nothing at all. At r = 1 there is no paradox; at r = 0 it is the classic case. Does my view yield a determinate answer about where, if anywhere, along that continuum local reasoning becomes authoritative? If it says never until r = 1, is that defensible or is it insulation? If it posits a threshold, what fixes the threshold — and does having one concede my friend's main point, that the plan's authority is a function of the agent's evidence at the time of action? Please also check my claim about which deterministic plans are available and about the shape of the behavioral optimum, since I worked that out on a napkin.

The unexecutable plan. Suppose the driver knows at the bar that he is constitutionally incapable of holding to a plan once a temptation appears: whatever he plans, at the decision point he will do what seems best then. If the ex ante optimal plan cannot be executed by the very agent whose plan it is, in what sense is it the authoritative evaluation of his options? My third claim says the temptation is irrational, but an irrational disposition that is guaranteed to fire makes the plan look like a fiction. Tell me whether this is a real objection or whether it trades on confusing normative authority with implementability, and if it is the latter, what my view must say about cases where the two come apart.

5. Distinguish what a defender of the planning-authority view would himself concede counts against it from what critics merely assert. I want the concessions, not the rebuttals.

6. Give an honest verdict on whether the view risks anything at all, or whether it has been qualified into safety by the moves available to it — and if it is insulated, whether that is a problem for a view of this kind, given that its rival may be insulated in a parallel way. If both are insulated, say what would break the symmetry.

Finally, tell me which single defeater you would press if you had one move to break the view, and be blunt about whether I should have conceded the argument. What would move me most is a case in which the plan-governed recommendation is one that no coherent account of the driver as choosing, then and there, could endorse — I think that is the intuition my friend was reaching for without being able to state it, and I want to know whether it can be stated.
