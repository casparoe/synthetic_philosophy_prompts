**The paradox isn't in the vote, it's in the question**

Everybody who's read anything about this knows the setup, so I'll go fast. Our constitution says the board can approve a spend only if three things are all true: it's within our charitable objects, it's affordable, and we have the people to actually run it. So we voted on the polytunnel-and-a-part-time-coordinator thing.

- me: within objects y, affordable y, people n → spend n
- Dez: y, n, y → n
- Priya: n, y, y → n
- Alan: y, y, y → y
- Ruth: y, y, y → y
- Tom: n, n, n → n
- Sita: n, n, n → n

Down the columns: within objects 4-3 yes. Affordable 4-3 yes. People 4-3 yes. Spend 2-5 no.

So if you count the premises and apply the rule, we fund it. If you just count the bottom line, we don't. Same seven people, same opinions, opposite result. This is the old problem — I believe there's a version of it about courts, where a claim only succeeds if several elements are all found, and a more general version that people argue about in political philosophy. And the argument, as far as I can tell, is about which procedure you should use: the people who say vote on the premises say it's because a group that has to keep going needs a record of reasons it's committed to, and the people who say vote on the conclusion say the group is just a machine for getting the right answer on this one question and shouldn't be constrained by its own bookkeeping.

Here's the thing though. I think that whole argument is a distraction, because both sides assume the question has already been cut up correctly. Ours hadn't.

Halfway through the meeting Dez said hang on, what does affordable even mean here — affordable out of this year's float, or affordable if you spread it over three years like we did with the irrigation? Fair point. So we split "affordable" into two, call them a1 (fits this year) and a2 (fits over three years). Everyone's underlying view stayed exactly the same, we just wrote it out finer:

- me: a1 n, a2 y
- Dez: a1 n, a2 n
- Priya: a1 y, a2 y
- Alan: a1 y, a2 y
- Ruth: a1 n, a2 y
- Tom: a1 n, a2 n
- Sita: a1 n, a2 n

Now: a1 fails 2-5. a2 passes 4-3. Taken as "either one is enough," affordability passes 4-3, same as it did before. Taken as "you need both," affordability fails 2-5 and the whole spend fails.

Nobody voted on which of those it was. Our secretary just wrote the two new boxes on the sheet with an "and" between them, because it looked neater and because the other three boxes had "and"s. The project died on that pen stroke. And note it gets worse, not better, if you're a conclusion-voter: once the rule is written as an "and," Ruth has to change her own bottom line to stay consistent with her own a1 answer, so the spend goes from losing 2-5 to losing 1-6. An unvoted editorial decision moved a vote by a whole person.

So. The standard lesson is that the group's answer depends on the procedure. What I think our meeting shows is that it also depends on the agenda — the list of questions and, crucially, the connectives between them — and the agenda isn't produced by any vote at all.

The obvious fix is: fine, vote on the agenda too. But that just relocates the problem. "Should a1 and a2 be alternatives or joint requirements" is itself the kind of question that can be cut up. The right connective is the one that (i) matches what the constitution actually says, (ii) matches what members thought they were voting on when they raised their hands, and (iii) doesn't make the rule unsatisfiable in practice. You could easily get a majority for each of those and no majority for all three — that's exactly the shape of the original problem, one level up. So you need an agenda for the agenda vote. And an agenda for that one. I don't see where it stops.

Where I think it actually stops is not another vote. It stops at a person, or at a habit. In our room it was the secretary with a biro. In a court it's whoever settles the questions put to the bench. In a parliament it's the speaker and the standing orders, which nobody re-votes on every session. So my claim is: any group that has to reason rather than merely count contains a component that isn't the group — one human being, or one inherited convention — who gets to say what the question was. And in exactly the cases that matter, the cases where the group is genuinely split and the aggregation is supposed to be doing the deciding, that component is doing real work. Which seems to me a bigger and more uncomfortable point than the paradox itself. The paradox is about how you count. This is about the fact that nobody counts the counting.

Two objections I can already hear.

First: this is just "someone has to chair the meeting" with philosophy sprinkled on it. My answer is no, because the chairing is normally treated as neutral plumbing, and what I'm claiming is that the plumbing is load-bearing precisely when the votes are close. If everyone agreed, nobody would notice the connective.

Second: in a court the agenda isn't arbitrary, it's fixed by the statute, which lists the elements. My answer is that then the choice just moves inside the reading of the statute, and the judge who writes "the elements here are X and Y" has done the same thing my secretary did with the biro, only with more ceremony.

What I'm least sure about. Three things, and I'll say them honestly. (1) Maybe the regress in the middle is fake and I'm producing it by being deliberately thick about what a vote on the agenda would look like — in real life you'd just vote on whether a1 and a2 are alternatives and nobody would think to decompose that. If that's right, is my point merely "it could happen," which is weak? (2) Maybe the bottom of the regress doesn't have to be a person. It could be a convention nobody holds, like a rule of a game that's just followed. I'd prefer that version because the person version is pretty bleak, but I can't see how a convention settles the connective on the one occasion when the convention is itself what's in dispute. (3) I'm not sure I've earned the leap from "somebody sets the agenda" to "the group's reasoning isn't really the group's." That last step might just be rhetorical heat.
