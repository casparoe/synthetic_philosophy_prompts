third year philosophy PhD, second chapter of the dissertation is on reasons-holism, and before it goes to my committee I want someone to stress-test the actual package of views I'm committed to. Not a survey — I know the landscape, I've read the literature, I don't need the options laid out for me. What I want is an audit of this specific set.

Here's what I hold:

1. Holism about reasons. For any non-moral feature there's no guarantee it keeps the same valence from case to case. What counts in favour of an act here can count against it there, and which way it goes depends on the rest of the case.

2. Because of 1, I don't think there are exceptionless moral principles worth having — anything true enough to survive the counterexamples ends up so hedged with provisos that it stops being a guide to anything. And I don't think principles do the work in good deliberation either. Someone who has to consult the rule that lying is wrong at the moment of acting is already failing to see the situation in front of them.

3. Supervenience is safe and I want to keep it: no moral difference without some natural difference. I lean on this in the chapter, it's part of how I argue particularism isn't just quietism dressed up.

4. Moral education has to run on rules. Kids get "don't take what isn't yours," "if you break it you fix it." And I don't think that's a stage to be outgrown — a well brought-up adult still thinks of these things under those headings and there's nothing second-rate about that.

5. Some features I don't think ever flip. That it would be cruel always counts against, as far as I can tell. That it would give someone pleasure always counts for something. I'm not embarassed about this, because I don't think those are principles in the sense 2 rejects — they're not the kind of thing you could apply mechanically.

6. Moral testimony is defective. If I end up believing some act was wrong just because a person I trust said so, then even if it was wrong and even if they were right, I've got the belief in the wrong way.

7. At the same time, when I'm out of my depth I think its flat-out rational to go with whoever's been around the block more. There was a thing this term where I took my advisor's word that a joke in a footnote was out of line and cut it, without ever seeing what was wrong with it myself, and I don't think I did anything epistemically shady.

The ones I feel totally solid about are 3, 5 and 6. Those strike me as obviously right and I've never understood why anyone would push on them. I honestly have no idea where the trouble is in this set, or whether there is any.

So go through the pairs. Which ones actually pull against each other, and how badly — rank them, don't hand me a flat list where everything is equally alarming. Which ones only look like they're in tension and aren't, and say why not, because I want to stop worrying about those. For each real tension, what has to give and what does giving it up cost me — some of these I'd fight hard to keep, and you should tell me the price rather than assume I'll pay it. Then tell me which single revision buys the most cleanup. Be concrete: point at the numbered claims and say what has to change in them, don't wave at the general spirit of the view.
