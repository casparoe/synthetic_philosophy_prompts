I cover neuroscience and the philosophy of mind for a general-interest science magazine, and I am reporting a feature built around pain asymbolia: the condition, typically following damage to the left hemisphere in the region of the insula and cingulate, and sometimes seen after certain psychosurgical procedures or under opioid analgesia, in which patients report that they still feel the pain and can localize it and rate its intensity, but are not bothered by it. They do not guard the injured limb, do not dread the next examination, and on occasion laugh while describing what they are feeling. My editor has approved an interactive sidebar posing the question this condition raises in its sharpest form — what exactly is bad about pain, and does asymbolia show that the badness comes apart from the feel?

Please build me the instrument that answers it: a decision tree that sorts a reader into a position on the unpleasantness of pain, together with the case-based questions that form its branch nodes and a scoring key. Three parts.

**Part one: the branch claims.** Construct the tree from the logical space rather than from a list of theorists. I want the splitting questions drawn from claims that are genuinely independent of one another, roughly along these lines — but tell me if you think a better set exists, or if two of these collapse into one:

- whether the asymbolic patient's own report should be taken at face value: is there a real sensation present that the patient is strangely indifferent to, or is the report itself compromised?
- where the badness lives: in a phenomenal quality, in representational content about bodily damage, in a motivational push, or in the subject's attitude toward a feeling they are having.
- whether whatever analysis is right for pain generalizes to other unpleasant states — nausea, itch, breathlessness — or whether pain is a special case.
- whether deliberately injuring someone who will register the injury without minding it wrongs them, and how strongly.

**Part two: the questions.** Eight to eleven items, each a concrete situation with two or three options, written for a reader who has never encountered the word asymbolia and should never be asked anything resembling "do you think pain is intrinsically bad?" Sort people by what they say about cases, not by which label they prefer. The set must include: a case asking whether an unbothered patient is harmed by an injury nobody minds; a parallel case involving a non-pain unpleasantness, such as a nausea stripped of its aversiveness; a case in which the subject's introspective access is itself in doubt, so that the reader has to decide whether to trust the report; a case of ordinary endurance — someone who says the pain is there and awful but that they do not mind it, the way an athlete or a person in labor might — which your instrument should be able to distinguish from genuine dissociation; and a case about whether one could be simply wrong about how much one is suffering.

**Part three: the leaves and the key.** Map answer patterns onto positions: views on which an intrinsic phenomenal quality carries the badness; the view that pain is a perception or evaluation of bodily damage; the view that pain is something like a demand to protect a body part; the dispositional view on which pain is whatever is apt to produce the desire that it stop; attitudinal views that locate unpleasantness in displeasure taken toward a sensation; the two-state view on which there is a sensory-discriminative pain and an affective one and the badness belongs to the second; and the deflationary option on which there is no such thing as asymbolia for pain at all, the reports being artifacts of language loss, impaired self-knowledge, or damage to the concepts needed to describe suffering. Name each leaf where a standard name exists and write "unnamed" where nobody defends the combination. Every leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment. Then note the combinations of answers that come out unoccupied and diagnose each: incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Finally, flag the pairs of leaves your instrument cannot separate rather than papering over them. I suspect the evaluation-of-damage view and the demand-or-command view will generate nearly identical answers to anything a lay reader can be asked; if that is right, say so plainly, and tell me what sort of question would in principle pull them apart. Do not attach references or a reading list — I will fact-check names and sources myself before publication, so it is better that you give me none than give me any you are not certain of. Roughly twelve hundred words is fine; I will cut it down.
