I run a small interdisciplinary seminar on historical explanation and we've spent three weeks on the "would there have been a Nazi Germany without Hitler" question and its gone nowhere. The weird part is that both sides agree on the Weimar facts — hyperinflation, the Depression, the emergency-decree presidency, proportional representation, the politics of the army, how deep antisemitism ran. Nobody disputes any of it. The structuralists say those conditions made some authoritarian revanchist regime close to inevitable; the great-man people say Hitler was doing the work at each fork: getting appointed chancellor in a coalition that was supposed to contain him, settling the Röhm question, then the decision to invade the USSR. And nobody budges.

My working hypothesis is that this is really three arguments stacked on top of each other:

(a) a hidden disagreement about what the counterfactual is asking — is "Nazi Germany" a regime type or one particular token event, and does the answer flip depending on which you mean;
(b) a disagreement about which closeness metric you're allowed — how much downstream difference you hold fixed before it stops being the same question;
(c) a disagreement about whether "would have" means something like objective probability above a threshold, or something like what a defensible historical narrative licenses you to say.

Sort out which of those is actually load-bearing, tell me whether any of them is a real substantive dispute rather than people answering different questions under one label, and say what would count as progress here — a definition, a modelling move, an argument from cases — or whether the question is just ill-posed and the seminar should pick something else.
