The draft, condensed but keeping its own wording and order:

[1] Physicalism says every fact is either a physical fact or is metaphysically necessitated by the physical facts. So the way to attack it is to find a possible world that duplicates ours physically but not phenomenally. Chalmers's zombies are that: beings physically identical to us down to the microphysics and the laws, functionally identical, behaving exactly as we do, with nothing it is like to be them. If such a world is conceivable, and conceivability is a guide to possibility, physicalism is false, since physicalism is supposed to hold of necessity.

[2] Chalmers runs this on the model of Kripke's account of a posteriori necessity. Water = H2O is necessary but could only be found out empirically; likewise pain = C-fiber firing would be a necessary a posteriori identity, and Chalmers argues this is exactly the right thing to say about psychophysical identities — which is why the possibility of zombies has to be established empirically rather than from the armchair.

[3] The knowledge argument is the same argument in different clothes. Mary knows every physical fact about color vision and has never seen red; when she is released she learns something, so there are facts the physical facts don't fix. Take the story away and the premise is the one the zombie argument needs: you can have all the physical and functional information and still be missing something, and what's missing is what the zombie lacks. So the two stand or fall together, and they fall at the same place.

[4] The place is a single move. Call it the acquaintance fallacy. To conceive of a situation as lacking X you have to be able to hold X in mind, and holding a phenomenal property in mind requires acquaintance with it. So the arguer reasons: I cannot fully conceive of the property except through acquaintance with it; therefore I can conceive of a world without it. That's a slide from a fact about the conceiver to a fact about the world. Nagel's bat arguement makes the same slide — we can't grasp what it's like to be a bat, so there must be a fact about bats a complete physical account leaves out. Same with inverted spectra.

[5] Which means the hard problem is not an ontological problem about what the world contains but a conceptual one about what a certain kind of concept can license. Physicalism survives, not because zombies are impossible, but because the inference that was supposed to show they are is fallacious. It also explains why the debate is stuck: both sides grant that if you can conceive it it's possible, when what needed arguing was that anybody had concieved it.

[6] Chalmers sees the epiphenomenalism problem coming and turns it into a premise for his own view. If zombies are possible, my zombie twin says and writes exactly what I do about qualia; so my talk about qualia can't be caused by the physical facts, and since qualia are causally inert it isn't caused by them either. That coincidence, he says, is the thing physicalism has no answer to, and it is why we should accept dualism.

[7] So the two arguments are one argument, and it fails for a reason that has nothing to do with whether zombies are possible.

One thing the instructor will specifically look for: whether [4]–[5] is doing any work that isn't already done by a reply to Chalmers that's on the table, or whether it's genuinely a new diagnosis. Say which, and say what the student would have to add to make it new.
