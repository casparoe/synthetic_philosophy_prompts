I am one of the lay members of the Standing Committee on Official Acknowledgement in the Republic of Marvane, and last night a friend of mine — a barrister who reads our published reports for pleasure — and I argued about what rule this committee is actually running until well past one in the morning. Neither of us budged. I should like a careful third opinion, and I want it argued rather than diplomatically split.

Some orientation. The republic was proclaimed in 1946. Its constitution expressly repudiates the National Directorate, the collaborationist regime of 1940–45, and disclaims succession to it; it says nothing about the Kingdom that preceded it, whose archives, treaty holdings and territorial administration the republic inherited. The committee, established in 2004, advises the cabinet on whether the state should make a formal apology for a past wrong and on what form the acknowledgment should take — apology, apology with a compensation fund, memorial, educational endowment, or nothing. It cannot itself award money.

Our four decided petitions:

1. 2008, granted; apology with a compensation fund. Compulsory sterilisations under a public-health statute enacted in 1938. In 1954 the constitutional court held the statute's compulsory provisions invalid as exceeding the enabling act; the ministry nonetheless continued to apply them until 1962. There were 214 identifiable living survivors, most of them sterilised after the annulment. The responsible ministry is the direct predecessor of today's health ministry. No party in the assembly defends the programme.

2. 2011, refused. Dispossession and forced relocation of highland pastoral communes between 1871 and 1886 by the Kingdom's provincial administration, in breach of the communal tenure guaranteed by the Kingdom's Fundamental Law of 1849. No living person had been an adult participant; the petitions were brought by descendants' associations, two of which have title claims pending in the civil courts. The committee gave two grounds together: that there was no one to whom an apology could be addressed, and that the question of title was before the courts.

3. 2014, granted; apology with an educational endowment and a day of remembrance, no individual payments. Internment of a coastal minority in 1943–45 by decree of the National Directorate — lawful within the Directorate's own legal order, inconsistent with a minority-protection treaty the state ratified in 1931 and never denounced. About forty internees were then living, and the claimant association asked for remembrance rather than payments.

4. 2017, refused. Compulsory resettlement of three valley villages between 1968 and 1979 for a hydroelectric scheme. The displaced are living and mostly still in the country; the responsible body is today's water and energy authority; the resettlement complied with a statute duly passed by the assembly, and the affected communes' own councils voted for it at the time; compensation was paid then, at a fraction of present values. Again two grounds together: that the policy was lawful and democratically authorised when carried out, and that its merits remain live, a regional party holding nineteen seats maintaining that the scheme was a legitimate public benefit and objecting to its description as a wrong.

The petition we decide next month. Between 1958 and 1964 the revenue service seized and auctioned the boats and gear of a fishing community in Sarnet Bay, on the footing that its customary licence had lapsed, under an administrative circular that was never published or laid before the assembly. The file contains three things. The service's own legal adviser wrote in 1960 that the circular exceeded the enabling statute and that seizures under it were unlawful; the memo went to no minister and surfaced only in 2019. In 1963 one skipper sued and won: the court held the seizure of his vessel unlawful, awarded nominal damages, and the judgment was never applied to the others. And two men who were teenage deckhands on the confiscated boats are alive, both in their early eighties; one of them was held nine days in the service's cells for obstructing an auction and was never charged. The skippers and owners are all dead, and the petition is brought by a descendants' cooperative.

Two features complicate it. The bay's licences are the subject of a bill now before the assembly that would give the cooperative a preferential allocation; the offshore fleet association opposes the bill, and a party holding eleven seats has said publicly that the seizures were part of a legitimate rationalisation of an overfished stock and that it will resist any official apology as an attempt to prejudice the bill. No litigation is pending. And the two survivors disagree: one wants the apology; the other has asked us to say nothing while the bill is alive, on the ground that an apology would sink it.

Where my friend and I are stuck. He thinks the committee is a lawfulness body: it apologises when the state's own law was broken and somebody is alive to receive the acknowledgment, and refusals happen when one of those conditions fails. I think it is a settlement body: it apologises only where the past has stopped being an argument, and the lawfulness language in our reports is decoration. We agree that nothing turns on succession — petition 2 concerned an inherited predecessor and was refused, petition 3 a repudiated one and was granted — and we agree that neither lawfulness nor living addressees suffices on its own, since 2011 had the lawfulness and 2017 had the addressees. We also disagree about whether the deckhands count: he says they were employees and not the persons whose property was taken, so that on his own rule the petition has no addressee at all; I say nine days in a cell without charge is being subjected to the wrong.

What I want from you. First, set out the candidate rules that all four decided petitions leave open — I expect there are more than the two of us have been defending — and say what each commits us to on the pending petition. Be explicit about which hypotheses the record has already eliminated, and about whether the committee's habit of stating two grounds at once is precisely what makes the underdetermination unavoidable. Second, give me your single best prediction of how the committee rules next month, with your reasons for preferring it, including how much weight the fact that the surviving rules have never yet disagreed should be allowed to carry. Third, describe a further petition — by its features rather than its subject matter — that would settle which rule we are running, and explain why none of the four decided petitions can do that work. Fourth, and separately: if I have to draft the recommendation, which of the four is the closest analog to this one, what ruling and what form of words would cohere best with our own record, and what principle would we be adopting rather than merely applying if we go my way instead of his? Deferral is on the table, and I want to be told whether deferring is itself a substantive commitment or a way of avoiding one.
