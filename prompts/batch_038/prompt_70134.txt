Thirty-five years in a water quality lab — mostly flame photometry and chromatography — and since I stopped going in I've been reading philosophy of science badly and out of order. Last week I finished a popular book about how scientific reasoning works, by a science writer who clearly did his homework, and the chapter on evidence had a long section on Hempel's ravens. I'm not going to retype the whole thing, so here's the argument compressed into his own phrasing as much as I could manage; where I've squashed a paragraph into a sentence I've kept the claims he actually makes.

"Hempel's raven paradox is the classic demonstration that common sense about evidence can't be trusted. Start with the hypothesis that all ravens are black. Logic tells us this is exactly the same claim as its contrapositive: everything that is not black is not a raven. And evidence that supports one formulation of a claim has to support the other, since they are the same claim. Now, your white sneaker is a thing that is not black and is not a raven, so it supports the contrapositive, and therefore it supports the hypothesis that all ravens are black. By the same token, since the two formulations are one claim, the support they recieve can't depend on which side you look at: a white sneaker and a black raven are equally good evidence, and anyone who says otherwise is quietly treating the same sentence as two different propositions.

Hempel took this as a reductio of the whole project of giving a purely logical account of evidence — if your rules of confirmation tell you that you can learn about birds from your footwear, the rules have to go. His way out was to make confirmation quantitative. Because there are incomparably more non-ravens than ravens, each individual non-raven you happen to inspect shifts the probability of the hypothesis by a vanishingly tiny amount, while each black raven shifts it by a much larger amount. Nothing paradoxical there: it's just that the tiny shifts are too small for us to notice, and we mistake a very small quantity for zero. The lesson for working scientists is a practical one — always ask how big the pool of things you could have looked at is. An observation is only worth much if it rules out a large fraction of the alternatives, which is why negative results matter enormously when you're screening a small set of candidates and are worth nothing at all when the field of candidates is effectively the whole universe."

I'd like an accuracy audit of this, because I liked the chapter and I don't want to absorb a muddle along with the good parts. My own hunch is that the sneaker business is overcooked and that the last paragraph doesn't follow from anything before it, but my gut here is worth about nothing.

Specifically: take the argument apart sentence by sentence and tell me what someone who works on confirmation theory would accept without complaint, what they'd wince at, and what's simply wrong. Then a few things I keep coming back to. Is the population-size story actually Hempel's own solution, or did he do something else and the book has handed him a later fix? If he did something else, I want the something else spelled out — in particular what work the background knowledge is doing, because the book never mentions that idea at all and something feels missing. On the equal-evidence line: the book seems to think that because the two formulations are logically equivalent, a black raven and a white sneaker must carry the same evidential weight. I can't tell whether that's a legitimate inference from the equivalence principle or a slide between two different things that get called the same name. Relatedly, the book mentions in passing that Hempel also had an example involving holding a piece of pure ice in a colorless flame and noting that it doesn't turn yellow, which he relates to a claim about sodium salts burning yellow. I spent three decades with a flame in front of me, so that's the one example in the chapter I understand completely, and I cannot see what it's for. Was it meant to show that the ice fails to confirm the sodium-salt claim, or the opposite, or neither?

Two more. A former student of mine who went on to do a philosophy degree says there's a well-known case where the shoe counts against the hypothesis rather than for it, depending on what else you already know; on the book's account, where the answer is entirely about how many non-ravens there are, is that even possible, and what would have to be true for it to happen? And finally, does the practical lesson survive once the errors are corrected — is there anything in the raven paradox that tells a bench scientist when a negative result is worth having and when it isn't, or has the book bolted a plausible-sounding moral onto an argument that doesn't support it?
