I write about science and ideas for a magazine and I'm working a piece on the "just-so stories" fight over evolutionary psychology. My main source kept reaching for the same image the whole interview: the mind isn't one general-purpose learning machine, it's a Swiss Army knife — a bundle of seperate tools, each shaped for one job. He uses the knife to do three things. (1) If a behaviour shows up in every culture, that's evidence of a dedicated mechanism rather than a learned one. (2) You can read off what a mechanism is for from how its built, so reverse-engineering from present-day function back to ancestral job description is legitimate. (3) His critics are just assuming the general-purpose mind they're supposed to be arguing against. A developmental psychologist I also talked to says the knife is carrying all the weight and falls apart if you look at it.

Can you play philosopher of biology who's spent real time on modularity here? Brainstorm a bit before you settle on a structure, and think carefully about which disanalogies actually do damage. I want: the correspondence laid out (what plays the blade, the corkscrew, the factory, the moment of manufacture, the handle); the disanalogies sorted into ones that bite on his three uses versus cosmetic ones; which of the three uses survive once the disanalogies are in, and whether they survive on some footing other than the analogy; what the source quietly imports — a designer with foresight, tools pre-formed rather than grown, one tool one job, a fixed inventory — and whether that's what's in dispute rather than what both sides grant; and if the knife is bad, a better analogy, plus whether the better one helps or hurts him. Last thing: does the just-so objection bite harder on a Swiss-Army-knife mind than on a general-purpose one? No citations or reading list needed.
