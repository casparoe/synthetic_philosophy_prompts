I TA a second-year interdisciplinary seminar at my uni called "Life, Mind and Machines" — 14 students, all bio majors except two pre-meds who are extremely confident. They've each done a semester of molecular biology, so transcription/translation, triplet codons, start and stop signals and the fact that DNA polymerase needs a whole crew of proteins before anything gets copied are all totally comfortable territory for them. What they have never met in their lives is a formal system: no axioms, no rules of inference, no Gödel numbering, and if you said "isomorphism" to them they'd hear "kind of similar". I've got the fifty-minute section next Thursday, whiteboard only (the prof dislikes slides and will be sitting at the back), and my assigned topic is the Typogenetics material in Gödel, Escher, Bach and the DNA–Gödel isomorphism Hofstadter builds out of it. I read GEB over the summer and I could follow the strand-tracing while I was doing it, but I couldn't tell you cold why the loop is the actual point rather than a cute party trick, which is precisely the thing I'm suppsed to be teaching.

The trouble is that the book's presentation order assumes the mirror image of my students. Hofstadter's reader has been grinding through string games and a formal arithmetic for hundreds of pages and knows nothing about real cells; mine know real cells and nothing about string games. So the usual order doesn't transfer and I don't know what to put first instead. There's also a specific landmine I can already see coming: the Typogenetic Code reads duplets, not triplets, and gives you 15 little command-amino-acids plus the AA punctuation duplet. The second I write that up, atleast three of them will say "that's wrong, codons are three bases," and if I fumble the response they'll file the whole chapter under bad biology and stop listening to me for the rest of the hour. The opposite failure is equally likely — one guy has read some pop-science Gödel piece and will try to steer everything toward "so DNA shows the mind can't be a machine, right?" I want the session to land the claim that seems to sit between those two: not that cells are computers, and not that it's all just a metaphor.

So please don't explain Typogenetics back to me, I've read it — plan the session for me. Give it to me in these sections, in this order:

1. **Dependency spine** (~250 words): the order the pieces have to go in, and for each piece one line on what it depends on, i.e. why it can't come any earlier.
2. **The carrying strand** (~200 words): the single example the whole fifty minutes hangs on. Tell me what it has to have and what it has to lack in order to survive being traced by hand on a whiteboard in front of fourteen people, whether I should use Hofstadter's own self-replication puzzle from that chapter or cook up a shorter one, and why not the obvious alternatives.
3. **Misconception map** (~350 words): each misconception they'll walk in with or form in the room — I'm counting on the triplets-vs-duplets one, plus "the enzyme understands the strand it grabs" and "self-replication just means getting copied" — with the point in the sequence where it surfaces and the specific move that heads it off before it sets.
4. **The check** (~100 words): one question I ask at the end, and what an answer that shows the idea landed sounds like versus one that just plays my vocabulary back at me.
5. **Cut list** (~100 words): what I leave out entirely at this level, and the sentence I say out loud about having left it out.

No readings or citations, they already have the chapter and I can't hand anything else out.
