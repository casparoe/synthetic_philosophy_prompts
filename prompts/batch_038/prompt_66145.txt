I am trying to settle an argument with a colleague, and I have come to suspect that the argument is less about the world than about what one pair of sentences is actually claiming.

In a seminar we were discussing two quite different proposals: (a) that the state should acquire and hold a large stock of a fixed-supply cryptoasset, on the ground that scarcity makes it a genuine store of national wealth; and (b) that the monetary authority should expand the money stock substantially to close what some of us took to be a shortfall of aggregate demand. My colleague dismissed both with a single gesture toward Hume's *Political Discourses* of 1752, quoting the opening of the essay "Of Money":

> Money is not, properly speaking, one of the subjects of commerce; but only the instrument which men have agreed upon to facilitate the exchange of one commodity for another. It is none of the wheels of trade: It is the oil which renders the motion of the wheels more smooth and easy.

and then the sentence a few lines later:

> If we consider any one kingdom by itself, it is evident, that the greater or less plenty of money is of no consequence; since the prices of commodities are always proportioned to the plenty of money, and a crown in Harry VII.'s time served the same purpose as a pound does at present.

His conclusion was that both proposals commit the same mercantilist confusion: they treat the medium of exchange as though it were itself an object of commerce.

What I want is a disambiguation, not a survey of Hume's monetary thought. Please lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that the oil-and-wheels image together with the "of no consequence" sentence can be made to express. For each reading, tell me: what exactly is being asserted; whether it is a conceptual or definitional thesis, an empirical regularity, or a policy prescription; what its scope conditions are — a closed economy or an open one, a one-off change in the level of the money stock or a sustained change in its rate of growth, specie convertible into commodities or credit money, the interval before new money has circulated through all ranks of people or the situation after it has; whether it is a platitude, substantive and defensible, genuinely contested, false, or true of Hume's object but not of ours; and, decisively, whether it licenses the dismissal of proposal (a), of proposal (b), of both, or of neither. Where a reading does license a dismissal, say what further premises my colleague needs in order to get from it to his conclusion, since I doubt the slogan alone gets him there.

Two specific problems I would like you to take up. First, what content does the metaphor itself carry? Calling money oil rather than a wheel seems to exclude something, but it is not obvious what: a store of value, an asset whose holding yields liquidity services, an object of demand in its own right, a source of seigniorage. Is there a determinate claim there, or is the image doing its work purely by connotation — lubricant, therefore no independent worth — which is not an argument? Second, my colleague treats the proportionality of prices to the quantity of money as analytic, but in "Of the Balance of Trade" Hume argues for it by thought experiment: suppose four-fifths of all the money in Great Britain annihilated in one night, and prices must sink in proportion, cheapness abroad draws the money back, "and the farther flowing in of money is stopped by our fulness and repletion." That looks like a claim about an adjustment mechanism — with premises about price flexibility, about real output and the demand for money being unchanged, and about arbitrage in goods and specie across borders — rather than a claim about meanings. Please separate the mechanism from the slogan and say which of the mechanism's premises would have to fail for the slogan's strong readings to fail.

I would also like an honest assessment of the tension inside the essay itself. The same essay argues against artificially enlarging paper credit, floats a public bank that "locked up all the money it received" and never returned it to circulation, and observes that only the public draws advantage from a greater plenty of money, and then only in its wars and foreign negotiations — which does not sound like neutrality for a state among states. If I remember rightly, Hume elsewhere recommends that the magistrate keep the quantity increasing where possible, on the ground that this keeps a spirit of industry alive; please say whether that is right and, if it is, how it sits with the sentences my colleague quoted. Which of these positions can Hume hold consistently, and does that tell us anything about whether the quoted passage is his considered doctrine or a rhetorically compressed opening?

Finally: which reading is my colleague in fact relying on, is he entitled to it, and — this is the part I keep circling — is the fixed-supply asset case even inside the slogan's scope at all? Something acquired precisely in order to be held rather than spent looks less like oil than like a commodity in a vault, which may cut against his dismissal rather than for it. I would like the readings as a numbered list I could bring back to the seminar, followed by a short assessment of where the argument actually stands. I want your reasoning here, not a list of sources.
