I am preparing a graduate seminar paper on the limits of Bayesian idealisation, and I have written down the commitments I actually hold. Before I present them I should like a consistency audit, because I suspect that something in the list will not survive contact with the rest — but I genuinely cannot tell which, and I have no view in advance about what I ought to give up. Here they are:

(1) Degrees of belief are governed by the probability axioms. Coherence is the constitutive norm of credal rationality, and an incoherent credence set is defective, full stop.
(2) Part of what coherence demands is probability 1 for every logical truth.
(3) The problem of logical omniscience is a substantive problem rather than a quibble: a theory on which every ideally rational agent knows all of arithmetic has got something wrong.
(4) What it gets wrong concerns how propositions are individuated. Supply a sufficiently fine-grained account of content and the problem dissolves; the coarse possible-worlds picture of propositions is the culprit.
(5) It is also, independently, a matter of computational limits: agents lack the time and memory to derive remote consequences, and no amount of semantic refinement alters that.
(6) The only legitimate basic rule of belief revision is conditionalisation on what one comes to know. Anything else is a stopgap.
(7) Working through a proof is a paradigm case of rational learning: an agent's credence in a theorem she has just proved should rise, and she is to be commended for the change.
(8) No empirical proposition ever merits credence 1, however strong the evidence.
(9) It is appropriate to criticise an agent who believes p and believes q yet declines p∧q; it is not appropriate to criticise an agent who fails to see that a theorem follows from axioms she accepts.
(10) Norms of rationality must be usable by the agents they govern: a standard that bounded agents cannot even approximate in deliberation is not a norm for them.
