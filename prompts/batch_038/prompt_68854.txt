I am going to compare diets — plant-based, chicken-heavy, beef-heavy — by the animal suffering they cause, and before I look at anyone's numbers I want the specification of what such a comparison has to do to be any good. Write the desiderata: the questions an adequate account of a diet's animal-harm footprint must answer; the distinctions it must not collapse (at minimum: the number of animals killed versus the badness of the lives those killings cut short; the average harm associated with a food versus the marginal harm of one person shifting toward or away from it; harms the food system inflicts on the animals it raises versus the condition of the wild animals living on the land it occupies); how it is to trade a death against a day of suffering, and a day in the life of one species against a day in the life of another; the empirical constraints it must respect — that grazing land and cropland are not freely interchangeable, that much of the crop harvest goes to feed and fuel rather than to people, that estimates of the small animals killed per hectare of cultivation differ by orders of magnitude — and the normative commitments it must state openly rather than assume. For each requirement give the case that motivates it, and mark which are non-negotiable for an account meant to guide a single person's choice of what to eat and which are the preferences of a particular camp. Then tell me which one requirement, applied consistently, does the most to overturn the rankings people usually arrive at, and whether any pair of requirements cannot be jointly satisfied: I suspect that demanding robustness to the uncertainty about wild animal populations conflicts with demanding that the calculus represent harms in proportion to their true scale, but I want to know whether that suspicion survives scrutiny.
