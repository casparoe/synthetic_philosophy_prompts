**Nothing Is About Anything (Including Searle's Brain)**

Everyone says there are two kinds of aboutness. Derived intentionality belongs to things like words on a page, road signs, the patterns a computer keeps in memory: they're about stuff only because somebody with a mind treats them that way. Original intentionality belongs to minds themselves, and it's not supposed to need anybody's help. Searle's whole campaign is that you can never build the first kind out of the second, because symbols can only inherit their meaning from an interpreter, and interpreters have to have original intentionality already.

Dennett attacks this with the two-bitser. Roughly: take a tiny physical device with just two states, something like a flip-flop, and wire it into a circuit. In one setup its state A counts as 0 and its state B counts as 1. Move the device somewhere else — replug it, invert a connection, embed it in a different machine — and now A counts as 1 and B counts as 0. Nothing intrinsic to the device changed. Same two physical states, same voltages, same everything you could measure on the device alone. What changed was its context, and the context is what settles what the states are about. So the physical state never carried its own meaning; it was always borrowed from the arrangement it happens to sit in. Dennett's punchline is that this is equally true of neurons and of whole people, just with vastly more of them and vastly more arrangement, so there's no clean line where derived aboutness stops and original aboutness begins. It's one gradient all the way up, and calling the top of the gradient original is just a compliment we pay ourselves.

My argument is that Searle's side actually sets up Dennett's conclusion better than Dennett does. This sounds backwards, so here goes.

Searle's reason the two-bitser doesn't worry him is that the meaning lives in the interpreter, not in the device. Fine, grant it. But then ask what makes an interpreter an interpreter. Searle's answer is that the interpreter has original intentionality — states that are about things without borrowing that aboutness. Notice that original is doing all the work here by definition. It's just the name for whatever ends up at the top of the borrowing chain. It isn't an independent property you could go and detect, or test for, or find in one system and not another. So the debate quietly turns into a single question: is there anything at the top of the chain?

And that's where Searle is in trouble. Suppose we hand him his brain. The brain is a physical object sitting in a physical universe. Who is interpreting the brain's states? Not other people. If my belief that it's raining were about rain only because you or I read it that way, then it wouldn't survive my being alone on a deserted island, and obviously it would. Not the brain itself — unless Searle wants to say a system can interpret its own states, which is precisely the circular move he says a running program can't make. So: nobody. And if nobody is interpreting them, then by Searle's own standard — a physical state is about something only if something interprets it as being about that thing — his brain states aren't about anything either. Searle is left with two options. Either he accepts that there is no such thing as original intentionality, which is Dennett's conclusion. Or he accepts that being-about doesn't actually require an interpreter, which deletes the only reason he had for insisting that a two-bitser can't genuinely be about something.

The example I like for this: imagine a recording studio that's been running for a hundred years with nobody inside. The reels capture the state of the room continuously, and if you ever played them back you could reconstruct exactly what happened — the chair scraping, rain on the roof, a bird that flew in once. Those tapes are about the room in a real sense. There's a fact of the matter about whether a given stretch of tape matches what went on. But nobody interpreted them, not once, not ever. Searle wants to say the tapes are only potentially about the room, waiting for a listener. I want to say: either potentially-about is a genuine kind of aboutness, in which case there are at least three grades here and not two and the whole original/derived contrast was a simplification, or it isn't a genuine kind, in which case the tapes are simply about the room with no interpreter, and whatever is true of them is true of a neuron and of a person.

Conclusion: the original/derived distinction isn't a distinction between two kinds of content, one self-sufficient and one borrowed. It's a distinction between content that happens to have an interpreter standing by and content that doesn't. Since almost nothing in the universe has an interpreter standing by, either everything counts as derived — at which point the word has stopped doing any work — or nothing does. Searle's argument against strong AI needs the second horn, and I don't think it's available to him.
