I am preparing a handout for a graduate seminar in the history of economic thought, and I would like a decision tree that sorts students by what they actually believe about the socialist calculation debate, rather than by which camp they say they belong to. The problem I am trying to solve is that students arrive having absorbed a three-line summary — Mises said rational calculation under socialism is impossible, Hayek said the issue is dispersed knowledge, Lange said trial and error by a planning board solves it — and they then self-identify with one of those names before they have articulated a single commitment of their own. Names at the root of a tree let them do exactly that, so the instrument must be built the other way round.

Please construct it in three parts.

First, lay out the logical space explicitly before drawing any branches. I want the debate decomposed along at least the following axes, which you should refine, rename, or extend if you think my list is malformed:

1. The nature of the binding obstacle to rational allocation without market prices for the means of production. Distinguish at minimum: an informational obstacle (the relevant data are dispersed and cannot be collected); a computational obstacle (the data could in principle be collected but cannot be processed in the time the economy allows); an incentive obstacle (both are feasible, but no actor gains by reporting truthfully or acting on the result); a discovery obstacle (the relevant data do not exist anywhere yet, because they are generated only by rivalry and experiment, so allocation is not a problem of finding a known answer); and a conceptual obstacle (the claim that without private exchange in capital goods there is simply no determinate cost, so "calculation" is not hard but undefined).
2. The scope of the claim being defended: static allocation of a given resource bundle at a moment in time; dynamic allocation under change and novelty; or comparative institutional performance, where both systems are assumed to be imperfect.
3. The modal status of the conclusion: impossibility in principle, severe impracticability, or comparative inferiority.

Second, write the tree itself. Around twelve to fifteen nodes, each phrased as a single concrete case or claim that a student can answer yes, no, or "not sure" without knowing which leaf it leads toward, and without having read the primary texts. No node may contain a proper name, a date, or a school label. Please build the nodes out of scenarios of this general kind, adapting or replacing them as you see fit: a planning board required to fix the relative prices of twelve million distinct commodities within one planning year; a plant manager who privately knows a machine is about to fail but has no channel that makes it worthwhile to say so; a firm of two hundred thousand employees that allocates internally by managerial direction rather than by internal prices, and the question of whether whatever argument condemns a national plan also condemns that firm; an auctioneer who reaches equilibrium by announcing prices and adjusting, with no actual market anywhere; a producer who discovers demand for a good nobody had previously described; an industry with natural-monopoly cost structure that even capitalist economies regulate administratively; a large retailer that runs its own internal allocation system without internal markets; and the practice of setting next year's output target from whatever the plant actually delivered last year.

Third, the leaves. There should be between eight and twelve, and the following positions must each appear somewhere, whether or not you keep my labels: the conceptual impossibility view about capital-goods prices; the dispersed-knowledge view; the competition-as-discovery-procedure view; market socialism in which a board sets parametric prices by trial and error and instructs managers to minimize cost and price at marginal cost; the hybrid holding that the market-socialist answer is formally correct but practically unworkable for reasons the formal model excludes; the modern computational-planning view that labor-time accounting and current computing power dissolve the calculation objection; a mechanism-design view on which an incentive-compatible scheme can aggregate what prices aggregate; the internal-planning reductio about the firm; the comparative-institutions view on which both systems fail and the only question is which fails better; and the view that the debate was settled empirically rather than theoretically. Each leaf should carry a short name, one sentence stating its most awkward commitment — the bullet its defender has to bite — and one sentence on the strongest reply available to it.

Then please do three things that handouts usually omit. Identify at least two pairs of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and say why the nodes fail to distinguish them rather than pretending otherwise. Identify at least three cells in the logical space your axes define that end up unoccupied, and for each say whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is unattractive to defend, or because nobody has simply explored it. Finally, append a short note separating what is settled from what is genuinely contested among economists and historians of economics who know this literature well — for instance, what the original impossibility argument was actually claiming, whether the trial-and-error model answered it or accepted its terms, whether the argument generalizes from the capital-goods case to the whole economy, and whether the collapse of the planned economies in Eastern Europe counted as theoretical evidence. Mark clearly which of my stipulated axes and leaves rest on a contested reading, and if any position I have asked you to include is not in fact a coherent position, say so instead of forcing it onto a branch. I do not want a bibliography or reading list; the deliverable is the classification instrument and the note on its own reliability.
