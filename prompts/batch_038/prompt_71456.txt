ok, me and my flatmate had a proper two hour arguement over dinner last night and neither of us budged, so I'm drafting in a dead philosopher as a tiebreaker. Here's the raw material we were fighting about: we'd both just read that Appiah book about honor codes and moral revolutions — the one that does the British duel, Chinese footbinding and the Atlantic slave trade as its three big cases. His pitch, roughly as I've got it, is that in each case the argument against the practice had been sitting around for ages without moving anyone, and what finally moved people was that the practice stopped being compatible with the honor of the group doing it: the duelist stopped counting as a gentleman, the Chinese elite stopped being able to see themselves as a civilized nation while binding women's feet, the British stopped being able to square thinking of themselves as a free people with buying and selling Africans. So the practice died when honor got re-pointed, not when somebody won the argument. My flatmate says that means it wasn't moral progress in any meaningful sense — it was a change of fashion with better PR, because at no point did anyone's beliefs get closer to the truth of the matter; the same esteem machinery could just as easily have been re-pointed at something horrible. I said no, that honor is what made the argument effective, that reasons had to travel through social esteem to get anywhere, and that a change brought about by reasons is still a change brought about by reasons.

So: where does Bernard Williams come down? He died before the book, obviously, which is the point — I want a prediction, and I want one that's specifically Williams rather than a generic wise-old-skeptic answer. Commit to a verdict: does he land on my side, on hers, or somewhere neither of us thought of, and what's the single strongest thing he'd say against the side you put him on? I want you to actually use his machinery rather than name-drop it — e.g. his line that ethics doesn't accumulate toward truth the way science does, but that a change can still count as progress when the old practice was propped up by false non-moral beliefs (that slaves are naturally inferior, that a duel demonstrates courage); his insistence that ethical life runs on thick concepts embedded in a social world, which is exactly what honor is; and that distinction he draws between a genealogy of a belief that vindicates it and one that undercuts it. That last one seems to me like the crux — is the honor story a vindicatory or a subversive genealogy of abolition, and does Williams think Appiah even gets to ask that question across two centuries, given how allergic he was to standing outside a form of life and grading it? Also say whether he'd object to the whole project of explaining ethical change by a general mechanism.
