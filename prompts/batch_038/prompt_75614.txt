I am putting together a diagnostic handout for an upper-level seminar on moral development in early Chinese thought. The students will have read the Mencius passages on the four sprouts and on Gaozi, and Xunzi's chapter arguing that human nature is bad, in English translation — but nothing else, and they have no Chinese. Every year they arrive already sorted into camps by which slogan they like, and the discussion collapses into good versus bad nature. I want an instrument that sorts them instead by what they would actually say about concrete cases, so that the camp labels come last rather than first.

Please design it as follows.

Structure. Build the tree from the logical space before you place any thinker in it. Lay out the branch axes, in order, and for each axis state the claim that separates the branches. Then give ten diagnostic items. Each item must be a concrete situation — a child's reaction, an apprenticeship, a punishment policy, a case of someone who does the right thing without being able to say why — with two or three answer options, and each must be answerable by someone who has never met the vocabulary. No item may ask whether human nature is good, bad, mixed, or indifferent, and no item may contain a thinker's name. After the items, give the scoring key mapping answer patterns onto leaves, including guidance for patterns that are incomplete or internally inconsistent: say which leaf such a pattern lands nearest, and which inconsistencies should be treated as philosophically interesting rather than as a scoring error.

Branch axes. The first split must not be the good/bad question. It should turn on what counts as nature at all: everything one is born with and cannot help, versus the characteristic direction a human being develops in under favorable conditions, versus something closer to raw capacity with no direction built in. Downstream, the axes I care about are: whether the material for virtue is already present at birth or has to be brought in from outside; whether cultivation extends and nourishes what is there or restrains and remakes it — the difference between tending a plant and either straightening warped timber or carving a bowl out of wood; whether wrongdoing is the default outcome absent intervention or the loss and corruption of something originally present; whether the sage who establishes ritual and norms discovers a pattern already in things or makes an artifact the way a potter makes a vessel; and whether differences between people reflect one shared nature differently nourished or genuinely different kinds of nature. Add further axes if you think the space needs them, and say why.

Leaves that must appear. The sprout view, on which the beginnings of the virtues are universal and moral failure is a matter of not extending them; the deliberate-activity view, on which goodness is a human achievement imposed on an inborn tendency that left alone leads to strife; the indifference view associated with Gaozi, which I would like handled carefully because his two comparisons — water that can be channeled east or west, and willow wood that can be carved into a vessel — may not in fact belong on the same leaf; the mixed view defended in the Han, on which both strands are in everyone and cultivation decides which is developed; the graded view associated with Dong Zhongshu and taken up later by Han Yu, on which natures differ in kind across people and teaching reaches only some; the Legalist position, on which the whole good/bad framing is idle because people respond reliably to interest and to the threat of punishment, so the design of incentives and laws is the only thing worth arguing about; the subtraction view, on which inborn spontaneity is already adequate and it is the imposition of norms, distinctions, and craft that ruins it; and a leaf for Confucius's own remark that people's natures are near alike and it is their practices that carry them far apart, treated as a deliberately underdetermined starting point rather than as a hidden commitment. Each leaf gets one sentence naming its most awkward commitment.

Empty cells. The axes should generate combinations nobody in this period occupies — nature bad and untransformable, nature good and needing no cultivation, norms invented out of nothing while nature is indifferent, and whatever else falls out. Name at least three such cells and diagnose each: is it incoherent, merely unattractive, foreclosed by assumptions the period shared, or simply unexplored and live today?

Limits. Flag every pair of leaves your instrument cannot reliably separate. In particular, since both Mencius and Xunzi hold that anyone can become a sage, tell me plainly which of your ten items does the separating work between them, and whether that separation depends entirely on your first axis — if so, say so rather than dressing it up as ten independent measurements. Also flag any leaf whose placement rests on a contested reading of what the thinker meant by nature.

Constraints. No bibliography, reading list, or citations; I am not asking you to source anything, only to reason. Keep romanized terms to a minimum and gloss each on first use; no Chinese characters. The whole instrument has to be usable inside a thirty-minute session, so items must be short.

Finally, an epistemic note on your own work. Tell me where you are least confident: which leaf placement you would most expect a specialist to dispute, which branch axis you think is the weakest and why, and what would change your mind — what interpretive consideration, or what pattern of student answers in an actual run of the instrument, would lead you to re-sort a leaf, merge two of them, or redraw a branch. If you think some part of the design is likely to fail as a diagnostic in practice, say which part and what the failure would look like.
