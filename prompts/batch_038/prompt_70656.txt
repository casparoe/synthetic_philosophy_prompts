I'm pitching a piece on Adam Smith and empire and a colleague sent me a graf from some magazine feature she read, which she thinks is basically right:

'Smith's verdict on empire was arithmetic. The colonies cost Britain far more than they returned — the monopoly of the colonial trade fattened a handful of merchants while diverting capital away from employments that would have kept more British labour at work, and the navy bill for holding America was dead weight. His remedy was just as brisk: give the colonies up, immediately and unilaterally, and let free trade pick up where empire had failed. He was no sentimentalist about any of it, and he'd have scrapped the Navigation Acts along with the rest of the mercantile system if anyone had asked him. What Smith conspicuously lacked was any interest in the colonised: on his account slavery and conquest were bad business arrangements rather than moral catastrophes. That's why he now gets claimed as a founding father of anti-colonial thinking — the empire, he said, was a losing proposition for the conqueror.'

Also: he does say Britain derived nothing but loss from dominion over the colonies, but is that the same as saying the colonial trade itself was a loser? And does Smith really have nothing but bookkeeping to say about the people on the receiving end of empire?
