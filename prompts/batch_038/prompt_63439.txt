I am taking a seminar on the metaphysics and ethics of death, and our unit on whether death is bad for the person who dies has produced no movement in three weeks: the Epicureans and the deprivationists agree on all the non-philosophical facts, agree that nonexistence involves no experience, and agree that we ordinarily describe an early death as a misfortune — yet each side reports that the other's arguments beg the question. My working hypothesis, which I would like you to test rather than flesh out, is that there are at least two disagreements here and not one: a substantive one about what harm requires — must a harm be located at some time at which its victim exists and is in some state, or can it consist in her life's being worse than a life she might have had? — and a merely verbal one about whether "bad for S" names a property of S at a time or of S's life as a whole, with each camp tacitly answering the question the other is not asking.

Diagnose the debate on that model, or on a better one, using what each side actually says about the timing problem and about the symmetry between prenatal and postmortem nonexistence. Then say what would count as progress given your diagnosis, and put this case to both camps: Cleo dies at thirty; two centuries later, someone creates a person who is Cleo in every respect she herself would have cared about — memories, projects, character — and that person lives forty more years. Either the badness of Cleo's death is unaffected, in which case explain what the original counterfactual comparison was doing; or it is affected, in which case say at what time Cleo's condition changes. I want a committed diagnosis, argued for, rather than a menu of possibilities, and if you think my framing is itself confused, say where and why.
