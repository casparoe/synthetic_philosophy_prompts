So I write a weekly-ish column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and on the side I do a joke newsletter that mostly goes to people on my beat. Need a piece for the next issue.

Write me a launch/sales page for a self-optimization program that runs your entire life on Kelly-betting logic — call it The Geometric Life, or something in that register. Form: hero copy, The Protocol as a set of numbered rules, a Sample Week, two testimonials, a three-question FAQ, fine print at the bottom. 700-800 words, deadpan all the way through, no winking.

For the first half he should just be right. Get the actual content correct: arithmetic versus geometric mean of a repeated gamble; his signature example is a coin that pays +60% or −40%, which has a +10% expected return but shrinks your bankroll by roughly 2% a round if you bet the whole thing every time; ruin is absorbing, so you cant average your way back from it; and when you don't trust your estimate of the odds you bet a fraction of Kelly. Anyone whos read this literature should be nodding and slightly annoyed at how well it's written. Someone who's never heard of any of it should still find the rules funny on their face.

Then it turns, and the turn is that he keeps applying long-run growth logic to things that happen once and don't repeat. He refuses any gamble with a nonzero chance of total ruin, however small, which is why he wont drive, wont eat shellfish, and has declined to have children — unhedgeable, no exit, no second play. He runs half-Kelly on friendships because the parameter estimates are noisy and there's no backtest. There's a premium tier that leverages up, and the testimonials are where the bodies are. The FAQ eventually concedes that he has never personally lost money, because a family trust is the risk-free leg of everything he does.

Target is the discourse — life-as-a-portfolio optimization content, plus the way people invoke ergodicity to win arguments — not any real person. Every voice quoted is invented; don't put words in a real economist's mouth.

Rules: every number has to actually compute, a reader with a calculator gets the same answer you got; the phrase Kelly criterion appears exactly once, down in the fine print; he's funny because he's consistent, not because he's stupid; and the last line should be a plain detail that lets the reader perform the reductio themselves instead of you spelling it out.
