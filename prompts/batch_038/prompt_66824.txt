I convene a master's-level module on the philosophical foundations of probability, and I am setting the take-home assessment. I need one question on Bertrand's chord problem, worth 25 marks, on which candidates will be advised to spend about ninety minutes and to write no more than 1,000 words. Please write the question, together with everything needed to mark it.

The question must not be answerable by reproducing the familiar catalogue — random endpoints, random radial point, random midpoint, yielding one third, one half and one quarter respectively, capped with the observation that the principle of indifference has been applied to three different parameters. Nearly every script I have marked in the past three years has said exactly that and then stopped, and all of them were awarded the same middling grade. I want a question that pushes candidates past that recitation and into a crux where they have to take a position.

As stimulus material, I intend to hand candidates a transcript of a seminar discussion which runs roughly as follows, and I would like you to write it up properly as part of the question:

Three students have already produced the three classical answers. A fourth points out that the second and third of them are in fact assigning distributions to one and the same quantity — the perpendicular distance from the centre of the circle to the chord — the one making that distance uniform along the radius, the other making the midpoint's position uniform over the area of the disc, which amounts to making the square of the distance uniform rather than the distance itself. A fifth student then declares the whole dispute empty: there is a laboratory apparatus that fires thin rigid rods from a great distance at a circular target, records the chord each rod cuts across it, and will converge, over enough repetitions, on whichever of the answers is right. The question should require candidates to adjudicate among these four positions rather than to describe them.

The specific things I want assessed are these. First, once it is conceded that the distance from the centre is the shared parameter, what could possibly decide between uniformity in that distance and uniformity in its square, other than a demand that the answer be invariant under some specified family of transformations — and whether such a demand is a genuine discovery about the problem or a disguised restatement of one of the original choices. Second, the treatment due to Jaynes, which insists that the problem statement fixes neither the size nor the position of the circle and so requires any defensible answer to be indifferent to both, and which derives a unique distribution from that requirement alone: candidates should confront the fact that this derivation lands on precisely the answer the random-radial-point procedure gives, and say whether that convergence shows the problem has been rendered well-posed or is a coincidence that carries no evidential weight. Third, the apparatus: what would have to be true of it for a limiting frequency to settle the matter at all, whether it is genuinely independent of the invariance argument or quietly implements it, and what becomes of the question if what is at stake is a rational assignment of credence rather than the behaviour of a physical process. Fourth, the generalisation of which the cube-factory style case is the standard illustration — a box labelled with a range for a cube's side length seems to license uniformity over side length, over surface area and over volume simultaneously, which cannot all hold — and whether this generalisation refutes the principle of indifference outright or merely reveals that it was never a principle about parameters in the first place.

Please structure your response in the following sections, in this order:

1. **The question as it will appear on the paper** — up to 350 words, the stimulus transcript included, phrased exactly as candidates will read it.
2. **Model answer** — about 900 words, and within the 1,000-word limit I have set candidates, so that it demonstrates the question is answerable in the time allowed.
3. **Marking scheme** — a table distributing the 25 marks across five named components. For each component, state what full credit requires in terms of the specific argumentative moves a candidate must make, and then give a one-line note on the characteristic error that earns no credit for it. Errors should be the ones candidates actually commit here: treating the three answers as a refutation rather than as an underdetermination result; assuming that naming a physical generating procedure automatically makes the problem well-posed; reading the invariance requirement as an extra empirical hypothesis rather than as a constraint on the question; and sliding between the epistemic and the frequentist reading of the probability without noticing.
4. **The first-class line** — about 150 words on what separates a first from a strong 2:1, stated as a difference in what the answer does rather than in how much of it there is.

Write throughout in British English. Do not include a bibliography, footnotes, page references or any citation apparatus: candidates will have no access to sources during the assessment, and I do not want invented attributions in the marking scheme. Names may appear in the body of the model answer where the argument genuinely requires them, but no marks anywhere in the scheme should be allocated for naming a philosopher rather than for making a point.
