I cover science and research policy for a monthly, and I'm putting together a piece on Imre Lakatos's research programmes — hard core, protective belt, the difference between a problemshift that's still generating novel predictions and one that's just soaking up anomalies. Rather than open with a normal lede I want to open with a short story, call it 900–1200 words.

Keep the setting unglamorous and concrete: not particle physics, not a lab chasing a cure. Something like a soil-carbon group, a traffic-modeling outfit, or a team maintaining a decades-old method for reading climate out of lake sediment. Everyone in the story treats the founding claim as simply not up for discussion, and I want to watch the belt of auxiliary assumptions thicken around it — each patch reasonable on its own, and at least one of them genuinely predicts something new that later shows up in the data. Then the patches start costing more than they explain.

Two things I really want in there. First, nobody is allowed to use any philosophy-of-science vocabulary — no paradigm, no falsified, no degenerating — the structure has to come through in what people actually do and what gets funded. Second, and this is the bit people always skip: what finally turns the room isn't the accumulating anomalies, it's the arrival of a rival programme that accounts for those same anomalies and offers the junior people actual work to do. Make that the hinge of the story, not a subplot.

And please don't make the senior figure a villain or a fool — the dumb-old-guard version of this is boring. Point of view belongs to whoever invented the patch that once saved the core. End on the moment they work out which side of the line their progamme is on, and leave it there.
