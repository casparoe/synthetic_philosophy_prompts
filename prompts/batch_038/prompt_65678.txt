Right, so — I did thirty-odd years of statistical mechanics, retired in the spring, and to stop the grey matter going soft I've been working through moral philosophy, which turns out to be a good deal less tidy than I'd been lead to believe. I've built an argument entirely out of habits from my old job. I'm fairly sure it's wrong somewhere. My reading group has been unhelpful in the particular way reading groups are: everybody agrees the conclusion is daft and nobody will say which line to strike out.

The set-up, as premises so you can attack them one at a time. Suppose the universe is spatially infinite and matter is spread more or less evenly through it. I know, I know — we haven't the faintest idea whether there's anybody out there at all. Grant it anyway, it's the sort of universe we used to sketch on blackboards.

(1) The total value of a world is the sum of the values of the individual lives in it, in the same straightforward way the total energy of a system is the sum over its parts.
(2) In an infinite, roughly uniform universe there are already infinitely many lives: infinitely many being rescued, infinitely many drowning, infinitely many happy, infinitely many wretched.
(3) Adding a finite amount to a total that is already infinite leaves the total unchanged. ω + n = ω; ∞ + n = ∞.
(4) So the world in which I wade in and pull this child out of the pond has exactly the same total value as the world in which I stay on the bank and check my phone.
(5) One world is better than another only if its total value is greater.
(6) So pulling her out is not better than letting her drown — and nothing there turns on which child, which pond, or which of us does the wading.
(7) By the same steps it holds for any finite act by any finite number of people: whether a million of us spend our lives building hospitals or our lives burning them down, the totals come out identical.
(8) So in an infinite universe nothing anybody does makes the world better or worse. Not "hard to tell" — not better and not worse. A world where a billion people are given wonderful lives and a world where a billion are tortured slowly are, on this account, exactly equally good.
(9) And (8) isn't a limit case I can ignore. It says whether morality is possible at all is settled by a fact about the size of the universe that I have no way of finding out and that could go either way. Ethics resting on cosmology seems the wrong way round.

Here's the bit I want your view on most, because I tried to repair it and made it worse. My instinct from the old life is that (3) is doing something illegimate with a divergent sum, so I stuck in a regulator: score each life with a decaying weight, say e^(−d/L), d being its distance from me and L some fixed scale, so that every total converges; compare the worlds; let L go to infinity only at the end. Very neat, except the verdict now depends on where I put the origin and on how fast I let L grow. Two worlds can come out one way for one centring and the other way for another. And a series with both signs in it can be rearranged to land on whatever number you fancy. If I have to choose the regulator to get the answer I already want, I haven't done philosophy, I've done curve fitting.

So: which line goes, and what do I pay for striking it out? I'm not asking you to reassure me. If it's a hidden equivocation, name it and say why it looked innocent. If the thing is actually valid and I just have to eat a premise, tell me which one is cheapest and what breaks downstream. If I'm forced to choose I'd rather give up (8) — the torturing-a-billion line — than (1), since additivity is about the only tool I brought with me out of physics, and I can't see any way to keep (1) and lose (8). Which may be the mistake, for all I know. I'd also like to hear whether the regulator business is salvageable in principle or whether it's a dead end that just looks respectable because it has an exponential in it.

And when you've finished, say how confident you are in the diagnosis you land on, and single out the one point in it you'd be least happy defending in a room full of people who do this for a living.
