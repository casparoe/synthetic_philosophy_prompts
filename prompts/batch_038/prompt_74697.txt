I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am reporting a feature on a puzzle I keep running into: the analysts I interview agree about the magnitudes — the same carbon budget, the same modeled probability of a bad outcome, the same cost of the precaution that would avert it — and then recommend opposite courses of action, apparently because they disagree about the rule for converting those magnitudes into a decision. I want to build a diagnostic instrument, a decision-tree questionnaire, that sorts people by the rule they are actually using, so that I can put the same handful of choice problems to every source and readers can put them to themselves.

The deliverable is a tree of ten to twelve branch questions, each a concrete two- or three-option choice problem stated in plain English — no formulas, no probability arithmetic, nothing a bright non-specialist cannot answer in under two minutes from the armchair — together with a key that maps answer patterns onto named positions. Every question should be built to isolate a single assumption: independence, betweenness, dominance, continuity or the boundedness of the value scale, whether probabilities are treated as precise or as imprecise, whether outcomes are evaluated as absolute levels or as gains and losses from a reference point, and whatever else you regard as load-bearing. The key should say, for each item, which assumption it probes and which answers pull which way.

Four requirements:

- Construct the tree from the logical space first rather than from a roster of names. Show me the assumptions and the combinations of answers they license, and only then locate positions in that space. My reporting has surfaced the following labels, and each should appear as a leaf unless you argue it should not: expected-utility maximization; rank-dependent or risk-weighted expected utility; cumulative prospect theory; ambiguity-averse treatments such as maxmin expected utility or Choquet expected utility; minimax regret; precautionary maximin; and Pascalian views that allow the value scale to be unbounded. Add any leaf I have missed. For each combination the assumptions license that has no defender, say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has simply tried it.

- Include two or three items that re-run a structurally identical choice at very different stake levels, and once as a decision the respondent makes about their own affairs and once as a decision they would endorse for a public agency, since I gather that several of these theories hold the two answers may legitimately differ. State in the key which positions require that divergence and which forbid it.

- Include one deliberate trap: a pair of items whose answers, taken together, are not jointly permitted by any leaf on the tree. Say what the key should record when a respondent falls into it.

- Flag every pair of leaves your instrument cannot genuinely separate with questions of this kind, and say what kind of evidence would separate them. Do not paper over the failures.

Format: the items, with a one-line diagnostic note beneath each; the scoring key as a table; and a closing section of fewer than 250 words aimed at readers, giving one short paragraph per leaf that says in ordinary language what that pattern of answers commits a person to.
