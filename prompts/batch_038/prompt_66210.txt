I run a small conflict-intake office at a mid-size public university — think of it as the informal half of student conduct. People come to us instead of filing a formal charge: we can coach them, mediate, refer, or close the thing with no record. We can't make findings of fact and we can't sanction anyone, that's the formal office two floors up. We get about 25 intakes a month, I've got maybe 20 minutes per case, and two of the three people doing this with me are part-time staff with no background in sociology or philosophy whatsoever. So whatever you give me has to survive being handed to someone who has never heard of the theory it's built on and isn't going to look it up.

The frame I want you to work from is the honor / dignity / victimhood account of moral cultures, the one Bradley Campbell and Jason Manning laid out in their microaggression paper and then in the 2018 book. As I've got it: honor cultures make social standing the main engine of social control, so people are acutely sensitive to insult and are expected to handle slights themselves, personally, sometimes forcefully, with little reliance on outside authorities. Dignity cultures say a person has a worth that an insult can't actually dent, so you're supposed to have a thick skin about minor stuff and bring in third parties — courts, deans, HR — only for harms that are serious. Victimhood cultures keep honor's hair-trigger sensitivity to slight but combine it with dignity's dependence on third parties, and then add the piece that makes it its own thing: moral status attaches to having been wronged, so publicizing the wrong, aggregating incidents, and performing for an audience become ways of making the claim stick. Please don't send me a bibliography or a reading list, I can't chase anything down.

What I actually need is a triage procedure: ordered questions, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the exit points (close it, coach the person to handle it directly, offer mediation, refer up to the formal office), and explicit markers on the steps that are really judgment calls wearing a procedure costume. Two things I want you to get right, because I think naive uses of this frame botch them. First, classify the interaction, not the person — I do not want a checklist that outputs a personality type. Second, the culture in play can switch mid-incident: a slight being handled dignity-style becomes a third-party case the moment someone screenshots it or an RA writes it up on her own initiative, so the procedure should track which channel the thing is currently traveling down. And the thing that keeps me up is that this whole frame reads like a credibility discount — if my staff hear "victimhood grammar" they'll quietly start treating those complaints as less real, which would be bad both morally and practically, since plenty of genuinely awful conduct arrives wrapped in exactly that vocabulary. Build the guardrail in, and then tell me where the guardrail leaks.

Then run it on three cases shaped like ones we actually get. (1) A senior comes in about a classmate who keeps mangling her name after being corrected twice; she says she doesn't want him sanctioned, she wants it logged so it's on the record, and she's already posted a screenshot of the exchange to a public account with about four thousand followers. (2) A first-year says a guy in his hall said something to his face over a shared kitchen, he confronted him, it got heated, and then the RA documented it anyway — now he feels he has to press it or he looks like he backed down, and what he's asking me for is whether we can get the write-up deleted. (3) Eleven seperate reports in one term about the same TA, each one small on its own — interrupting, a remark about someone's accent, answering an email with a joke — and all eleven using nearly identical vocabulary. My gut says contagion; but I also know my gut is exactly the instrument that would let a serial offender hide inside a pile of trivia, and there's an unflattering third reading where our own log is the mechanism, because we built a third-party channel and then people used it.

Finish by telling me what the procedure can't do and where it will misfire most often — and, the part I care most about, what would change your mind about using this frame at all. If there's some argument or finding that would make you tell me to throw it out and triage on something else entirely, say what it is and how I'd recognize it in my own caseload. Also point at the step in your own reasoning you think is most likely to be wrong. American English, please.
