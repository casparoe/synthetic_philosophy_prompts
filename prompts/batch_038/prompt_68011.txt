I write a science-and-ideas column — mostly neuroscience, consciousness, and whatever's happening with AI — and I've hit a wall on one phrase.

Working on a piece about why everybody now calls LLM outputs "hallucinations" and whether that word is doing any honest work. I ran the question past a neuroscientist who does predictive processing, and he basically waved it away: perception is controlled hallucination anyway, he said, so your brain is doing the same thing a language model does when it makes stuff up — the term either applies to everything or to nothing, take your pick. He looked extremely pleased with himself and I couldnt push back in the moment, but I'm pretty sure something is wrong, or at least much sloppier than he made it sound.

The trouble is the phrase is everywhere. Podcast intros, book blurbs, the comments under any article about visual illusions, conference talks where it gets a knowing laugh. And nobody ever pins down what's being claimed. Sometimes it reads like a trivial "the brain builds its own experience" and sometimes it reads like "there's no world out there," which is way more than I thought anyone in the field was signing up for.

Can you unpack it for me as a menu rather than picking a favorite reading? I'd like:

- The distinct things "perception is controlled hallucination" could actually be asserting, ordered from the most innocuous to the most inflammatory. I'd guess there are at least five. Number them.
- For each: the claim in plain words, whether it's a platitude everyone accepts, a live empirical hypothesis, or just false, and what has to be true about priors, prediction error, and the flow of information for it to hold.
- What "controlled" is doing in each version. My hunch is that word is where the smuggling happens — it means something modest in one reading and something enormous in another.
- Which reading, if any, actually gets my source his conclusion about language models. Does the brain-is-just-a-hallucinating-LLM move survive once the words are nailed down, and if not, at which step does it break?

No citations or reading lists please, I've got my own pile — I just need the disambiguation in a shape I can argue from on deadline.

And end with exactly one line in this form: Verdict: [number of the reading he was leaning on] — [holds / doesn't hold / equivocates], where that last word is your call on whether that reading supports the LLM comparison.
