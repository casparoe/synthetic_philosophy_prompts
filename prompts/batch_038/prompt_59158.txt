I am a third-year doctoral student in the history of science, and the chapter I am drafting concerns how Newton's theology and his natural philosophy fit together rather than merely coexist. My adviser has a standing rule before any drafting meeting: write your interpretive commitments down on one page, in your own words, without hedging, and let her attack the whole set at once rather than sentence by sentence. She is away this week, so I would like you to take her place. Please adopt the standpoint of a historian of early modern natural philosophy who has worked extensively on Newton's unpublished theological manuscripts, on the successive editions of the Principia, and on the Clarke correspondence, and treat everything below as a position I am prepared to defend in print. Think carefully before you respond, and begin by sketching, briefly and explicitly, what each commitment metaphysically commits Newton to and which pairs are actually doing work against one another; only then move to the verdicts.

Here is my set. (1) Newton was an anti-Trinitarian who dated the corruption of Christian doctrine to the fourth century, believed the principal proof-texts had been altered, and denied the Son any co-equal or co-eternal status in the sense the councils intended; I hold this is not a compartmentalized private heresy but structurally tied to his conception of divine sovereignty. (2) Newton was a voluntarist: God's will is answerable to no standard of goodness or rationality prior to it, God could have made a different system of nature under different laws, and what makes God God for Newton is dominion, so much so that a deity stripped of dominion, providence, and final causes would on his account amount to nothing more than fate and nature. (3) Newton's arguments for the existence of God nevertheless run through final causes of an evidently fitness-based kind: the planets sit at distances from the sun suited to the amounts of heat and light each receives; the eyes and other organs of animals are contrived with as much art as any mechanism we make; the fact that the planets all revolve the same way, in nearly one plane and in nearly concentric orbits, cannot have arisen from any merely natural cause. I hold that Newton intends these to work evidentially on any competent reader, including a hostile one. (4) Newton maintains that gravity is universal and strictly proportional to quantity of matter, so that every body attracts every other, while also treating the idea that gravity should be innate, inherent, and essential to matter as an absurdity so great that, in his view, no one with a competent faculty for philosophical thinking could fall into it. I read the two claims as fully compatible, and I read Newton as simply agnostic about how gravity is produced: he describes the law and refuses to name the cause. (5) Newton's methodological refusal to feign hypotheses about anything not deduced from phenomena governs his natural philosophy but does not bind his theology, where he speculates at length and with confidence about prophecy, church history, and the divine nature. (6) I read the Clarke correspondence as showing that Newton's side accepts that the solar system would drift into irregularity without God's continuing attention and correction, so that Newton is willing to own a corrective, intervention-shaped providence over nature. (7) I read Newton's statement that God perceives all things by their immediate presence to himself in boundless space, describing that space as though it were God's sensorium, as supplying a continuous and non-mechanical divine presence within nature, and I take it to be doing explanatory work for gravity rather than expressing piety.

What I want back is an audit, not a lecture on Newton's religion. Go through the set and tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, and rank the genuine ones by severity rather than listing them as equally bad. For each genuine tension, state it precisely, identify the ambiguity or hidden premise that generates it if there is one, and give the cheapest revision that removes it together with an honest statement of what that revision costs me elsewhere in the package. Please pay particular attention to any pair I will be tempted to treat as contradictory when it is not, and any pair I have treated as harmonious when the harmony depends on a gloss I am not entitled to. Finish by naming the single revision that would clean up the largest number of problems at once and by saying what I lose by making it. Two further constraints: do not give me a bibliography, reading list, edition details, page references, or any quotation from memory, since I cannot check anything this week and a confident fabrication would be worse than a gap; and if any of my commitments is simply false as a matter of historical fact rather than internally unstable, flag that separately and keep it out of the severity ranking, noting where the verdict depends on the exact wording of a document you cannot reliably reproduce.
