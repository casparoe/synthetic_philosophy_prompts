I have no academic training in philosophy or history — I work in logistics — and for about six years I have kept a private spreadsheet of every case I can find in which a society that had state-level organisation lost it and then regained it. The project started as an argument with my brother, who believes history runs along rails, and it has left me unable to say which of us is closer to Popper, whose attack on historicism I read years ago in a battered paperback and remember chiefly for two moves: that a trend is not a law, and that even a genuine law would let us rule outcomes out rather than select one. I would like you to draft the joint statement that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between a convinced Popperian about the social sciences and a defender of large-scale historical generalisations — call her the pattern historian, though she regards the label historicist as a smear — could both put their names to.

To keep the drafting concrete rather than a survey of the debate, here is my own conjecture, which I will call the Reconstitution Principle. Take any society that has crossed into state-level organisation, by which I mean a durable administrative monopoly on organised violence, regular exaction in kind or coin, and record-keeping adequate to administer it. The conjecture has three parts: no such society, once across, has permanently returned to non-state organisation; where statehood is re-established in the same region after a collapse, the elapsed time to re-establishment is shorter than the elapsed time of the original formation; and the successor reuses at least one institutional form of its predecessor — a tax unit, a calendar, a provincial boundary, a scribal language. I can gesture at candidate cases, mainly the long fragmentation between the Han and the Sui–Tang reunification and Western Europe after the western Roman empire, though I am not confident that my Mesopotamian examples are re-ascension at all rather than continuity, and I expect several of my dates are wrong. Alongside this I have been circling a distinction I cannot name properly, and I want it applied to each part of the conjecture: some generalisations seem law-like, supporting counterfactuals and licensing predictions about particular future cases; others seem merely constraint-like, ruling possibilities out without selecting one; and a third sort seems channel-like, making certain outcomes much likelier than others without excluding any. For each part, classify it, and say whether Popper's objection bites on a generalisation of that kind.

The deliverable should have four pieces. First, a numbered list of claims both parties can sign, and please do not pad it: I suspect both can accept that singular historical narratives do not by themselves establish universal generalisations, that a generalisation which only tells us what is excluded has done real work, that we cannot run a controlled civilisation, and that the number of independent state-formation events on this planet is small — but state these with enough bite that a reader can see what has actually been given up. Second, a disagreements table with a row per live dispute, showing the pattern historian's formulation of her own claim and the Popperian's formulation of the claim he rejects, each worded so that its own side would accept it, plus a column marking whether the dispute is substantive or turns on the word law itself, and a column for what would settle it. Third, the cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would genuinely move at least one of the two — each paired with a test, case, or argument both would accept as bearing on it and with each side's prediction written down in advance, as if registered before anyone consults the evidence; where a crux cannot be decided by anything accessible to us, say so plainly instead of inventing a resolution. Fourth, a short closing section giving each side's concession conditions: what the pattern historian would take as a fatal refutation, and what the Popperian would accept as a real law of history rather than a trend, were one ever produced. I do not want a bibliography or a reading list, only the drafting work, and I would rather you flag the formulations that are compromises neither side loves than paper over a gap with a sentence each party can read their own view into.
