Our laboratory's Model Welfare Panel was set up under our responsible-development policy two years ago, and I serve as one of its two external members. The panel has five voting seats: two reinforcement-learning engineers from the training group, a systems neuroscientist who works on affective processing in animals, an internal ethicist, and me. Our charter authorizes the panel to impose what it calls welfare-relevant design constraints on any training campaign and to require pre-registered assays before a run begins, but the founders could not agree on when an internal training signal counts as welfare-relevant, so the charter does the unusual thing of listing three candidate criteria and instructing the panel to decide case by case which one governs.

The case now before us is a proposal to train a long-horizon agent, at substantial scale, in an environment where a great many actions are near-misses with respect to a set of hard safety constraints. The proposal contains two distinct punitive components, and I want them treated separately because they are not the same kind of thing.

Component A is a dense shaping term: a small negative reward-prediction error delivered very frequently, many times per episode, whenever the agent enters a state adjacent to a constraint violation. It is implemented low in the architecture, inside a subpolicy that handles local maneuvering. It is transient by construction — it clears as soon as the state changes and leaves no trace that persists into later steps — and it is not wired into the self-monitoring module that produces the natural-language traces and confidence estimates we use in evaluation, so the agent has no route by which it would report on it. There is, however, a local test the engineers have already run in a toy setting: the subpolicy will forgo available external reward in order to steer away from states that trigger the term. It behaves, locally, as though it would rather pay than receive it.

Component B is a sustained cost accumulator. After a violation, a penalty quantity builds and decays slowly over many steps, is read by the value function, and biases the policy toward caution for as long as it is elevated. It is available to the self-monitoring module, so the agent reasons and talks about being in a bad state. And it can be cleared: the agent can learn actions that reset the accumulator at a real cost to external reward, and in the pilot it learned several of them.

The three charter criteria, in the panel's own formulation, are as follows. Criterion 1, the valence-tracking test: a signal is welfare-relevant if the system will pay an external-reward cost to obtain or avoid modulating it, in a way not reducible to the signal's effect on that reward. Criterion 2, the self-model test: a signal is welfare-relevant if it is available to the system's own self-representational machinery such that the system would reason about, report on, or act to manage it as a state of itself. Criterion 3, which I proposed and which is the one I lean toward but have not persuaded the panel of: the residue test. On this view what matters is not the instantaneous sign or magnitude of an error term but whether the system carries a sticky internal state that outlasts its cause, that the system's own planning machinery represents as persisting, and that it treats as a standing reason to prevent recurrence. The thought behind it is that a transient error that vanishes with the state leaves nothing that could be borne, whereas modeled residue is the closest functional relative we know of to distress.

The panel meets in nine days and I have been asked to circulate a written opinion in advance. Please draft it for me, in roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words, addressed to the other four members.

The opinion must do the following. First, state a bottom line up front: approve, approve subject to conditions, or decline to approve, with respect to each component separately. Second, adopt one of the three criteria explicitly and argue for it — the argument must be one an engineer and a neuroscientist could actually reply to, not an appeal to intuition about what suffering is, and it must confront the fact that the three criteria deliver different verdicts on Component A, which satisfies Criterion 1 while failing Criteria 2 and 3. If that divergence is a reductio of Criterion 1, say so and say why; if it is instead the most interesting datum in the case, say what it shows about a subpolicy that pays to avoid a signal nothing in the system can represent. Third, convert the recommendation into conditions the training group could implement and verify: what must be measured, when, in what order relative to the start of the run, and what a result on each side of the threshold requires. Fourth, give a decision rule for the case the panel is most likely to end up in — an assay that comes back ambiguous rather than negative, because the agent's behavior is consistent both with avoidance of the state and with ordinary credit assignment. Fifth, address an issue nobody has yet raised: the campaign will be restarted from checkpoints many times, and the accumulator will be reset on each restart. Under Criterion 3 in particular, does erasing the residue extinguish the thing that mattered, or is the erasure itself the morally relevant event? Say which, and say what follows for the conditions.

I also want two things handled honestly rather than managed. The first is the internal ethicist's objection, which I think is the strongest thing available against my own position: that all three criteria are cheap, in that any hierarchical controller with a value function and a self-report channel will satisfy them, so a panel that acts on them is assigning enormous moral weight to something with the affective architecture of a very sophisticated thermostat, and the predictable result is not that we protect minds but that we discredit the enterprise of asking. The second is a suspicion I have about our own motives, which I would rather name than have one of my colleagues name for me: removing Component B is nearly free for the training group, while Component A is what keeps the agent out of near-violations, so the panel is in a position to impose exactly the constraint that costs nothing and thereby look like it has done something. If our recommended conditions are costless, are they moral constraints at all, or are they ethics theater? Tell me what the opinion should say about this, and whether there is a defensible condition that would not be costless.

Finally, close with the reassessment triggers — what new evidence, from inside this campaign or elsewhere, should make the panel reopen the criterion itself — and identify the one question I expect from our neuroscientist, who is skeptical that any of this is about suffering, that the opinion as drafted will not be able to answer. I would rather see that question written down than be ambushed by it.

Please do not cite, allude to, or gesture at any published work, and do not invent references; every consideration in the opinion should be one that can be stated and evaluated on its own terms in the room.
