You're a novelist who moonlights as a cognitive scientist — someone who could write a proper fable about how minds make analogies and still get the machinery right. Think carefully before you start: I'd like a short brainstorm first (six or eight bullets at most) about how you'll stage the thing, and then the story proper. Roughly 900 words of story.

The conceit below is invented by me, so don't go looking for a source, and please don't transpose it back into letter strings. It's the same underlying machinery as those analogy puzzles people use to study how concepts bend — the jostling little half-ideas, the hall that's hot while nothing is settled and cold once one reading wins, the concept that has to slide sideways onto a neighbour — only rebuilt out of coins.

So: in a small valley town the only concepts anyone has ever had are about shelfs. Every household has one shelf, and every shelf holds exactly three stacks of coins, each stack having one, two or three coins — never none, never more than three. Three is the ceiling, the rafters, the top of the world. The town's entire intellectual life is the nightly Rite, in which the Keeper recites the one Rule over a shelf: *put a coin on the tallest stack.* Everyone agrees what that means when there's a single tallest stack with room under the ceiling. Nobody has ever had to consider what happens otherwise, because otherwise has never come up.

Tonight it comes up. The apprentice Keeper — young, frightened, very good at her job — faces a shelf of 3-1-2. The tallest stack is already at the ceiling and cannot take another coin. The Rule cannot be applied as given. And there is no "just ask": no elders with a bigger book, no second Rule waiting in a drawer, no neighbours who've seen it before.

What I want dramatised is the slippage itself — the moment where a concept that can get no purchase slides onto a neighbouring one that can, and the slide afterwards feels obvious, inevitable, almost like it was there all along, when a minute ago it was unthinkable. I want the reader to feel several rival slips pressing at once: *tallest* slipping to *shortest*; *put a coin on* slipping to *take a coin off*, being the mirror image of the Rule and therefore horribly tempting; and the ceiling itself slipping, so that three-and-one wraps round to one and the stack is reborn rather than blocked. (If you want more pressure, note that some of these leave a shelf with no single tallest stack at all — a second impossibility the Rule was never built for.) And I want one of them to win, not by argument but by a kind of cooling: the hall loud and sweltering and full of josstling half-ideas while nothing is settled, then the noise dying away as one reading gathers support, until everything is quiet and certain. That heat-to-cold arc is the emotional spine; the coin puzzle is only the stage.

A few constraints:

- The winning slip has to be earned. Make me feel why it beats the rivals without anybody delivering a speech about it — if one candidate is genuinely stronger, let that show in the texture, not in a summary.
- Give the half-ideas a physical presence (imps, whispers, apprentices crowding the rafters, whatever suits you) but let them jostle rather than deliberate. They shouldn't hold a meeting.
- No modern-world framing, no wink at the camera about brains or computers, no moral tacked on the end, and dont resolve it by having a stranger arrive who knows the answer.
- Make the valley feel lived-in: weather, wet wool, the particular sound of a coin dropped onto a stack.

End with the story, and then one single final line with nothing after it, in exactly this form:

Slip: <the part of the Rule that couldn't apply> -> <the concept it slid onto>

e.g. `Slip: tallest -> shortest`. That line is for my dataset, so keep it to one pair — the pair that actually wins in your version.
