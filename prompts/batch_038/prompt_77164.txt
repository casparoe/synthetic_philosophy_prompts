**Rigidity Is the Whole Theory**

Bayesian orthodoxy treats learning as coming to be certain of something. Conditionalization is defined on propositions you end up with probability one on, and everything else follows by dividing out. Jeffrey's complaint was that this describes almost none of our perceptual life. I look at a swatch in bad light and my probability that it is teal moves, but nothing becomes certain, and there is no proposition I can point to as the content of what I learned.

His replacement is usually stated for a partition. Suppose experience bears on E and moves your probability for E from p to q. Then for every hypothesis A:

Q(A) = P(A|E)·q + P(A|¬E)·(1 − q).

Concretely: a card is drawn from a shuffled deck and I glimpse it in dim light. Let A be "ace of hearts," so P(A) = 1/52, P(A|red) = 1/26, and P(A|black) = 0. If the glimpse moves me to Q(red) = 0.9, the rule gives Q(A) = 0.9/26 ≈ 0.0346, about 1 in 29 — an 80 percent increase over the prior, produced without my becoming certain of anything.

The textbook reading is that this generalizes conditionalization — conditioning on E is the special case q = 1 — and that it lets us model vague, non-propositional input. I think the formulation is worth more than that, because it is unusually transparent about where its content sits.

The rule is pinned down by three conditions and nothing else: Q(E) = q; Q(A|E) = P(A|E); Q(A|¬E) = P(A|¬E). Those determine Q uniquely. The first is just a restatement of the input. So the entire substantive commitment is in the other two: rigidity. Experience may push your probability for the proposition it bears on wherever it likes, but it must leave your conditional probabilities on that proposition exactly where they were.

Two consequences follow, and I think they are underappreciated.

First, rigidity is not checkable the way an empirical assumption is. After the experience I can ask what my conditional credence in A given E is now. What I cannot do is step outside my current state and compare that number with the one I would have reported before, because the only route to the old conditional runs through the old total state, which is gone. Rigidity is therefore not a hypothesis about the world or even about my evidence; it is a rule for how to describe an experience at all. This is why the usual objection — "but sometimes experience really does change your conditionals" — feels unsatisfying to me. It cannot be cashed out without abandoning the only notation in which the change could be written.

Second, the minimum-relative-entropy derivation is not independent support. It is standard to note that the Jeffrey update is the unique distribution minimizing relative entropy to P subject to Q(E) = q, and to present this as showing that the rule is compelled by a consistency requirement rather than stipulated. But the constraint set is affine and the divergence is minimized within it precisely by holding the proportions inside each cell fixed, so the derivation returns rigidity in variational clothing. It says that if you accept a certain measure of informational change you get the rule. It does not say to accept that measure.

I also want to defend the rule against the objection I hear most often: that it takes q as a gift. Where does the new probability of E come from? The rule constrains the rest of the distribution but never says how experience fixes that one number, so the hard part looks hidden inside a variable. My answer is that this is partly a category complaint. Any account that supplies q mechanically has to represent the experience as a likelihood ratio, and the "virtual evidence" style of update does exactly that: attach a ratio λ to the experience and let the prior odds do the rest, so that the posterior odds on E are λ times the prior odds. On a 50/50 prior and λ = 9 this yields Q(E) = 0.9, the same number a direct Jeffrey stipulation of 0.9 would give. But the two come apart exactly when the prior is unbalanced, and the virtual-evidence version is the one that composes, since it keeps experience and prior as separate inputs. It buys that compositionality at the cost of a representation: it assumes the experience is a reading from an instrument whose error profile you know. Much perception is not like that. There is no λ. So the q objection does not defeat Jeffrey; it marks where his account is silent by design.

One further selling point deserves to be stated plainly, because I think it is what makes the rule feel like a logic of uncertain input rather than a bookkeeping trick. Jeffrey updates commute. If I first judge the drawn card 80 percent likely to be red and then 80 percent likely to be a heart, I arrive at the same final distribution as if I had made those two judgments in the opposite order. The sequence in which ambiguous impressions arrive therefore carries no information the rule pretends not to have.

Finally, the framework does better than orthodoxy on old evidence. If you never become certain, you never generate the pathology of a theory confirmed by evidence you already assigned probability one. On this account the evidence never had probability one; it had something like 0.999, and the likelihoods remain well defined.
