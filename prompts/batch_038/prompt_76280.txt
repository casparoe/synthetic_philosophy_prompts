I serve on the external advisory panel for our national statistics office's well-being measurement program, and I have been asked to circulate a decision memo before our vote in three weeks. Here is the situation, as concretely as I can state it.

The program is replacing its well-being module. Three proposals are on the table.

- **Proposal A** keeps a single item, asked by experience sampling about eight times a day for a week: "Taking everything together, how much pleasure or enjoyment did you feel just now?" on a 0–10 scale. The national index is the mean of all person-samples.
- **Proposal B** asks seven separate items covering what the drafting group treats as distinguishable kinds of positive experience — sensory pleasure, relief from pain or discomfort, amusement, aesthetic absorption, warmth or closeness with another person, satisfaction in doing something well, and anticipatory savoring of something still to come — each on its own 0–10 scale, then combines them into a single index using fixed weights the office would set by expert judgment.
- **Proposal C** asks the same seven items but publishes seven sub-indices and no aggregate, on the ground that there is no single quantity behind them to aggregate.

The panel is split roughly evenly, and the split is not about survey craft. Two members argue that our cost-benefit mandate requires one cardinal number, and that refusing to produce one abdicates the office's job, which is to state results in a form like "this program adds n units of well-being per person per year." Three members argue that B's weights are indefensible and that C is the honest option; one of them keeps saying the whole exercise is the old pushpin-versus-poetry problem wearing a clipboard — that if we build one number out of heterogeneous goods, the number will quietly end up tracking whichever kind of positive experience respondents find easiest to report, which is probably the most banal one.

There is also a pilot that neither camp has digested. In a 900-person pilot, answers to the single Proposal A item tracked two things and not a third. They tracked (i) whether the respondent said they would want the episode to continue if given the chance, and (ii) how much the respondent said the episode mattered to them. They did not track the companion item we asked about the intensity of the feeling itself — "how strong was the pleasant feeling, setting aside everything else" — which came back nearly uncorrelated with both. And two of the seven modules produced what look like response-splits rather than response-errors: a majority counted relief from a headache as pleasure, but a substantial minority insisted that relief is the absence of something bad rather than the presence of something good; and on the savoring module, some respondents reported looking forward to a visit as among the most pleasant hours of their week, while others reported anticipation as an ache that made the visit itself less enjoyable once it arrived. Both camps cite the same pilot as support for their own view.

The questionnaire must be locked four months before fielding, which for the next cycle means the instrument has to be final by the end of March.

What I need from you is the memo itself, written in American English (as should your whole response), roughly 800 to 1,100 words, in a form I can circulate to the panel as written. It should:

1. Open with one sentence stating the recommendation — A, B, C, or one of them under specified conditions — and the date by which the office must act if the recommendation is adopted.
2. Give the reasoning for it, aimed at members who disagree, such that a proponent of a rival proposal could answer the reasons rather than merely be outvoted. If the recommendation is conditional, state the conditions concretely enough to be adopted as written: what gets asked, what gets published, and what gets excluded from the cost-benefit mandate.
3. Engage the pilot's central result directly: whether the A item is measuring a felt quality, a disposition to want continuation, a judgment of importance, or no one thing at all — and what that implies for whether B's fixed weights can be defended or must be abandoned. Say what the office should do about the two modules where respondents appear to be answering different questions with the same words: redefine, split, drop, or annotate them, and why.
4. Treat the strongest consideration on the losing side at full strength. If you recommend C, that means giving the cardinal-cost-benefit objection its best run rather than waving at it; if you recommend A or B, that means giving the arbitrariness-of-weights and banality-drift objections their best run.
5. Take up the underlying question on the merits — whether there is some one property that all seven kinds of positive experience share, which a single number could be tracking, and if there is not, whether a single number can still be defended as an ordinal ranking or an avowed policy construction rather than a measurement. I want the substance of that question, not a tour of who has said what about it. No citations, references, or reading list; argue it from the reasoning.

After the memo, give me a table of the substantive claims the recommendation depends on, using exactly these columns in this order:

- **claim** — one sentence, the claim as the memo actually relies on it.
- **role** — one of: load-bearing, supporting, background.
- **status** — one of: settled; contested among people who know the topic; not decidable by any evidence the office could gather.
- **what_would_settle_it** — the kind of evidence or argument that would resolve it, or "nothing empirical" if the issue is conceptual.
- **effect_if_false** — which of A, B, or C the recommendation shifts to if the claim fails.

Include at least nine rows. Flag as contested anything on which competent people working on well-being or on measurement genuinely disagree; I would much rather you over-flag than let a contested position into the memo dressed as background consensus. And wherever a contested claim is load-bearing, say so in plain language in the memo itself, so that a member who rejects that claim can see exactly where the recommendation stops following.
