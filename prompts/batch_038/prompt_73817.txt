The student's answer:

Maimonides' explanation of the sacrifices is the most famous and most controversial piece of his project of giving reasons for the commandments. In Book III, chapter 32 of the Guide he argues that it is not in human nature to abandon suddenly a way of life to which a people has become accustomed. He illustrates the point with the wilderness itinerary: God did not lead Israel straight from Egypt to the land of Israel, because a people freshly released from slavery would not have had the fortitude for it, and so led them round about for forty years, during which hardship produced a hardier generation. In the same way, the Israelites had been steeped in an Egyptian and Mesopotamian religious culture in which worship of the deity just was the slaughter and offering of animals. It would have been impossible to ask them to worship God through inward contemplation alone and to give up sacrifice entirely; they would not have accepted a Torah that demanded it, any more than a modern person would accept a religion that forbade prayer.

What Maimonides therefore says God did is not to abolish sacrifice but to transform its direction. Sacrifice was permitted, indeed commanded, but only to God and only in the one place God designated, so that the very institution through which the nations worshipped idols became the instrument by which Israel was weaned off idolatry. He calls this a gracious stratagem on God's part. Chapters 29 and 46 of Book III extend the strategy across the cult as a whole, arguing that many particular rites — and many prohibitions — take their shape from the practices of the Sabians, so that the Torah simultaneously borrows a familiar form and inverts its content.

The difficulty is that Maimonides elsewhere writes as a codifier of law with no trace of this historicism. In the Mishneh Torah, at the close of the Laws of Kings, he rules that the messianic king will rebuild the Temple and that in his days the whole observance of the statutes will return to its former condition: "we will offer sacrifices," and the sabbatical and jubilee years will be kept in all their particulars. He adds that the Torah and its statutes are everlasting and that nothing may be added to or taken from them. If the sacrifices were a concession to an ancient religious environment, then their reasons have lapsed along with that environment, and on Maimonides' own premises the cult is in principle abrogated; the messianic restoration he legislates is not something he can consistently affirm as a genuine requirement, and his insistence on the unchangeability of the Torah is best read as a prudential bow to popular expectation.

The standard way of resolving this is to distinguish the two works by audience. The Mishneh Torah, addressed to the communities that would actually live by it, states Maimonides' considered position as a halakhist, while the Guide is the exoteric work, in which he repeats the traditional account for the benefit of the perplexed reader who is not yet ready for philosophy. Once the two books are assigned to different audiences, the contradiction dissolves.

There is a further support for taking Maimonides' account as a general theory rather than a local one. Chapter 32 does not confine itself to sacrifice; the argument from the impossibility of sudden change is an argument about the commandments as a whole, and the polemic against the Sabians sweeps in large tracts of ritual law. The correct conclusion is therefore that Maimonides regards the Torah's ceremonial legislation as a compilation of expedients shaped by the circumstances of its promulgation.

This is what Nachmanides saw, and what his commentary on Leviticus objects to. Nachmanides accepts that the commandments were shaped by their historical setting, and he accepts a symbolic layer in the rites, but he thinks Maimonides has got the direction of the symbolism wrong: on his reading of Maimonides, slaughtering the very species the idolaters revered cannot cure idolatry, since it honors the powers they were said to govern. Nachmanides dismisses Maimonides' reasoning as "mere expressions" that heal a severe wound casually, and presses the point that Abel and Noah offered acceptable sacrifices before idolatry existed at all, so the rites cannot be explained as a response to it. On Nachmanides' own account the offering works on the offerer through the correspondence of deed, word and thought.

So the tension is real, and the most Maimonides can claim is that a stratagem directed at one historical moment survives, in codified form, into moments for which its reason no longer holds.
