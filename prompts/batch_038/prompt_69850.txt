For thirty-eight years I worked as a bacteriologist, much of it on how populations of organisms respond to sustained pressure — which is to say I spent my career watching things with no intentions outmaneuver things with plenty of them. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy and the history of science, and I keep encountering arguments that treat pathogens, climate systems, and volcanic aerosols as non-human actors in history. I find these arguments half compelling and half infuriating, and I want to locate exactly where my resistance belongs. I have decided to test them against a novel.

The novel is Camus's *The Plague* (1947), which I assume you know well enough that I need not summarize it: Oran, the dying rats, the closed gates, Rieux, Tarrou, Rambert, Grand, Cottard, Father Paneloux's two sermons and the death of the magistrate Othon's son, Tarrou's own death within sight of the end, and the narrator's disclosure of himself in the closing pages.

Here is what I should like. Over the past year I have been sorting non-human claims into three levels, and I want you to apply the scheme to the novel, criticize the scheme, and say where the text refuses to be sorted by it.

(i) Mere causal contribution: the entity makes a difference to how events go — but so does the weather in any story, and so does the price of grain.
(ii) Responsive or dispositional agency: the entity adapts, resists, and changes its behaviour as a function of what is done to it — the sense in which I would say, without metaphor, that a population of organisms has outflanked a control measure. No mind, no goals, no experience implied.
(iii) Moral agency or moral patienthood: something to which obligations can be owed, or by which someone can be wronged.

Take the principal non-humans of the book — the bacillus, the rats, the fleas and the town's sanitation, the heat and the wind, the gates and the cordon, the sea that Rieux and Tarrou swim in, and Oran itself as a habitat — and assign each a level, saying what in the text earns the assignment and where the prose slides between them.

Then the harder questions. Paneloux's first sermon looks to me like the precise inflation of a level (i)–(ii) process into a level (iii) one: punishment implies a plaintiff and a verdict. Does the second sermon retract that inflation or harden it? Tarrou's confession — that all of us carry the plague and that decency lies in not breathing it onto others — seems to be the one place in the book where a level (ii) fact is converted into a level (iii) obligation, and converted in a human rather than in the microbe. Is that right, and is it what makes the book work?

Third: the opening pages give us Oran in what I can only call habitat terms — a commercial town without birds, trees, or gardens, with a climate, a temperament, and habits of work and pleasure. That is very nearly environmental determinism. Yet Rambert stays when he could leave, Rieux refuses the priest's consolation, and Tarrou becomes something like a saint without God. Has Camus committed himself to a habitat account that he then breaks for dramatic convenience, or is the point that a habitat can overdetermine conditions without determining choices? Tell me which, and whether the text can settle it.

Fourth, the reverse peril. Much of this recent writing restores agency to non-humans by way of martial vocabulary: the microbe as invader, strategist, enemy. Camus's narrator is a physician who counts cases, hesitates over the diagnosis, and worries about naming a disease before it is confirmed; he conspicuously declines to personify, and I believe the organism is never given a scientific name anywhere in the book — correct me if that is wrong, and tell me what the genericness does. But does the novel succumb to the war metaphor anyway? A chronicle with no antagonist is a strange thing to write, and the closing reflection that the bacillus never dies but lies dormant among our linen and papers and may one day wake its rats again and send them to die in a happy city sounds to my ear less like an epidemiological forecast than like a threat from an adversary.

Finally: is the dominant allegorical tradition — the plague as the Occupation, as sin, as death — itself a determinism of meaning that empties the biology out of the book, or does Camus need the allegory to make the biology bearable? You may use that tradition as a foil, but please do not give me the standard account of it; I have read it many times.

Two requests about manner. Do not quote the novel at all — I read it in translation and have no wish to argue about whose wording we are using — and give me no bibliography, no reading list, and no citations of any kind, since I cannot check them and would rather they not be invented. Speak of the secondary literature in general terms if you must.

Above all, separate for me what is settled from what is genuinely disputed among people who know this material. Where Camus scholars divide — over the second sermon, over whether the chronicle amounts to reliable testimony, over how much political weight the book will bear — say so, say which side you think better supported and why. Where philosophers of biology disagree about whether anything like level (ii) is a coherent category at all, or merely intention smuggled in under a new name, mark that too, and mark whether my three levels are my own idiosyncrasy or a distinction the field already recognizes in some other form. I am a scientist by training, and I should be sorry to come away mistaking a live controversy for a settled result, or a settled result for a private hunch of mine.
