I am three-quarters through a novel whose climactic sequence is the seventy-hour deliberation of a literary prize jury, and I need your help drafting a document that my two principal jurors should have produced but never did. Both characters are, I hope, intellectually serious; neither is a strawman, and I have no intention of letting the novel adjudicate between them. What I need is the joint statement that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between their positions would yield, so that the dialogue I write is exact rather than theatrical.

The two positions are as follows. The first juror — call her the Humean — holds that there is a standard of taste in something like the sense Hume sketched in his essay of 1757: sentiment has authority over the pleasing and the disagreeable, yet some sentiments are correct and others defective, because delicacy of imagination is a capacity to detect features of a work that a coarser palate misses, the way Sancho's kinsmen detected the iron and the leather in the hogshead and were vindicated by the key and its thong at the bottom. On her view this capacity can be trained by practice and sharpened by comparison, can be corrupted by prejudice, and where it is intact in people of good sense the resulting verdicts converge enough to justify ranking works; that works have pleased across changes of climate, government, religion, and language is evidence for uniform principles of taste, not merely a record of who held power. The second juror — call him the contextualist — holds that the machinery of the true judge is a way of laundering a local consensus into a universal one: which responses count as delicate and which count as prejudiced is settled in advance by the institutions that confer the title, so the standard is not discovered by qualified judges but constituted by their being qualified. He does not think anything goes; he thinks the objectivist cannot say why it does not without circularity.

To keep this from becoming a seminar, I want the statement to work through three cases I have built into the plot, which both jurors accept as genuine rather than as tricks. First, the shortlisted work is a posthumous manuscript assembled by an editor from fragments, notebooks, and cut chapters, with transitions supplied; the jurors disagree about whether the object of judgment is even determinate, and if so whether its assembledness is a defect in it or a fact about it. Second, I have invented an attribution study within the novel: the same nine-hundred-word passage, unaltered, circulated to two matched panels, one told it is by a Nobel laureate, one told it is by an unknown; ratings diverge sharply and the divergence tracks the panels' prior exposure to prize culture. Third, two jurors whom both parties are willing to grant are equally delicate, practised, comparative, sensible, and unprejudiced rank the shortlisted work at the top and at the bottom respectively, and can each give articulable reasons.

Please produce: a numbered section of claims both parties could sign without reservation, including claims about sentiment's role, about the variability of verdicts, and about what any standard would have to do; then a disagreements table with one row per crux, each row giving the Humean's formulation in wording she would sign, the contextualist's formulation in wording he would sign, the case or argument both accept as bearing on it, and each side's prediction about that case written down in advance, before the outcome is known. The cruxes I suspect, and which you should confirm, refine, or replace: whether delicacy is a two-place relation between a judge and features of a work, and so corrigible, or a stable disposition to be pleased, and so indexed to a psychology; whether true judges can be identified except by their agreement with a canon that was itself fixed by judges taken to be true; whether the attribution study shows defective judges or socially constituted properties; whether Hume's own appeal to the endurance of works across centuries is testimonial in a way that sits badly with any requirement that one judge a work from one's own acquaintance with it; and whether the clause requiring a judge to be cleared of all prejudice is informative or question-begging. For each, state what each side would count as evidence against its own view, and say plainly whether there is anything both agree would actually change a vote on the prize.

Finally, include a short section identifying which parts of the dispute are merely verbal. I need this in particular because my characters slide between at least four senses of the claim that beauty is objective — independence of responses, correctness conditions on responses, convergence among qualified responders, and authority to bind dissenters who remain unconvinced — and I want to know exactly where they are talking past each other, so that I can write one of them winning a victory that is hollow and know which of the four senses made it hollow. No bibliography and no citations, please; I want formulations I can put in a mouth, not a reading list.
