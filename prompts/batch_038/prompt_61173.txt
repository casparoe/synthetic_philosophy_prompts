I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am reporting a feature on the long-running argument over whether the world is becoming less violent. Before I let a certain paragraph anywhere near my copy, I want a rigorous accuracy audit of it.

The paragraph comes from a review essay in another general-interest outlet, written by a social scientist who is broadly sympathetic to the decline thesis and who writes well. I am tempted to use it as a model for my own framing and may paraphrase parts of it. Transcribed in full, it reads:

"For most of human history, violence was the default. Our ancestors killed one another at rates that would make a modern conflict zone look orderly, and the long decline since then is a measurable fact rather than a matter of opinion. The evidence is straightforward: wherever bodies can be counted — skeletons bearing weapon trauma, the ethnographic record of foraging peoples, the parish registers of medieval and early modern European towns — the share of people killed by other people falls steadily, from something on the order of one in ten in pre-state societies to a few per hundred thousand per year in today's wealthy democracies. Two engines did the work: the state's monopoly on force, and commerce, which made other people worth more alive than dead. And the change goes all the way down — it is not merely that we are held in check by police and courts, but that people themselves came to find cruelty distasteful. The magnitudes are large enough that the conclusion is not really contestable: a child born today, anywhere in the world, faces a smaller chance of dying violently than a child born in 1900, and the number of people killed in wars has fallen right along with the rate. Skeptics point to Ukraine and Gaza, or to the American homicide spike of 2020, but that is precisely the noise a long-run series smooths out; those who insist otherwise tend to be people with a taste for bad news. Violence, in short, is not a permanent feature of the human condition. It is a problem with a solution, and we have very nearly solved it."

Please write as a philosopher of social science who works on measurement, social kinds, and the epistemology of quantitative claims about the deep past — someone equally at home with homicide series and skeletal assemblages and with the question of what would have to be true for a category like "violence" to have a determinate extension. Assume your reader is intelligent, has no statistics training beyond averages and rates, and has to decide what to print.

Think hard before you commit to verdicts, and work in the following order.

1. Begin with a brief planning pass. Break the paragraph into its distinct claims — these are not the same as its sentences — and tag each as descriptive, causal-explanatory, conceptual or definitional, or rhetorical. Ten lines at most; do not belabor this.

2. Then audit the claims one by one. For each, assign a label: accurate; fair simplification; subtly wrong, in which case say precisely which two things it conflates; unsupported by anything in the evidence offered; flat wrong; or a non-sequitur, in which case identify the missing premise. Where the label is anything other than the first two, give me the careful version of the claim in a single sentence.

3. Name the distinctions the paragraph slides across. I suspect at least the following are in play, but tell me which actually matter and which I have imagined: a rate versus an absolute count; a period rate versus a lifetime risk for a birth cohort; violent behavior versus a violent disposition; a long-run trend versus a law-like tendency; several heterogeneous bodies of evidence versus one continuous series; and how much violence there is versus what counts as violence in the first place. For each, say whether correcting it changes the conclusion or only the confidence with which the conclusion may be held.

4. Steelman both directions. First, what would the most careful defender of the decline thesis reject in this paragraph — noting that a defender of the thesis may object to more of it than a skeptic does. Second, what would the most sophisticated critic say, and is the remark about critics having a taste for bad news a fair diagnosis or an evasion of an argument?

5. Then the question my piece actually turns on, so please give it the most thought: is there a fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent, independent of how we choose to count? Take a position. I do not want "it depends on your definitions" and I do not want "the data settle it." Tell me where the empirical question ends and the conceptual choice begins, whether that choice is arbitrary or constrained, and whether the paragraph's confidence that the issue is not a matter of opinion is earned.

6. Finally, give me what I can use: a rewritten version of the paragraph, 150 words or fewer, that retains as much rhetorical force as the true parts will support; and a short list of claims I must not make in my own piece even where a better sentence wants me to.

Two constraints. Do not send me a reading list, and do not name studies, authors, datasets, or dates unless you are confident they are correct — I cannot verify anything from where you sit, and a plausible-sounding citation is worse to me than no citation at all. Where a correction of yours depends on a specific figure or study you cannot check, say so explicitly and give me the version of the claim that is safe without it. Flag genuine uncertainty rather than papering over it, and be blunt: I would rather lose a good line than print a sloppy one.
