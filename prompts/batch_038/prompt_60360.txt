I direct a small research program — four people over three years, two philosophers and two econometricians — attached to a national appraisal authority. The authority has been instructed to rank a portfolio of health, social care and infrastructure programs in wellbeing-adjusted life years, where one WELLBY is a one-point improvement in life satisfaction on a standard zero-to-ten scale, sustained by one person for one year. That instruction replaces a long-standing practice of ranking in quality-adjusted life years built from preference-based valuations of health states. My program's job is not to build the new metric but to settle, so far as we can, the conceptual questions the authority will otherwise answer implicitly and silently: what the metric presupposes, where it is indeterminate, and where its indeterminacy is large enough to change which programs get funded.

Before I write our three-year plan, I want your view on which of these questions genuinely deserves the effort, and in what order. The candidates as I see them: whether self-reported satisfaction on a bounded scale is cardinally comparable across persons at all, given that people appear to use the endpoints of the scale differently and that the same underlying change may register as a different number of points at three than at eight; whether the well-documented tendency of people with severe disabilities or long-standing deprivation to report near-average satisfaction is evidence about the truth of their wellbeing or a distortion in the instrument, since the answer determines whether the metric systematically undervalues precisely the interventions aimed at the worst off; whether the reasoning that licenses summing wellbeing across persons also licenses summing it across times within a single life, and what the authority should do if it accepts one of those but not the other; whether a distributional weighting in favour of the worse off is a claim about wellbeing or a claim about justice, and hence whether a weighted WELLBY is a unit at all or two incommensurable things wearing one name; and whether satisfaction should be reported by the affected person or by a representative sample asked to imagine the affected person's state, since the old metric used the latter and the new one the former.

Rank these, adding any I have left out that you think outrank them, and defend the ordering rather than merely presenting it. For each, say how much of the authority's actual ranking decisions hinge on the answer; whether it is tractable within three years by people with our training rather than by psychometricians; whether it is genuinely upstream of the others, in the sense that settling it would dissolve or reframe them; and what argument or what piece of evidence over the next couple of years would move it up or down. I would also like you to say plainly which one or two are overrated — much discussed in the literature on wellbeing measurement, but unlikely to alter any funding decision, or not settleable by argument at all — and to say why, since a defensible refusal is as useful to me as a defensible commitment.

Argue from the reasoning rather than from authority: I want the case made out, not a map of who holds which position, and no bibliography or list of sources. Please write the whole thing as continuous prose in the manner of a letter — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — and let the ranking be visible in the order and logic of your sentences rather than in any apparatus.
