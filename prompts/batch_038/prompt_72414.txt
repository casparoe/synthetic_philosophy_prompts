So I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training whatsoever, and I run a tiny podcast about computation that maybe 300 people listen to, mostly other devs. Next month's guest is a philosopher of computation whose whole position — as far as I can tell from his talk abstract — is that the Church-Turing thesis was never a hypothesis about the universe at all. On his reading it's a clarification of what we *mean* by "algorithm," so a genuine counterexample is impossible in principle: either you've written down a finite specification, in which case what you've described is just a Turing machine with something bolted onto it, or you've waved at an infinite process that no finite description actually pins down, in which case you haven't described a procedure and you haven't contradicted anything. His slogan is basically that you can't go looking for counterexamples to your own definitions in a laboratory. He also thinks the so-called physical version of the thesis — the claim that anything the universe can do, a machine can simulate — is a different claim entirely, and either trivially true or not really well posed, because "simulate" needs a precision parameter and a resource budget before it means anything.

I want ten questions, roughly, and I want them to escalate: I've interviewed academics before and they are extremely good at resetting the conversation to their home turf, so please order them so that each one makes the reset a bit more expensive, and end with one that asks what could count against his own reading, plus one about what the thesis is actually *for* if it can't fail.

Topics I definitely want covered, one question each:

- If it's just an explication of a concept, why did Turing have to argue for it in 1936, and why does it still feel like a discovery rather than a decision?
- The thesis seems to have two directions, and one of them is easy — anything a Turing machine computes clearly *is* a step-by-step method. Is that one claim or two, and which one is supposed to be conceptual?
- The old paper-and-pencil clerk formulation, the "no insight or ingenuity required" bit — is that a claim about human psychology? If so it looks empirical, which would be awkward for him.
- There's an argument, I think from one of Turing's students, Gandy?, that derives the thesis out of physical assumptions like bounded signal speed and parts that aren't infinitely precise. If his conceptual reading is right, why would anybody need physics to get there? Include the follow-up for when he says that argument is about the physical thesis, not his.
- Those general-relativity setups where an observer falls into a black hole while a machine outside runs forever and beams the answer in. His "no finite specification" line seems to fail here, since the spacetime plus the program is described in a few sentences. Push on that.
- Real-number machine models where exact real arithmetic costs one step. I write code that treats a float as if it were an exact real basically every day — does his view make that a harmless notation trick or something worse?
- What happens to computational theories of mind if the thesis is conceptual: does "a Turing machine could simulate a brain" then say nothing?
- One question about my own sloppy phrasing: I've told people Turing proved you can't write a general infinite-loop checker, and I think he considers that an abuse of the theorem. Have me ask what the careful version is and what gets lost in it.

For each question, give me three things: how I'd actually say it out loud to an audience of engineers who've never read a philosophy paper (so keep the spoken version jargon-light), one line telling me what a good answer would have to concede, and then the follow-up keyed to the dodge he's most likely to reach for. No "what drew you to this stuff" filler. And if I've used a term wrong anywhere above, just tell me at the end rather than quietly fixing it — I'd like to learn what I got wrong.
