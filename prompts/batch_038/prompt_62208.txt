I am a second-year graduate student, and the seminar I am taking this term on minds and computation closes with a workshop session in which each of us presents our term paper and then fields questions for a full hour. My turn comes in two weeks.

The paper defends a deliberately narrowed descendant of the Lucas–Penrose argument. I do not argue that human mathematical insight is uncomputable. I argue instead that the mechanist thesis cannot be known, or even rationally believed, by anyone in a position to state it. Here is the skeleton I will put on the board:

1. Suppose for reductio that a given mathematician's arithmetical judgments coincide exactly with the outputs of some Turing machine M whose program can be written down.
2. For that supposition to amount to more than an idle hypothesis, it must come with a specification of M; otherwise there is nothing determinate for the claim to be about, since infinitely many programs yield any given finite stretch of behavior.
3. Handed that specification, the mathematician can construct M's Gödel sentence and can see that it is true — provided they can see that M is consistent.
4. By the second incompleteness theorem, if M is consistent then M cannot establish its own consistency; so whatever insight licenses step 3 cannot come from M's own reasoning.
5. Turn the same requirement around: to know that they are M, the mathematician would have to know that M is consistent, and on the mechanist account the only route to that knowledge available to them is M's own reasoning, which step 4 rules out.
6. Hence even if mechanism is true, no one can know it; and a thesis that could not be known even if it were true is not a claim about cognition but an unfalsifiable piece of metaphysics.

I call this the self-transparency objection, and I want to be clear in the talk that I am trading away the strong conclusion in order to buy a weaker one I think I can actually hold.

The room is specific, and I want the objections calibrated to it rather than drawn from the general literature. Two faculty members work on computation and cognition: both are relaxed functionalists who spend their time on large-scale neural models, and both regard the whole Gödelian debate as a historical curiosity about idealized symbol manipulation that has no bearing on how real systems behave. One graduate student is in mathematical logic, knows the incompleteness theorems properly, and is visibly irritated by philosophers who use consistency and soundness loosely; he will not let an imprecise phrase go past. About eight other seminar students have read the standard critiques of Lucas and Penrose and will mostly reproduce those. One visiting researcher works on paraconsistent logic.

I am not asking you to defend the paper with me. What I need is preparation for the question period:

- The eight to ten objections I should genuinely expect from this room, in roughly the order they will arrive, flagged by how much damage each would do.
- Each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it. The logician's versions should turn on the difference between proving and knowing; the two computationalists should not end up voicing the same complaint twice.
- For each objection, a reply I can deliver in two or three minutes, together with a candid note about where that reply is softest.
- The concessions I should make in the opening minutes to defuse the predictable attacks, and the premises I cannot surrender without losing the paper entirely.
- The one reply that has to land for the argument to survive the hour.
- And the objection I am hoping nobody raises, the one I have no good answer to.

I am after the reasoning here, not a list of sources.
