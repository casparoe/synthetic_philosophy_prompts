so, I got a jury summons for the first time in my life last month (I write backend code, zero legal or philosophy training) and the whole thing is kind of stuck in my head, not because of the case but because of how the selection worked.

here's what happened in my county. the pool they summon from is built out of voter registration and DMV records, and the lady doing the orientation said the point of that is to get a fair cross-section of the community into the building — randomness is the mechanism, representativeness is the goal. then they spend two days whittling it down: anyone with a felony conviction, anyone who knows a party or a lawyer, anyone who was the victim of a similar crime, anyone who already has a view about that kind of case, anyone who'd have trouble sitting through a trial, anyone with a hard financial situation. both sides also get a bunch of strikes they don't have to justify. a guy two rows ahead of me got excused because he does network security for a living and the case involved compromised accounts — the judge's reasoning was basically that he'd know too much and wouldn't defer to the experts. felt backwards to me but whatever. then once you're seated the instructions are: don't look anything up, don't read coverage of it, don't discuss it with anyone, decide only from what's presented in the room.

what I can't get straight is what theory of knowledge and of fairness all this is running on. it looks like it wants randomness because a random draw gives you a slice of the community, but then it filters that slice until the people left are, on exactly the dimensions that matter for the case, *not* a slice — they're the ones with no relevant experience, no relevant expertise, and no relevant prior beliefs. so is the goal representativeness or emptiness? which one is actually load-bearing?

can you do the reconstruction for me — separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it just happens to fit nicely with, and flag where it's underdetermined so that several different theories would license it equally? specifically: does it need impartiality to mean having no information, or no stake, or no preconceptions, or something like a symmetry condition between the two sides? those feel like four different claims and the process seems to be running on more than one at once without noticing. does it also presuppose something about how accurate a verdict can get — like that naive reasoners shown both sides converge on the facts, that expertise is a liability because it imports priors nobody got to cross-examine, that twelve roughly independent blanks beat one informed person? and what does it quietly rule out — I keep thinking of setups where the factfinder acts more like an investigator who goes and digs up evidence himself, or where the finder is chosen *for* relevant knowledge.

then the thing that made me write this. after jury duty I watched a talk about lottocracy — replacing an elected chamber with a few hundred citizens chosen by lot, stratified so the body matches the population on age, sex, education, region, maybe class, paid properly, sitting for like a year, with expert briefings and research staff at their disposal. those folks seem to want the filter run the *opposite* direction from my county courthouse: pick for demographics, then deliberately pour information in, hire the experts, commission the studies. same lottery, opposite attitude toward knowledge. is that just because the jobs differ — settling a claim about a past event vs deciding what to do about a future one — or does it mean one of the two is confused about what sortition actually buys you? and concretely: what would my county have to change about how it picks jurors if it adopted the lottocracy theory of what a random draw is good for, and what would the sortition people have to give up if they adopted the courthouse's?

not looking for a verdict on which system is better, I want the assumptions underneath each one made explicit and then checked against each other for consistency. also fine with you telling me the two practices are compatible with way more theories than the people defending them admit.
