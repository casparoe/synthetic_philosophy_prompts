A funder's advisory panel has spent two days trying to settle a single parameter: the weight to give the welfare of farmed insect larvae in a cost-effectiveness model that the funder uses to compare a programme reducing suffering among black soldier fly larvae against programmes whose effects on vertebrates are well established. The panel is deadlocked and must file a number by Friday. Write a dialogue between the two members whose disagreement is doing the blocking, conducted during a break in the corridor outside the meeting room.

Characterise the two positions as follows and hold to them throughout.

The first panellist holds that the model's rule is sound: expected moral weight is the number of individuals affected, multiplied by one's credence that they are sentient, multiplied by an intensity factor and a duration. On the parameters the panel has been given — suppose, purely for the purposes of the exchange, that the facilities in question handle three hundred billion larvae a year, and that this panellist's considered credence in larval sentience is two in a hundred — the product is enormous, and it would be indefensible for so small a credence to do so little work. They take themselves to be arguing for recklessness in the good sense: when the stakes are vast and the probability small but not negligible, one ought to act on the expectation rather than on the most likely outcome.

The second panellist is not a sentience denier and does not think the first is wrong about the arithmetic. Their objection is that the number is not the kind of thing that can be entered into a multiplication at all. Three commitments should be visible in what they say. First, the credence is not a probability over a well-posed question, because nobody on the panel can say what would make it true that a larva is phenomenally conscious, so the numeral is a tidy-looking placeholder for conceptual confusion rather than a measure of uncertainty. Second, whatever value one writes depends entirely on the reference class one picks — animals, arthropods, insects, organisms with a brain of some particular architecture — and there is no non-question-begging way to choose among them, though defensible choices range across three orders of magnitude. Third, and most damaging, the credences are not independent across individuals: if the panel ever learns anything decisive about one honeybee it should update on all of them at once, so multiplying by three hundred billion is not buying three hundred billion chances but copying a single uncertain hypothesis that many times over. On this view the scale of the numbers does not rescue a low credence; it merely reveals that everything turns on one binary question, which the panel should be trying to settle rather than converting into a weighting.

Here is the twist that ought to drive the exchange. When the chair presses each of them to write something down, the first writes 0.02 and the second writes 0.01. They are a factor of two apart, and they disagree about everything. The dialogue should make the reader feel how much that near-agreement in numerals is concealing.

Requirements. Both panellists must be fully competent, and neither may be softened for the sake of the other's rhetoric. The first must have a real answer to the reference-class charge and to the non-independence charge; the second must have a real answer to the accusation that their position amounts to a comfortable refusal to act until the question has been settled, which is to say never. They must respond to what the other has actually just said rather than to a prepared speech, and they must be allowed to interrupt, to press a point the other has evaded, and to notice when an answer has not been given. Each must concede at least one substantive point to the other. The dialogue must not end in agreement or in either panellist changing sides: it ends with the chair calling them back in, the parameter still unwritten, and each able to say why the number the other wants to file would be a mistake. Keep the argument inside the panel's own concrete numbers and procedure; do not let either character reach for a famous thought experiment, an appeal to a named philosopher's authority, or a general plea that the matter is complicated. Aim for roughly 1,300 to 1,600 words, and give the two panellists names of your choosing.

Please write in British English throughout. Give me the dialogue as continuous prose with the speakers' names used for attribution, in the manner of something written out in a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, and no tables.
