I sit on the student senate of a mid-sized university, and this term a committee I'm on has been asked to choose between two competing dining-services contracts: the incumbent per-item pricing model and a single all-inclusive flat-rate plan. The proposal on the table, from a member who is serious about it, is that we not vote at all. Instead the university would open an internal prediction market for a month in which students and staff trade contracts paying out according to which plan produces the higher mean score on the spring dining-satisfaction survey; whichever side ends up priced above a set threshold gets adopted, and the senate's role is reduced to ratifying the result. In the meeting, the objection came immediately and from several directions: undergraduates know nothing about food-service cost structures, contract terms, or how satisfaction surveys are worded and administered; a market like that would be thin, dominated by a dozen activists with strong priors and free time; and we would be handing a decision with real distributional consequences — flat rates transfer money from light eaters to heavy eaters — to whatever price emerges from that thin crowd.

The proposer's reply was a story about a country fair in 1906, where several hundred fairgoers paid to guess the weight an ox would dress out at after slaughter: farmers, butchers, students, and people who by all accounts guessed wildly. When the statistician who had attended went through the tickets afterward, the crowd's central estimate came within about a percent of the weight the ox actually turned out to be, a fact that had been settled by a scale nobody in the crowd controlled and that was only revealed after the guessing was done. From this he drew two conclusions. First, expertise is not required: the ignorant guesses are not a defect to be filtered out but the noise whose cancellation is the mechanism, and whatever the crowd is right about, it is right about for that reason rather than despite it. Second, and more pointedly, our objection was therefore not a prudential worry about accuracy but elitism in disguise — a preference for being ruled by people like ourselves, dressed up as a concern for getting things right. He added, in passing, that unlike a vote a market does not make anything true: satisfaction under each contract is already a fixed fact about the world, and the market is merely reading it off, so there is no room for a self-fulfilling-prophecy worry.

I found the story genuinely persuasive when I heard it, and I still cannot tell whether my residual unease is an objection or only the discomfort of being overruled by a mechanism I do not understand, so I would like the analogy audited rather than retold. Please give the mapping explicitly — what in our case plays the role of the ox, the fair, the guessers, the prize, the scale that settles the bet, the moment when the truth becomes public — and then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on the use he made of it from the ones that are merely unattractive without being load-bearing (for instance, whether it matters that the fairgoers were guessing for amusement and small stakes while our traders would be gambling on an outcome they are themselves subject to; say so either way rather than assuming). I especially want to know what the analogy quietly imports into the target: conditions about the quantity being estimated having a determinate value independent of the estimating, about who settles it and whether they can be moved by the estimators, about how many estimators there are and whether their errors are independent or correlated, about who absorbs the cost when the crowd is wrong, and about the difference between arriving at an estimate and issuing a decision. Then tell me which of his two conclusions survives that audit and which does not, whether the charge of elitism is licensed by the ox story or is being carried in from somewhere else entirely, and whether a redesign — a settlement metric less exposed to the decision it settles, thicker trading, a rule that tips the balance rather than dictating it — would rescue the conclusion by making the analogy do less work or by making it do more. If there is a better analogy for what such a market actually does in this setting, offer it and say what it would license; if there is not, say that plainly. Finally, I want you to be explicit about where your own reasoning is most likely to fail here: what finding, or what version of the case that differs from mine in some specific respect, would lead you to a different verdict, and whether I have mis-stated his position in a way that makes the analogy look weaker than it actually is.
