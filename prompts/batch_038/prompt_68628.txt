I am a novelist, and I would like a piece of philosophical housekeeping done properly, because the argument about my manuscript has come to rest on a sentence I cannot quite take apart.

The sentence is: "He isn't the same person anymore."

Where I met it. I have spent six years on a novel whose protagonist does something unforgivable at twenty-two and then lives another fifty-nine years alongside it; he is eighty-one when the book ends. The novel runs in two registers. The young man is narrated in the third person, as "he"; the old man narrates his own chapters in the first person. The seam is deliberate — the reader is meant to feel a little unsure whether "I" and "he" are picking out one thing. My spouse, who read philosophy as an undergraduate and has never entirely let it go, objected not to the seam but to the moral architecture it supports. In the final movement the old man is called to account: by a daughter, by a civil proceeding, and by himself. My spouse says the book is cheating, that it wants both the comfort of survival (one continuous life, one guilty self who can atone) and the drama of transformation (a stranger who must be made to learn what he did), and that these are not jointly available: the young man, on my spouse's account, "isn't the same person anymore," so the reckoning is either misplaced or merely sentimental.

I do not want the slogan defended or refuted. I want it disassembled.

1. Lay out, as a menu, the distinct and precise claims that "he isn't the same person" could be making in a case like mine. I expect at least the following: a claim about qualitative similarity between two person-stages; a claim about strict numerical identity; a claim about degrees of psychological continuity or connectedness; a claim that personhood is a status defined by the accountability it carries rather than by any underlying substance; a claim about temporal parts of a longer four-dimensional whole; and something merely biological about metabolic turnover. Please add readings I have missed. For each, say whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false — and where it is substantive, say what has to be true for it to hold.

2. For each reading, say whether it would actually do the work my spouse needs: whether it would show that holding the eighty-one-year-old answerable for the twenty-two-year-old's act is confused, or that my two-register structure is a cheat rather than a device. My suspicion is that several of the readings are entirely true and entirely useless to that argument, and I want to see which ones, and why.

3. Tell me how the readings interact with the metaphysics of persistence. If persons endure — wholly present at each moment of their history — is the slogan available at all, or does it collapse into an ordinary claim about qualitative change in a numerically identical subject? If persons perdure, so that the young man and the old man are distinct temporal parts of a single four-dimensional whole, does the slogan gain force (the earlier part is literally a different object) or lose it (the whole, not the part, is the agent who acted and the one who answers)? Which of the two pictures is the better ally for my spouse, and does either one actually license the conclusion about responsibility rather than merely restating it?

4. Say which reading people who repeat this sentence in ordinary life — at funerals, in courtrooms, in workshops — most often intend, and how much of the sentence's rhetorical force comes from the hearer supplying the strongest reading while the speaker is entitled only to the weakest.

5. Finally, as a craftsman rather than a metaphysician: if the accurate picture is that survival and accountability come apart — that whatever persists need not be what responsibility tracks — is there a coherent position on which the seam between "I" and "he" is not a sentimental blur but the honest rendering of a real distinction? I am not asking for plot advice. I want to know whether such a position exists and what it commits me to.

Please keep the readings genuinely distinct rather than restating one thesis in successive vocabularies, and do not let the answer flatten into a survey: I want verdicts on each reading, and I want to know which reading my spouse is most likely to have meant, since that is the one the manuscript has to answer.
