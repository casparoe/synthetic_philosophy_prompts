I work on build infrastructure and there's a thing in our industry I can't get my head around. I'd like someone to take it apart properly — I have zero philosophy background so please don't assume I know the names for any of this.

Half my job is trying to make origins stop mattering. Reproducible builds: the entire point is that if you compile our source on your own machine you get bit-for-bit the same bytes we ship, same hash, and once that's true we tell people it no longer matters who built it, on what server, in what country, or whether you trust us at all. Identical bytes means identical artifact. Git is the same idea baked into the substrate — a blob's identity literally *is* the hash of its content, so if two people on opposite sides of the planet write the same file, that's not two copies that happen to match, it's one object with one address. Content is the whole thing.

The other half of my job is treating origins as the only thing that matters. The license scanner screams bloody murder if a function in our tree matches GPL code byte-for-byte and makes us delete it or relicence the entire product — but if I pay someone to write that exact same function from scratch without ever looking at the original, same name, same logic, same compiled bytes, the scanner is silent and legal signs off, and every user gets an identical program. When an AI writes a hunk we require disclosure and sometimes deletion; when a junior dev retypes someone's snippet character by character off a blog post we call that learning and nothing fires. And a fork is treated as a different project from the original even when the trees are the same and half the contributors are the same people — the original author of MySQL forked it into MariaDB and everyone just agreed that was a new thing with its own reputation and its own trust level, even though on day one it was the same code by mostly the same humans. Same bytes, different worth, purely because of where they came from.

Both of those are official policy where I work and I've implemented both of them, sometimes in the same quarter.

So what does each one assume about what a piece of software actually *is*? One seems to say a work is just its content — a bitstring, and matching bitstrings are the same work and worth the same. The other seems to say a work is content plus a history, that who made it and how is part of the thing itself and not metadata hanging off it. Can both be right? Or is one of them the real commitment and the other just convenience we haven't noticed?

Please be careful to seperate what the practice strictly forces from what's just a plausible story that happens to fit it. If several different theories would license the exact same scanner, or the exact same build system, say so — because then I don't have a contradiction, I have an underdetermined tool, and I'd rather know that. Also tell me what each policy quietly rules out, i.e. what views we've stopped being able to hold without noticing.

Then run the swap for me. If we made the scanner content-only, so independent-but-identical code is still a violation, what breaks, and does anything else in the industry have to break with it? If we made the build system provenance-only, so a hash match from a builder you don't trust counts as nothing, what breaks? Which of those two worlds is closer to the one we're actually living in?
