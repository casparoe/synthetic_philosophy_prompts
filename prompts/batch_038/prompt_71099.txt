I'm the thesaurus editor for a public photo archive — about 2 million images, 30-some curators doing the indexing — and I keep getting the same kind of request with no principled way to answer it.

A subject term gets coined for one kind of picture and then people stretch it. Our seaport term, call it HARBOR, is now being applied to marinas, dry docks, canal locks, a freight terminal two hundred km inland, and one curator wants it for a photo of a model ship in a bottle. Some of those feel like obviously fine and one feels obviously wrong, and I can't articulate the difference beyond taste. That curator's counter is the part that gets me: if you draw the line anywhere you have to justify it, and you can't, so don't draw one.

The reverse happens too. Two terms coined years apart turn out to be used interchangeably by everyone, somebody proposes a merge, and the merge feels like it loses something I can't name.

We get maybe twenty of these a month. Stakes are asymmetric and annoying: undoing a bad merge means re-indexing tens of thousands of records, but if I split everything to be safe the vocabulary turns into mush and retrieval gets worse.

A former indexer sent me some of the old Copycat material — Hofstadter, the letter-string analogies, the idea that concepts are fluid and that slippage from one concept to a neighbour is constrained by which relationships you actually noticed rather than being a free-for-all, plus something about some concepts being deeper and harder to slip out of than others. That sounds exactly like my problem, but I have no idea how to turn it into something a curator can use.

So: write me a decision procedure for term-extension and term-merger requests. Two versions — a card a curator can run in fifteen minutes before submitting, and my longer version for the quarterly committee, who are smart but have no philosophy or cognitive science background. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the actual tests that separate a legitimate stretch from a hijacking, and explicit stop points where the procedure hands the decision back to a human becuase it's really a policy question about retrieval rather than a fact about the concept. Also tell me which parts of that analogy machinery genuinely survive translation into a bureaucratic process and which are just a pretty metaphor — I don't want the procedure resting on anything that only works for strings of letters.

Then run it on three requests: the model ship in a bottle; extending our STRIKE term, coined for industrial walkouts, to a hunger strike and to a school climate walkout; and the proposed merge of two near-synonymous roadside-signage terms that everyone already uses interchangeably. For at least one of the three, the procedure should come out against the curator pushing hardest — I need to be able to show them it wasn't just me saying no. And be honest about which steps are my judgment wearing a procedural costume.
