I do part-time work on an interpretability team, and last week we burned a solid two hours on an arguemnt about whether a feature we'd found in a 7B model was "the same" as one another group had reported in a completely different model, and in the end nobody could say what would even settle it. My housemate, who is a patent attorney and does not care about this at all, listened to me explain it over dinner and said "so you're both describing the same invention and you have no way of telling?" — which was annoying because he was basically right, and because it made me realise that if anyone ever tried to certify a deployed model's internals instead of just its outputs, some standards body would have to write down identity criteria for mechanisms, and the resulting paperwork would be magnificent.

So here's what I want: a parody technical specification, roughly 800 words, issued by an invented certification body (name it something blandly bureaucratic, give it a document number and a revision letter), setting out the rules for "mechanism identity and continuity" — i.e. under what conditions a circuit or feature in version N+1 of a model counts as the same mechanism as the one that was audited in version N, and therefore whether the existing safety certification carries over or has to be re-earned. Format it the way these documents actually look: numbered clauses with sub-clauses, a definitions section, at least one annex, and a compliance checkbox table the applicant has to fill in and sign. Please include: a definitional clause that tries and visibly fails to define "mechanism" without circularity; numeric tolerance thresholds that are obviously arbitrary but stated with total confidence; a clause covering the case where the mechanism can't be located but the behavior hasn't changed, i.e. the superposition excuse and how many times you're allowed to use it before it's a nonconformity; a rule on whether a sparse-autoencoder latent can be registered under a reserved name at all, given it's basis-dependent and the dictionary is someone's choice; a grandfathering provision for legacy features nobody can reproduce any more; a dispute-resolution procedure for two labs claiming the same feature name; and a short annex on functionally equivalent but structurally different implementations. Somewhere in there, one drafting note in which a committee member's dissenting comment is recorded and then overruled.

The jokes have to come from getting the interpretability right, not from making the researchers dumb — I want someone who does this for a living to laugh because they recognize the actual mess: that ablating something changes the model you're measuring, that effect sizes depend entirely on your patching setup, that features are polysemantic so any boundary is a judgement call, that a circuit found in a two-layer toy model may not exist in the frontier one, that "we diffed the two checkpoints and it's basically the same" functions as an unfalsifiable comfort blanket, and that a monitor watching internals is only as good as its story about which internals count as the mechanism. The target is the practice of writing precise-looking identity criteria for something whose boundaries nobody can draw, and more generally the assurance-document genre that manufactures decimal places where there's no measurement underneath. Keep it deadpan throughout, no winking, and no closing line that admits the whole thing is silly. Don't name real papers, real companies or real people, and don't give me a bibliography or a further-reading list — invent all the institutional furniture, keep the technical vocabulary honest.
