Play a New Testament scholar who's also spent real time in general historiography and epistemology — someone who can step outside the Jesus guild's habits for a minute.

I'm starting a methods seminar on the quest for the historical Jesus and I don't want to open with another book that just *applies* the criteria. I want the spec first. So: what does a set of criteria of authenticity have to deliver before it counts as a method at all?

Fix the purpose, because adequacy is relative to it. Mine is this: a method good enough to adjudicate between the big competing portraits — say, Jesus as an apocalyptic prophet expecting an imminent cosmic reversal, versus Jesus as a non-apocalyptic teacher of subversive wisdom — without the outcome being decided by who's doing the adjudicating. If you think that purpose is already incoherent, tell me why and pick a better one, but say which you're using.

Then give me the desiderata list, with the consideration or case behind each one, not just a label. Some candidates I've got, add freely and throw some out as camp prejudice rather than real requirements:

- it must be able to assign material to Jesus without already presupposing a picture of Jesus. The classic worry: a dissimilarity filter can only surface what's unlike both first-century Judaism and the later church, so what it hands back is a figure who belongs to neither — a Jesus who looks like nobody in particular. Is that a fatal defect of that criterion, or a fair price for getting something checkable?
- it must be consistent with what we otherwise know about oral tradition and collective memory in a non-literate setting, including the fact that a community preserves things *because* they matter to it, not despite that.
- it has to leave the data explained — criteria so strict that the existence of the tradition itself becomes inexplicable are self-undermining.
- it must handle the near-universality problem: almost everything is multiply attested at some level of generality. Mark, Q (if you want it), Paul, John, Thomas. So does attestation of a *theme* across sources count the same as attestation of a *specific saying*? And what if the sources aren't as independent as the counting assumes?
- it must say what to do when criteria conflict, and whether they're weightable or just vetoing.
- embarrassment: a criterion that runs on the historian's own guess about what would have been awkward for the early church — does that pass any test of intersubjective checkability, or is it just the historian's theology in reverse?
- a criterion has to be able to fail. If nothing in the gospels could in principle be rejected by the method, is it a method?

Mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of one camp (form-critical, positivist, memory-approach, whatever). Tell me which single requirement knocks out the most existing proposals. And — this is the part I most want — say whether some subset is jointly unsatisfiable. Specifically: can you have criteria that are (a) independent of the historian's own reconstruction, (b) able to find material that is continuous with Judaism and with the early church rather than only material that's discontinuous, and (c) strong enough to underwrite confident individual attributions? If the list turns out to be an impossibility result, say so plainly and say which of the three has to go.

End with a very short scorecard, two lines max per entry: the classic package (dissimilarity, multiple attestation, embarrassment, coherence, Semitic linguistic environment) and the memory-based alternative that wants to replace it. Keep it short, the list is the deliverable.

Think carefully and actually brainstorm before you commit — a first pass that just relabels the existing criteria as "desiderata" is exactly what I don't want. Bottom line up front: your very first sentence should be your verdict on whether an adequate set of criteria is possible for the purpose above, then the list and reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate my question back at me.
