I have run the country office of a small engineering nonprofit for fourteen months. We build and maintain piped-water systems in a district where the provincial administration effectively stopped functioning several years ago, so in practice we are the only institution around that fixes anything. Our funder requires — and I agree with the requirement — that siting and maintenance decisions be made by the elected district water council rather than by us. Last spring the council voted to route the new system past the chair's own village, which sits uphill and already had a working borehole. Two hamlets on the far side of the ridge, roughly nine hundred people, have been walking about four hours a day for water ever since. None of them have a vote on the council: they fall below the population threshold for a seat, and nobody at the time the threshold was set thought that mattered. I have now drafted a rule that we would simply impose on every project we fund. No household is to be more than fifteen hundred metres from a functioning tap; if a council's plan leaves anyone outside that band, we redraw the plan ourselves and fund the redrawn version. The council would still vote, but it would be voting inside a box I built and can move.

My program officer thinks this is obviously right, on the grounds that you have to hold the line at the beginning and that the people in those hamlets will be grateful in ten years even if the council is furious now. My predecessor thought the opposite and devolved everything; two of her systems were sited for much the same reason, then went unmaintained, failed inside four years, and we ended up rebuilding them at something like triple the cost. So I have unflattering data on both sides of my own organization's practice, and every report I sign still contains a sentence about self-determination that I wrote and meant. There is a further complication I can't work out how to weigh. The district was administered directly by a foreign power for about sixty years — my own donor's country, as it happens — which left behind a land code, a road network, a courthouse and a salaried civil service; within roughly five years of the handover the whole apparatus had been captured by two families, and the current council chair is a grandson of one of them. When I say that these people ought to decide for themselves, I am saying it in a district where the last people who decided for them were us, at length, and where the specific reason the council looks the way it does can be traced fairly directly to how that administration drew its boundaries and staffed its offices.

What I want is a principle, not process advice. I do not want to be told to consult more stakeholders, to build local capacity, or to look into participatory budgeting, and I do not want a survey of governance frameworks. I want the test that separates the decisions I may take away from a body like this council from the decisions where I have to fund their choice and live with the consequence. State it precisely enough that I could apply it to a case nobody has described yet, and then tell me which of the two things my organization has already done — my distance rule, or the systems my predecessor let fail — your test condemns. Beyond that, four specific questions. Does it matter that we are the ones paying, or would the same override be defensible if we were only advising? Is a rule more defensible for being announced in advance and applied identically in every district we work in, or does announcing it merely make the override more efficient and harder to resist? Is there a version of such a rule that retires itself — something I could impose once, in a way that reliably ends my standing to impose anything — or is that simply how arrangements of this kind always describe themselves in the paperwork? And finally: could your principle survive being read aloud, in translation, at a council meeting, with the chair in the room and the two hamlets outside it? If it could not, I would rather know that now than discover it after the rule is in a policy document with my name on it.
