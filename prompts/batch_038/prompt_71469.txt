I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training beyond what I've picked up reading stuff at night. Most of my work is on ranking systems, and our team's stated goal in the quarterly planning doc is literally "maximize user utility." I've been stuck on that phrase for a couple weeks now, mostly because I can't tell what we think we mean by it, and my manager gets annoyed when I bring it up. Anyway, a friend of mine is a grad student in philosophy and she runs an informal reading group on the philosophy of economics — five or six people, Tuesday nights, her friends plus a couple of her advisor's students — and she asked everyone to write down their actual views beforehand so people could take swings at them. Here's what I wrote down.

1. Welfare is preference satisfaction, full stop. There's no objective list of things that are good for a person regardless of whether they want them, and I'm not a hedonist either — pleasure isn't the good. Something is good for you to the extent that you get what you want.
2. I want the strong version of revealed preference, not the weak one. "She prefers A to B" just means she'd take A if she were offered the choice. There's no extra fact about what someone "really" wants sitting behind the behavior. I know the weaker reading — choice as evidence of preference rather than constitutive of it — is more popular, but the weak version smuggles the inner state back in, and that's exactly what I'm trying to avoid.
3. Utility numbers are bookkeeping, nothing more. Ordinal only. No cardinal magnitudes, no summing across people, and whenever someone says a policy "produces more total utility" I don't know what they think they're doing. (A friend who's an econ PhD told me expected utility theory gives you numbers that are unique only up to a positive linear transformation, which I took to mean: real enough to compare one person's own options against each other, meaningless across different people.)
4. Despite all that, I'm a fan of allocating public healthcare by cost per QALY, and I specifically think the fact that it lets you trade one patient's benefit against another's is a feature and not a bug. Better that than a bright-line rule about never trading.
5. Defaults are hugely underrated as policy. Opt-out organ donation, automatic enrollment in retirement savings, that sort of thing. Nobody's coerced, you can always opt out, and it makes people better off. Honestly this is also what I'd want for our product.
6. Akrasia is real. I installed a site blocker on my laptop and I think the version of me who installed it understood my interests better than the version of me who wants to turn it off at 11pm. Sometimes you choose against what you most want.
7. Methodologically I don't trust self report at all — surveys, stated preferences, user feedback forms, introspection. Its all wording effects and social desirability. Only behavior counts.

So: does this hang together? I'd like an audit, not a lecture — please don't walk me through the history of the concept of utility, I've already read the overview pages. Go through the pairs and tell me which ones are in genuine tension and which ones only look like they are, and rank the real ones by severity instead of presenting them all as equally bad. For each, say what would have to be given up or watered down, what the cheapest available fix is, and what that fix costs me elsewhere in the list. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most damage. I have no idea where the problem actually is, and I'm deliberately not telling you which of these I'd rather drop, because I don't want you steering toward the one I'd find least annoying to lose.
