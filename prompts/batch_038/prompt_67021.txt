I sit on the modelling sub-committee of a public body that allocates research funding for long-horizon risk-reduction programmes. Our governing instrument directs us to rank candidate programmes by expected benefit, and to make that operational we maintain a straightforward expected-value model in which each programme is represented as a gamble over a countable state space. Ordinarily the resulting series converge absolutely and nothing is contentious. One live proposal, however, has exactly the structure of the Pasadena game: branch *n* occurs with probability 2⁻ⁿ and yields utility (−1)ⁿ⁺¹ · 2ⁿ/n, so the probability-weighted contributions are the alternating harmonic terms +1, −1/2, +1/3, −1/4, … and the expectation converges only conditionally. The chair has asked me to recommend which philosophical response to this problem we should write into our standing decision rule, and I would like an independent assessment of the options before I draft anything.

One feature of the case should shape the whole evaluation. The very same gamble can be re-described by enumerating its branches in a different order. If we list them in the order 1, 2, 4, 3, 6, 8, 5, 10, 12, … — that is, each odd-numbered branch *n* followed by branches 2*n* and 2*n*+2 — the contributions come out as +1, −1/2, −1/4, +1/3, −1/6, −1/8, …, which still converges, but to ln 2 + ½ ln(1/2) = ½ ln 2 ≈ 0.35 rather than to ln 2 ≈ 0.69. Nothing about the gamble has changed: it is the same set of probability–utility pairs under a relabelling. Treat these two presentations as the central test case for every candidate below.

Please rank the following seven responses, and only these seven, as I have characterised them:

1. **Bounded utility.** Insist that admissible utility functions be bounded above and below; every gamble's expectation then converges absolutely, and the Pasadena-type case is ruled out as a misdescription rather than solved.
2. **Absolute-convergence requirement.** Grant that an expectation is a sum over an unordered countable set of outcomes, and so exists only when it converges absolutely. The proposal in question simply has no expectation; the theory falls silent and we supply a separate default rule for such cases.
3. **Truncation in the generating order.** Define the value as the limit of the partial sums taken in the order in which the state space is generated by the underlying process — here, the order of the coin tosses — yielding ln 2, and treat alternative enumerations as mere notational variants with no normative standing.
4. **Summability methods.** Assign a value by some regularisation procedure (a discounting factor taken to a limit, Cesàro- or Abel-type averaging), on the grounds that such methods extend ordinary summation and are better behaved on divergent series.
5. **Dropping completeness.** Abandon the completeness axiom and represent the gamble's value as a set or interval of admissible sums, or as incomparable with other options, then apply a decision rule designed for imprecise or incomplete comparisons.
6. **Unrealisability.** Deny that such gambles are genuine objects of choice: no finite agent or institution can offer payoffs that grow exponentially without bound, so the case is an artefact of the mathematics and decision theory need not accommodate it.
7. **Bypassing expectation.** Compare gambles without aggregating at all, using statewise or distributional dominance relations, and accept that some pairs of options will remain unranked.

Score each response from 0 to 5 on four criteria, then rank them in tiers:

- **Enumeration-invariance:** does it deliver the same verdict for the two presentations above, and if not, does it have a principled account of why one presentation is privileged?
- **Coverage:** does it continue to yield determinate rankings, unchanged, for the absolutely convergent cases that constitute almost all of our workload?
- **Parsimony of added machinery:** how much unmotivated apparatus does it introduce — a cut-off point, a choice among summation methods, a default rule for gaps?
- **Administrability:** could a board of non-specialists apply it consistently and defend it publicly when it produces a verdict that looks counter-intuitive?

Weight the first two criteria as decisive and the last two as tie-breakers, and say so explicitly if you would weight them differently.

Please write in British English, and organise your answer in exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **Bottom line** — no more than 150 words: the single response we should adopt, followed by the full tier list with one line per response.
2. **Score table** — the seven responses as rows, the four criteria as columns, with a total column and a tier column.
3. **Justifications** — roughly 700 words, tier by tier, engaging explicitly with the two presentations of the gamble and with what each response would imply for our ordinary, well-behaved programmes.
4. **The best case against** — around 250 words: the strongest argument available to the highest-ranked response that you did *not* place first, argued as its advocate would argue it, not as a straw man.
5. **Implementation note** — around 150 words: the exact wording of a one- or two-sentence rule we could insert into the decision manual, plus a note on what the rule tells the board to do when it is triggered.

Write from your own understanding of the issues rather than from sources: no bibliography, no citations, and no reference lists. If you think one of my seven characterisations is inaccurate or conflates two distinct positions, flag the problem briefly where it arises rather than quietly substituting a list of your own.
