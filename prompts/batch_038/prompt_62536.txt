I'm writing a feature for a general-interest science magazine about the recurring argument over whether viruses are alive, and I have come away from my reporting with a metaphor that seems to be doing an enormous amount of work in the origins-of-life world. At a prebiotic chemistry workshop I covered last month, one of the principal investigators waved away my whole framing of the virus question and offered a replacement: life, he said, is like a flame. Not a thing but a process — a pattern sustained by the throughput of energy and matter, with every molecule in the flame replaced many times over while the flame itself persists. He pointed out that this is close to Schrödinger's old insistence that an organism keeps itself away from equilibrium by drawing order from its environment, and that it has a rigorous pedigree in Prigogine's dissipative structures, where flames sit alongside convection cells as things held together by flux rather than by their material. He then used the metaphor for three distinct jobs. First, against the virus dispute: a single virion sitting inert in a droplet is no more alive than an unstruck match is a fire, but a virus population burning through a host is a flame in full roar, so the ninety-year argument is a category error caused by asking about the wrong unit. Second, against definitions in general: flames have no edges you can mark — nobody can say where the flame stops and the hot air begins — so demanding a sharp criterion of life is a philosopher's tic, and the honest position is that aliveness comes in degrees. Third, in favor of a research program: if life is fundamentally a mode of energy dissipation, then the way to look for it on an exoplanet or in a vent field is to look for persistent chemical disequilibrium rather than for particular molecules or particular chemistries, which would free astrobiology from carbon chauvinism.

I want to be fair to the image, but I also don't want to run three paragraphs of it on the authority of one enthusiastic chemist. A virologist I spoke with the following day gave me the objection that unsettled me: the flame picture seems to make dormancy unintelligible. A bacterial spore, a seed in a soil bank, a tardigrade in cryptobiosis, an embryo in liquid nitrogen — each is a fire that has gone out and can be relit, and biologists count them, insure them, and argue about their moral status precisely as living things, whereas a cooled wick is simply not a flame any more. Her second worry was that a process without boundaries dissolves the individual, and an awful lot of biology — censuses, lineage tracing, counting organisms for conservation purposes — needs countable individuals. So: audit the metaphor for me. Lay out the mapping explicitly, in a table if that is cleanest, showing what in the flame answers to metabolism, reproduction, heredity, the individual organism, development, death, and evolution, and then where the correspondence fails. I especially want to know which features of the source the image quietly imports that living systems may not share — a flame keeps no record of itself, has no descendants that differ from it in a way anything could select on, cannot be mistaken about its surroundings — and which of the three claims above is actually carried by independent reasoning rather than by the picture. My hunch is that the disequilibrium point would survive even if the flame talk were pure decoration, while the virus point depends entirely on it; please test both hunches rather than accept them. Rank the disanalogies by whether they bite for the intended use as against being merely interesting, state plainly whether each of the three conclusions stands or falls, and finish by proposing a better metaphor for the same three purposes if one exists — or by arguing that none does, and saying what that would tell us about the concept of life itself.

Please write in American English and keep the whole response under 900 words: a short verdict on the three claims up front, then the mapping and the audit, then the replacement metaphor or the case against one. I need something tight and self-contained enough that I can lift substantial portions of it straight into my reporting notes.
