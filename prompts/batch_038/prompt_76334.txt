The idea. There's a family of explanations that cite a rationale but no reasoner. A termite mound is built to buffer temperature and humidity, though no termite represents any of that. A market keeps nudging production toward what people will pay for, though nobody plans it. A trained network reliably steers towards an objective that is written down nowhere in the system and that its own designers can't state. In each case the rationale looks real in the sense that actually matters — it supports counterfactuals, it tells you how the system will respond when you change its surroundings — but there's no representation of it in any head, and often nobody around who could supply one. Reasons without reasoners. Call them free-floating rationales, that's my working label.

The standard treatments are roughly these three. Eliminativism: it's a façon of speaking and everything real gets stated in non-teleological terms. Full-blooded realism: ascribing rationales latches onto genuine facts about the system, so a rationale needs a reasoner about as much as a centre of gravity needs somebody thinking about it. And the instrumentalist middle, where it's true-ish and pragmatic. Everyone agrees on the cases, nobody agrees on what's happening, and meanwhile an enormous amount of normative work is loaded onto this talk — proper function in medicine and biology, functional explanation in social science with the familiar objection that there's no feedback mechanism, and now 'the model is implicitly optimising for X, so we're entitled to hold it to X'.

Here's the hunch I want the proposal built around, and it isn't the usual question. The interesting problem isn't whether free-floating rationales are real; it's that there is usually more then one on offer for the same system, all equally supported by the counterfactual structure. The mound reads as thermoregulation or as a by-product of pheromone-following. The firm reads as profit-maximising or as empire-building by its managers. The network reads as honest or as sycophancy-avoidance taking a detour. And nothing inside the system adjudicates, precisely because there's no reasoner whose point of view could fix which reading is the system's own. So: what makes one candidate the rationale of the system, if anything does? And if the tie-break has to come from the ascriber's interests, is that fatal across the board, or fatal only for some of the uses? My guess is that it survives explanation cheerfully and is lethal for the attribution-and-responsibility uses, but I haven't shown that, and I suspect there's a wrinkle: in the machine-learning cases there may be an asymmetry, because the training process supplies a historical etiology that the mound has and the market arguably doesn't — or maybe it's the other way round. I genuinely don't know, which is why it's a project.
