The reference class problem is, on its face, one of the better-behaved disputes in the philosophy of probability: the object of disagreement is formal and public. Anyone who assigns a probability to a single case — this coin toss, this defendant, this patient — must place that case in a class and cite the class frequency, and everyone since Reichenbach agrees that the choice of class is not free. What they cannot agree on is how to make it. Reichenbach says to take the narrowest class for which reliable statistics exist; Salmon replaces narrowness with homogeneity, requiring that the chosen class admit no statistically relevant subdivision, and then concedes that what we can ever actually identify is epistemic rather than genuine homogeneity; Kyburg turns the matter into a rule of specificity, instructing us to reason from the most specific class about which we have statistical knowledge. Against these, running from Venn's observation that any individual sits in indefinitely many series, each with its own proportion, to Hájek's conclusion that no rule can be given at all, stands the claim that the problem is not merely unsolved but insoluble, and that it is fatal to any interpretation on which probabilities attach to singular events. Meanwhile a third group of broadly Bayesian writers insists that there was never a metaphysical problem to solve, only a prior-setting problem, and that the debate is a residue of a discredited frequency theory. Three positions, fully articulated, mutually familiar, unmoved for the better part of a century, each claiming the others have simply failed to answer the question asked.

I want a diagnosis of this stalemate rather than a verdict on which camp is right, and I want you to engage what each side actually says rather than to survey the literature. My own working hypothesis, which you should treat as a hypothesis and attack if it is wrong, is that the reference class problem is not one problem. Under the single label sit three questions with different success criteria: a semantic one about what, if anything, makes a singular probability claim true; a normative one about which class a rational agent is licensed or obliged to use given the evidence in hand; and a practical-institutional one about which comparison groups may permissibly be used when predictions are acted upon, as in clinical risk scoring or sentencing. On this view the rule-givers answer the second, are attacked for failing the first, and are dismissed by the third group for conflating both with the third — and the impression of a single unresolved debate is an artifact of that cross-examination. A related suspicion: the same machinery quietly governs disputes that never cite this literature, such as the argument over self-locating credence where the parties disagree about which centred possibilities an agent should count as her peers, which looks to me like a reference class dispute wearing different clothes.

Specifically: do the rule-givers and the insolubility camp genuinely contradict one another, or is one making a claim about truth-makers and the other a claim about the deliverability of a decision procedure? Is the standard circularity charge — that narrowness, homogeneity and specificity are each defined relative to a partition of properties, and so presuppose the very selection they are meant to justify — vicious, or benign, given that ordinary statistical practice seems to work perfectly well without a rule? Why has no one produced a case where a proposed rule determinately fails, as opposed to merely underdetermining an answer? Does relocating the problem in priors and conditionalisation dissolve it or rename it, and if it renames it, what does that show about whether the problem was ever about classes? Finally, construct for me a hard instance — a diagnostic case in which two of these rules yield conflicting numbers for the same singular case and each remains defensible on its own terms — and say what the case reveals about the structure of the disagreement.

Conclude with what would count as progress. A theorem, a convergence result showing that all defensible rules agree in the cases that matter, a reframing that replaces the question which class is correct with the question which choices the evidence and the decision context jointly exclude, or something else entirely. If your diagnosis implies that no progress should be expected because the camps are holding an account of probability to incompatible standards of success, say so plainly and defend it. No bibliography or reading list is wanted or useful; I want the argument.
