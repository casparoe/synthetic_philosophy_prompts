I teach a senior seminar that's half philosophy of social science and half moral psychology, and this term the running topic was "does religion make people better?" The final is a 90 minute in-class essay, one question, 25 marks. Nobody gets any sources in the room and I don't want them citing anything, so the question has to be completely self-contained: I've invented a study for them to reason about, and I want all the marks to come from reasoning about that study rather than from remembering the actual religion-and-prosociality literature.

Here's the study I made up. Please use it exactly as given — don't add findings or smooth out the awkward parts, the awkwardness is the point:

- National panel, 14,000 adults, four waves over twenty years, in an unnamed Western European country where monthly religious attendance fell from roughly two-thirds of adults to about a fifth over that period.
- Each wave measures: (A) monthly attendance; (B) self-reported volunteering hours and giving as a share of income; (C) a real one-shot anonymous money transfer to a stranger in another city, no repeat interaction, recipient knows nothing about the sender; (D) the same transfer but the recipient is a fellow member of the sender's congregation or community group and both sides know the pairing; (E) two neighbors rate the participant on whether they'd hand over their house keys; (F) the standard generalized-trust item about whether most people can be trusted.
- Results: attendance predicts B and E strongly and D moderately, but predicts C barely at all, and the tiny C effect vanishes when the anonymous recipient is described as having no community ties whatsoever. Participants who stopped attending between waves 2 and 3, matched to continuing attenders on demographics and on their wave-2 scores, show no drop in C, a modest drop in D and E, and a large drop in B. F rises across the whole country over the two decades even as attendance falls, with the rise concentrated in the under-35s and the highly educated. And E ratings of attenders by their own neighbors go up over time while E ratings of non-attenders by their neighbors go down.

What I want from you:

1. The exam question stem, 400 words at most, laying out the design and results compactly (a small table is fine) and then asking one question. It should be unanswerable by reciting the debate, and the crux should be: what would "more moral" have to mean for these results to be evidence about it at all, and what precisely do they license?

2. A model answer, around 750 words, written as a strong student would write it under time pressure — so it takes a position rather than listing considerations.

3. A marking scheme out of 25, broken into the specific moves a top answer has to make, with marks for each and one line on how students typically botch each one. Two constraints: measurement invariance across the two decades has to be one of the must-hit moves (do E and B even mean the same thing at wave 1 and wave 4, and what happens to the interpretation if not?), and the selection problem in the leaver comparison should be worth more than the social-desirability point, because literally everyone will spot the social desirability one and it shouldn't be where the marks are.

Then tell me which single move, if a student makes it, lifts them from a solid B to an A — something beyond the listed must-hits. And please flag anything in my invented design that isn't internally coherent; I put it together fast and I'd rather fix it before I seperate it into a handout and print it.
