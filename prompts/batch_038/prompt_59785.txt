Here is the draft, at roughly the length it will run:

**THE GLOBE IS TOO SMALL**

The man who did more than almost anyone else to make the computer age possible did not leave us a warning about machines that think. He left us a warning about a planet that has run out of room.

Almost every retrospective invocation of von Neumann on the future quotes him on speed — on acceleration, on the quickening tempo of invention. But speed is not really his variable. The mechanism he identifies is stranger and, I think, more useful. Most human time scales are anchored to reaction times, habits, and other physiological facts about us, and those do not move. What moves is the footprint of any single technological act: "the effect of the increased speed of technological processes was to enlarge the size of units — political, organizational, economic, and cultural — affected by technological operations." In his own gloss, "instead of performing the same operations as before in less time, now larger-scale operations were performed in the same time." Acceleration therefore shows up not as a shorter interval but as a bigger unit — a village problem becomes a regional problem becomes a planetary one — and enlargement, unlike speed, has a ceiling. "This important evolution has a natural limit, that of the earth's actual size. The limit is now being reached, or at least closely approached."

The institutional consequence follows directly. Our arrangements are territorial: jurisdictions, sovereigns, treaties, markets, all cut to the shape of the map. Technology, in von Neumann's account, quietly deletes the map. He writes that there is "an intrinsic conflict with geography — and institutions based thereon — as understood today." Intrinsic is the operative word. This is not a case of institutions failing to keep up because the people running them were slow or corrupt or underfunded; it is a case of institutions being built out of a material that technological change dissolves. And because the conflict is structural rather than moral, it cannot be settled by exhortation. Progress, on this view, cannot be slowed by agreement, since somebody always gains by going further and everyone else knows it: the ratchet turns in one direction only. Von Neumann was, in short, a fatalist about technology, and he did not apologize for it.

That fatalism is why the essay keeps getting read as a prophecy of automation and joblessness. He does discuss automation, and he does expect the disruption to reach well past the factory floor. But the deeper anticipation lies elsewhere, in work he left unfinished at his death and his collaborators completed afterward: the theory of self-reproducing automata. A machine that can build a copy of itself is the first machine whose output is not bounded by a factory, and that, rather than unemployment, is the hinge on which the essay's argument turns. Read the two bodies of work together and you have a theory of runaway capability assembled seven decades before anyone coined a term for it.

Which is why von Neumann now surfaces constantly in arguments about the governance of frontier computer systems. If the ratchet only turns one way, then any negotiated pause is waste paper: the defector captures the entire advantage, and the compliant party merely loses. On this reading von Neumann identified the central coordination problem of machine-intelligence policy before there was machine intelligence to coordinate about — and identified it, notably, from inside the government agency charged with managing the first technology that made the problem visible.

He offers no program in return, and he means not to. Asked what safeguard remains, he answers: "Apparently only day-to-day — or perhaps year-to-year — opportunistic measures, a long sequence of small, correct decisions." It is a bleak place to end, and von Neumann intended it bleakly: no architecture, no treaty, no authority, only the hope that the sequence of correct decisions turns out to be long enough.
