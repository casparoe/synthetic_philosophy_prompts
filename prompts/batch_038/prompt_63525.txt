I'm in my third year doing a self-designed major in law and religion, and in three weeks I'm presenting my thesis at our department's undergraduate research symposium — 20 minutes plus a 15-minute Q&A, with three assigned discussants. I've been putting the prep off but I think I need to do it now. My argument is basically that the separation-vs-establishment framing everyone in my classes uses is measuring the wrong variable. The thing that actually does the work, I claim, is what I've been calling *fiscal personhood*: whether a religious community gets to exist as a legal person that can levy dues on its own members and have the state's collection machinery collect them. Sort the countries by that and you get two clusters that cut right across the usual state-church map — Germany, Denmark, Sweden (which formally ended its state church on paper in 2000 but still has the tax office collecting the membership fee), and England sit in one; the US and France sit in the other, despite being miles apart on pretty much everything else. And here's the part I expect to get hammered on: I argue that American hostility to the second cluster isn't derived from neutrality at all. It's a residue — a secularized Free Church voluntarism, the picture of a church as a gathered congregation you join as an adult and support freely, which got frozen into constitutional principle and then handed back to us as though it were the neutral default. On that basis I claim the German-style arrangement can actually be defended as *more* neutral between comprehensive doctrines than strict separation, because separation quietly imposes one ecclesiology on communities whose belonging isn't opt-in-at-eighteen — infant baptism, Judaism, Islam, traditions with obligatory almsgiving.

I want the prep sheet to reflect that I'm defending a limited version, though, because I already concede a lot: I say the fiscal-personhood model only earns its neutrality claim if exit is genuinely cheap and doesn't cost you anything non-financial (which is why the German courts refusing to let someone leave the church as a legal corporation while remaining a believer in faith is such a bad fact for me), and if public-law-corporation status is available on the same terms to Muslim and other newer communities, which in practice it mostly isnt. I also concede up front that the US has a hidden version of the same thing — property-tax exemption plus deductible donations is a real fiscal subsidy to religion, it's just routed through the tax code instead of showing up as a line item, which is why nobody talks about it.
