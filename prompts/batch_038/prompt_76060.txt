I'm a type designer and every friday crit at my studio ends up in the same argument: what makes every 'a' an 'a'. Here's six accounts I've been collecting — (1) template: there's one abstract shape and any given a counts by resembling it closely enough; (2) family resemblance: no feature in common at all, just a chain of overlapping similarities; (3) invariants: an a is anything that preserves some stroke/topological structure under deformation; (4) community-normative: it's an a because literate readers are disposed to take it as one; (5) causal-historical: it's an a because it descends, by copying, from a lineage of a's; (6) functional: it's whatever mark does the work of disambiguating the words it sits in.

Sort them into tiers by how well they handle these cases, score each out of 10, one sentence of justification per score: the single-story a everyone learns to handwrite vs the double-story a in most printed fonts (one letter or two?); a ligature where the a has dissolved into the letter next to it; a graffiti tag whose a is legible only to people in that scene; a single-story a that in Greek would be an alpha; a font where the a has been swapped for something that just looks like a circle. Then say which one you'd actually bet on if you had to build a classifier that labels glyph images, and whether the ranking flips between "counts as an a for a human reader" and "counts as an a for the machine."

Lead with the bottom line — tiers and scores in the first sentence, then the reasoning, no preamble and no restating the question.
