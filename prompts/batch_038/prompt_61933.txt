I have just started as associate ombudsperson at a mid-sized European university with an unusually large visiting-fellow program, and I have inherited the office's case log from my predecessor, who built the office, staffed it alone for eleven years, and retired in the spring. Her rulings almost all concern the same recurring genre of dispute: a newcomer — a fellow, an exchange student, a spouse on a dependent visa, a short-term staff hire — who declined to follow some local convention, and a local who complained about it. The standing committee hears a new case of exactly that kind next Thursday and has asked me to prepare what it calls a consistency memo: not my own view of the merits, but a reconstruction of how she would have ruled, since her entries are treated as the office's case law and will be cited again later. I have read the log closely enough to know that her stated reasons do not collapse into any single principle I can name, and my training is in economics rather than philosophy, which is part of why I want a second pair of eyes. Please write in American English, and please do not give me references, a bibliography, or a reading list on social norms and conventional obligation — I want your reconstruction of her rule, not a survey of the literature.

Her entries, in my paraphrase, with the ruling and the rationale she gave:

1. A visiting lecturer boarded a city bus without joining the queue at the stop, at a moment when the bus was nearly empty and the driver waved him on; nobody was delayed. Breach upheld. Her note emphasized that the boarding was performed in front of roughly twenty people who had been standing in line, and that the wrong lay in what the act announced to them rather than in any cost it imposed.
2. A doctoral fellow crossed against the pedestrian signal at three in the morning on an empty street, entirely alone. No breach. Her note said that at that hour the signal is not coordinating anything, so there was no one whose settled expectations he defeated.
3. A visiting researcher left nothing beyond the printed twelve-percent service charge at a restaurant where the floor and kitchen staff visibly expected more and divide the additional amount among themselves. Breach upheld. Her note said he had eaten a meal priced on the assumption that the extra would materialize, and had taken the benefit of a practice sustained by other people's contributions without contributing to it.
4. An exchange student wore her shoes inside her host family's flat after the cohort's orientation session had announced that shoes come off at the door; the host said nothing at the time and raised it later with a third party. Breach upheld, and she explicitly rejected the argument that the host's silence waived the expectation: once the cohort had been told, the student's compliance was owed because others had been given something to plan around.
5. A fellow who finds the local cheek greeting uncomfortable offered her hand instead at a departmental reception and explained why. No breach. Her note said the greeting admits of several variants that are all perfectly intelligible to everyone present, and that where a convention has that property it does not oblige a newcomer to select the local variant.
6. A staff member refused to pay the small unofficial fee that everyone pays to have a residence document processed within the week, and instead waited the official six weeks at real cost to himself. No breach — and she added that he should not have paid. Her note said that a convention's being universal and convenient to its participants can count against joining it when what the convention coordinates is the extraction of money the system ought not to require, because every payment makes the arrangement harder to remove.

The new case is this. One department holds a weekly working lunch. The local practice, undocumented but unbroken for as long as anyone in the department remembers, is that the most junior person present arranges the food, serves it, and takes the minutes. In this department the most junior faculty member is also the only woman on the faculty, and the second-most-junior is a man. A visiting fellow, new this term and male, attended the first lunch of the term, watched the woman perform all three tasks, and was then asked by the chair — in a tone he took for an invitation, but which several locals afterward described as an instruction — whether he would take the next turn. He declined, in the room and in front of everyone, saying he would not take part in a practice that allocates the work by gender, and offered instead to take the minutes for the remainder of the term while leaving the catering alone. The chair assigned the turn to the woman. She later told the fellow, privately but within earshot of another visitor, that she would rather he had simply taken the turn, because when a visitor declines, the work reverts to her and she has no local standing to refuse it. She has now filed a complaint with this office. The fellow's supporters have filed a counter-statement arguing that he was protesting an unjust arrangement rather than shirking a shared one. One complication I should flag: the fellows' orientation packet describes the lunch as involving a rotating administrative duty shared by the department, which the fellow says he read as describing a duty of faculty members only.

What I need from you, in this order. First, lay out the candidate rules that are jointly consistent with all six entries — as many as you can find, stated so that I could apply each one mechanically — and say explicitly if any candidate rule you considered is actually ruled out by one of the entries, and by which. Second, for each surviving rule, give its verdict on the new case, including on the narrower question of whether the complaint should be upheld against the fellow personally, given that the person most burdened by his refusal is the complainant herself. I also want to know whether her framework can separate the refusal from the manner of it — public, addressed to the chair rather than to her, and offered together with a partial substitute — since entry 1 suggests she thinks what an act announces can matter independently of what it costs, and I cannot tell whether that cuts for or against him here. Third, give me your best single guess at how she would have ruled, and defend the preference: was she running a weighted trade-off among these considerations, a lexical ordering, or a disjunction with a tiebreaker, and what in the entries makes you say so? If you think the log genuinely underdetermines the case, say that plainly and tell me which of the two or three possible rulings the committee should adopt to keep the line of cases most coherent. Fourth, propose one further hypothetical case — described in enough detail that I could pose it to the committee as a thought experiment — that would most cleanly discriminate between the two rules you take most seriously, and say what each of those rules predicts about it. I am not asking what you think the fellow should have done as a matter of first-order morality; I am asking what her case law commits us to.
