So I got into a two-hour argument with my friend Dana last night and I still can't let it go, and honestly your the tiebreaker at this point. I freelance designing puzzle content for a kids' app — the format is those "this is to that as this is to what" picture puzzles. Our whole little world is a set of cartoon critters, and each critter has five features that vary: how many eyes it has (one, two, or three), its body shape (round, tall, or squat), whether it has horns, its skin pattern (plain, striped, or dotted), and which way it's facing. Every puzzle shows three critters plus four answer options, and the player picks the one that stands to the third critter the way the second stands to the first.

The easy ones are easy: critter 1 is round and plain with two eyes facing right, critter 2 is identical except it has three eyes, critter 3 is a tall striped two-eyed thing facing left — everybody picks "tall, striped, three eyes, facing left," no debate, the eye count is obviously the only thing that moved. But the good puzzles are the ones where two or three features change between critter 1 and critter 2, and then some *different* feature is what's varying in critter 3. Example: critter 1 is round/plain/two eyes/facing right, critter 2 is tall/striped/three eyes/facing right, critter 3 is round/dotted/two eyes/facing left. Our playtesters split three ways on that one — some carry over all the changes and say tall/striped/three eyes/facing left, some say only the eye change is "the rule" and the shape and pattern were just decoration so it's round/dotted/three eyes/facing left, and some get hung up on the fact that critter 3 faces the opposite way from critter 1 and decide facing is part of the rule, so they flip it back to right. And here's what actually drove the argument: the *same* feature is filler in one puzzle and the entire point in the next. Facing does nothing in ninety puzzles and then in one puzzle it's the whole answer. I cant write down ahead of time which features matter.

I tried, though. I built a little script that ranks the features (eyes > body > pattern > horns > facing) and generates puzzles plus an official answer key straight off that ranking. Technically airtight, and the puzzles were garbage — playtesters called them "random," and worse, the script marked the intended answer wrong on a bunch of the good puzzles I'd made by hand, because in those the ranking isn't what's going on. Dana's take is that I'm chasing a ghost: there's no fact about which feature "really" matters, an answer key is just a decision somebody makes, so either publish both defensible answers or run every puzzle past twenty playtesters and let the majority vote be the truth. My gut says she's wrong — that when a puzzle is good there's a single answer that clicks for almost everybody, and that what clicks is set by the whole configuration rather than by any list I could write in advance, like the solver slides between readings until one of them makes the most of the card hang together. But I can't defend that beyond "trust me, I can feel it," which I admit sounds like mysticism. So: is there anything more to what I'm feeling than my own taste, is Dana right that it all collapses into a popularity contest, and practically what should I do about the answer key — because I've got forty puzzles due Friday and a boss who wants a definite letter next to each one.
