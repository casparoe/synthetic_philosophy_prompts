So, context: I've been reading rationalist stuff since roughly 2010 — Overcoming Bias, then LW, then the SSC years — and I've absorbed far more framings than is strictly healthy, none of which prepared me for the thing I actually have to vote on in April. Three years ago I let myself get talked onto the board of the historical trust that holds my college's sesquicentennial bequest, and its been nothing but procedural drudgery until now, because an alumna who died in 2019 left us $2.6M restricted by her will to "the study and commemoration of the founding of the College." The college turns 150 in 2027 and there are three funded proposals sitting in front of us, and they add up to $2.8M, which is why we can't just bless all of them, though two of my colleagues have proposed exactly that. Proposal A ($900k) is a permanent exhibit series plus an annual lecture and a short film on the founders and the famous graduates: the argument is that current students need exemplars to measure themselves against, and the development office swears it will be leveraged into more gifts. Proposal B ($1.4M) is preservation: climate-controlled storage, digitization of 150 years of ledgers and student papers, oral histories of retired staff and old townspeople, and restoring the 1881 chapel doors — not the famous people, the whole texture of the place. Our conservator says the newsprint from the 1920s through the 50s is going brittle and is maybe fifteen years from being unusable, so B has an irreversible-now-or-never shape to it, which I find emotionally compelling and simultaneously suspicious, since every budget fight I've ever been in has an irreversible-now-or-never item. Proposal C ($500k) is a documented public reckoning: a retired local historian who's 81 and has been sitting on the material for decades wants to publish what the college's own founding narrative has skipped over — the land came out of a free Black farming settlement that was cleared away through tax liens and a suspiciously convenient county road rerouting in 1876, and the seed endowment came from a partner who later sued the college over fraud. The alumni association has already told us privately that one major donor will pull a $1M pledge if we spend the anniversary trashing the founders.

Two wrinkles that the memo has to deal with rather than paper over. First, college counsel has flagged that C might not qualify under the will's language at all — you can arguably "study" a thing without "commemorating" it — so there's a real question whether C is eligible for bequest money or has to be pushed onto an operating budget that is, charitably, not robust. Second, anything we don't allocate by the April deadline reverts to unrestricted endowment and gets quietly absorbed into general spending, i.e. effectively the athletics facility fund, so "commission another study and revisit next year" is not a free option and I'd like the memo to say that out loud.

Here's what I need from you: draft the ~2-page written opinion I'll circulate to the board the week before the meeting. It should come to a concrete recommendation with actual dollar figures across A, B, and C — including zero for one of them if that's the honest answer — plus conditions and a timeline the trust can hold itself to, and it should engage each proposal's strongest case in terms its own proponents would recognize rather than in terms they'd have to translate. Also please handle the losing side seriously; I know which way I'm leaning and I don't want a memo that just hands me back my lean with better vocabulary. One more thing: my partner did a philosophy degree and she says all three proposals are just the three ways of using the past that Nietzsche writes about in that essay on history, that each one has a characteristic failure mode, and that our meetings keep going in circles because nobody's admitted which of the three they're even trying to do.
