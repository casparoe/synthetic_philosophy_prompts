I'm helping run a two-week workshop for a mixed group — roughly half economists who work on mechanism design, half political philosophers — whose stated job is to produce a short position paper on whether there is a defensible epistemic case for majority rule: one strong enough to be used in actual institutional-design arguments rather than merely invoked at conferences. The concrete design questions on the table are the familiar ones: how large a citizen assembly should be, whether juries should decide by unanimity, by supermajority, or by simple majority, and whether structured deliberation should be permitted before the vote is taken. My own contribution is the framing section, and what I want to write is not a defense of any particular account but a specification — the list of requirements that a formal account of the epistemic value of majority rule has to meet before designers should take it seriously. Please write that list for me.

For each requirement, give the consideration or the case that motivates it, and mark whether you regard it as genuinely non-negotiable or as a prejudice of one camp. The candidates I keep hearing bandied about include: that an account not simply assume the thing that does all the work, since everyone grants that if each voter is right more than half the time the majority converges on the truth, so what actually has to be explained is where above-half competence comes from, how it could ever be estimated from the outside without begging the question against the voters themselves, and competence with respect to which set of questions — a body that is competent on local planning matters may be quite incompetent on macroprudential policy; that it say what is to replace Condorcet's independence assumption, given that shared evidence sources, common media environments, and common priors make votes correlated, and correlated error is precisely what defeats aggregation; that it be internally coherent about its own agents, since if voters share a prior and update on private signals, casting a vote for one's sincere posterior is not generally an equilibrium strategy, so an account that purports to describe real strategic agents cannot have it both ways; that it make claims at the group sizes institutions actually use rather than only in the limit, including the case where admitting more members lowers average competence; that it take an explicit stand on deliberation, because the very feature that pools dispersed information can also generate cascades and destroy whatever independence the account needed; that it say what happens when a group must decide a set of logically connected propositions rather than a single one, since majority voting on such a set can yield collectively inconsistent outcomes, and an account that ignores this is at best an account of single-issue votes; and that it state its domain — which questions have answers of the right sort for correctness to be meaningful at all, and what it falls back on for questions of value.

Two things matter to me more than the completeness of the list. First, identify any subset of these requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied, and say which one has to give: my suspicion is that coherence about strategic agents, the demand that deliberation be allowed, and the demand for independence pull against one another, so that an account keeping all three is describing a body that could not exist — but I want the argument rather than my intuition. Second, tell me which single requirement does the most work in excluding otherwise attractive accounts, since that is where I want the framing section to place its weight. Only once that is settled should you score archetypes against the list, in a sentence or two each: a classical Condorcet-style defense resting on independent and roughly uniform competence; a game-theoretic account on which the vote is a device for eliciting dispersed private information; a judgment-aggregation account that gives up tracking an antecedent truth in favour of keeping collective outcomes consistent; and, if you like, a pragmatist or experimentalist account that treats accuracy as something discovered through repeated low-stakes trials rather than assumed at the outset. No references and no reading list, please — I need the specification itself and your view of where it bites hardest. One last thing worth flagging: my economist colleagues and my philosopher colleagues will disagree about which items on this list are assumptions and which are conclusions, so it would help a great deal if you noted anywhere you think the line between the two is being drawn question-beggingly.
