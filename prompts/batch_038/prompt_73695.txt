You are a philosopher of probability who has spent real time inside a technical regulator, equally at home with the literature on Lewis's Principal Principle and with the practice of quantitative risk assessment. Please think carefully before writing; I would rather have a slower and more exact answer than a fluent one.

**Context.** I am in the third year of a PhD in philosophy of science and two days a week I sit inside the probabilistic safety assessment unit of a national regulator. We review safety cases submitted by operators of a small number of high-consequence facilities: research reactors, one reprocessing line, one geological disposal test facility. Every safety case contains a validated stochastic model — fault trees, Markov chains, event trees — which outputs an annual frequency for a specified target event. Our unit's technical standard contains a clause to the effect that where such a model has passed validation, its output is to be treated as the unit's estimate of the objective chance of the target event, and the reviewer's degree of belief is to be set equal to it. Nobody drafted that clause with any philosophy in mind. I have been asked either to make it defensible or to say what should replace it.

The decisions are frequent and the stakes are real. Around twenty-five reviews a year reach my desk; each concerns a number that feeds directly into a licensing condition, and a wrong number in the complacent direction is the failure mode the organisation fears most. I have roughly half an hour of analysis time per case, I am not the signatory (a three-person panel signs off), and the engineers who will actually run the procedure have no training in philosophy and no patience for it.

**What I want.** Not an essay on the Principal Principle and not a survey of its problems. I want a decision procedure: an ordered sequence of questions a reviewer asks about a safety case, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the sequence terminates in *defer — set your credence equal to the model output*, where it terminates in *do not defer*, and where it hands the matter up to the panel because the question is not a factual one about the world but a question about which modelling choice we are prepared to hold operators to. It must produce a short written record the panel can audit.

Please structure the answer as follows.

1. **Diagnostic opening.** Begin with a brainstorming section, in your own words and without naming anyone, of the distinct ways the naive rule — set your credence equal to whatever the validated model outputs — can go wrong. I expect at least six, and they should be genuinely different failure modes rather than restatements of one. Derive the procedure from that list, and say explicitly which items on it the procedure can actually repair and which it can only flag.

2. **The procedure proper**, as numbered steps.

3. **The hard cases it must handle explicitly:**
   - (a) Reviewer information that bears on *whether the event will occur* rather than on *whether the model's number is the chance*. This distinction is the whole thing, and I suspect the clause is either empty or simply wrong once it is drawn. Take at least four realistic kinds of reviewer remark — a maintenance log showing early degradation in an identical component at a sister site, a whistleblower's claim about a bypassed interlock during last month's outage, a published meta-analysis suggesting the model family systematically underestimates common-cause failures, and a site visit in which the reviewer noticed poor housekeeping — and say which side of the line each falls on and why.
   - (b) A model whose own output implies that the chances are not what the model says: the case where the model assigns non-trivial probability to a future regime in which its assumptions no longer hold.
   - (c) Two validated models producing different numbers for what the operator describes as the same target event, because they carve the event differently — different reference classes, different horizons, one counting a partial loss of function and the other only a complete one. Which governs, and on what grounds? Say plainly if this is a question the underlying principle cannot answer.
   - (d) An output that is in substance a frequency estimate from a finite sample of test data rather than a chance at all. What, if anything, constrains the reviewer's credence there, and does the procedure need a separate branch for it?
   - (e) The triviality worry: if "the chance" means nothing more than "whatever the validated model says", does the instruction to match one's credence to it have any content? Give me your honest view of whether the clause is doing work on its most charitable reading, and if it is not, what should replace it.

4. **Three worked applications.**
   - A research reactor whose validated model gives 2 × 10⁻⁵ per annum for the target end state, where the reviewer holds an internal maintenance log showing early seal degradation in an identical pump batch installed at three sister sites — information the model does not encode.
   - A reprocessing line whose own model output assigns probability 0.3 to a configuration change within ten years that would invalidate the model's assumptions, with the target frequency stated conditional on no such change.
   - A case where the procedure's verdict is one I will not like. Choose it deliberately and say which of the two you have given me: either it forces deference to a number a reasonable panel would find complacent, or it refuses deference at exactly the point where the standard was designed to compel it. Explain why the honest reading of the theory produces that result rather than a comfortable one.

5. **Limitations and costs.** Mark, step by step, which stages are judgement calls wearing the costume of procedure; list what the procedure cannot do at all; and identify which technical distinctions you have deliberately collapsed for usability, together with what that collapsing costs in accuracy.

6. **A one-page run sheet** at the end, suitable for keeping beside a reviewer during a case.

Write in British English throughout. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or inline citations; where you want to appeal to a particular philosophical view, describe the view rather than attributing it, and do not name a philosopher or a result unless you are confident of the attribution.
