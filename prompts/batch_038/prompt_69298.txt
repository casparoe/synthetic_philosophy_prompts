I'm TA-ing a second-year seminar on probability puzzles and I've been handed the week on Bertrand's chord problem, which I dread, because every handout I can find ends the same way: derive 1/3 for random endpoints, 1/2 for a random point on a random radius, 1/4 for a uniform midpoint in the disc, then shrug and say the principle of indifference was vague all along, ha ha. That's not a result, it's a vibe, and I don't want to teach a vibe. So before I hand the students any rescue, I want a spec: what would an indifference-style rule actually have to deliver to count as *solving* the problem rather than dodging it? Write the desiderata list. For each entry give me the requirement itself, the case or consideration that motivates it — point at which of the three generation methods, or which variant of the setup, makes it bite — and a label saying whether it's non-negotiable or just a preference of one camp. I especially want that last part argued rather than asserted: is invariance under relabeling or under rescaling genuinely demanded by the problem, or is it just whatever assumption happens to make the answer come out at 1/2? And flag hard which subsets of your list are jointly unsatisfiable, because my hunch is that "the credence is fixed by the physical situation and not by how we describe it" plus "the rule must output a determinate number" plus "the rule must apply to every well-formed question we can ask about that situation" cannot all be true at once, and if that's right the list is itself the main result of the week. I'd rather you shoot the hunch down than flatter it.

Three seeded things to run the list against, since I want it tested and not just recited. (1) A student in last year's group proposed what he swore dissolved the whole mess: flip a fair coin to decide which of the three methods to use, and then the probability is just the average, 13/36. Tell me whether that move satisfies or violates each relevant desideratum, and separately whether "be indifferent between descriptions" is even a coherent requirement when there isn't a finite menu of descriptions to be indifferent between. (2) A friend who's read more of the literature than I have claims you can re-run the invariance argument to recover the other two answers as well, depending on which transformations you decide to privilege — if that's right, say what it does to invariance's status as a desideratum. (3) I've been toying with two extra requirements of my own and I suspect at least one is junk: first, that whatever rule you give shouldn't be special to circles but should extend to random chords of an arbitrary convex body; second, that the rule must agree with the long-run frequencies of a physical device that actually implements the procedure, when there is one. Tell me for each whether it's a genuine desideratum, a tie-breaker, or a red herring I should strike before class — and if it's a red herring, say what work people think it's doing.

Then, briefly, a few sentences each, score the main contenders against the list: the invariance/max-ignorance camp that lands on 1/2, the deflationist who says the question was never well-posed so there's no fact of the matter, and a subjectivist who thinks the whole thing just shows credences are indexed to the agent's chosen description. Name the single requirement that knocks out the most candidates. Lead with your bottom line on joint satisfiability in two or three sentences before the list, a table is fine for the desiderata themselves, and hard cap the whole thing at 800 words — I'm putting it on a handout.
