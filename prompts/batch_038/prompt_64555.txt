I am setting the final examination for an MA seminar titled Memory, Testimony, and Political Ethics, and I need one essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme. The paper is three hours long and closed book; candidates are told they need not cite, quote, or name sources, only argue. The question is worth 25 marks and should be answerable in roughly 1,500 words.

The question must be built on the following case, which I have invented and which you should reproduce on the paper in a tightened form of no more than 200 words, flagged as fictional so that no candidate thinks it tracks a real country's statute:

For forty-one years the interior ministry of the Republic of Karst maintained a domestic surveillance programme. Its surviving files, known as the Registry, run to some 1.8 million entries: reports on subjects, lists of informants, and written denunciations. Audits have established that informants were sometimes compelled, that regional offices worked to numerical quotas, and that in one district fabrications account for a majority of entries. A transitional bill proposes the following regime: public access restricted to a five-member commission, empowered to release names only where independent corroboration exists; sealing of the remainder for forty years; automatic destruction at the end of that period; and a right, for any individual named in the Registry as either subject or informant, to petition for the destruction of the entries concerning them. The stated grounds are threefold. First, unreliability: the Registry does not establish what it purports to establish. Second, harm: in two towns granted early full access, longitudinal studies found no measurable rise in interpersonal trust and a marked increase in private retaliation, ostracism, and contested inheritances. Third, civic effect: participation in public life among the youngest cohort runs inversely to exposure to Registry content. Opposing the bill, an association representing the families of roughly six thousand people who disappeared argues that for many of the missing the Registry is the only surviving trace, and that allowing the successor state to decide what is destroyed simply continues the old regime's control of the record. Two further facts should feature, because they are where the case bites: about two fifths of the petitions lodged so far come from informants rather than subjects; and the only Registry entries concerning the disappeared are arrest-and-transfer notations produced by the same district offices whose fabrications generated the unreliability finding.

The point of the question is not to elicit a verdict on the bill. It is to see whether candidates can hold apart three things they will otherwise run together: whether a duty to remember is owed to the remembered or to the remembering community, and what follows from each answer about who may waive it; whether archival custody without uptake by any audience counts as remembering at all; and whether a document that misdescribes an individual's conduct can still be a reliable record of the institution that produced it. Please stipulate, in the question itself, two terms candidates are to use: "custodial remembering," meaning preservation of a record by an institution on behalf of a community that need never read it, and "communicative remembering," meaning preservation that secures actual uptake by some community, and "standing to forget," meaning the authority, if anyone has it, to release another from a duty to remember. The A/B boundary should turn on whether candidates can explain why the petitioning victims' and informants' own wishes do not by themselves settle the permissibility of destruction, and on whether they can state what would have to be true for a sunset clause to be a mode of remembering rather than of forgetting.

You may assume candidates have read Margalit's The Ethics of Memory (2002), including his distinction between ethics as the domain of thick relations and morality as the domain of thin relations, and his claim that radical evil, in attacking a shared humanity, grounds an obligation to remember its victims; and Rieff's The Triumph of the Therapeutic (1966), including his account of how moral and religious demands come to be recast as questions about individual psychic well-being. Do not require any further reading, and do not put words in either author's mouth: the question should ask candidates to construct the strongest available case for forgetting out of Rieff's premises about therapeutic culture, rather than to report conclusions he is supposed to have drawn about memory, since he is not a straightforward partisan of forgetting.

Please supply three things. First, the examination question exactly as it will appear, including the case summary, the stipulated definitions, and an explicit instruction that candidates must take a position and defend it rather than survey both sides. Second, a model answer of about 750 words that does precisely that: argues a defensible line on the bill's central provisions, and shows its work on the three distinctions above, including the awkward fact that the notations concerning the disappeared come from the least reliable offices. Third, a marking scheme totalling 25 marks, allocated across five or six must-hit points, each given with what the point is, what an A-range answer says about it, what a B-range answer says about it that keeps it out of the A range, and the marks available. Close with a list of specific errors that should cost marks — for instance, treating forgiveness and forgetting as the same operation, assuming the duty attaches to survivors' feelings rather than to anything owed to the dead, invoking collective memory without saying who the bearer of the duty is, or reasoning from the unreliability of denunciations to the unreliability of every entry in the Registry — each with the deduction and one line on why it evidences a misunderstanding rather than a stylistic lapse.
