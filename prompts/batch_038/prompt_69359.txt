I've been using a piece of decision theory as a general-purpose norm, and I want to check it against my own case-by-case verdicts to find out which of the two has to give.

The norm is Good's theorem in the form it's usually taught in decision analysis: the value of free information can never be negative, because an agent who learns something can always ignore it and choose as if uninformed. My working gloss has been stronger than the theorem, and probably sloppier: refusing to look is never the rational option — it may be understandable, or forgivable, but it is never what the calculation recommends.

Here are the cases and what I actually judge.

1. Untreatable. A free, perfectly reliable test for a late-onset degenerative condition with no treatment, no preventive measures, and nothing that turns on when the diagnosis is made; my prior is roughly even. I would decline the test, and I would pay to have an already-drawn sample destroyed rather than sequenced. I do not regard this as a failure of nerve.

2. Treatable. Everything the same, except that early detection permits treatment that substantially changes the course of the disease. I would take it, and pay for the privilege.

3. The race. I bet on a horse an hour ago. The result is settled, my payoff is unaffected by anything I now do, and someone offers to tell me for free. I would rather not know until the money arrives.

4. The shipment. A package has either shipped or it hasn't, nothing I can do about either, and I check the tracking page several times a day and would pay for a notification.

3 and 4 are the pair I cannot reconcile. Both are free, both concern matters wholly outside my control, neither changes any act available to me, and in both the only thing at stake is how it feels to know. Yet in one I want the surprise and in the other I want the news, and I resist the thought that the difference is nothing but temperament. If that is all it is, I'd rather be told plainly than have it absorbed into a clause.

5. The leak. I am negotiating to buy a property, and an acquaintance at the seller's firm will tell me the seller's true reserve price for free. The seller's side, however, will learn that a leak occurred and that I am the likely recipient. I think knowing actively costs me money here.

6. The inadmissible fact. I am on a jury. Before trial someone offers to tell me something about the defendant's past that I know is legally inadmissible, that I judge irrelevant to the charge, and that I know will nonetheless move my estimate of guilt. I would refuse — not because the fact is distressing, but because I do not trust my own updating.

7. The cheesecake. I have undertaken a month without sugar. A flatmate offers to tell me free of charge whether there is cheesecake in the fridge. I am confident that if I learn there is, I will eat it, and that my later self will count that as a failure. I would pay not to be told.

8. The predictor. A very accurate predictor of my choices decides in advance whether to deposit ten thousand dollars in an account I control, and deposits it only if it predicted I would decline to be told the account's contents. I am then offered a free and reliable report of whether the deposit was made. I take declining to be the rational choice, and I do not think the case is exotic — I think it isolates something. I also suspect 5 and 8 are the same case with the social machinery stripped away, and I want to know whether that is right.

9. The register. A free screening whose positive result would be entered in a shared medical register and would make me uninsurable for a policy I currently intend to buy. I would refuse. But I notice this looks like case 1 with an extra step, and the explanation I want to give here — the information removes options — is not the explanation I gave there, which was simply that I do not want to know. I want to be told whether case 1 collapses into case 9 or whether something survives the collapse.

What I would like from you:

- State the theorem as precisely as you can, enumerating its assumptions rather than its slogan: what it requires of the act set, of how the agent updates, of whether the outcomes themselves may depend on the learning, and of who is evaluating and at what time. Flag where the "you can always ignore it" step is doing real work, since I suspect that step is quietly assuming something about me that several of my cases deny.
- Test my gloss. If there is a principle that delivers all nine verdicts, state it clause by clause and say which case each clause exists to handle. If it can be made to work only by accumulating enough clauses that it stops being a principle and becomes a list of my prejudices, say so and give me the simpler principle instead, with the casualties identified.
- Distinguish verdicts that merely need a new clause from verdicts that contradict each other and force a choice. I expect the pressure to be at 3/4 and at 1/9, but I may be wrong about where it lies, and I'd like the diagnosis rather than my own guess back.
- Tell me which of my verdicts you would overturn, and what makes the surviving cases something other than my preferences laundered through a principle.
- Apply whatever principle you end up defending to three cases I have not listed: declining to learn the sex of one's own child before birth; checking the market value of a long-run portfolio daily when the only decision is a fixed allocation; and accepting a free, reliable evaluation of one's own teaching from people whose opinion of you it will change.
- Finally, cases 6 and 7 both turn on a disagreement between my earlier self and my later self, and the theorem appears to side with whichever of them happens to be doing the arithmetic. Tell me whether "the value of information" is even a well-defined quantity across that kind of change, or whether the question I opened with is malformed in a way that would have saved me the trouble.

I am not after the literature on any of this. I want the principle, its clauses, its casualties, and its predictions.
