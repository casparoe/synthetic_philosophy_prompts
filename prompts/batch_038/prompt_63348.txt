You are a poet who has also taught value theory, and I would like you to think the formal problem through carefully before drafting. I am closing a seminar on value theory next week, and the last session is on hard choices. The account we have been reading (Ruth Chang's) holds that in the genuinely hard cases the options are not better, not worse, and not equally good either: they are on a par, and when reasons run out the will steps in and commits, thereby creating a reason rather than discovering one. I want a poem that makes this structural instead of declarative.

Write seventeen lines: four quatrains and then a single line standing alone. Give the two options two separate rhyme families. The quatrains about staying — my speaker's father has had a stroke, and there is no one else within a thousand miles — draw on one set of end-sounds; the quatrains about going — a position abroad that would define the work — draw on another, and no sound may appear in both families, not even as a slant rhyme, so that the two scales never touch. The final line rhymes with neither: it is the commitment, arriving where no comparison could have produced it. Keep the line lengths roughly uniform, so the two families read as parallel systems rather than as a broken form.

Do not let any line rank the options or announce that they are equally good, and do not let the poem resolve into tragedy or into a shrug. Keep the vocabulary concrete: no "incommensurable," no "parity," no "utility." A reader who knows the debate should be able to identify the fourth relation from the form alone; a reader who does not should simply find it a good poem about a difficult decision.

Settle your two sound families and your line count first, but show me only the result: begin with the first line of the poem and end with the last — no title, no preface, no commentary, no postscript.
