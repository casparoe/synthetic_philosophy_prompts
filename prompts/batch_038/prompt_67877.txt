Consider the following proposition, which I will call P.

Epiphenomenalism about qualia holds that phenomenal states are caused by neural states but are themselves causally inert: they produce no further neural events, no reports, and no behavior. The reliability objection runs as follows. If qualia are causally inert, then every neural event underlying my reports and beliefs about my own experience is produced wholly without causal contribution from the experience reported on; yet those reports are systematically and exquisitely accurate about that experience. Accuracy of this kind, when its subject matter played no causal role in producing it, looks like a coincidence that stands in need of explanation. Epiphenomenalism can supply none, whereas interactionist dualism and reductive physicalism can each explain the accuracy by ordinary causal tracking of the facts reported. By "substantially lowers" I mean a likelihood-ratio shift of at least an order of magnitude: roughly, that the accuracy of our phenomenal judgments is at least ten times more probable on the leading rival theories than on epiphenomenalism, holding the rest of our evidence fixed. So understood, the objection is not a mere restatement of the causal inertness that epiphenomenalism already concedes, and P is the claim that it genuinely does the probabilistic damage described.

I want your credence in P, as a single number between 0 and 1, with the derivation exposed. Please decompose it into at least the following terms, assign each a probability with one or two sentences of justification, and then show explicitly how they combine:

(i) Whether the accuracy of phenomenal judgment requires a *causal* explanation at all — that is, whether a causal or reliabilist account of knowledge and of reference-fixing is the right account for phenomenal concepts, or whether acquaintance-based concepts can be accurate without any causal coupling to their objects.

(ii) Whether the common-cause reply succeeds: if psychophysical laws nomologically link a neural state N both to the quale Q and to the judgment J, then J's accuracy is arguably no coincidence but a consequence of the same lawful structure, so that nothing further is owed.

(iii) Conditional on (ii) succeeding, whether the burden merely relocates — to the question of why the best available set of psychophysical laws is one on which the judgments come out *true* rather than systematically false, inverted, or wildly coarse-grained — and whether that further question is one epiphenomenalism can answer or must swallow as brute.

(iv) Whether the objection over-generates. If it has force against epiphenomenalism, does it not equally threaten platonism about mathematics, on which abstract objects cause nothing yet our arithmetical beliefs are systematically true, and non-naturalist moral realism, on which moral facts likewise appear explanatorily idle? Tell me which way you read the pressure here: does our reluctance to abandon those views on this basis count against the objection, or does the fact that we already feel its force in those cases count for it?

(v) How much discriminating power the objection actually possesses. Interactionist dualism faces the interaction problem; reductive physicalism faces the explanatory gap and the knowledge argument. P's force depends on the rivals being better placed specifically on the dimension of judgmental accuracy. Assign a probability to the claim that their advantage on this dimension is not cancelled by their disadvantages elsewhere — or, if you think that comparison lies outside the scope of P as stated, say so and explain what P's scope then is.

Having derived your number, tell me which single term your final credence is most sensitive to: which term, if moved from your estimate to its extreme, would shift the overall probability the furthest? Then describe the particular argument or piece of evidence that would move *that* term most, and in which direction.

Finally, locate a disagreement. A property-dualist colleague of mine puts her credence in P at 0.80 and treats the reliability objection as the decisive reason to reject epiphenomenalism in favour of a causally efficacious dualism; a friend sympathetic to epiphenomenalism puts his at 0.15 and regards the objection as a pseudo-problem generated by an illegitimate demand. Given your decomposition, identify the one term on which these two most likely diverge, and state what each would have to concede for the gap to close.

Please do not survey the literature, name partisans, or offer references; I want your own number and your own reasoning. Number your terms in order, show the combination as a formula or an arithmetic sketch, and end your response with a single line of exactly the form

\answer{p, t}

where p is your final credence in P to two decimal places and t is the label of the term, chosen from i through v, to which that number is most sensitive.
