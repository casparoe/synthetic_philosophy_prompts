I cover science and ideas for a magazine and I've spent the last year doing stories about state apologies — the anniversary ones, the ones that only happened because an archive opened, the ones that never came. Now I'm trying to work out what I actually think by going case by case, and I want to know whether there's a principle under it or whether I'm just muddled.

My verdicts:
- A state that still exists, same army, same civil service, apologizing for a massacre it carried out 150 years ago with nobody alive who was there: owed.
- That same state apologizing for a wrong committed by a regime it overthrew and officially repudiated: also owed, because it kept the archives and the treaties.
- A state that dissolved into two successors, the wrong predating the split: only one of them owes the apology, the one that kept the capital and the UN seat. I can't really defend this.
- Apology with no money attached: hollow, but still worth having.
- Apology delivered three weeks before a trade negotiation: worse then no apology, even with identical wording.
- Apology delivered thirty years late because the evidence only just surfaced: fully fine, no discount.

That last pair is the one bugging me — both are apologies given for reasons that had nothing to do with remorse at the time. Two more I have no verdict on at all: does a state that annexed another inherit the annexed state's unapologized-for wrongs, and does an apology travel when the perpetrator was a chartered trading company whose assets the state later absorbed?

Give me exactly five numbered candidate principles, a sentence or two each: what the principle says, which of my verdicts it explains, and which one it forces me to give up — and where it settles one of my two open cases, say so in the same breath. No introduction, no conclusion.
