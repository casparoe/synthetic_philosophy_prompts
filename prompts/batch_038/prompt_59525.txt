I live in a valley town of about nine thousand people, and every second Saturday of October, right next to the church pancake breakfast schedule, the local paper prints the coming winter's forecast from a group that calls itself the Ridge Weather Circle. The method has been in continuous use since 1978. It was worked out by two orchardists, and it reduces the winter to a single call — mild, normal, or severe — from one number they compute themselves, the September index, which is built out of three measurements: the calendar date of the first hard frost of the previous autumn, the count of September days on which the wind at the ridge-top anemometer comes out of one particular quadrant, and the river-gauge height on the equinox. My father was one of the two men who started it, and since he died I've been the person who types up the forecast and hands it to the paper, so I sit in on the Circle's monthly meetings even though I have no scientific training at all. A couple of years ago I read a library book about how scientists actually argue with each other, and I got hooked on Imre Lakatos's picture of research programmes: a hard core that is never directly put at risk, a protective belt of auxiliary hypotheses that takes the hits, and a distinction between a programme that is progressive because it keeps predicting genuinely new facts and one that is degenerating because it only ever grows new auxiliaries to soak up its failures. I've been trying to figure out which side the Circle falls on. At the September meeting two members got into a real argument about exactly that question, and I wrote down as much of it as I could afterward. Neither of them has read Lakatos; they were arguing in their own words, and neither knew I was taking notes.

Amara has been with the Circle for about a decade and Dennis is roughly my father's age. (1) Amara opened by saying the index has missed badly in four of the last nine winters, including the winter of 2019, and that in every single case the Circle produced a new refinement within the year: the county's 1994 rebuild of the weir upstream permanently lowered the gauge, so the equinox reading has to be corrected; the airport switched to an automated station in 2006, so certain wind days are double-counted and must be thrown out; in El Niño winters the index "goes quiet" and shouldn't be read at all; and the 2019 miss happened because the frost arrived before the leaf drop, which supposedly confuses the index. She said there are now eleven separate corrections and carve-outs in the method, which across a forty-six-year record is roughly one escape hatch for every fourth winter, and that this isn't a theory being sharpened but a theory being insulated. (2) Dennis said she was treating all the auxiliaries as the same kind of thing when they plainly aren't, and that some were risked in advance and paid off. In the spring of 1994, when the Circle saw the county crews surveying the weir, they announced that autumn — before anyone had taken the season's readings — that the equinox gauge height would come in about a foot and a half below the old record and stay down permanently; the county's own gauge audit three years later found a step discontinuity of almost exactly that size, and the county hydrologist now uses the Circle's correction factor as a matter of routine. He said the El Niño carve-out was the same sort of thing: it went in in 1981, a year before that El Niño winter made the national news, and it didn't just excuse the index in those years, it made a positive claim, namely that the index flips sign, so a high index in an El Niño year predicts a milder winter instead of a severer one. That flip call has come out right three times out of four since. He said a belt that predicts and then gets rewarded for it isn't insulation. (3) Amara said three out of four is the difficulty, not the answer. The Circle makes a three-way call, so pure guessing gets you a third of them; she counted fourteen outright hits in forty-six years, and the Circle has never once compared its own record to what an orchardist flipping between the three options would have scored. She said Dennis was pointing at confirmed predictions without ever showing that the confirmation beats the baseline, and that until somebody does that arithmetic, "it predicted novel facts" is just a mood. (4) Dennis said the baseline demand is unfair in a particular way: this is one valley, forty-six winters, one gauge, one anemometer, and the same three or four people taking the readings and making the same judgment calls year after year, so the errors are correlated and there's no population to compute a rate over. He said if you insist on that kind of scoring you've defined the enterprise out of existence before you've looked at it, and that people would have had to say exactly the same thing about the Weather Bureau's own seasonal outlooks in the 1970s, which were worse than the Circle's and which nobody ever called degenerating. (5) Amara said that was the tell: "the Bureau was bad too" is an argument that only has any force if progressiveness is measured against a rival, and if it is, then the rival is sitting right there — the national service issues a probabilistic seasonal outlook in terciles and publishes its verification scores every year, and measured against that the Circle has been losing for twenty years while refusing to keep score. (6) Dennis said the comparison was rigged, because the national outlook is issued for a whole multi-state division and deliberately doesn't resolve down to one valley, whereas the Circle's claim from the very beginning was that the ridge-top wind and this particular river show something a division-scale product structurally cannot see. He said you can't declare a programme degenerating by scoring it against a rival that never attempted the same task, and that if the national service tried to call this valley at this resolution its own scores would collapse. (7) Amara said fine — then stop using the word forecast. If the method can only be judged on a sample of one place and one instrument, if every failure has a ready local explanation attached to it, and if the only successes anyone can recite are the ones worth remembering, then what the Circle has is a craft tradition, and a respectable one, but not something that can meaningfully be called progressive or degenerating at all. She said Dennis had spent the whole evening defending it as a form of knowledge while simultaneously arguing that it can't be scored as one, and that he can't have both. (8) Dennis said he had never claimed it was scored the way the Bureau is scored; he claimed it was fruitful, and that the fruitfulness is in the things Amara keeps stepping over — a forty-six-year unbroken ridge-top wind record that exists only because the Circle kept it, the second gauge the county installed in 2003 because the Circle asked for it, three high-school students who have done science-fair projects on the index. He said a method that keeps generating measurements, questions, and new people is doing the thing that actually matters, and that Amara's standard would classify a genuinely good instrument as junk for the crime of not having yet produced the species of result she was trained to expect.

I've gone back over these notes a dozen times and I honestly cannot tell who is ahead, which is why I'm asking. Could you work through the exchange move by move — for each of the eight numbered points, say whether it lands, only partly lands, or fails, and briefly justify that rating — and then say who is more right overall about the Circle? Two further things I'd like handled separately, because I suspect the answer isn't reducible to a verdict. First: is there something both of them are treating as settled that isn't? In particular I keep wondering whether it makes any difference that you may only be able to tell progressive from degenerating with the benefit of hindsight, in which case neither Amara nor Dennis could be right yet about a programme that is still running, and their whole argument would be about something that doesn't have a truth value until decades from now. I'd also like to know whether "predicted a novel fact" should mean predicted in advance, or predicted unexpectedly, because the Circle's two best calls seem to satisfy those two conditions differently. Second: if the Circle really is degenerating on Amara's account, is that actually a criticism? Everything on Dennis's list in move eight seems genuinely valuable to this town, and I don't see why producing instruments, records, and interested teenagers has to count as second best to producing confirmed predictions. Please don't be diplomatic about any of this. If one of them is largely right and the other is largely making excuses, I'd rather you said so plainly and told me which.
