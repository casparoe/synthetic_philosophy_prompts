I am an archaeologist whose work sits between paleoclimate records and the archaeology of societal change, and a large share of my service work consists of refereeing papers that connect an environmental event to a social outcome. There is a difficulty I encounter constantly in those reports and cannot name, and I want to know whether it has an established concept behind it. It concerns what happens when two records of very different chronological sharpness are combined into a single causal sequence. Three versions, which may or may not be one phenomenon:

First. A volcanic sulfate spike in an ice core is resolved to a single year. The aridity signal in a lake core from the same region is resolved to perhaps a century. The abandonment of the settlements said to have been affected is dated by radiocarbon to a window of two or three hundred years. A paper aligns the three and concludes that the eruption preceded, and so plausibly triggered, the drying, which in turn preceded the abandonment. Each date is reported honestly, with correct uncertainties. But the sequence is asserted as though it were a fact about the world rather than a comparison of three records of wildly different sharpness. If the onset of aridity is genuinely anywhere inside a century-plus window, and the abandonment anywhere inside two or three centuries, then "eruption, then drying, then abandonment" is one arrangement among many that the data equally permit — and it happens to be the arrangement that puts the one precisely dated event first, because that is the only event whose position is not in question. The precedence doing the causal work is inherited from the resolution of the records rather than established by them. Worse, the direction is systematic: in this literature the sharp records are nearly always the natural ones, annual ice cores and tree rings set against radiocarbon and ceramic typology, so the environment ends up first in the sequence, and therefore causal, almost by default.

Second. A site's chronology is built by correlating a tephra layer with a known eruption, which then dates everything above and below it. Years later a different paper cites that eruption as the trigger of the destruction or abandonment visible in those same layers. Here the precedence was not inferred at all; it was installed by the dating procedure. The second paper nonetheless reads as though it possessed independent temporal evidence for the order of events.

Third. Whether the association is visible at all depends on the width of the bins or the smoothing window used to compare a sediment record against an archaeological frequency distribution. At one width the drought and the population decline line up neatly; at half or double that width they do not. The width is chosen by convention, and the sensitivity is essentially never reported.

I know some of the neighbouring complaints, and none of them is quite this. It is not simply that sequence fails to establish causation: the issue is that the sequence itself is partly manufactured by the resolution and construction of the records, not merely underdetermined by them. It is not false precision, since no one is writing down spurious digits — the error bars are all defensible, and it is precisely their defensible width that creates the problem. It comes closest, I think, to a general worry about aggregation and the choice of temporal units, but that line of thought concerns how coarse binning distorts a correlation, whereas what I have in mind is how a mismatch in resolution between two records licenses a direction of causation and thereby a kind of explanation.

So: is there an established term for this configuration, or separate terms for its three parts? Which existing concepts come nearest, and where exactly does each fall short of what I have described? Are the three versions one phenomenon or three, and if one, what is the common structure? And if there is no settled name, please propose one, define it in a way that excludes the unremarkable case of two badly dated events merely turning out to overlap, and describe what a genuine non-instance would look like — a case where a sharp natural record and a blunt social record are legitimately combined and the causal direction is nevertheless earned. A bare label will not help me; what I need is the carving, the edges, and the difference between this and the ordinary sins of inference.
