So, fortnightly reading group in a pub back room, about 14 of us, two hours, no budget and no printer beyond my crappy one at home. The crowd is very LW-adjacent — lots of people who will happily argue about priors for an hour and consider it a good evening. Composition matters here: two people who have actually worked in benefits administration, one hardline libertarian who reads economics for fun, and three social work students who will smell a strawman from across the room. I don't want anybody to feel like the game was rigged for their side.

Next session is conditionality — work search requirements, workfare, sanctions, benefit fraud, the whole deserving/undeserving poor thing. Reading stuff aloud and discussing it will just collapse into everyone restating their priors with more confidence. I want them to run into the problem instead.

Here's the seed I've got, and I want you to build it into something actually runnable. The core idea I want them to *feel* rather than be told: deservingness is a counterfactual about a person — what they'd do if the rule weren't there — and it's not observable from inside the system. So every eligibility test is a proxy for something unmeasurable, and the claimants can see the proxy even though the caseworkers can't see them. Which means over repeated rounds the proxy decays, and a rule tight enough to catch the person gaming it is also tight enough to exclude the person who's genuinely stuck, and from the caseworker's chair those two look identical.

Concretely I'm imagining something like four rounds. Round 1, caseworkers set a threshold without knowing the claimant population. Round 2, they see the error rates they produced and get to revise. Round 3, claimants have now learned the rule and start optimizing to it. Round 4, some kind of audit or budget shock. But I'm not attached to that shape — if you think the four-round structure won't actually generate the thing I want, say so and give me a better one.

Some claimant roles need to encode stuff that's real but unverifiable: an undiagnosed illness, a caring responsibility, a job offer that evaporated, a partner's income nobody declared. Caseworkers should be restricted to a fixed list of questions so they can't just interrogate their way to the truth. I want a few roles where gaming is the rational move and a few where it isn't and nobody can tell which is which from outside.

What I need from you:
- Full role set and materials list. Index cards, tokens, a whiteboard are all fine, no real money.
- A scoring scheme that produces felt cost without money. I've been mulling a shared pool that the whole room loses from when someone is wrongly sanctioned, but I don't know if that just makes everyone soft — you figure out what actually creates pressure, and tell me why.
- Minute-by-minute timeline for the two hours, including where the rounds can overrun.
- A debrief that maps specific moments ("when Dave refused to ask the third question") onto the concepts, and — importantly — surfaces the hidden normative premise that the two error types are or aren't equally bad, without me announcing it from the front. They should notice that they've been assuming something.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. My guesses: someone plays the claimant as a cartoon villain, the social work students refuse to play caseworker on principle, the libertarian wins round 3 and half the room disengages, and someone figures out how to break the scoring. Handle each.
- An honest paragraph on which feature of the real thing the game cannot reproduce, that I'm going to have to supply myself, and what I should say.
- How it degrades if only 8 people turn up, which is the likelier scenario.

Don't send me a reading list or citations, I've got plenty and it's not what I'm asking for. Also feel free to tell me the seed is bad, but if it is, bring a replacement I can run in a pub with index cards.
