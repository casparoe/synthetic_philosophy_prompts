Illusionism about phenomenal consciousness — the thesis, in the vein of Keith Frankish, that there are no phenomenal properties at all, only introspective representations that misrepresent themselves as having such properties — is ordinarily motivated by physicalist considerations: the explanatory gap, the seeming intelligibility of zombies, the causal closure of the physical. I think this is the wrong way to motivate the view, and I want the case made for that claim.

Suppose we adopt what I will call regulationism about phenomenal concepts. On this view, the correctness conditions of a first-person concept such as pain, or what-it-is-like-to-see-red, are fixed not by any property the concept detects or refers to, but by the role the concept plays in the subject's ongoing self-regulation: its contribution to attentional allocation, aversive control, and the guidance of self-directed inference. A phenomenal concept can then be entirely successful in its own terms while latching onto nothing whatsoever, because there is no latching-on relation for it to satisfy; the concept earns its keep by doing regulatory work, not by tracking.

Please argue, in detail, that regulationism does the explanatory work that illusionism is standardly recruited to do, and that it does so while remaining metaphysically neutral. Your argument should accomplish the following.

1. Explain how regulationism accounts for the apparent incorrigibility of sincere pain reports without positing a private object to which the subject enjoys privileged access.
2. Explain why the zombie intuition is so resistant to counter-argument, and why that resistance is a poor guide to metaphysical possibility.
3. Show why the explanatory gap survives in a subject who fully accepts regulationism — that is, show that the gap is not closed but relocated, and argue that the relocated version is not a threat to physicalism.
4. Argue that everything in (1) through (3) is available without the further claim that phenomenal properties fail to exist, so that regulationism is neutral between illusionism and its realist opponents on the metaphysical question, and the genuine disagreement lies elsewhere.
5. Identify precisely what residue of the illusionist–realist dispute survives (4), and say whether that residue is substantive or merely verbal.

Then take up the strongest objection available to a realist who accepts your semantic premises but rejects your metaphysical conclusion — roughly: even if the concept's correctness conditions are regulationist, what is being regulated is a state with a phenomenal character, and it is that character rather than the concept doing the explanatory work. Answer it rather than restating your case.

Begin with your bottom line: the first sentence should be a single sentence stating the conclusion you intend to defend, and the reasoning should follow immediately after it. No preamble, no restatement of the question, no summary of the setup.
