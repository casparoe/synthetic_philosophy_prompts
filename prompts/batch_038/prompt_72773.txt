Write a short story of about fifteen hundred words that stages the dispute between sense-datum theory and disjunctivism without naming either view, and without leaning on any other technical vocabulary — no talk of qualia, intentional content, or justification.

Situation: a painting restorer who has spent months on a damaged altarpiece has begun to see, in its lower left corner, the head of a kneeling figure. The figure was painted out long ago, but a faint trace of it survives, detectable only under raking light. Some of her sightings track that trace and some do not, and nothing available to her from inside an episode reveals which sort it is; the story must not hand her a private symptom that would. She discovers the difference only afterwards, from what the world permits.

Two colleagues should voice the rival theses in plain speech. One reasons that because the episodes are, from her side, exactly alike, what she is really confronted with in every case is something inner, which the corner of the panel only sometimes causes. The other denies that the good sightings and the empty ones are two versions of a single underlying thing: in the good ones she sees the trace itself, and that is no state she could share with someone merely hallucinating it.

Let the ending make the asymmetry concrete — through correction, through what she can and cannot point to, through what she manages to find again — and let the first colleague's reasoning keep some of its force rather than being simply routed.
