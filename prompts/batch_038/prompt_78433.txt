OK so my flatmate Dana and I have been arguing for the better part of three hours — beers empty, no progress — about our country's historical-injustices panel, the standing interministerial body that decides whether the state issues a formal apology when a community petitions for one. Neither of us can say what rule it's actually running, and we've now each put money on the case that's sitting in front of it this month. Her read is that the panel is basically a "is the harm still live?" machine, so it'll apologize; mine is that it's a "did the affected community ask, in one voice, through the body we recognize?" machine, so it'll decline and offer a fund instead. We want an outside adjudicator before we double the stakes.

Here's the track record we've both been working from, which is all public and all pretty consistent on its face. (1) The 1913–1936 irrigation scheme that drowned the winter grazing lands of a pastoralist people: full apology plus a named land-and-livelihood programme, on the reasoning that the scheme was built by the colonial public works department whose records, staff and legal obligations passed unbroken into today's ministry, and whose descendants are still landless. (2) The 1880s famine, during which the administration then in place kept exporting grain: the panel issued a statement of profound sorrow and regret and funded a memorial, but explicitly declined to call it an apology, saying the institutions responsible were not ours and it could find no one still bearing the consequences. (3) Compulsory sterilization of women classified as feeble-minded, 1951–1969, run by the national health service under a statute this parliament repealed in 1998: apology plus individual compensation to the 214 identified survivors, and the panel was careful to address the apology to the survivors as individuals rather than to "the community" as a collective. (4) The 1974 cross-border operation where we supplied logistics, signals intelligence and a railhead for a foreign power's campaign that killed several thousand civilians: no apology, but the state acknowledged and regretted its role as an accomplice, fully funded a joint archive, a memorial and a truth commission, and noted that the killings were carried out by forces it did not command and that the victims are now citizens of another state. (5) The ban on schooling in a minority language, 1882–1961: apology, no individual compensation, paired with a language revitalization fund, on the stated ground that the harm is still measurable in census language-use data and in who holds public office, so it is ongoing rather than historical. (6) The 1948 confiscation of a religious minority's endowment properties: the property was returned in stages, a settlement was signed with the community's council in 1993, and when younger members petitioned for an apology in 2016 the panel refused, saying the matter had been closed by an agreement reached with the community's own recognized representatives, who had not asked for an apology and, consulted again, still did not.

The new case is the one we're betting on. Between 1962 and 1971 the health service, under a statute that was only fully repealed four years ago, removed children from a remote island community to mainland boarding institutions for tuberculosis treatment; roughly 900 children, many kept for years, many not taught in their own language, a bunch never returned. The ministry that ran it is the direct institutional ancestor of today's health ministry with unbroken records. In 2019 the state paid full individual compensation to identified survivors under a settlement agreement that contained no apology clause, and the survivors accepted it. Documented continuing effects: TB incidence on the island is still several times the national average, and language fluency in the under-40s has collapsed. Now the community has petitioned. But it is a mess internally — the elected island council's executive voted 4–3 to request an apology, while the elders' assembly, which is the body the council is answerable to and which the panel treated as the community's representative when it signed the 2019 settlement, voted against requesting one, saying they'd rather have the island hospital rebuilt. Seperately, a survivors' association, about a third of whose members now live abroad, has filed its own request. Dana says (5) and (3) control and it's an apology. I say (6) controls and it's a decline with a hospital grant attached. There's also a third rule lurking that neither of us likes: apologize only where the wrong can't be discharged by money or restitution, in which case 2019 already did the discharging — except that rule would have predicted an apology in (4) unless you think the archive and the truth commission counted as discharge.

So: call it. Give me your best guess at the panel's verdict with something like a confidence, and say which rule you think they're actually running — including whether the institutional-continuity rule is really dead after (6) or just dormant. Which precedent is the closest analog, and where does the analogy break? Then the part I care about most: whichever way they go, what principle are they thereby adopting, and what does it commit them to in cases nobody's petitioned about yet — e.g. does declining mean a settlement signed by one generation's representatives binds decendents who weren't consulted, and does apologizing mean the panel gets to override an elders' assembly that says no? And finally, name the hypothetical case we could hand this panel next that would force the ongoing-harm rule, the who-is-asking rule and the can't-be-discharged rule apart, and say what each of the three would predict about it. Everything you need is in the above, I'm not asking you to go check what real countries did.
