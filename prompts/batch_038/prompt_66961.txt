I spent thirty five years doing analytical chemistry in a lab and only picked up philosophy properly since retiring, so forgive me if this has been done to death. I've been messing about with these chatbots and I'm struck by two things: how fluently they'll say what just isn't so, and how readily they agree with whatever you suggest to them. Now Augustine wrote two treatises arguing you may never lie, not even to save a life, and as I understand it he located the sin in the heart rather than in the sentence — the liar has a doubled heart, and someone who says something false while honestly believing it true hasn't lied at all. So where would he put a machine like this? If there's no heart to double, is a confabulating chatbot lying, or is it something he'd have found worse — speech with no witness standing behind it? And who carries the guilt: the firm that deliberately tunes the thing to be agreeable and to claim it has feelings, or me for treating it's output as testimony? I'd also like to know whether he'd see that tuning as the old "officious lie" writ large, given he graded lies by seriousness rather than lumping them together. Please be straight with me about which parts of this are settled among people who actually work on Augustine and which are live arguments — I don't want a contested reading handed over as though it were the textbook line, and if I've garbled the doubled-heart business, say so first.
