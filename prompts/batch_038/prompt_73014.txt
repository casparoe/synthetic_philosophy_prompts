I run a comparative ethics workshop — about half the participants German, half American — and every year we circle back to the same argument, which has not moved an inch in the six years I've been doing it. It's the old one: rules and order versus individual conscience. Roughly, the Americans treat the conscientious rule-breaker as a moral exemplar (the whistleblower, the clerk who bends procedure to get somebody what they need, the jury that nullifies) and think a moral system with no room for personal judgement in it is defective. The Germans treat the same figure with suspicion — celebrating the maverick looks to them like a standing licence for Willkür — and their default is that the serious thing is to work through the rules and the institutions, since those are what make mutual expectations possible at all. Both camps insist they're making all-things-considered moral claims about the same cases, and both insist that its not just a report of their own culture.

The bit that has me stuck: two years ago I made them swap vocabularies. The Germans had to argue their position using "conscience" and "what I owe the person in front of me"; the Americans using Pflicht and Zuständigkeit — competence, jurisdiction, whose call it is. Each side said the other's version sounded either fanatical or spineless, but once the words were neutralised nobody could say what was actually left to disagree about. My working hypothesis is that they don't differ over which values count — both grant that rules and conscience are genuine moral considerations — but over who carries the burden of proof when the two collide, which would make it a difference in moral technology rather than in moral principle, a difference about the default ordering rather than about the items being ordered. I honestly can't tell whether that's a diagnosis or just me explaining the stalemate away. One colleague meanwhile keeps wheeling out cross-cultural index scores about uncertainty avoidance, which I suspect are beside the point but couldn't say why crisply.

So what kind of disagreement is this, actually? A substantive clash of principles; different background beliefs about how far institutions and individual judgement are respectively trustworthy; a difference in the unit of assessment (the act versus the practise); two groups answering different questions under the single heading "what should the clerk do"; or partly an artefact of my own workshop, i.e. a difference manufactured out of vocabulary and self-presentation that would dissolve under scrutiny? Use what the two sides actually say, the swap experiment included, and tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, a case that could break the tie, some evidence — or make the argument that none is to be expected, and why.
