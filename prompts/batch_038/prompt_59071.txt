I am a high school junior writing a paper about a student council meeting we had last month on whether our school's harassment rules should cover what students say to one another in group chats. The same proverb was quoted by both sides. A council member arguing against the extension said that "sticks and stones may break my bones, but words will never hurt me," and that because a school may only restrict conduct that harms someone, speech falls outside the rules. Our dean, arguing for the extension, said the proverb is what adults have always told a child who has been called names, that it is obviously untrue, and that its untruth is precisely why the rules must reach speech. I have read part of On Liberty and know the dispute turns on what harm is supposed to mean; my suspicion is that they were reciting one sentence while asserting several different things.

Could you separate the distinct claims the proverb might be making -- a claim about what words are causally capable of doing, a claim about which kinds of damage count as harm, a claim about how one ought to receive an insult, and perhaps a claim about how much of an insult's force belongs to the person insulted -- and say for each whether it is a platitude, a substantive and defensible thesis, or simply false? Then tell me which readings, if any, actually license the council member's conclusion about the code, and which support the dean's. There is one argument I have not been able to answer: if an insult damages someone only by way of what she already believes about herself, the damage seems to come from her own judgment rather than from the speaker, which would put the whole exchange back on the self-regarding side of the line. Say whether that objection works. And since I will have to defend whichever reading I build the paper on, please say what would change your mind about these verdicts, and where you think your own reasoning is likeliest to fail.
