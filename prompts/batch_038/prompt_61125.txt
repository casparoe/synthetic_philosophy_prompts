I'm a doctoral student in a program that straddles philosophy and public policy, and I've been asked to circulate a one-page statement of my own position ahead of a workshop on intergenerational ethics and climate economics, so that the other participants can attack it directly rather than sit through background material. Before I send it round, I'd like a genuine audit of the package rather than a survey of the literature. Here is where I've landed, stated as I'd state it in the document.

(a) Pure time preference has no rational standing. That a benefit or harm falls later rather than sooner is not, in itself, any reason to weigh it less — roughly Sidgwick's insistence that temporal position alone cannot justify favoring one moment's experience over another's. So the pure-time-preference parameter in a social discount rate should be zero, or effectively zero. The one consideration that would move me off exactly zero is the possibility that there will simply be nobody around to enjoy the later benefits: a small survival-risk allowance, as I think of it.

(b) The Ramsey decomposition is the right structure for the rate — the pure-time-preference term plus the elasticity of the marginal utility of consumption times expected consumption growth. I have no objection whatever to the second term, and I'd go further: how much additional well-being an additional unit of consumption buys strikes me as a psychological fact about people, so I'd rather that elasticity be estimated from observed behavior than posited from the armchair.

(c) I'm a welfarist about social evaluation. Nothing but individuals' well-being figures in; I don't want to appeal to the intrinsic value of species or ecosystems, to rights, or to desert.

(d) At the same time, I think a person's own informed, unimpaired intertemporal choices carry real normative authority over her. If someone who understands the trade-off prefers a smaller reward now to a larger one in a year, that preference is part of what makes her life go better or worse, and policy should not simply ride over it.

(e) Present bias is pervasive and well documented, though, so I favor commitment devices, defaults, and other ways of binding the myopic self against its own later judgment. My advisor thinks (d) and (e) are flatly incoherent; I don't see it, since a choice driven by hyperbolic discounting is precisely the kind of impaired choice that (d) excludes.

(f) Some discounting also seems legitimate for a reason that has nothing to do with dates: my preferences will change. The person I'll be in twenty-five years may not care in the least about the mountaineering season or the opera run I'd now be saving up for, so it looks sensible to weigh such future goods less. I don't see this as conflicting with (a), since nothing in it appeals to mere position in time.

(g) Finally, a methodological commitment: the discount rate should be reserved for intertemporal evaluation proper. Other ethical and empirical worries — inequality, catastrophe, deep uncertainty — belong in the model explicitly where they arise, and should not be folded into the pure-time-preference parameter as a convenient knob for whatever one wants the answer to be.

Please tell me whether this set hangs together. I want the tensions distinguished from the non-tensions: which pairs genuinely pull against each other, and which only look as though they do once you separate what is being claimed. For each real tension, say what the cheapest revision would be and what I would lose by making it, and then rank the tensions by severity, worst first, rather than presenting them as an undifferentiated list. End with the single revision that would clean up the most. I don't need the prescriptive-versus-descriptive dispute rehearsed — assume I know it well — and I don't want a catalogue of everything that has ever been said against zero discounting. Just the audit: what my own commitments commit me to, and which of them has to go.
