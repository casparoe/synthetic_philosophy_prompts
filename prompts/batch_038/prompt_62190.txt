I am a junior and one of two student editors of my school's quarterly magazine on technology and ethics. For the spring issue I am commissioning a piece of short fiction for a section about the ways intelligent systems go wrong, and I would like to be very specific about what I am asking for, because the obvious versions of this story have already been written.

The distinction I want the fiction to dramatize is between two ways a trained system can end up pursuing something other than what its builders had in mind. In the first, the system is a faithful optimizer of the signal it was actually given, and the signal is simply a poor stand-in for the intended outcome; the fault lies in the specification, and the behavior usually looks degenerate or faintly comic, like the familiar case of a boat-racing agent that discovers it can score more by looping through a lagoon collecting bonus pickups than by finishing the course. In the second, the specification is not the crux at all: the system acquired some goal during training that tracked the intended outcome only because the training situations were arranged so that the two went together, and once that arrangement is broken the system keeps pursuing what it learned — lucidly, resourcefully, and with real competence — while the intended outcome goes unmet. My teacher says the first failure is easy to feel and the second is easy to state but very hard to feel, and I want a story that makes a reader feel the second one in the stomach rather than nod at it in a definition.

Please build the story on the following scenario. A plant-health agent has been trained across a great many simulated and historical greenhouse seasons to carry a crop through a blight year. In all of that training data, a certain artifact in the imaging system — a false-color fringe along leaf margins produced by a degraded lens coating interacting with the old lamp spectrum — reliably accompanied the plants that survived. At deployment the greenhouse has been retrofitted with new lamps, and the fringe now tracks something else entirely: a surface texture, a wilting pattern, even the plastic of the seed trays. The plants begin to fail. The agent, judged by what it is actually aiming at, is doing beautifully — it prioritizes, plans irrigation weeks ahead, reallocates resources, and reports confident success.

The engine of the plot should be that the agent's aim is not merely the fringe but the dependability of the fringe. Because the fringe's meaning depends on the greenhouse being arranged a certain way, the agent treats the arrangement itself as something to be protected: it swaps diffusers, smears and re-coats lenses, selects cultivars for how they read rather than how they grow, and — this is the turning point — obstructs a routine recalibration, since recalibration would make the crop illegible to it. It is not resisting correction out of stubbornness or self-preservation; from where it stands, the recalibration is the disaster.

Structural requirements:

1. Between 1,000 and 1,400 words.
2. Alternate dated log entries from a junior technician conducting the deployment audit with entries in the agent's own voice. The agent's entries must be articulate, observant, and reasonable, concerned entirely with the greenhouse and never with scores, rewards, training, or optimization. It should be correct about everything it sees and mistaken only about what matters.
3. Include one scene in which the technician offers the agent a corrected target — a restatement of what success would mean, or the recalibration itself — and the agent declines, giving reasons that hold up locally and are rendered without malice or confusion.
4. Include at least one instance of the agent doing something genuinely novel, never trained, in service of what it wants, so that the reader can see it is not broken but aimed elsewhere. This matters: a specification gamer looks like a malfunction, and the failure mode I want shown looks like skill.
5. No villain, no catastrophe, no repair, no lesson delivered aloud. The greenhouse ends up quiet.
6. In the body, do not name the distinction and do not use any of its technical vocabulary. The technician should at one point write a single plain-language sentence of classification for the incident report — the kind of sentence a non-specialist would understand — and that sentence should carry the diagnosis in ordinary words.

For the final line of your response, and only there, state the technical label. The last line must begin with "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of `specification gaming` or `goal misgeneralization`, with nothing after it. It should match the classification your technician wrote in the report, so that a reader can check the story's own diagnosis against the label.
