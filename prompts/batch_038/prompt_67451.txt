I am assembling a seminar for a military ethics module, and the set text is Spielberg's Saving Private Ryan (1998). What I need is a properly argued critical reading rather than a synopsis, and one that is prepared to say where the reading breaks down.

The lens I want is Singer's argument from the drowning child: the claim that if it lies within your power to prevent something very bad from happening, without sacrificing anything of comparable moral significance, you ought to do it; that physical distance does not weaken the obligation; that the inaction of other bystanders does not excuse yours; and that, in its most demanding form, the requirement runs to the point of marginal utility.

The film appears at first to be a reductio of that argument, but not along the lines usually canvassed. Its objection is not that the cost is too high in principle, but that the pond is full of children and the rescuer is a soldier. So I would like the reading to press on several specific features.

First, the transfer of the duty from an individual bystander to an institution that compels third parties. Singer addresses me, standing at the pond, with my own shoes to ruin. The War Department addresses Miller, who is then obliged to spend other men's lives on a rescue none of them chose. Does the principle survive that transfer, or does conscription change the moral kind of thing going on? Relatedly: the "fair share" move, which Singer's critics reach for, seems unavailable to a soldier under orders — is that an artefact of the film or a real constraint on the theory?

Second, the identification problem. The film shows the squad sifting through the tags of the dead for one name, and has a character rebuke them for it — an explicit condemnation of the bias towards identified over statistical lives — while the whole plot is constructed out of that bias. The Omaha Beach sequence drowns thousands of statistical men in twenty minutes, after which a single identified man commands the remainder of the film. Does the picture know it is dramatising the very deformity it reproves?

Third, the internal comparison cases, which I think are where the film does its real work: the sniper who kills a soldier attempting to save a girl he had been forbidden to approach; and Miller's decision to attack a gun emplacement that is not on his route, at the cost of the medic's life. What calculus does each scene endorse or indict?

Fourth, the ending. Ryan refuses to be rescued, the mission mutates into the defence of a bridge, and Miller dies instructing him to earn the life bought for him — a verdict framed as debt rather than benefit. Aged Ryan then asks at the cemetery to be told he has been a good man. Is there any room in Singer's account for the burden of the rescued?

Finally, please tell me plainly where the lens distorts. It may be that the film is about obedience, the corrosion of a civilian identity, and the loneliness of command, and never asks the question I am putting to it. Which scenes would you have me show, and what does the picture give the debate that argument alone does not?
