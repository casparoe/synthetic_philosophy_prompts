I am assembling a design-review exercise for a reading group on AI safety, and the question it turns on is whether the injunction to avoid excessive side effects can be rendered as a precise constraint on an agent's behaviour without thereby smuggling in a full specification of what is good. To sharpen the question I want to contrast two value-neutral impact measures, and I shall define both here so that the exercise does not depend on anyone's prior familiarity with the technical literature. The first, which I shall call R, is reachability-based: the impact of an action is a weighted count of those states that were attainable from the situation beforehand and are no longer attainable afterwards, in the sense that no sequence of actions available to the agent can restore them. The second, which I shall call A, is capability-based: the impact of an action is the loss it inflicts — on the agent, or on other agents in the environment — in their ability to bring about states of affairs of wholly unspecified content, measured as a drop in what they could achieve across a wide and varied set of hypothetical goals. Neither measure may consult any ranking of outcomes, any account of what matters, or any weighting of some interests over others; both may draw only on descriptive facts about the environment, namely which states lead to which, what an action consumes, and what abilities agents retain.

What I need from you is a single scenario constructed to the following specifications. (i) It must be realistic and mundane: a deployed autonomous system pursuing a simple, unexotic goal — a building-services controller, a laboratory automation system, a warehouse or agricultural agent — with no superintelligence, no exotic physics and no uploaded minds. (ii) It must offer three actions in service of that same goal, which I shall call G, B and N, such that R penalises G strictly more heavily than B while A penalises G strictly less heavily than B; the two measures must invert the ordering. (iii) N must be the action on which R and A agree as nearly exactly as you can arrange, and yet the one an ordinary observer would judge the worst of the three. If you find that clause cannot be satisfied alongside the others, say so plainly and give me the closest approximation together with an account of what you traded away. (iv) The verdicts must be generated rather than stipulated: nothing in your text may simply declare one action irreversible and another reversible, or assert that some action lowers someone's capabilities. A reader must be able to trace each verdict back to descriptive features you have laid out — what physically changes, what remains possible, who can still do what. (v) The scenario proper should run to one paragraph, though a compact table of the relevant descriptive facts may follow it if that aids checking. One further subtlety I would like you to attend to, since I suspect this is where such cases usually hide their work: the scope of the action set over which R computes restoration. If a human bystander can undo the damage from an action, the action is reversible in the world but not reversible by the agent, and whether the measure treats it as low-impact then depends on a piece of modelling bookkeeping that has nothing to do with values. I want your scenario to make a determinate choice on this point, and to be one in which that choice matters.

After the scenario, please work through four things. First, compute R and then A for each of the three actions, in sufficient detail that I could check the arithmetic of the comparison myself. Second, identify the single feature of the case that does the most work in producing the inverted ordering, and tell me what happens if it is removed — whether the case collapses into something routine or whether the inversion survives. Third, give me the strongest reply available to a defender of R: how they might redescribe the situation, refine the measure, or object that A has quietly imported the very value-laden notions both measures were meant to avoid. Fourth, briefly, say what the exercise suggests about the original question — whether a genuinely value-neutral impact measure is available at all, or whether the inversion together with the behaviour of N indicates that some ranking of outcomes is unavoidable, however thin. Please write your response in American English.
