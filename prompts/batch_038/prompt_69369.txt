My sister Dana is the clinical ethicist at a mid-size transplant center, and every time I visit we end up arguing about who counts as dead. She refuses to pick a camp when I ask her straight out — she just says "I don't do metaphysics, I do consults" — so I've started keeping a notes app of her actual verdicts, sort of like case law but about my sister. Everything below is a real consult she's had, with the identifying stuff stripped:

1. Man in his 40s, catastrophic bleed, no brainstem reflexes, failed the apnea test, on a ventilator, warm, kidneys making urine, surgical incisions healing nicely. Dead, flat out, no hedging. She corrects families who say "he's still fighting," and she once made a nurse rewrite a chart note that said the patient was "being kept alive."
2. Woman with a high cervical cord injury, fully awake, talking, can't breathe without the vent, heart paced. Obviously alive, obviously a person. Dana went of on a resident who described her as "basically a body on a machine."
3. Baby born with anencephaly, brainstem working, breathing on his own, no prospect of awareness ever, died on day four. Alive the whole time, and she blocked the parents' request to take his kidneys before he died.
4. Woman six years into a permanent vegetative state, breathes on her own, sleep-wake cycles, swallows, zero chance of recovery. Alive. When I pushed her on how that's supposed to differ from case 1, she said she couldn't explain it to a family in one sentence and would never claim she could.
5. Woman declared brain dead near the end of her first trimester, kept on support about nine more weeks, delivered a healthy baby by c-section. Dana insisted the mother had been dead since the day of declaration, and wrote an angry letter to the local paper about a headline that ran something like "her body kept the baby alive."
6. Controlled donation after circulatory death: support withdrawn in the OR, heart stops, five-minute hands-off period, declared dead on circulatory grounds, organs recovered. She signed off, called it textbook.
7. Same setup, except the heart restarted on its own about seventy seconds into the stand-off. Procurement aborted. Dana: "he was never dead, and that isn't a technicality, that's the entire reason the five minutes exists."
8. Man on ECMO for nine days, heart and lungs both doing nothing, machine doing all of it, awake and texting his kids. Alive.

Here's the new one. Their organ procurement outfit wants to adopt an abdominal normothermic regional perfusion protocol for circulatory-death donors. As far as I understand it, the front end is identical to case 6 — withdraw in the OR, wait out the five minutes, declare death — but then, before anything is removed, the surgeons clamp the aorta up near the diaphragm so no blood can reach the heart or the brain, cannulate, and pump warm oxygenated blood back through the abdominal organs on an extracorporeal circuit for something like forty minutes, so the liver and kidneys get reconditioned and the team can watch them function. Then they recover the organs. Dana votes in three weeks. There's also a thoracic version floating around for the spring, where they'd restart the donor's own heart in his chest and let it beat for a while, but that's not the vote I'm asking about.

So what does she vote, and how does she word it? Work out what her eight verdicts actually pin down. Some of them clearly knock out whole families of rules — I don't see how she can be running any "death is the loss of the capacity for consciousness" line given 3 and 4, and 2 and 8 look fatal to a naive "circulation and breathing have stopped" line — so tell me which candidates survive all eight, and what each of those survivors says about the pump case. Where exactly do they come apart? Then give me your best guess at her vote with the reasons you'd actually bet on, name which of the eight is the closest analog and where that analogy starts to strain, and be straight with me about whether her record genuinely underdetermines this or whether one answer is just more consistent with it than she'd like to admit. Finally: propose one further case — something concrete you could really table at a committee — that would separate whatever rules are still tied, say what each surviving rule predicts about it, and tell me whether the spring version with the beating heart would be easier or harder for her and why.
