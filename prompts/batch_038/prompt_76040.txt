I write novels, and for three terms now I have held a residency in a philosophy department where I co-teach a seminar on moral psychology and the craft of fiction. This year I took on the marking of one short-answer question from the seminar's final examination. I have deliberately left the paper unmarked and formed no view of it that I want to defend; I would rather your assessment came from the paper alone.

The question, as it was printed, ran as follows.

---

**Question 3 (25 marks).** The three extracts below are from Part I, Section I, Chapter I of Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments*.

(i) "Whatever is the passion which arises from any object in the person principally concerned, an analogous emotion springs up, at the thought of his situation, in the breast of every attentive spectator."

(ii) "Pity and compassion are words appropriated to signify our fellow-feeling with the sorrow of others. Sympathy, though its meaning was, perhaps, originally the same, may now, however, without much impropriety, be made use of to denote our fellow-feeling with any passion whatever."

(iii) "It is the impressions of our own senses only, not those of his, which our imaginations copy."

**(a)** With reference to extracts (i) and (iii), explain the mechanism by which, on Smith's account, the spectator comes to feel what he does; and say whether that feeling is derived from the person principally concerned. *(8)*

**(b)** With reference to extract (ii), say what turns on the distinction Smith there draws between pity and sympathy, and whether it is a distinction merely between words. *(6)*

**(c)** Distinguish the propriety of an affection from its merit, and say how each is judged by a spectator. *(5)*

**(d)** A novelist reports that while drafting she feels a pleasure in the cruelty of a character she is writing which she would condemn in life, and, in another scene, a grief she does not otherwise have. Using Smith's account, say what is going on in each case; and say whether her verdict on her own draft is the verdict of the impartial spectator. *(6)*

---

The candidate's answer, with paragraph numbers I have added for reference:

**(1)** Smith's starting point is epistemic. We have no immediate experience of what other men feel, and so we cannot read their feelings off them; we can form an idea of how they are affected only by conceiving what we ourselves should feel in the like situation. The imagination effects a change of place in fancy: we place ourselves in the other's circumstances, conceive ourselves enduring what he endures, and thence come to feel something which, though weaker in degree, is not altogether unlike what he feels. Sympathy is thus a copy — a fainter reproduction, in the spectator, of an original in the person principally concerned. Extract (iii) says as much: the imagination copies, and what it copies is the other's impressions. The degree of the copy varies with the vivacity of the conception.

**(2)** The distinction drawn in extract (ii) is, I think, largely terminological. Smith concedes that the word has come to cover fellow-feeling with any passion whatever, but his own account is governed by pity: the paradigm cases are the man upon the rack, the beggar's sores, the mourner, and what he needs to explain is how we come to feel another's misery. Where the passion in question is not a painful one, the mechanism is unchanged but the explanatory pressure on it is lower, and less rides on getting it right.

**(3)** Smith does allow a second route. The passions may seem to be transfused from one man to another, instantaneously and antecedent to any knowledge of what excited them: a smiling face cheers the spectator who knows nothing of its cause. So sympathy has two sources — the change of place in fancy, where the situation is known, and immediate transfusion, where it is not.

**(4)** Propriety concerns the relation between an affection and its cause or object: an affection is proper when the spectator who enters into the situation finds in himself something that answers to it, and improper when what he finds is different in kind or in degree. Merit concerns the relation between an action and its consequences: an action has merit when it deserves reward and demerit when it deserves punishment, and Smith analyses this as a compound of two sympathies — one with the motives of the agent, one with the gratitude or resentment of the person affected. Propriety may therefore be granted to an action that has no merit, and merit to an action that has no propriety; the two come apart.

**(5)** On (d): the grief the novelist feels in her mourner's scene is straightforwardly sympathy. She changes places in fancy with the character, and feels what she would feel in that situation, weaker in degree; Smith's own appeal to our joy and grief for the heroes of tragedy and romance supports this. The pleasure in the character's cruelty is something else. It cannot be sympathy, because sympathy is pity, and there is no misery in her hands to pity. It is an aesthetic pleasure in the vividness of the portrait, and Smith's theory has nothing to say about it.

**(6)** Her verdict on the draft is the verdict of the impartial spectator, which for Smith is the internalised voice of the company one has lived among: a habit installed by society, which thereafter serves as a stand-in for actually consulting it. Because the character does not exist, that voice has nothing to say about him; what it says about the passage is what her readers would say, which is why an editor can reassure or alarm her. Underneath the device, Smith's substantive criterion is utility — the spectator is a means of estimating the tendency of an affection to produce happiness, which is the criterion Hume also endorses, and once one has the estimate one no longer needs the spectator.
