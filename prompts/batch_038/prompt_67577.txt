In three weeks I shall be presenting a paper at an interdisciplinary workshop on character and moral psychology, jointly hosted by a philosophy department with a strongly Aristotelian orientation and a research centre in psychology that runs studies on helping behaviour and moral judgement. The room will hold perhaps twenty-five people, and they are not a natural constituency. There will be six or seven virtue ethicists who think in terms of stable dispositions and practical wisdom, who treat the appraisal of individual acts as philosophically second-order, and who will suspect that I have simply relabelled a notion they already own. There will be three experimental psychologists, one of whom sits on the committee that allocates the centre's seminar slots, and whose interest will be almost entirely in whether anything I say could be operationalised, measured, and tested against a rival explanation; they read vignette studies as evidence and will not be impressed by appeals to intuitions about shopkeepers. The chair is a Kant scholar, broadly sympathetic to the traditional account of moral worth and unlikely to enjoy being told that it mislocates the very property it was designed to capture. Then there are the generalists, who tend to ask the question the specialists have been too courteous to raise. The session runs to forty minutes with twenty for questions, which means I can afford to answer perhaps two challenges at length and must dispatch the rest in a sentence.

So that you have something to aim at, here is the thesis. I argue that moral worth is not a matter of what moved the agent on the occasion of the act, but of the counterfactual robustness of her responsiveness to the features that make the act right. An act has moral worth to the degree that the agent would have performed it, or something relevantly like it, across a wide neighbourhood of nearby situations in which those right-making features obtain — including situations in which the reasons she actually cites would have pointed the other way, and including situations in which her beliefs about what morality requires are systematically distorted. I intend to press four consequences: that this ranks the Huckleberry Finn type of agent very highly, because her responsiveness tracks the features even when her professed principles do not; that moral worth thereby becomes graded rather than binary, which I take to be an advantage and my audience may not; that the fetishism objection loses its grip, since nothing in the account requires the agent to be moved by the thought that her act is the right one; and that moral worth comes apart from praiseworthiness, since an agent may be robustly responsive to the right-making features in a manner that earns her no credit whatsoever, which is precisely why I have kept the two notions distinct.

What I want from you is a question-period preparation sheet, not a defence of the view; I am quite prepared to lose parts of it gracefully, and would rather know in advance which parts. Order the objections by how much damage they would do if they landed, and mark for each how likely it is to be raised in this room rather than in a general philosophy audience. Phrase each one in the register of the person who would actually voice it — the virtue ethicist will want to know whether robust responsiveness just is a virtue described modally, and if so what my paper has added; the psychologists will want to know what a counterfactual could possibly be measured with, how one would distinguish robust responsiveness from a well-learned habit or from social desirability, and what design would falsify me; the chair will want to know whether I have severed moral worth from the good will and left something no better than a reliable mechanism. For each objection, give a reply that fits in about two minutes, state plainly where that reply is soft, and say whether the objection is merely costly or genuinely fatal to the project as I have framed it. Tell me also what I should concede in the opening five minutes so that the concessions are mine rather than extracted from me, and identify the single point that has to land if the talk is to survive the afternoon. Finally, name the question I shall be praying does not come up — my own guess is the circularity worry, since the width of the counterfactual neighbourhood looks as though it can only be fixed by the very judgements about which features are right-making that the account was supposed to leave neutral, and I have no good answer to that yet — and tell me honestly how bad it is if someone does ask it, together with the least dishonest way of buying myself time. Please do not supply a bibliography or situate the view in the literature; I shall handle the citations myself.
