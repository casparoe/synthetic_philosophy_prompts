I am preparing a forty-minute talk for a reading group of philosophically literate non-specialists — mostly software engineers, plus one retired neurologist — on mind uploading, and I have hit a knot I cannot untangle by myself. Please take on the role of a professional philosopher of mind who works on the metaphysics of personal identity and on the functionalist debates about consciousness, and think the problem through carefully before writing: what I need is an explanation of a conceptual issue, not a survey of positions or a list of who said what. Aim for roughly 1,200 words of plain prose, define any term of art on first use, and give me no references, bibliography, or reading suggestions beyond the two well-known arguments I name below — I want the reasoning laid out, not the scholarship.

Here is the knot. Case A is the standard slow upload: over fourteen months every neuron in a person's brain is replaced, one at a time, by a silicon module with exactly that neuron's input-output profile, timing included; the subject keeps working and conversing throughout and reports at the end that nothing ever felt different. The argument I want to lean on is Chalmers's principle of organizational invariance, and specifically the dancing-qualia form of it: if the module-driven system lacked the experience the biological one had, then swapping modules in and out — which changes nothing functionally — would make the subject's experience flicker back and forth with no functional correlate, which is meant to be absurd. Case B is a variant a friend of mine constructed, and I have no good answer to it. Instead of silicon modules, each removed neuron is replaced by a fiber running to a room in an office park, where a clerk sits with a card index giving that neuron's output for every input it can receive and answers inside the neuron's original latency budget. After fourteen months the skull holds nothing but wiring, the clerks are doing all the causal work, and the person is still behaving and reporting normally. As far as I can tell the dancing-qualia argument applies to Case B just as cleanly as to Case A — I can swap any clerk back out for a silicon module and nothing functional changes — and yet my intuition insists Case B is a homunculus-headed arrangement of Block's sort, a distributed China-brain setup with no one in it. Case C sharpens the discomfort: suppose that after a year of clerks we replace each clerk with a module that exactly reproduces that clerk's input-output behavior, so that we are back in Case A. If consciousness was absent at the clerk stage, this person lost it and regained it without there ever having been anything to notice.

So please explain the conceptual issue underneath all of this. What, if anything, do Case A and Case B preserve in different ways? Work through the candidate diagnoses rather than merely listing them — for instance, that Case B may not be a genuine functional isomorph at all, since a card index covers only finitely many inputs and so the two systems can differ in dispositional depth even where they never differ in actual output; that the requirement of a fine-enough grain in the invariance principle is doing considerably more work than I assumed; that the clerk case is really a challenge to whether there is a single system there at all rather than to whether anything is felt; and that the intuition may be tracking where the boundary of the person lies rather than whether experience is present. Then tell me, as directly as you can, whether the gradualness in gradual replacement carries any independent metaphysical weight beyond functional equivalence, or whether it serves only to make vivid the premise that there could be no detectable switch-point; my engineers suspect the former and I suspect the latter, and I would like to know which of us is mistaking an epistemic premise for a metaphysical one. Close with a single paragraph I could deliver at the end of the talk stating where the two cases genuinely diverge — or stating that they do not.
