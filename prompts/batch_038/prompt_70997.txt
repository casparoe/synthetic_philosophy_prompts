Ok so I've been developing a position about archival silence and I want you to actually attack it — not steelman it and then nod politely at the end.

The position. Take a surviving documentary corpus from a early modern fiscal state — tax assessments, parish registers, court depositions, probate inventories, that sort of thing. The standard move in my corner of the field is to say that the people who are missing from these records are missing because of power: somebody's priorities decided what got written down, so the gaps are themselves evidence about that somebody. I want to push that further and make it bite, because as usually stated it's a mood rather than a method. So here's my claim, in two parts:

1. There is a documentation gradient. The probability that a given individual appears in the record falls off in a smooth, fittable way with their distance from what the recording apparatus cared about — taxable capacity, marriageability, criminality, inheritable property. Not everyone is equally recordable, and the inequality is graded rather than binary.

2. Given that gradient, silence becomes decomposable. You estimate the falloff using parts of the corpus you can check against independent evidence, and then the residual — the people who should have shown up on those terms and didn't — is your signal of active exclusion, as against passive loss (damp, fire, weeding, informal events nobody ever wrote down). Call the two parts together a residual-silence model. The payoff is that being unrecorded because you were poor and being unrecorded because someone suppressed you are in principle distinguishable, as a deviation from a baseline you can fit.

My own material is probate and parish stuff, a couple of English counties, roughly 1660s to 1750s, but treat the question as general — I want to know whether the model works anywhere, not just in my fonds.

So: what would count against it? I want the real map of its exposure, not generic cautions about reading sources critically. Things I already lose sleep over, tell me whether each is fatal or merely annoying:

- the baseline is estimated out of the same corrupted corpus I'm trying to diagnose, so I may just be fitting noise and reading my own priors back out at the end
- survivorship operating at the level of whole series — if an entire category of record was destroyed or never created, my falloff curve is fit to a truncated sample and nothing in the data would tell me so
- the worry that scribal practice is so patchy and so locally idiosyncratic that there is no smooth gradient at all, meaning the noise floor swamps any residual and my signal is an artifact of smoothing
- the possibility that the apparatus recorded for reasons I can't reconstruct, so my "should have appeared" is just a projection of what seems recordable to me

Also, and this is the part I most want you to be straight with me about: what does the residual-silence model predict that its rival does not? The rival being the deflationist line — gaps are basically accidental and unstructured, and whatever pattern a historian finds was brought there by the historian. Is there any conceivable state of the corpus where the two come apart? If there isn't, have I quietly made my view unfalsifiable through qualification, and is that fatal here or tolerable because this is an interpretive framework rather than an empirical theory? I'd rather hear that I've insulated myself than be told I'm rigorous.

And please separate what a defender of the model would themselves concede as counting against it from what they'd wave off as beside the point. I don't want to be attacked as a strawman; I want to know which hits land on the position as actually held.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and say for each roughly what evidence or argument would settle it. Then, at the end, tell me what would change your mind about your own ranking: which objection you'd downgrade if I pushed back well, and where you think your reasoning here is most likely to be wrong.
