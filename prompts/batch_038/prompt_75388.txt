I cover neuroscience and the philosophy of mind for a monthly British ideas magazine, and since the spring I have also been commissioning on the desk, which means four to six pieces a month now pass across my screen in which the evidence consists substantially of what someone says about their own experience: anaesthesia awareness, patients who meet every behavioural criterion for unresponsiveness yet appear to follow commands in a scanner, attempts to decode the content of a subject's dream from imaging data, psychedelic phenomenology, and lately claims about cerebral organoids and about large language models. Two complaints have landed in the last twelve months, and they pull in opposite directions. A philosopher wrote that we had laundered introspective report into evidence about consciousness itself, treating what a subject says as though it settled what a subject has. A clinicians' group wrote that we had done the reverse, producing a piece so heavy with caution about a study of unresponsive patients that ordinary readers took away the impression there was nobody home. I have come to think both complaints are symptoms of a missing distinction rather than of bias, and I want a house protocol that supplies it.

The framework I want it built on is heterophenomenology, and my working understanding of it — which you should correct wherever I have got it wrong — is roughly this: take the subject's report seriously as data; interpret it the way one would interpret any text produced by an agent worth taking at their word provisionally; but refuse at the outset to grant that what the report describes exists, treating the world the subject describes as a theorist's posit rather than a given; and hold neutrality between the possibilities that the subject is accurate, that they are sincere but confabulating, and that they are not sincere at all. What I need is a procedure a reporter with no philosophical training can run on such a study in under an hour, before a commissioning meeting rather than after the piece is written: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops and writes down that something is a fact about what was said rather than a fact about what was experienced, and the points at which it hands the decision to me because the question has turned out to be one of editorial values rather than of method. Please be explicit about which of the distinctions have to survive translation into newsroom language and which can safely be dropped; my own suspicion is that the technical apparatus of the intentional stance can go, while the difference between a report's being sincere and its being accurate absolutely cannot, but tell me if that is the wrong cut. I also want the limits stated rather than smoothed over: what the procedure does with a subject who can produce no text at all, whether its neutrality is genuinely neutral or is a way of assuming from the start that the report and not the experience is the thing to be explained, and how we would defend a verdict reached this way to a researcher trained in one of the disciplined first-person methods that see heterophenomenology as throwing away the datum.

Please run the finished procedure on three cases. The first is a paper reporting that a minority of patients who are behaviourally unresponsive show command-following brain activation when asked to imagine playing tennis or walking through their own house, with no verbal report at any stage: here I want to know whether the method, which was built for texts, should be stretched, adapted into something adjacent, or declared simply inapplicable, and what a reporter is then entitled to write. The second is a study in which a classifier trained on waking viewing is applied to the seconds before subjects are woken from early sleep, reporting that broad object categories can be guessed above chance, with the verbal reports collected retrospectively after waking. The third is a piece I have already commissioned, and it is the one where I expect the protocol to tell me something I would rather not hear: it profiles a laboratory reporting stress-like activity in organoids, and the group leader has said in interview that he cannot rule out that something is felt. I would prefer to learn that from my own procedure than from a letter to the editor. The deliverable should be a numbered procedure with a plain-language rationale for each step, a shortened version suitable for our newsroom wiki, a flag on every step that is a judgement call wearing the clothes of a rule, and a closing section stating what the procedure cannot settle no matter how carefully it is followed. British English throughout, please.
