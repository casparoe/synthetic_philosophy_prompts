I am a software engineer at a mid-size company, with no formal training in philosophy beyond a single undergraduate elective taken years ago. For the past year I have run an informal lunchtime discussion group for colleagues: usually nine to twelve people, nearly all of them engineers or product managers, none of whom have studied philosophy. We meet for seventy minutes in a conference room equipped with a whiteboard, a document camera, index cards, and a box of poker chips I distribute as tokens, and I can supply snacks. I would like your help designing the next session, and I will describe the material as I understand it — please correct me where I have got it wrong, and design around my errors rather than around my phrasing.

The topic concerns a phenomenon I recently read about. Certain patients, typically after a brain injury of a specific kind, or after particular neurosurgical procedures, or under some analgesic drugs, report something striking: they say they can still feel the pain, and can localize it and rate its intensity, but that it no longer bothers them. They recognize the sensation and are more or less indifferent to it. My instinct as an engineer is to describe this as a system with two channels — a sensor channel that detects and localizes a noxious signal, and an alarm channel that makes the signal aversive and drives you to stop it — with the sensor intact and the alarm muted. I understand there is a clinical label for the condition; please keep that label out of everything the participants see, introduce it only at the end, and tell me what it is so I pronounce and use it correctly.

What I want the group to feel is that this is not a neurology curiosity but a wedge into the question of what makes pain bad for the person who has it. If the alarm can be muted while the sensor keeps firing, then either the badness of pain was never part of the sensation itself, or these patients are not in pain in the full sense of the term, or the badness lies somewhere other than the felt character of the experience. I also want them to hit the practical consequence head-on: when a muted-alarm patient and a full-alarm patient both report the same intensity, and there is one dose of relief left, who should get it, and does the answer depend on how you resolved the first question?

Please design the session with the following in mind.

1. The group should arrive at the tension through their own choices rather than through explanation from me. In particular I would like a staged sequence in which they commit publicly, early, to judgments they will later want to revise, and in which the revision is visible to them as it happens.
2. Include at least one element carrying real, if small, stakes. A mild, safe, self-administered discomfort is acceptable to me if you design it carefully: it must be medically innocuous, genuinely optional, and structured so that opting out carries no social cost and requires no explanation. If you judge that a discomfort element is a bad idea, say so plainly and give me a stakes-free substitute with comparable force.
3. Supply a token and allocation mechanism with exact numbers: how many chips each participant starts with, what chips can buy, how the relief pot is divided, and what the arithmetic forces participants to trade off. I want the welfare question to have numbers attached so that disagreement becomes concrete.
4. Give me roles, if any, on cards I can print, plus a full materials list limited to what I described above.
5. Provide a minute-by-minute timeline that fits in seventy minutes.
6. Write a debrief that maps identifiable moments in the activity onto the positions at stake. Describe each position in ordinary language first, and only then give me any standard labels. Do not provide a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and do not attribute wording to any author or text; neither I nor the group can verify anything during the session, and I would much rather have an accurate description than an invented reference.
7. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, and how to handle it — including how the session breaks if a participant has a chronic pain condition, and what to do if someone turns the mild discomfort into a contest.
8. Be frank about which feature of the phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce, and that I will therefore have to supply verbally. In the same spirit, tell me where my sensor-and-alarm framing actively misleads. My worry is that a room full of engineers will find the two-channel model so natural that the metaphor settles the philosophical question for them before they have noticed it was open; tell me specifically how to prevent that, or whether the metaphor should be withheld until the debrief.
