My paper argues that the simulation argument, taken seriously, is an argument against fanaticism about the far future rather than for it. In three weeks I present it in a session on self-location and modelling at an interdisciplinary workshop on simulation across the sciences, and the room is badly suited to the paper in a way I want to prepare for precisely.

Roughly half the audience are philosophers of mind and formal epistemologists, most of them hostile to the simulation argument on the grounds that it smuggles in a typicality assumption and yields no likelihoods. Three are working computational scientists: two climate modellers who spend their days on parameterisations of subgrid processes, and one who builds emulators trained on ensembles of cosmological N-body simulations. They have definite, technical intuitions about what a simulation can and cannot be. The rest are graduate students, several of them sympathetic to longtermism and considerably more fluent in decision theory than in modelling practice.

Here is the thesis they will be attacking. Conditional on our being inside an ancestor simulation: (1) the run is almost certainly instrumental, undertaken for a bounded purpose, and instrumentally motivated computations are terminated, checkpointed, or repurposed once the purpose is served — so the expected remaining runtime of our branch is far shorter than the smooth, indefinitely expanding trajectory the astronomical-value case assumes; (2) fidelity is allocated, not uniform — a run cheap enough to be worth running very many times cannot resolve everything, and where welfare-relevant detail falls in the abstracted band, the putative astronomical number of future persons exists in the run only as an aggregate variable rather than as individuals, so the number driving the fanatic's calculation is not a number of beings; (3) multiplicity therefore cuts against the fanatic: cheap runs are cheap because they are coarse and truncated, and the measure over simulated futures concentrates on exactly those. I then claim the discount survives mixture with the possibility that we are un-simulated, on the grounds that a posthuman civilisation running its own descendant simulations is precisely the sort of agent that truncates them. Conclusion: the value term shrinks by orders of magnitude in the scenarios the simulation argument makes probable, and since the fanatic's case needs that term to be overwhelming rather than merely large, the Pascalian leverage disappears.

What I want is a question-period prep sheet, not a defence of the thesis:

1. The objections this room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do if I handled it badly, each tagged with who raises it and phrased the way that person would phrase it. The modellers' objections should sound like modellers — fidelity, resolution, validation, spin-up, cost, and the difference between simulating a system and emulating its outputs — not like philosophers. The formal epistemologists should sound like people who work in credences and mixtures. Omit anything a general audience would say; if the unfalsifiability line is going to appear here, tell me the specific shape it takes in this room.
2. For each objection, a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds, plus an explicit note on where that reply is soft: what it commits me to, or which premise it quietly trades away.
3. What I should concede in the first five minutes to take predictable attacks off the table, and what I must refuse to concede on pain of losing the thesis.
4. Which of these are the same objection in different clothing. I would rather answer three real problems than eight variants.
5. The single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the session.
6. The question I am hoping nobody asks. I suspect it concerns whether the discount survives being weighted by the probability that we are not simulated at all, but tell me whether that is genuinely the worst thing in the room or whether something worse is sitting there. For that question, give me the least-bad answer rather than a good one, and say how much of the thesis it costs me.

I want the reasoning, not the references: no citations, no reading list, and no attributing any of these moves to a named philosopher.
