I sit on a panel that has to decide whether a certain engineered architecture should be treated as one subject of experience or two: two processing modules that share some memory and some control channels but not others, with no global workspace and no single bottleneck through which reports must pass. Before we argue about the engineering, I want the standard of evaluation fixed by drawing on the human case. Write the desiderata a theory of the unity of consciousness must meet in order to count as adequate for this purpose. Include the commissurotomy phenomena any such theory has to account for — verbal denial of a stimulus that the contralateral hand nevertheless identifies correctly, intermanual conflict in which the two hands work at cross purposes, and the more recent testing in which a patient appears able to respond accurately to either visual field by any route — the distinctions it may not collapse (unity of consciousness versus unity of the subject or agent; binding within a single percept versus coherence across a stretch of time; access to information versus there being something it is like to have it), and any established results it must not contradict. For each requirement, give the consideration that motivates it and label it non-negotiable, genuinely contested, or a parochial preference of one camp. Then do the part I most need: say which requirements survive abstraction away from neural tissue entirely and which are hostage to human implementation, and whether any subset of the surviving ones is jointly unsatisfiable — if the list turns out to generate a contradiction, state that plainly rather than smoothing it over. Omit the scorecard against named theories; we will do that separately.
