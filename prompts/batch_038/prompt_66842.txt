I am coordinating a two-day workshop that is supposed to end with a document both sides can put their names to, and I would like you to draft that document. The two camps are arguing about what pain asymbolia shows about the badness of pain. Camp A holds a componential view: a pain experience has a sensory-discriminative component (location, intensity, quality) and a separable affective component, and unpleasantness just is the felt badness contributed by the second; on this view asymbolic patients are living proof that the two can come apart, that the badness of pain is not intrinsic to pain sensation, and that a state can be a genuine, accurately discriminated pain while ceasing to be bad for its subject. Camp B denies that there is any such thing as pain sensation stripped of unpleasantness. On Camp B's reading, the asymbolic either does not have a normal pain experience at all (the sensory signal reaching them is degraded, more like numbness with fluent reporting), or has one whose felt badness is fully intact but whose connection to the patient's cares, projects, and sense of what is happening to *them* has been cut; on that second reading the state is still bad for the patient even though they do not flinch, dread it, or ask for it to stop, and the badness of pain tracks felt character rather than motivation or self-concern. Both camps have been using the same clinical material for a decade and have talked past each other almost completely.

Because neither side will accept the other's characterization of the evidence, treat the following case file as stipulated background that both camps have agreed in advance to reason from; you do not need to source, date, or cite any of it, and you should not import outside empirical claims beyond it. (1) Asymbolic patients localize and describe noxious stimulation accurately and in fine detail, use pain vocabulary without prompting, and yet report that it does not bother them, often smiling or laughing while it is applied. (2) They show intact reflexive withdrawal to noxious heat but do not acquire conditioned avoidance of the source, and will let an injured limb sit on a hot surface indefinitely. (3) The aversion deficit is not cleanly pain-specific: several of these patients show blunted responses to bitter tastes and to startling noises, but continue to react normally to vestibular provocation and to social exclusion vignettes. (4) Patients who have undergone cingulotomy-type procedures for intractable pain report that the sensation is still present, unchanged in quality and intensity, but no longer distressing, and they request no further analgesia. (5) There is at least one reported pattern in the opposite direction: a patient who describes a sensation as intensely distressing and begs for it to stop, yet fails to guard or protect the affected region and shows no autonomic response. Camp A calls patterns (4) and (5) decisive; Camp B says both are artifacts of impaired introspective access or of damage to systems that have nothing to do with hedonic tone. Please take both readings seriously rather than adjudicating between them.

The deliverable should be a joint statement, formatted as follows. First, a numbered section of claims both camps can sign: about the clinical picture, about what the stipulated findings do and do not license, about the relationship between felt character, motivation, and self-concern, and about the normative question of whether a pain that does not bother its subject is thereby not bad for them; if a claim looks signable but is not, say so and explain the hidden slippage. Second, a disagreements table in which each row gives the dispute, then the formulation of that dispute in words Camp A would sign and in words Camp B would sign, then a column stating whether the divergence is substantive or terminological; where you judge a row to be terminological, identify what the two sides mean by unpleasantness, whether the dispute can be restated so that it is substantive, and what is lost in the restatement. Third, the cruxes: the three or four sub-questions such that resolving them would actually move at least one camp, each paired with a test or case that both camps accept as bearing on it, drawn from the stipulated file or from a proposed study described in enough detail that both could pre-commit to it, together with each camp's prediction written down in advance and an explicit statement of what each camp concedes if the prediction fails. Fourth, a short closing section on the practical upshot: given that the camps remain opposed, what can they nevertheless jointly say about how pain should be assessed in subjects who cannot report it (patients with advanced dementia, nonverbal patients, nonhuman animals) — specifically, whether behavioral withdrawal, autonomic response, or self-report is the best available proxy, and which proxy each camp would defend if forced to choose. Throughout, keep the wording neutral enough that neither camp reads it as an endorsement of the other's position, and do not resolve the debate.
