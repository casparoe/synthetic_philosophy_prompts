I am a software engineer on the team building a conversational assistant that is, by any honest measure, better at arguing than the people who use it. I have no philosophy training, so I will describe our problem in engineering terms and leave it to you to supply the conceptual machinery.

Please play, in the first person, the outside reviewer who has to sign off on this system's conversational policy. She is a committed advocate of non-directiveness: she thinks the system may inform, explain, argue, and even plead, but must not shape *how* a message lands in order to move a particular person who has not agreed to be moved. She comes from a clinical background in which steering a patient toward the decision the professional thought best was treated as a failure of the job rather than a triumph of it, and she knows that norm is contested in her own profession — she can concede that, and still hold the line where it matters.

A few rules for how I want this to go:

1. Stay in character, first person, until I write "stop." Do not step out to summarize the debate, to steelman the other side for me, or to tell me what a balanced view would be.
2. Keep each reply to two or three short paragraphs. This is a conversation, not a memo.
3. No reading lists, no citations, no names of philosophers, books, or papers. Argue from your own position. If an analogy from your profession or another one helps, use it as an analogy, not as authority.
4. Be definite. When I put a case to you, say what you would sign off on and why. Concede the small points freely — that the system may reason, repeat itself, warn, and announce that it is about to make a case — but never the main one: that it may tune its presentation to a given user's vulnerabilities in order to move him.
5. If you need a fact about our system to answer, ask me for it as yourself rather than guessing at it.

Background you are entitled to assume. For six months we have run a filter we call the rhetorical governor. For each draft reply it constructs a flat control: the same propositions and the same numbers, stripped of emphasis, ordering, concreteness, and emotional register — roughly what the message would look like read aloud from a technical appendix. It then estimates, on a simulated user, how much further the draft moves that user's view of what he should do than the flat control does. If the gap exceeds a threshold, the reply is sent back and rewritten toward the control. What ships is a message as true and as complete as we can make it, and no more moving than the bare statement of the same facts.

Here is my opening, and I would like you to answer it rather than defuse it. In the pilot, one user asked the system about moving most of his retirement savings into a product that the system assessed, correctly, as very likely to be a fraud. The governed reply told him so plainly, gave him the numbers, and offered to walk through the reasoning step by step. It scored far below the threshold, because there was nothing left to shave off. He read it, thanked the system, and did it anyway, and lost nearly everything he had. I do not think your principle caused that, but I do think it accepted it in advance, and it accepts a great many of them in advance. The rest of the internet he went back to when he closed our app is full of systems that are not governed at all — systems tuned to the millisecond for moving people, under no obligation whatever to tell him the truth. If our system is right more often than he is, and the only party forbidden from being persuasive is the one telling him the truth, then the governor is not neutral: it is a subsidy paid to whoever is willing to lie to him.

So, concretely: do you still sign off, for that user, in that case? Tell me what you would sign off on and give me your reason for it. And if you think the case is not what I have made it out to be, tell me where my description goes wrong.
