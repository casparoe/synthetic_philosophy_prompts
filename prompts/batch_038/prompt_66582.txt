I attend a monthly reading group on the ethics of war — nine of us, mostly non-specialists, one former military lawyer among us. At last week's session two members, Nora and Simon, got into a sustained argument about the doctrine of the moral equality of combatants: the idea that soldiers on both sides of a war have the same permission to fight, provided they observe the rules governing conduct in war, whatever the justice of their side's cause. I stayed out of it and took notes afterward. I'm reporting the exchange as evenly as I can; I don't want to signal a preference for either of them, so please evaluate the arguments rather than the framing.

1. Nora opened by saying the doctrine cannot be true. Killing a person is permissible only if that person has done something that weakens his right not to be killed. A soldier fighting for an unjust cause threatens people who have done nothing of the kind. So his killings are unjustified, and however scrupulous he is about discrimination and proportionality, that cannot launder the underlying wrong. Her point was that the question whether one may fight at all comes apart from the question how one may fight.

2. Simon accepted the structure but attacked the verdict. He said Nora's view implies that the conscripted nineteen-year-old on the wrong side is, strictly, a murderer — no matter how carefully he fights, and no matter how little he knows about how the war started. He added that our actual practice of prosecuting war crimes and the planning of aggressive war by leaders, rather than mere participation by ordinary soldiers, reflects a sound moral judgment and not merely a political compromise.

3. Nora said this confuses liability with blameworthiness. She offered an analogy: someone who is shoved, drugged, or seized and thereby bears down on you lethally may be entirely blameless, yet most people think you may kill him to save yourself. Blamelessness does not entail that you are not liable. She granted the conscript is largely excused but insisted he is still liable to defensive force, because liability tracks what you are doing to others, not how you came to be doing it or how you feel about it.

4. Simon said the analogy fails in exactly the place that matters. The blameless threat is barely an agent at all — morally speaking, he is a falling object. The conscript is an agent who keeps fighting day after day and who understands that he is trying to kill people who are trying to kill him. More importantly, he said, we usually do not even know that his side's cause is unjust from his perspective: soldiers are fed their own state's account and have no reliable access to the facts about how the war began. He claimed that a lethal threat posed under reasonable ignorance of its own injustice is not an unjust threat in the sense that generates liability, or at least not unjust enough to license killing.

5. Nora answered that whether a threat is unjust is a matter of fact, not of the agent's beliefs. A man who reasonably but falsely believes you are attacking him may be blameless, and you may still defend yourself against him. Excuses bear on punishment, on praise and blame, and on our assessment of him — they do not retroactively confer a right to kill. She pressed the consequence: on Simon's principle, a soldier's liability to be killed would depend on the quality of his government's propaganda, which makes liability hostage to epistemic luck in a way she called arbitrary.

6. Simon then shifted ground. He said he would grant everything Nora claimed about individual acts. But the doctrine is not a claim about individual acts; it is a rule for institutions and for soldiers under radical uncertainty. Tell every soldier that he must first satisfy himself that his state's war is just and you get either armies that cannot fight or — worse — soldiers who take themselves to be licensed against opponents they have judged to be rightless, which is precisely the reasoning that dissolved constraints on conduct in the total wars of the last century. The symmetrical rule is a second-best institution that saves lives, and that is enough to make it morally right in the only sense in which rules can be right.

7. Nora gave three replies. First, that is an argument for a legal convention, not for the moral claim, and if Simon is right the honest description is that the law of armed conflict deliberately enforces a falsehood — a very different position from saying that soldiers really are moral equals. Second, the empirical premise is doubtful: making participation conditional on a just cause raises the political price of starting wars, and in any case the symmetrical permission has not been conspicuously good at restraining conduct, since compliance with the rules of conduct is a separate matter. Third, a rule that has to be taught as though it were true rather than as though it were useful fails a basic publicity test for moral rules.

8. Simon's last move — which Nora began to answer when the session ended, so I have no reply from her — was that Nora's own position, pressed consistently, does not deliver a clean asymmetry either. If liability depends on what each individual is doing rather than on which side he is on, then a just combatant who attacks with disproportionate force, or who kills opponents whose liability does not extend to being killed, acts impermissibly as well. On an act-based view, he said, some killing by soldiers on the unjust side is licensed in a fair range of circumstances and some killing by soldiers on the just side is not, so the side-based asymmetry largely collapses and their practical upshots nearly coincide. He concluded that the claim that the doctrine is false costs Nora more rhetorical energy than it is worth.

Could you adjudicate this properly? Specifically:

- Go through the eight moves in order and rate each one — clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right — with a short justification for each rating rather than a general impression.
- Identify every point at which Nora and Simon are talking past each other rather than disagreeing, and say what the substantive disagreement that remains actually consists in.
- Take a position on move 4 against move 5: can reasonable ignorance about the justice of one's cause defeat liability to defensive killing, or does it only excuse? Say which of them has the better of it and why.
- Evaluate the publicity objection in move 7 on its own terms: is a rule that must be presented as true rather than as useful defective as a moral rule, and does that objection touch Simon's institutional argument or miss it?
- Address move 8 directly. Does an act-based account of liability really erode the asymmetry Nora insists on, or is there something about the difference between resisting justified force and resisting unjustified force that blocks Simon's inference? If Simon is partly right, say how much of Nora's conclusion survives.
- Say what neither of them said that a fair referee would want said.
- End with an overall verdict: who is more right, and about exactly which proposition, since I suspect they are not simply right or wrong about the same thing.
