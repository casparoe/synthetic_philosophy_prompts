I am in the second year of a master's programme in the philosophy of physics, and this term our seminar has been working through the epistemology of scientific reasoning: induction, idealisation, modelling, and, last week, thought experiments. We had two cases on the table — Galileo's argument about two stones of different weight tied together and dropped, and Einstein's adolescent recollection of wondering what he would see if he could ride alongside a beam of light. One of the other students, who came into philosophy from an experimental lab and has little patience for the topic, cut the discussion short with the line that you can't get out more than you put in. His gloss was that a thought experiment can only ever repackage beliefs we already acquired through experience, so the flash of conviction we feel when the stones come apart in imagination is a psychological event rather than an epistemic one, and nothing has been learned about the world that we were not already positioned to know. When our instructor pressed him — asking where the premise that a light beam cannot be overtaken is supposed to come from, if not from the thought experiment itself — he repeated the same sentence and said the premise must have been smuggled in from somewhere empirical, and the hour ran out before anyone pinned him down. He will be away next week, so the seminar is ours to run and I have twenty minutes to say what he was actually committing himself to.

That is what I would like your help with. Could you separate out the distinct, precise claims that a single sentence like this can be made to carry, ranging from the ones that are true because they are empty to the ones that would be genuinely radical if they were true? I have in mind at least the difference between a claim about deductive validity alone and a claim about inference in general, since the latter, if it held, would seem to condemn induction, Bayesian updating, and inference to the best explanation along with Galileo; the difference between a claim about content and a claim about warrant, that is, between saying the conclusion adds no new propositions and saying it adds no new justification for propositions we already had; the stronger reconstructability claim that any respectable thought experiment can be rewritten as an argument whose premises are explicitly empirical, so that the appearance of an a priori gain is an artefact of leaving premises unstated; and the further question, which is where the dispute really seemed to sit, of where the reconstructed premise comes from and whether its source — intuition, conceptual competence, tacit laboratory experience, idealisation — is a route to knowledge about the world at all. For each of these readings, tell me whether it is true, false, or true but beside the point, and whether it would in fact license dismissing the two cases we discussed; then tell me which reading our seminar-mate most plausibly meant, given that he used the very same sentence to answer a question about the origin of a premise. If some readings collapse into each other, say so rather than padding the list.

Please write it as continuous prose, the way you would write a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold type, no tables. I am not after references or a reading list — I want the conceptual geography itself, described precisely enough that I could defend it in a room containing people who believe the philosophy of science is a poor use of an afternoon. And do not hedge on the verdict: if the slogan, on its most natural reading, simply proves too much or confuses a logical point with an epistemological one, I would rather be told that plainly than given a diplomatic summary of the controversy.
