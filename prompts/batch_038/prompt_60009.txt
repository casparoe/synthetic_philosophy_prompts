I'm a second-year political theory PhD student and I'm pitching for our department's small internal grant (six months, part-time around teaching). I need the proposal written up properly. Here's my half-formed idea.

Everybody has an opinion about whether Sweden or Norway is the more liberal country, and both of the standard ways of answering are useless. The index answer points at aggregate scores: the most-cited freedom index in the field currently has them at 99 and 98 out of 100, one point apart, near enough to the ceiling that it cant discriminate, and it gets there by weighting dozens of sub-indicators in a way nobody argues for. The other answer is a list of policy contrasts that cut in opposite directions — Norway keeps a wealth tax Sweden abolished and mandates 40% of each gender on public company boards, while Sweden lets schools run for profit under its voucher system and effectively bans home education except in "exceptional circumstances"; Norway meanwhile puts tight limits on extracting profit from independent schools and lets people home-school under municipal supervision. That list shows the pair isn't ordered on any single dimension, but it doesn't tell you whether that means there's no fact of the matter or just that we haven't looked properly.

What I want to do is treat "X is more liberal than Y" as a comparative claim whose determinacy depends on a specified set of admissible weightings over dimensions, and then work out, for this pair and no other, whether the ranking is stable across all admissible weightings, stable only under some, or unstable everywhere. The wrinkle I keep coming back to: the admissible set can be fixed by the theorist's conception of liberty or by the two political communities' own self-understandings of what counts as freedom, which don't coincide, and I suspect which you pick is what decides the answer. That's the part I most want pressure-tested.

So write the proposal: a precise statement of the question that actually has an answer; why the two existing approaches leave it open; what I'd do month by month over six months; why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives (building a better index, a straight conceptual argument about what liberty is, or more case studies); what a result would look like — a verdict, or a mapped structure of the indeterminacy with the pivot dimension or dimensions identified; and what would count as the project failing. Also one honest line on scope: what would make this too big for six months. And a sentence I can use when someone asks why a political theory department should fund something that looks this close to measurement.

No bibliography, I'll add citations myself, but do name the positions and arguments you're leaning on. Hard cap: 700 words, and keep the month-by-month bit to a few lines.
