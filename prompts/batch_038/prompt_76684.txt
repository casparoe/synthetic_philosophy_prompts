A friend of mine works on interpretability for large language models, and we spent most of last night arguing about whether such models "know" things they decline to say. His position is that evaluation by output systematically understates a model's competence, because training toward acceptable answers distorts what the model would otherwise express, and that the right description is of knowledge that is present latently and merely fails to be elicited. I am a psychometrician; I design and validate instruments for a living, and all evening I had the sense of hearing a measurement problem being announced as a discovery about minds.

Please restate his position, and the standard objections to it, in the vocabulary I actually use: latent traits, indicators and item response functions, local independence, method effects, response distortion and social desirability, differential item functioning, convergent and discriminant validation, construct validity. Translate as faithfully as you can, and do not smuggle my framework's presuppositions in and then credit them to him — in particular, do not silently grant that there is a single respondent possessing a stable trait, or that a respondent's silence is the kind of thing that can be scored. Then give me the residue report: which of his claims translate cleanly, which survive only in distorted form, and which have no expression in measurement theory at all. For each piece of residue, tell me whether it marks a real limitation of my vocabulary or a place where his talk of latent knowledge was doing considerably less work than it sounded.
