I retired four years ago after thirty-three years as a research entomologist, most of them spent designing and evaluating insecticide programmes for a state agricultural experiment station. I have taken up philosophy in retirement, largely because I eventually noticed that my field had never once asked whether any of its work was permissible. The question is no longer academic for me: I sit on a county advisory panel that must choose between two mosquito-control strategies following a handful of locally acquired West Nile cases, and I would like to know what I believe before I cast a vote. I have therefore written down my verdicts on a set of cases and would be grateful for help identifying the principle they add up to.

1. Fogging a neighbourhood with a pyrethroid at dusk on a warm August evening: permissible. The adulticide takes a great many non-target insects, and I know the number is large.
2. Treating the same neighbourhood's storm drains and ditches with a bacterial larvicide that acts on the larval gut of mosquitoes and their close relatives and on little else: clearly permissible, and I would defend it as the better programme.
3. Injecting a systemic insecticide into the trunk of an ash rather than spraying its canopy: permissible, and less objectionable than spraying, even though the tree then contains a toxin lethal to anything that feeds on it.
4. Spraying a crop in full flower at midday, with foragers on it, and losing a third of a neighbouring honeybee colony: impermissible.
5. Destroying a paper-wasp nest under my eaves with a can of spray: permissible. I do it without a thought.
6. Deliberately killing the same number of honeybees in circumstances where they are doing me no harm whatever: impermissible. Cases 5 and 6 have identical methods and identical body counts; my verdicts differ, and I suspect the only difference is that bees are useful to me.
7. Insects struck on a windscreen over a lifetime of driving — by my own crude estimate from counts on my grille, on the order of a hundred thousand: not a moral question at all.
8. Ploughing a field, which destroys soil arthropods, earthworms and the brood of ground-nesting bees on a scale that dwarfs any single spraying: I privately consider this among the worst items here, and I have never heard anyone else treat it as a moral matter.
9. A hundred thousand mosquito larvae in a ditch feels like nothing; a hundred thousand adult bees feels like a catastrophe. I cannot say whether that tracks numbers, sociality, my ability to see the adults as individuals, or my doubt that a larva is the subject of a life at all.
10. Ant bait set out in a kitchen: permissible. Going into the garden and crushing, one at a time by hand, the slugs eating my lettuces: worse. I am aware this inverts the body counts.
11. Releasing radiation-sterilised male insects until a local population collapses: permissible, indeed elegant. I would balk at a chemical that produced the same reproductive collapse by the same route.
12. A gene drive intended to extinguish a malaria vector: the most troubling item on the list — more troubling than the fogging in case 1, which in a single season will end vastly more individual lives.
13. Paying a premium for organic produce on the belief that it kills fewer insects: I examined yield and treatment-frequency data years ago and could not support that belief. My purchasing behaviour did not change.

My own tentative formulation is that the wrong scales with the expected number of lives ended, weighted upward when those deaths are intended rather than merely foreseen, and weighted upward again when some method then available would have ended fewer lives at acceptable cost to the agent. It handles cases 1 to 4 and 7 tolerably. It tells me the gene drive in case 12 is the worst thing I listed; it tells me the hand-crushed slugs in case 10 are a trifle; and it is silent on why 5 and 6 come out differently for any reason other than usefulness.

What I should like is the following. State one principle, or a small number of competing candidates, precisely enough that I could apply it to an actual programme decision, and identify which clause is doing the work in each case and what each clause costs. Say which of my cases motivates each clause. Then apply the principle to cases I have not thought to raise; if on some new case it delivers a verdict you judge plainly wrong, say so, and say whether the principle or the verdict ought to give. Finally, tell me which of my thirteen verdicts cannot be saved. I would rather be told that case 6, or case 9, or case 12 must go than be handed a formula with half a dozen free parameters that can be dialled to fit anything. Please also treat it as an open question whether some of these reports are moral verdicts at all rather than squeamishness in respectable clothing; I do not assume my self-report is reliable.
