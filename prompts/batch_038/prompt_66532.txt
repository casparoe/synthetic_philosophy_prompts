I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and a cluster of stories I've been reporting over the past year has left me with a philosophical problem I can't resolve on my own. I'd like an essay.

The stories: near-universal myopia among young adults in certain East Asian cohorts; the steady downward drift of thresholds for prediabetes and prehypertension, which has moved most older adults in several countries into a diagnostic category that barely existed a generation ago; presbyopia and age-related hearing loss, which nearly everyone acquires and for which nearly no one is counted as ill; and, on the other side of the ledger, the persistent campaigns to have conditions recognized as diseases precisely in order to secure coverage and research money.

What I keep running into in every one of these is a configuration I've come to think of as saturation. The label is my own; I don't know whether the literature has a term for the thing. A condition is a genuine, measurable functional deficit; its cause is environmental and shared rather than individual and unlucky; and it becomes prevalent enough within the reference population that statistical normality flips. The majority is now the impaired group. Any account that ties pathology to departure from statistically typical functioning would then, it seems, have to declare the majority condition normal — and so would have to say that a population ceased to be diseased because everyone in it became so.

I know the naturalist side has resources here. Boorse can relativize the reference class to the young adult, or insist that the baseline is a species design recovered by suitable idealization rather than a poll of the current population, or hold that the universality of an environmental cause is beside the point of whether a function is reduced. And I know normativism looks at first glance like the better home for my cases: if a condition frustrates the goals a person actually has, or if it is what the institution of medicine is organized to treat, saturation shouldn't dissolve it. But I suspect the fit is worse than it appears. In a saturated condition no one is an outlier, no one is socially devalued for it, and often no one experiences anything as wrong — people with presbyopia do not typically report being ill — so a normativist account keyed to lived disadvantage or to social valuation may classify it as non-disease as well, and may do so for the same wrong reason. If that's right, my cases aren't a victory for either side: they're cases where the two theories converge on an answer I'm not confident is correct, which is a different and, I think, more interesting problem.

Here is what I want. Write an essay on whether saturation is a genuine counterexample to naturalist accounts of health and disease — to Boorse's biostatistical theory in particular, though you should say what happens on Wakefield's hybrid version and on any proper-function or environment-relative variant you judge relevant — and whether normativism, in whichever of its forms you choose to press (Canguilhem's line, Nordenfelt's ability-based account, or a social-role or medicalization account), handles it better, worse, or not at all. Set out the strongest defense of each side against these cases, including the defenses that strike me as promising, and give the objections I would press in reply. Then reach a verdict and defend it. I don't want a balanced survey concluding that the debate is deep and the issues subtle. I want to know which account, if either, should govern the question whether a saturated impairment is a disease, and what follows for the cases above. A conditional or restricted verdict is acceptable — something of the form "naturalism survives only at the price of X, and X is worse than the concession normativism must make" would satisfy me — but the essay has to commit.

Two further requirements. First, confront the worry that what I've described is a merely verbal dispute: that disease and health are being asked to do work they were never built for, and that the correct response to saturation is to draw a distinction — functional deficit versus clinical entity, say, or impairment versus disorder — rather than to choose a theory. Take that possibility seriously and say whether it dissolves the problem or dodges it. Second, tell me how confident you are in your verdict and in the main steps leading to it, and single out the one point where you think you are most likely to be wrong or to be misdescribing a philosopher's position. I would far rather have that flagged explicitly than find it later. Please don't pad the essay with a history of the debate or with suggestions for further reading; assume I have read the standard overviews.
