I've spent years reading arguments about tail risk and expected value, and I want to test whether a real regulatory regime is doing the reasoning those arguments assume it is.

Type certification for large transport aircraft sorts every credible systems failure into severity classes and pins a maximum average probability per flight hour to each. A condition classified as catastrophic — one that would prevent continued safe flight and landing, typically involving multiple fatalities — must be shown to be extremely improbable, which the regulators cash out as below one in a billion per flight hour, and there is a separate structural rule that no single failure may be permitted to produce such a condition. The targets are not fungible: an applicant cannot offset a somewhat-too-likely catastrophic condition by making a dozen minor ones safer, nor by demonstrating that the design's expected fatalities are competitive with the fleet's.

What theory of rational choice under risk does this presuppose? The obvious candidate is risk-weighted expected utility — the family of views on which a risk function transforms cumulative probabilities, so that a rational agent may be responsive to the shape of a distribution and not only its mean, with Allais-type preferences as the standard motivation. Reconstruct that reading as precisely as you can, then put pressure on it: does the one-in-a-billion target look like anybody's risk attitude, or is it doing different work altogether — a bright line against regulatory gaming, a claim about what can be known at design time rather than a preference over known gambles, a lexicographic safety constraint, or a rule about who bears the risk and whether they could have consented to it? Say which of these the practice strictly requires and which it merely sits well with, flag anything in the scheme that embarrasses the reading you favour, and say what would have to change if the whole regime were rebuilt around maximising expected fatalities avoided.

Argue from the concepts; no citations, reading lists, or named authorities beyond what I have supplied. British English throughout. End with a single line beginning "Verdict:" naming the account you think best explains the practice, or stating that the practice is underdetermined between them.
