I retired from sedimentology six years back and now I sit on the collections committee of our little university geology museum, and I've been reading philosophy for the first time in my life — the statue-and-the-clay business mostly — and it keeps feeling like our committee has been running that argument for decades without knowing it. Our precedents so far:

- The trilobite block where preparation took off 95% of the rock and left the animal: still the founding specimen, register number unchanged.
- Another block where groundwater dissolved the fossil and left only matrix: deaccessioned, "no specimen."
- A petrwood slab cracked in two, both halves in the same drawer: one specimen, not two.
- The 40-kilo iron meteorite the museum was founded on, cut down to 2 kilos over a century of thin sections, all the offcuts and slices in twelve drawers under the same number: committee said "same rock, just distributed."
- A resin cast made from a mold of that meteorite: an exhibit, not a specimen.
- A geode sawn into slabs in the 1950s, slabs sold off, bought back and glued up again in the case in 2004: recorded as a reconstruction, not the original.

Now a dealer offers us a 900-gram fragment of the same fall. It was cut from our own mass in 1912 and given to another institution, but the paperwork is gone and they can't prove provenance — composition will verify it's our rock, and it hasn't been in our custody since 1912.

So: is it part of our founding specimen, or a new object that happens to be made of the same stuff? Which precedent is the closest analog, what rule have we actually been applying — I'm pretty sure there are at least two that fit everything above and they don't agree here — and how should we minute it if we want to stay consistent with ourselves? And if our record genuinely underdetermines it, say what principle we'd be signing onto either way, plus what further case we could put in front of the committee next to tell those rules apart.
