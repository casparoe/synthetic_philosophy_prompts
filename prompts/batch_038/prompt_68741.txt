For eleven years a weekly current-affairs magazine has run a short column called *Small Obligations*, in which a writer I shall call Rosalind Vane answers readers' letters about the small-scale moral texture of ordinary life: queues, escalators, tipping, shoes at the door, whether to hold a lift. She is walking in the Pyrenees until the end of the month and unreachable by either telephone or email, and the editor has asked me, as her stand-in, to produce an answer that a long-standing reader could not tell apart from one of hers. Two things about her method are worth flagging before I give you her record. She is openly contemptuous of the way her readers theorise — several have written in about social contracts and coordination equilibria, and she has replied, dismissively, that most of the conventions worth caring about solve no problem and coordinate nothing, which is precisely why the standard toolbox keeps missing them. And her vocabulary is entirely home-made: nothing in it corresponds to a term of art in the philosophical literature, so there is nothing for you to look up and I do not want a tour of who has said what about convention. She talks of conventions that are "load-bearing" as against merely decorative ones; she asks whether an opt-out leaves a *residue*, meaning anything somebody else must pay for, clean up, or explain; and she distinguishes *keeping* a convention from *upholding* it, without ever saying how. Her verdicts on cases of this kind, so far as I have been able to catalogues them:

1. Crossing against the red light in central London at three in the morning, dry night, empty street, nobody in sight: not wrong at all. She adds that its illegality is neither here nor there — the law tells you what locals expect, not what you owe them.
2. The same crossing at twenty to nine in the morning outside a primary school, again with no traffic: wrong, and she maintains it is worse than the first case even though the material risk is lower.
3. Cutting into a queue at a Post Office counter with eight people waiting: obviously wrong, argument unnecessary.
4. Sitting on the Tube with a bag on the adjacent seat while the carriage fills: wrong, but she locates the wrong not in the sitting and not in the space taken, rather in what she calls the refusal to notice.
5. Tipping in American restaurants: she tips twenty to twenty-five per cent, considers the institution unjust, and has written that the diner is the only party in the chain who can walk away, so withholding punishes the one person who cannot. She does not tip in Britain and does not regard that as inconsistent.
6. Escalators: standing on the passing side at eight in the morning is wrong; doing the same at midnight on an empty escalator is nothing whatsoever. Abroad, however, she follows the local escalator custom even when it can make no difference to anyone, on the ground that non-compliance there is a small insult offered in public.
7. Shoes off in a host's home in a country where that is expected: she complies even when the host waves her off, but she says a guest who refuses has been rude rather than immoral, and she treats that distinction as doing real work.

The letter I must answer on Thursday runs as follows. The writer is a British postdoctoral researcher on a two-year fixed-term contract at a university in the Low Countries. Every Thursday his department holds a research seminar, and at the end a hat goes round: three to five euros each, which pays for the drinks and nibbles afterwards and for the first glass handed to the speaker. He does not drink and must leave at six to collect his son, so he has never once stayed; since the autumn he has been slipping out before the hat arrives. Last week a senior colleague told him that everyone contributes, including those who do not stay, and that as a foreigner he ought to fall in with what is done here. The postgraduate who organises the round says that if enough people opt out he will stop providing nibbles altogether, and points out that the administrative assistant who lays out the room and clears it afterwards does this outside her contracted hours as a favour. The writer also thinks the whole arrangement faintly ridiculous — the refreshments should come from the departmental budget — and has said so once, without effect. He asks whether he should keep slipping out, contribute and say nothing, contribute while continuing to argue against the practice, or make a point of refusing; and whether his being a visitor, for two years rather than two weeks, changes anything.

I want a settled answer, not a survey. Tell me which rule or rules would account for all seven of Vane's verdicts simultaneously — I have three candidates in mind, I cannot choose between them, and I half suspect she is not running any of them cleanly — and then say what each of those candidates implies about the hat, where exactly they come apart, and which her record most strongly supports. Give me the verdict she would actually deliver to this reader, choosing among his four options if one of them follows, and say whether his being a foreigner on a two-year contract does any independent work in her framework or is simply a red herring handed to him by the colleague. Then construct one further letter she has never received — a single case, yours, in the same register — that would best discriminate between the rival readings of her, and state what she would say about it on each reading. Finally: my own draft tells him to go on slipping out without guilt, on three grounds, namely that the nibbles are a luxury rather than a need, that the colleague's appeal to local practice proves far too much, and that both the organiser and the administrative assistant volunteered for what they now bear. Say plainly whether that draft is consistent with her record or whether I have written my own view into her mouth; I would much rather be contradicted than reassured. Commit to a verdict on the letter and to a reading of Vane, give your reasons for both, and confine your reservations to a brief closing note on where you think your reading is least secure.
