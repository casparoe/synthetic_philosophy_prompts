I sit on the ethics committee of a mid-sized professional association (about 4,000 members, mostly experimental biologists). We vote Thursday on a case that's turned into something else entirely. Quick version:

- Last spring a senior member filed a formal complaint alleging that a postdoc had duplicated gel images across two papers to make a weak result look robust. Our review confirmed it: two panels, same underlying image, flipped and relabelled.
- While assembling the file we found out the complainant has the same problem in three of his own papers from the early 2000s. It surfaced only because a junior colleague ran the images through a screening tool as part of the complaint. He hasn't denied it. He's also spent years publicly campaigning for harsher penalties, including saying that people who do this should lose their grants.
- Our bylaws let any member file a complaint about any other member — no standing requirement at all — but they also let the committee decline a complaint that is "vexatious or brought in bad faith." Half the committee thinks hypocrisy is a species of bad faith and we should dismiss; the other half thinks standing is a moral-philosophy worry with no purchase on a body that just applies rules, and duplication is duplication whoever spotted it.

The postdoc has separately asked us to treat the complainant's record as mitigation — basically, why me and not the guy who reported me.

Draft the decision memo I'll circulate before the vote. It needs a bottom line the committee can actually act on (proceed on the merits, dismiss, or proceed with some modification to the outcome), plus reasoning people on the losing side could respond to. Two specific things I need from it:

First, tell me which diagnosis of the complainant's conduct is doing the work, because I think the two pull in opposite directions. Either he doesn't really accept the norm he's invoking, in which case his complaint is a kind of fraud on us and the bad-faith clause bites — or he accepts it perfectly well and simply failed to live up to it, which is garden-variety weakness and seems to have nothing to do with whether the postdoc's panels are duplicated. We can't see inside his head, so say which of these a rule-applying body is entitled to assume, and what would count as evidence for one over the other.

Second, deal with the worry that's keeping me up, which isn't the moral address stuff but a structural point: if being a hypocrite defeats your right to complain, then the worst-behaved people in a field acquire a veto over complaints against them, since in a field where everyone is a bit dirty they can always find a dirtier accuser or just point sideways — and our jurisdiction would shrink exactly as the field got worse. Tell me whether that's a genuine objection or a slippery slope, and seperately whether the postdoc's selective-enforcement point is the same objection in disguise or a different one that could survive even if the standing talk doesn't.

No introduction, no closing summary, no bibliography or list of further reading — just exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each: (1) the recommendation, (2) which diagnosis you're assuming and why, (3) how the bad-faith clause should be read on these facts, (4) whether the structural objection lands, (5) what we do about the complainant's own papers.
