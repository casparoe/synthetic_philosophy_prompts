I'm a first-year graduate student in cognitive science, and I've just been assigned a chapter from an interdisciplinary handbook on consciousness for the reading group I run with two students from the neuroscience department. The chapter's author is a well-regarded researcher who works on metacognition and confidence judgments rather than on the philosophy of mind, and in the space of one paragraph he sets out the family of views I'm supposed to be defending next term. The paragraph reads:

"Higher-order theories hold that a mental state is conscious just when the subject has a thought about that state; consciousness is what you get when the mind turns its attention inward onto its own operations. On the classic version, what makes my seeing red conscious is that I also think, of myself, that I am seeing red — a further belief about myself, arrived at in the ordinary way beliefs about anything else are arrived at. This buys consciousness at a price nobody should want to pay: on such an account a dog cannot consciously see anything, and neither can an infant, unless it has the concept of seeing. It also makes consciousness hostage to error — if the higher-order thought is all that matters, a thought about a state I am not actually in would acquaint me with something that is not there, and critics have pressed exactly this point. Defenders therefore retreat: the higher-order thought need not itself be conscious, and on the more recent versions it need not actually occur, only be disposed to occur. But once the higher-order state is unconscious, automatic, and merely dispositional, the theory is no longer about thought at all. It has become a first-order account of consciousness with an unfalsifiable component bolted on, and it still has not told us why any of this amounts to something's feeling like anything."

I'd like a claim-by-claim accuracy audit of this paragraph, keyed to its sentences, in which you sort each claim into one of four categories: something a proponent of the theory would endorse as stated; a fair but lossy simplification; a misrepresentation of the theory's own commitments; or a genuine live objection that is being presented as though it were a settled refutation. For the third and fourth categories, say what the theorist actually holds and where the burden of argument lies. I'm particularly interested in three things. First, whether the phrase "turns its attention inward" and the description of the higher-order thought as a belief "arrived at in the ordinary way" are neutral paraphrase or whether they collapse the theory into a different account of higher-order consciousness — if there are two or three distinct versions in circulation (a thought-based one, a perception-of-states one, a dispositional one), tell me which version each of his objections actually targets, and flag every place where he scores a point against one version while writing as though it damaged all of them. Third, whether the closing argument works at all: given that the higher-order state has to be nonconscious to avoid a regress, is describing that as a retreat accurate, and does the move to a dispositional formulation really turn the theory into a first-order account, or is that a different complaint? Please also note where the paragraph's refusal to distinguish phenomenal from access consciousness does real damage, and whether the final remark about feeling is a substantive objection or a restatement of the thing to be explained.
