I have no academic training in philosophy — I run a small IT maintenance business and read for pleasure — but for a couple of years now I have been working through discussions of the proposal to replace the neurons of a living brain, one at a time, with synthetic parts that do exactly the same job, and of whether the being that emerges at the end of that process is the one who went into it.

In those discussions a maxim keeps appearing, usually in roughly the form 'a difference which makes no difference is no difference at all', and it is wielded to end arguments. I have caught it doing at least three separate pieces of work, and I do not believe they are the same claim.

First: someone argues that the replacement cannot have altered the subject's inner life, on the grounds that if it had, the subject would have noticed and said so, and by stipulation the subject reports that everything feels exactly as it did before. Since no difference could ever show up anywhere, there is no difference.

Second: someone argues, apparently in the opposite direction, that asking whether the resulting being is really the original is not a question with an answer, because nothing anyone could ever do would settle it — and a dispute no possible observation could settle is a dispute about words rather than about the world.

Third: someone grants that there might be a fact of the matter, perhaps even that the original has ceased and an imposter has carried on, and then observes that this would change no decision anyone should make, so there is nothing worth arguing about.

Could you take the maxim apart for me? What I want is a list I can keep and return to, setting out the distinct precise theses that one sentence could be standing for. I expect there are metaphysical versions, versions about what it takes for a question to be a real question, versions about evidence, and versions about practical relevance, and I suspect some of them shade into one another. For each, please state it precisely enough that I could see what would refute it; say whether it is trivially true, substantive but defensible, or simply false; and say whether it licenses the first, the second or the third use above, or none of them.

Three things matter to me especially. Is there any single reading that supports both the first and the second use, when they seem to point opposite ways — one of them says the inner life is settled by what could in principle be observed, the other says a whole question is empty? If a reading is strong enough to do that work, what else does it commit its user to: does it also settle whether other people are conscious, or whether someone under general anaesthesia has experiences that are merely never reported, and would the user welcome those consequences or be embarrassed by them? And which reading do people actually intend when they reach for the maxim mid-argument, as distinct from the reading their argument would need?

I am not looking for reassurance that the slogan is vague; I want the separate claims separated. Please write your answer in British English.
