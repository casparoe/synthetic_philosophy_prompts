For thirty-five years I worked as a hydrogeologist, chiefly assessing groundwater reserves for irrigation districts, and I have come to philosophy of science only since retiring. My reading has been desultory, so I hope you will forgive an outsider's framing of what follows.

Last month, at a dinner given by my old department, two people I know well argued for the better part of an hour about the Simon–Ehrlich wager, and I could not referee the exchange then and cannot referee it now. The disputants were Martin, a retired commodity economist, and Iris, a population ecologist who is still working. Neither is a fool, and both made points I could not answer. I give the argument as I remember it, in order, and I would like a verdict move by move. I do not need the Malthusian doctrine itself explained to me; I know the geometrical-ratio business.

The facts as I have them, in case they bear on the analysis. In 1980 the biologist Paul Ehrlich and the economist Julian Simon settled a wager, formalised on 29 September 1980, on the inflation-adjusted price of five metals — chromium, copper, nickel, tin and tungsten — with a notional two hundred dollars staked on each and the payoff date ten years out. If the basket had risen, Simon paid; if it had fallen, Ehrlich paid. World population grew by more than 800 million over that decade, the largest such increase to that point, all five metals fell in real terms, and in October 1990 Ehrlich sent Simon a check for $576.07. Simon afterwards offered a rematch at far higher stakes on any commodities and any timeframe Ehrlich cared to name; Ehrlich's side came back instead with a list of climate, cropland and pollution variables to bet on, and the two never agreed terms.

(1) Martin opened by saying this is falsification working exactly as intended. The Malthusian claim is that population pressure makes resources scarcer; over the test decade population grew faster than ever before and the scarcity indicator fell for all five commodities, so the claim failed on its own chosen ground, and the loser paid up.

(2) Iris replied that the wager never tested Malthus. The market price of five traded industrial metals measures what a purchaser pays, not what a system has left: it excludes everything without a market — soil depth, freshwater, fisheries, forest cover, the stability of the climate — and it can fall through a glut or a recession for reasons that say nothing about abundance. She added that Ehrlich himself later wrote that he and his colleagues would have preferred indicators drawn from renewable resources, and went along with Simon's terms anyway. On her account what was refuted was a proposition about metal prices, not one about carrying capacity.

(3) Martin said this is precisely the immunising strategy that renders a theory unscientific. If any failure may be charged to the indicator, no outcome is forbidden; and the asymmetry is telling, since he doubted Iris would have called the indicator inadequate had prices risen.

(4) Iris said the "right but early" reply is not a dodge but a structural feature of this class of theory: a growing population draws down a finite stock while substitution and new discoveries keep postponing the binding constraint, so the theory predicts eventual exhaustion without predicting the decade in which it arrives. She compared it to a fault that seismologists say will rupture eventually — nobody thinks ten quiet years refute them.

(5) Martin said the comparison fails because seismology measures how far along the process is, through strain on a named fault and recorded seismicity, so its "eventually" is disciplined by evidence about the clock, whereas Iris's formulation supplies no comparable state variable and therefore no way to tell whether the buffer is nearly empty or nearly full.

(6) Iris then cited a study that replayed the wager across every decade-long window since 1900 and found the Simon side winning in barely two-fifths of them, with 1980–1990 among the very worst windows for her side; she also noted that if the same five metals are run from 1980 out to 2011, Simon loses on four of the five. A verdict that reverses when the endpoints shift by twenty years, she said, was never a verdict.

My own contribution, such as it is. In groundwater work we distinguished a forecast that got the mechanism right and the timing wrong from one that got the mechanism wrong. We treated the first as a calibration failure and kept using it; we discarded the second. But the discipline was that you had to be able to point at the thing that caused the delay — an unmodelled recharge event, a leaking confining bed — and measure it. I do not know whether that transfers here, or whether it quietly settles the argument for Martin before the argument begins.

What I should like from you is this. Rate each of the six moves, and my own remark, on a scale from −2 (clearly mistaken) to +2 (clearly right), half-points permitted, with a sentence or two justifying each rating. Say who is right about which particular point, rather than only who prevailed overall. Say what both of them missed — including whether any terms at all could have made this a genuine test of the Malthusian claim rather than of a proxy for it, and if not, what follows from that. Where a recognised piece of philosophical machinery genuinely applies to a move — the difficulty of testing a theory only ever in harness with its auxiliary assumptions, the line between an ad hoc rescue and a legitimate revision of those auxiliaries, the progressive-versus-degenerating way of judging a research programme, or any criterion you know of for when a test is severe enough to count — name it and say what it implies there; where it does not apply, say so rather than reaching for it.

Finally, please mark clearly which parts of your assessment are settled among people who work on this and which are genuinely contested, and do not let a contested position travel as consensus. I am new enough to the literature that I cannot tell the difference unaided, and I would much rather be shown where the disagreement lies than handed a confident verdict I have no means of checking.
