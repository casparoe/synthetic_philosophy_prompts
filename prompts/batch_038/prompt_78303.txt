So, quick favor. I write crime novels — seven of them now — and my recurring character is a retired epistemology professor who does the whole consulting-detective thing and will not stop talking about Bayes. She's supposed to be the funny one, but I've been writing her jokes myself and theyve gotten thin.

The scene I need help with: back in chapter two she announces, loudly, in front of the entire incident room, that the killer is left-handed, drove a panel van, and got in through the conservatory. She has known all three of those facts the whole book. She has said them out loud maybe nine times. Then in chapter nineteen her long-suffering ex-student, now a sergeant, finally turns up with a theory that explains all three cleanly and elegantly, in a way nobody else saw coming. Everyone claps. She doesn't move an inch, and that's where the joke has to land.

The bit needs to run on the old-evidence problem in Bayesian confirmation theory — the thing where, if you already knew E, your probability for E is 1, and conditionalizing on something you already assigned probability 1 can't raise your credence in any hypothesis that entails it. So a gorgeous theory that "saves" everything she already knew is worth, formally, exactly nothing. (Some people patch the problem by saying what she genuinely learns here isn't the evidence but the fact that his theory entails it — you're welcome to use that route instead, it just makes the joke even more inside-baseball.)

Please play the role of a comedy writer who did a PhD in philosophy of science and has strong opinions about both setups and priors, and think it through properly instead of free-associating. Start with a short brainstorm: three or four different joke structures the scene could take, a line each, and flag which ones actually depend on the logic rather than just on her being insufferible. Then write the winner out as dialogue with a stage direction or two, under 200 words.

Constraints, and I do mean them: no "a Bayesian walks into a bar," no shrugging "well, statistically speaking," and the punchline has to fall apart if you swap her for any ordinary detective. Don't name-drop papers or hand me a reading list — I just need lines an actor can say.
