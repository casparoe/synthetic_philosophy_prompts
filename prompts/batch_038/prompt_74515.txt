I am one of two TAs marking the end-of-term exam for a second-year philosophy of mind course; the scripts are anonymised and I want to return a mark plus comments that would stand up if the instructor re-read the paper. The question the students were set is below, followed by one script. I have no view I want to put into the comments beyond what the marking warrants.

**Question (25 marks).** Commissurotomy patients appear, in ordinary life, to be single unified agents, yet under experimental conditions they sometimes behave as though what one hemisphere has been shown is unavailable to the other. Explain what these findings are supposed to show about the unity of consciousness, and assess the inference from them to the claim that such a patient has two streams of consciousness. Say what would have to be true for that claim to hold, and what could count against it.

**Script.** Because the corpus callosum is severed, a commissurotomy patient's hemispheres receive no information from one another at all. Visual input is crossed, so a picture flashed briefly to the left of fixation goes to the right hemisphere and one flashed to the right goes to the left. In an intact brain the two sides pool what they see; in the split-brain patient they cannot, so the left hemisphere is ignorant of what the right has been shown. Since speech is produced by the left hemisphere, the patient reports seeing nothing even while the left hand, controlled by the right hemisphere, picks out the object he has just denied seeing. Patients have likewise carried out commands presented only to the right hemisphere while insisting no command was given, and, asked to explain such behaviour, the speaking hemisphere invents a plausible reason on the spot. These confabulations are direct evidence that each hemisphere has a mind of its own.

Two streams of consciousness therefore follow. Each hemisphere has experiences the other cannot share, and having experiences is what makes something a subject of experience, so the patient is two subjects. Ordinary life seems to contradict this: the patients drive, work and behave as single people, and the doubling appears only when the experimenter controls where the patient is looking. But that shows the disconnection to be an artifact of the apparatus rather than a fact about the patients' minds; had two streams really been there, the effects would have shown up in everyday behaviour. So the work leaves the unity of consciousness in normal subjects untouched — parallel processing by two hemispheres no more makes two minds in a patient than it does in an intact brain — while establishing, as the neuroscience of commissurotomy now agrees, that the patient himself has two conscious minds.
