Construct a thought experiment designed to put Descartes' denial of thought to animals under maximal pressure, to the following specifications. He offers two tests for telling a real human being from a beast or an automaton: the ability to arrange words or other signs in various ways so as to declare one's thoughts to others, and a capacity to respond that is not tied to any particular disposition of the organs but adapts to whatever situation arises; and against talking birds and trained apes he holds a fallback — that animal sign use is always wrung out by passion or appetite, never freely deployed to express a thought. Give me a single case, of no more than a paragraph, in which an ordinary flesh-and-blood animal, with no machinery and no supernatural assistance, satisfies both tests while that fallback is specifically neutralised: it signs about things that hold no interest for it, declines the reward its training is supposed to be chasing, refers to what is absent, and at least once uses a sign in order to mislead. Do not stipulate that the animal thinks or has a soul; the pressure must be generated by the description rather than asserted. Then demonstrate that the design works: mark each specified feature in the case, state what a Cartesian can still say in reply and which of his commitments is bearing the weight of that reply, and identify the single feature whose removal collapses the case back into an ordinary animal-training anecdote, as against those whose removal leaves it intact. Close with the strongest objection available to a defender of the beast-machine doctrine against the experiment itself — for instance, that no finite record of performances could ever establish that the first test has been met. Please write your answer in British English.
