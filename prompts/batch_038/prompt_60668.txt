I'm in my second year of a philosophy PhD, working through the debate over whether an agent may rationally weight probabilities non-linearly — the family of views usually grouped as risk-weighted expected utility, and the orthodox replies to them. What has caught my attention is not the substance of any single exchange but a shape that the orthodox reply keeps taking, and I don't have a name for it.

Instance one. Someone notes that an agent prefers a sure fifty dollars to a fifty-fifty chance of zero or a hundred, and claims this violates the theory. The reply is that the options were mis-specified: once the description of what the agent receives on each branch is made to include further features — the absence of a lingering worry, the fact of having taken the safe route, the character of the prospect as she lives it — the gamble and the sure amount are no longer the same prize, and the preference conforms to the theory after all.

Instance two. The same shape appears with an agent who prefers a bet on a ball drawn from an urn with known proportions to an equally staked bet on an urn with unknown proportions. The reply is again not that the agent is confused, but that the states were drawn too coarsely: enrich them until her ignorance is itself part of the situation she faces, and the supposedly deviant preference becomes an ordinary one.

Instance three, briefly. An agent who cares about how a fixed sum is distributed across people rather than only about the total is told that she does not value the sum at all, but rather a richer object whose description includes the distribution — whereupon the theory that only totals matter accommodates her perfectly.

In every case the theory itself is left untouched. No new clause, no restriction of scope, no added parameter. What is rewritten is the case, and the rewriting is always available, because there is no evident limit to how finely a prize or a state can be described. The upshot is that no preference pattern whatever could embarrass the theory, and yet nobody I read treats that as a refutation.

I know the general neighbourhood: theories shielded from counterexamples by adjustments tailored to the purpose, the Popperian complaints about that, the no-true-Scotsman pattern. But none of these seems to fit, since nothing is added to the theory and no claim about essences is made; the theoretical content stays fixed while the description of the data changes. I also recall — possibly wrongly — that the originator of the expected-utility framework took it to be meant only for unusually tidy, small decision problems, and thought extending it beyond such settings indefensible; I'd like to know whether that bears on the pattern above.

So, four questions. First, does this move have an established name, and if so is the name attached to the defensive reply itself or to the more general worry that the framework goes vacuous once outcomes may be individuated arbitrarily finely? Second, what are the nearest concepts, and where precisely does the thing I've described come apart from each — I want the carving, not a label. Third, is there any principled criterion for when a finer specification of the prizes is legitimate rather than evasive, and if there is, does meeting it restore the theory's content or only relocate the difficulty? Fourth, if no established concept covers the meta-pattern — the universal availability of the reply and what that does to the dialectic — then coin one, define it, and say which nearby cases fall inside it and which fall outside.

I can't check sources, so please give me no references and no reading list; your own reconstruction is what I want. And please say how confident you are in the identifications you make, and single out the one point in your answer you are least sure of.
