I retired five years ago after 38 years in analytical chemistry — most of it spent arguing with colleagues about detection limits and what counts as a measurement — and lately I've been filling the hours by reading philosophy, slowly and probably badly. My wife's book group lets me tag along, and last month we worked through one of those fat popular surveys of modern political thought, the kind that gets you from Hobbes to the present day in four hundred pages with a lot of confidence and very few footnotes. The Rawls chapter is the one that stuck in my craw. I typed up the main stretch of it so I wouldn't have to haul the book home again; here it is:

"Rawls invites you to imagine choosing the basic rules of your society from behind a veil of ignorance: you don't know whether you'll be rich or poor, gifted or dull, healthy or sickly, or even what you'll care about most in life. You might end up as the very worst-off person in the society you're designing. Now, when the stakes are that high and you have no idea what the odds are, a rational chooser becomes extremely cautious — nobody gambles their entire life on a lottery ticket. So the parties agree to make the worst-off position as good as it can possibly be. That's the difference principle: inequality is permitted, and indeed encouraged, so long as it leaves the least advantaged better off than strict equality would. Note what this means. Rawls is not an egalitarian in any serious sense; he's a defender of a market order with a floor under it, and the height of the floor is set by whatever incentives the talented happen to need. He measures how well off people are by their welfare or happiness, so the difference principle is at bottom a claim that the poor should be happier. And it applies to everything: every time a government weighs a policy — a tax change, a trade deal — the test is whether the poorest gained from it. This is why Rawls is called the philosopher of the welfare state, and why libertarians and socialists both reject him. The fatal objection, which Rawls understood perfectly well, is that if you let the parties behind the veil have any information about probabilities, they simply maximize expected utility and the difference principle evaporates. He got his result only by defining uncertainty out of existence, which is rigging the game rather than arguing with your opponents."

Can you go through this claim by claim and tell me which sentences I can trust? I'd like the audit sorted into what a Rawls scholar would happily sign, what's a forgivable popular simplification that doesn't change anything, and what's actually wrong or a conflation of two things Rawls kept apart — I have a suspicion the bit about happiness and the bit about every individual policy are each sliding over something, but I can't say what. Two specific things I want pinned down: first, someone in the group mentioned that a Hungarian-born economist ran the same veil-of-ignorance trick and got a utilitarian answer out of it, so is that a real result, and does the passage's claim that Rawls's system "evaporates" once you allow probabilities actually follow from it, or is it more subtle than that? Second, I keep coming back to the risk stuff. In the lab and in instrument design we routinely engineer to the worst case instead of the expected case, and that instinct made the maximin move feel obviously sane to me — so tell me wether that's a genuine parallel I can lean on, or whether I'm reading my own habits into his argument and missing the specific conditions he thinks are needed to make it work. I'm not after a defense or a demolition of Rawls, just a clean list of what survives in that paragraph once it's corrected, and whether the book's conclusion — that he isn't really an egalitarian and that the whole thing collapses under a standard objection — still stands after you fix the errors.
