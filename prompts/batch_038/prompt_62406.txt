For the better part of two decades I have read and argued on rationalist blogs and forums, where the native idiom for questions like mine is Pareto improvements, threat points, and transaction costs. In three weeks I give a thirty-minute talk with twenty minutes of questions at an interdisciplinary philosophy–politics–economics workshop. The room is mostly political philosophers — two Kantians, a contractarian who works on legitimacy, several people who write about reasonable pluralism and the limits of justification to those who can reasonably reject it — plus two economists who do formal bargaining and mechanism design and one game theorist who lets nothing slide. Nobody there works on moral uncertainty, and the two or three who know the effective-altruism literature are friendly but will press hard on representation. They will hear the idiom of gains from trade and immediately start looking for what it hides.

The thesis is that moral compromise between value systems should be analyzed as a Coasean trade in control rights over conduct, not as a second-best aggregation performed under uncertainty about which axiology is true. My argument, roughly:

1. Each of two agents is fully certain that their own value system is correct; no moral uncertainty is presupposed anywhere in the account.
2. Suppressing the other system's demands costs resources — enforcement, monitoring, the forfeiture of the other party's future cooperation, the destruction of the information their practice generates — and those costs are denominated in the suppressor's own currency.
3. Hence it is possible, and I claim common, that each agent does better by their own lights under a reciprocal waiver of enforcement than under unilateral imposition, even while each regards the other's values as simply false.
4. Because such waivers are positive-sum on each party's own scale, and because declining them forgoes value the party could otherwise have had, agents are under a pro tanto obligation to seek compromises of this kind, not merely permitted to accept them.
5. The trade requires no interpersonal comparison of worth and no cardinal commensurability across systems — only each party's ordinal ranking of their own available options.

Please prepare a question-period sheet for me rather than a defense of the thesis. I want the eight or nine objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do to the paper if left standing at the end of the session, and each phrased in the vocabulary its raiser would use — the Kantian's, the contractarian's, the bargaining theorist's, the Williams-ian's — rather than in mine; flag which come from the philosophers and which from the formal people, since I need to know which half of the room I am answering at any moment. For each objection give a reply deliverable in about two minutes, an explicit note on where that reply is soft, question-begging, or dependent on a premise I have not earned, and a recommendation on whether to concede it up front during the talk in order to defuse it. Then tell me three further things: which single reply has to land for the thesis to survive the afternoon; which objection I should pray nobody raises, because I have no good answer to it; and exactly where the economists will say I have smuggled in an initial allocation of entitlements that is doing all of the work I claimed the trade could do without one. Write the entire sheet in American English.
