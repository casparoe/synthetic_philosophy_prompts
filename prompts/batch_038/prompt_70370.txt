I am auditing a seminar on the philosophy of international criminal law, and I have spent the last few weeks trying to pin down my own intuitions about the extension of the word *genocide* by going case by case. The background I keep in mind is that Lemkin's own 1944 coinage was much broader than the concept that survived into the 1948 convention: he described genocide as a coordinated plan of different actions aimed at destroying the essential foundations of the life of national groups, listing among its objectives the disintegration of political and social institutions, of culture, language, national feeling, religion, and economic existence. The treaty narrowed this in two ways at once — it restricted the protected groups to national, ethnic, racial, and religious ones, leaving political groups out, and it restricted the prohibited conduct to a list of five acts (killing members of the group; causing serious bodily or mental harm; deliberately inflicting conditions of life calculated to bring about the group's physical destruction; imposing measures intended to prevent births; and forcibly transferring children of the group to another group), all of which must be committed with intent to destroy the group in whole or in part. My gut verdicts track neither the coinage nor the treaty, and I suspect they track something else that I have not been able to name.

Here are the cases and my verdicts. **(A)** A colonial administration removes the children of a conquered group and raises them in households of the dominant group; the children are fed, clothed, schooled, and not beaten, and within two generations the group no longer reproduces itself as a group — genocide, clearly. **(B)** A state bans a minority's language in schools and courts, bulldozes its cemeteries and shrines, prohibits the group's name in official use, and pays bonuses for intermarriage — but every living member is healthy, prosperous, and legally equal, and their children adopt the dominant culture without coercion: not genocide, and yet the end state is the same as in (A), so I cannot say what is doing the work in my verdict except the presence or absence of force against the children. **(C)** An army besieges a city whose population is overwhelmingly of one group, cuts food, water, and power, and shells relief convoys in order to break the government's will to negotiate; tens of thousands starve: genocide, because the group's presence is why that city was chosen — but my verdict flips if the city is demographically mixed and everything else is identical, and I dislike hanging so much on that. **(D)** A central government sets grain requisition quotas for one national region knowing they will produce mass starvation; the aim is industrialization and the breaking of regional resistance, and the disappearance of that nation is foreseen, and welcomed by some ministers as a bonus, but is not what the quotas are for: I want to say genocide, while knowing that the intent clause exists precisely to exclude this. **(E)** Over three generations, ordinary migration, land purchase, road-building, and a state school system reduce a group from a regional majority to a scattered remnant; no law commands it, no single agent intends it, and most participants would sincerely deny it: not genocide — though in bodies and in the group's disappearance this exceeds most of the cases I do so label, and I am uncomfortable that my intuitions exempt the diffuse and indict the concentrated. **(F)** A regime identifies, list by list, and kills the membership of an opposition party and its sympathizers — hundreds of thousands: not genocide, because the victims are defined politically rather than nationally, ethnically, racially, or religiously; but I think this is the treaty's most indefensible narrowing, and I cannot decide whether my considered view is that it *is* genocide or that it is something exactly as grave under a different name. **(G)** A public health program sterilizes members of a group who have already had children; nobody dies, nobody is beaten, and the group's living members are otherwise well treated: genocide, because the group is being ended — though my verdict would flip if the same program were applied only to those who would have had no children anyway, which makes the entire difference counterfactual. **(H)** A decade-long anti-narcotics campaign kills, in one ethnic neighborhood, a number of young men comparable to a small war's toll; the state's aim is suppression of the trade and the ethnic concentration of the dead is incidental: not genocide, and this is my most confident verdict — which worries me, since whatever account I give of (D) looks likely to sweep it in.

What I would like is a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it mechanically to a new case. For each clause, tell me which of the verdicts above it exists to capture; be explicit about what does the work in the (A)/(B) pair and in the (C) pair, since those are the ones I would least like to lose; and say honestly which of my verdicts your account cannot save, rather than contorting it to save all of them. Then apply it to three cases I have not listed: a state that offers every member of a group a substantial payment, preferential access to housing and civil service posts, and full legal equality in exchange for ceasing to identify as a member, with the result that 97 percent accept within two generations — no coercion, no harm, group gone; a group whose own elected leaders sign a treaty agreeing to dispersal and intermarriage in return for security guarantees, and whose descendants are absorbed; and a state that forms a firm intention to destroy a group, mobilizes to do so, and is stopped before killing anyone — where I want to know whether a failed attempt is genocide, and whether your answer changes depending on whether you are doing moral philosophy or law. Finally, I would like your view on a suspicion of mine: that the reason any of this feels urgent is not metaphysical but political, since the label carries obligations, funding, and armies with it. If that is right, tell me which of the verdicts above you think are contaminated by it — and whether a moral concept of genocide can be specified at all without smuggling in the practical consequences of naming.
