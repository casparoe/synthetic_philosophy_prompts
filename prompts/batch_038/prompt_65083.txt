For most of the last decade my beat has been water: drought, allocation, and the legal machinery that decides who gets what when there is not enough. A pattern has recurred often enough across my notebooks that I have come to suspect it is a feature of the world rather than an artifact of the beat, but I have no name for it, and I no longer trust my own paraphrases of it. I am preparing a long piece and would rather get the concept right than get a good sentence out of it.

Here is what I keep seeing. A shock arrives — a five-year drought, a flood that takes out a diversion dam, a court ruling, a municipal bankruptcy — and it is genuinely disruptive in the short term: crops fail, a city comes within weeks of rationing by neighborhood, a government falls. Then the response is organized, and the response runs through channels that were already there. Emergency allocation follows the seniority order fixed in a water code written more than a century ago. Rebuilding money is distributed parcel by parcel along the very property lines that put those parcels in the floodplain. The new agency created to prevent a recurrence inherits the boundaries, the constituencies, and the budget logic of the body it replaced. A decade later the situation is worse or better in degree, but the arrangement that determined who was exposed in the first place is not merely still standing; it is larger, better funded, and better defended, and the shock has become part of its justification.

Two features of this are why I am asking rather than writing around it.

First, it is not simply the familiar observation that events float on top of slow-moving conditions. I have read enough history to know that terrain: the Annales school, Braudel's comparison of events to foam on the surface of much longer movements, the opening section of his Mediterranean book, which is a history so slow it barely seems to be history at all. What I am describing does not look like that to me. The layer I keep running into is not slow because it is massive and inert, the way a coastline or a rainfall regime is slow. It is slow because it digests disturbance: each shock is metabolized into material that reinforces it. Its stability is an accomplishment rather than a brute fact, and it is indistinguishable from plain inertia until you find the record of how many times it has been struck.

Second, I do not think it is what economists have in mind when they describe an early contingent choice becoming locked in, because in my cases there is rarely a fork that might have gone the other way. The layer appears to have been absorbing shocks for as long as anyone has kept records, and to have been doing so more successfully the longer it has existed.

So: does this have a name? I would like the nearest established concepts identified — in historiography if any exist there, though I suspect the more developed treatments are in ecology, systems thinking, or the sociology of organizations — and I want them distinguished from one another and from what I have described, not merely listed. Tell me where my description is only redrawing a line the field already draws, and where it is doing something those concepts do not do. In particular, I would like a clear verdict on whether the difference between slowness as inertia and slowness as the absorption of disturbance is one the literature takes seriously or one it collapses. If nothing fits cleanly, say so plainly and give me a term with a definition tight enough to be falsifiable, along with the boundary cases: what would look exactly like this and not be it.

The label matters less to me than the carving. What I need to know is whether this is an analytical resource I can lean on across a whole piece, or a turn of phrase I have fallen in love with.
