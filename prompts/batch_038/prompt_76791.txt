I spent thirty-eight years in sensory neurophysiology, most of it recording from nociceptive pathways in the spinal dorsal horn, and I retired four years ago. Philosophy is a late acquisition for me, and for the past year I have been working steadily through the literature on pain and its unpleasantness — in particular the extraordinary amount of philosophical work that has been built on the syndrome of pain asymbolia. I want a diagnosis of that debate, not an exposition of it, and I would like you to treat me as a careful reader who has done the reading but lacks the field's sense of which knots are real.

Here is the dispute as I understand it. People with pain asymbolia, first described by Schilder and Stengel nearly a century ago and given its modern anatomical framing in a 1988 case series of six patients with unilateral hemispheric damage, will localize a noxious stimulus, name its quality, and often scale its intensity, while showing little or no withdrawal and little or no distress; some smile, and some offer another limb for further stimulation. Philosophers have made this the crucial case for the structure of pain experience and for the badness of pain. Grahek's reading is that these patients genuinely feel pain without painfulness: the sensory-discriminative dimension survives, the affective-motivational one is disrupted. Klein, and Gerrans after him, locate the missing element one step up — not the felt badness but the caring about it. Bain takes over Klein's suggestion and argues that both the painfulness and the caring are absent, in service of an account on which unpleasantness is a matter of the experience representing a bodily condition as bad. The imperativist line treats unpleasantness as a kind of command to stop protecting nothing and instead to stop doing this, and has to explain the asymbolic either as failing to receive the command or as receiving it without purchase, on the ground that he no longer cares about his body. Others soften all of this into a matter of degree: the pain is unpleasant, but drastically less so. And now there is an aggressively deflationary challenge, argued in a philosophy of science journal, holding that the clinical record does not license any of these readings, that talk of a pain with one component subtracted commits a mereological error, that the reading collapses into the subjectivism it was meant to avoid, and that asymbolia is not a syndrome at all but a symptom scattered across several distinct conditions — a case study that should be struck from the arsenal entirely.

Three things about this record trouble me, and I want them taken seriously rather than smoothed over. First, the empirical base is absurdly thin for the structural conclusions drawn on it: a small case series, plus scattered reports over ninety-odd years, with etiologies running from stroke to tumor to trauma to prefrontal surgery to dementia. Second, everything we actually have is behavior and verbal report, and the same territory that is damaged in these patients — insular, anterior cingulate, parietal opercular — is territory that any physiologist would expect to matter for interoceptive awareness and for the affective vocabulary in which a patient describes his own state. So the dissociation may sit in the reporting channel rather than in the experience, and I do not see that anyone has argued otherwise rather than assumed it. Third, and this is my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: asymbolia has become evidentially inert. The same six-odd patients are claimed as decisive by evaluativists, by imperativists, by those who think the affect survives but the caring does not, by those who think everything is merely attenuated, and by skeptics who think there is no pain there at all. A body of observation that every camp can absorb is not discriminating among their theories, and I suspect that is the real reason nobody moves.

So: what kind of disagreement is this? I want you to say whether it is a substantive dispute about the structure of experience; a verbal one, in which "unpleasantness" is doing at least four different jobs — a phenomenal quality, a representational content, a motivational disposition, a second-order liking or disliking — and the parties are talking past one another; a dispute about standards of evidence, over what counts as a genuine dissociation and what a bedside case report can be made to carry; or a hidden empirical question about whether asymbolia names one condition or several, which if answered would dissolve much of the rest. Tell me whether the camps are in fact answering different questions under a single heading — I have in mind especially the slide from what pain experience is composed of to what makes pain bad, which looks to me like a change of subject that nobody has licensed. And tell me whether the impasse suggests the question is ill-posed, as the mereological charge alleges, or merely hard.

Then give me the second half: what would count as progress on your diagnosis. I am interested in whether any observation could break the tie — for instance a properly specified double dissociation, or the specificity control that seems to me never to have been run, namely whether these patients remain averse to other unpleasant things such as noise, bitter taste, disgust elicitors, and threatening gestures — or whether progress requires something non-empirical instead: a stipulated minimal case description that all parties agree to argue about, a shift from component analysis to graded psychophysics, or a formal account of how the sensory, affective, and motivational dimensions could relate that would show which combinations are even possible. If your honest conclusion is that no progress should be expected, argue for that and say what the field should do with the case instead.

Two constraints. Do not survey the theories of pain for my benefit, and do not give me a bibliography or a reading list; I have the papers, and I cannot check anything you cite. Argue from the positions as I have described them, correcting me where I have misdescribed one. Please write your reply in British English.
