I retired from a materials lab four years back after 38 years of it, and I've taken to reading philosophy of science the way other retirees do crosswords. Got to Wigner's Courant lecture of 1959, the one published the following year about the unreasonable effectiveness of mathematics in the natural sciences, and something bugs me: every discussion of it I can find leans on the same two or three stories, and I dont think any of them are the ones that would actually decide whether he's right. So rather than argue about the thesis, I want to run it against a single episode I know something about — the omega-minus.

Facts as I have them, tell me if I'm off anywhere. Around 1961 Gell-Mann and Ne'eman, independently, sort the hadron zoo into multiplets using SU(3), whose representation theory had been worked out by pure mathematicians decades earlier with no application in mind. The spin-3/2 baryons come out as a decuplet with one empty slot at the bottom. Gell-Mann, and Okubo independently through the mass formula, says the missing state has to have charge -1, strangeness -3, spin 3/2 and a mass near 1680 MeV, plus a fairly definite decay signature. Early 1964, a Brookhaven bubble chamber group reports essentially one event that fits, and the mass is right within a half percent — 1672 on today's numbers. Same year, the identical group-theoretic machinery takes Gell-Mann and Zweig to quarks, which pretty much nobody, including them, treated as real objects for years.

What I want out of you:

1. Wigner's thesis isn't one claim, it's several braided together. Separate them — the claim that mathematics is the right language for formulating laws of nature; the claim that theories so formulated have a range of validity far wider than the data they were built from; the "we got something out that we didn't put in" claim; and the claim that the mathematics arrives with aesthetic motives rather than being tailored to the phenomena. Which of these does the omega-minus actually bear on, and which does it leave untouched?

2. How risky was that prediction, honestly? The mass relation was fitted to masses people already knew, the symmetry is admittedly badly broken, and the confirmation rested on a single photograph. Is this an extrapolation into the dark or an interpolation inside a pattern that had already been adjusted to the data? Run the comparison with Mendeleev leaving a gap and getting it right, and tell me whether the comparison flatters Wigner or buries him — maybe this is a periodic-table case and not a Wigner case at all.

3. The color twist. The success is what made the statistics problem acute: three identical spin-1/2 constituents sitting in a symmetric state shouldn't be allowed, and the repair was to invent an extra internal degree of freedom. Is that further evidence for the thesis, mathematics dragging physics to a structure nobody would have guessed, or is it a case where the mathematics got it wrong and the rescue was made up on demand? Can Wigner's thesis even tell those two apart, and if not, is that a problem for it or a sign it's about something else?

4. Falsifiability. There was a rival scheme running at the time — Sakata's, if I remember right — same SU(3), but with the known baryons in the fundamental triplet, and it lost. Set that against supersymmetry as the modern control: gorgeous mathematics, half a century, nothing found. What observation in 1964 would have counted against Wigner? If none would have, say so plainly and tell me what the thesis is really doing instead.
