I am in the closing weeks of a graduate seminar on the semantic paradoxes and non-classical logic, and for the final session each of us has been asked to set out our own considered position as a numbered list, so that the rest of the group can attack what we actually hold rather than a reconstruction of it. My supervisor has urged me to get an independent audit beforehand, because I suspect I have assembled these views piecemeal over the term — picking up one commitment from the week on fixed points, another from the week on paraconsistent systems, a third from an epistemology elective I took last year — and never checked them against one another. A classmate who defends a gappy, paracomplete treatment of the Liar has already told me twice that my package is unstable, but he only ever gestures at it in general terms, and I want the tensions identified precisely before I stand up in front of everyone. I should say at the outset what I am not asking for: I am reasonably well read in this area, so please do not use the response to explain dialetheism to me from the beginning, and do not supply references, sources, or a reading list, which I will add myself. Treat the list below as the object of analysis.

Here is where I have landed:

1. Methodologically, I am an abductivist about theory choice in logic. A semantic theory earns acceptance by preserving as much as possible of the naive truth schema and of ordinary deductive practice while resolving the paradoxes, by being simple, and by keeping the cost of revision low. Crucially, I hold that where revision is unavoidable it should be local: confined as far as possible to the paradoxical contexts that force it, leaving the bulk of non-paradoxical reasoning untouched. This is a large part of why I resist revision-theoretic and contextualist treatments, which seem to me to make the instability of the paradoxical sentences global.

2. I accept the Liar. The standard Liar sentence — the one that says of itself that it is not true — is both true and false. Formally I work with the three-valued paraconsistent setup: sentences can be true only, false only, or both, and validity is preservation of truth, with the third value counted as designated.

3. I therefore reject explosion, and I accept that disjunctive syllogism fails in my logic; I regard that as the honest price of admitting gluts. I am emphatically not a trivialist. On my view gluts are rare and pathological, and the overwhelming majority of contradictions are simply false.

4. I reject bivalence, but I insist that excluded middle survives intact: every sentence is true or false, and the disjunction of a sentence with its negation always holds. I take the retention of excluded middle to be one of the main advantages of my view over paracomplete rivals.

5. On the conditional, I want only a truth-functional one in the object language, defined in the usual way out of negation and disjunction. Adding a new primitive connective with its own bespoke rules strikes me as an unmotivated complication, and I would resist it. At the same time, I hold that modus ponens is non-negotiable: a logic that cannot underwrite ordinary mathematical and empirical inference by modus ponens is not a logic I could actually use. Relatedly, I do not think Curry's paradox poses any special difficulty for me; I would treat a Curry sentence exactly as I treat the Liar, as another true contradiction, and I cannot see why that should be worse.

6. On revenge: I think strengthened liar sentences are what sink gappy theories, since those theories cannot cope with a sentence that says of itself that it is not true, and this is one of my principal arguments for admitting gluts in the first place. But I do not think my own view is hostage to the truth schema in the way the gap theorist's is. If some strengthened or revenge sentence turned out to create trouble for me, I would be prepared to deny the relevant instance of the schema. No individual instance is sacred; what matters is the overall balance of costs and benefits.

7. In epistemology I hold that a rational agent can never be justified in believing a contradiction. Belief aims at truth, and to believe that p is, constitutively, to take p to rule out not-p; so a state that took both p and not-p to hold would not be belief at all. I want to stress that I take this to be a constitutive norm of the attitude, not a defeasible rule of evidence that could be suspended in hard cases. And yes, I do take myself to have come to believe that the Liar is both true and false.

What I would like from you is an audit rather than an essay: tell me which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another and which pairs only look as though they do, and rank the genuine tensions by severity instead of listing them as equally damaging — some of them may be irritations that a dialetheist can live with, and I suspect at least one is not. For each genuine tension, say exactly what would have to give, what the cheapest revision is, and what that revision costs elsewhere in the package, including whether it damages the abductive criteria in (1) that I use to justify the view in the first place. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most, and flag any tension you identify that working defenders of this position already have a live and reasonably standard answer to, so that I do not announce as a fatal objection something the view has absorbed. Finally, please be careful to separate what is settled among people who work on these systems from what remains genuinely disputed among them — for instance about what a paraconsistent logic of this kind is and is not forced to surrender — and do not present your own preferred diagnosis of a contested point as though it were the agreed verdict.
