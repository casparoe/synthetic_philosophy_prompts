I run a reading group in the philosophy of science whose members come mostly from chemistry, biology, and the cognitive sciences, and I want to open our unit on natural kinds with a diagnostic exercise rather than a lecture. The difficulty I am trying to solve is this: everyone in the room is happy to call themselves a realist about natural kinds, no two people mean the same thing by it, and we typically discover only after an hour of argument that we have been answering different questions. So I want a decision tree that sorts participants by what they say about concrete classificatory cases, never by which label they would select from a list.

Please write it in two parts, in this order.

**Part one: the space, before the names.** Lay out the axes along which positions on this question actually differ. I have settled on the following as load-bearing for my purposes; you may add axes you think I have missed, and if you think one of mine is not a genuine distinction — or is not independent of the others — cut it and explain why.

1. Whether kind-hood is a matter of the world's own structure at all, or of our explanatory interests and classificatory practices.
2. Whether a genuine kind requires an underlying essence, or whether a stable cluster of co-occurring properties with no single responsible mechanism still qualifies.
3. Whether one and the same domain admits of many equally legitimate, cross-cutting kind systems, or whether legitimacy is unique.
4. Whether the unifying properties must be intrinsic, or whether relational, historical, and functional properties can be kind-constituting.
5. Whether successful scientific classification is a matter of kinds at all, given that at least one candidate — the biological species — may turn out to be an individual rather than a class.

For each axis, state the answer options. Then enumerate the combinations the axes jointly permit. I want the full grid before any position is named: that is the point of the exercise, and I would much rather see empty cells than have the tree reverse-engineered from a list of familiar views.

**Part two: populate it.** Locate the actual positions in that grid — microstructural or causal essentialism of the Kripke–Putnam type, homeostatic property cluster realism, promiscuous realism, a pluralism that keeps mind-independence and gives up uniqueness, the deflationary or skeptical line on which the phrase does no real theoretical work, the species-as-individuals move, and anything else you judge belongs. Every leaf should carry the name of the position (or "unnamed" if nobody defends it), one sentence on its most awkward commitment, and one sentence on what a participant who lands there owes us about the cases below. Then walk through the unoccupied cells: for each, diagnose whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, because it is coherent but unattractive, or because it is simply unexplored and might be worth someone's dissertation.

Constraints on the instrument itself:

- Eight to ten nodes, each answerable in a minute by a working scientist on the merits of the case, with no knowledge of the literature. No node may ask about realism, mind-independence, essence, or conventionality in the abstract; the abstraction has to arrive through a case.
- Build the nodes around cases I want used, chosen because I think they separate views that otherwise look identical: jade, which turns out to be two mineralogically unrelated substances answering to a single name; chlorine, whose samples share an atomic number but no exact isotopic composition and hence no single exact atomic weight; a biological species, where the question is whether the taxon is a class to begin with; the gene, where one term appears to cover several distinct things at once within a single science; money or another social kind, to test whether one account is meant to cover both the natural and the social; and one case of your own choosing where you think the standard examples are actively misleading.
- For each node, state which positions it separates and which it leaves together. Where two leaves cannot be pulled apart by anything in my case list, flag that as a limitation of the instrument rather than resolving it by fiat, and say what further question — if there is one — would do the work.
- The whole thing should be administrable in about twenty minutes, so keep the node wording tight.

Two requests about how you present this. First, I want to know how much of it is reporting and how much is reconstruction. Mark any leaf where what you are describing is a family of views rather than a doctrine anyone holds, and mark any diagnosis of an empty cell that you are reaching from the structure of the space rather than from anyone's arguments. Where you are not confident that a position is held in the form you have stated it, say so at that leaf instead of smoothing it over. Second, and more important: if you think the exercise cannot be done well as I have framed it — if the cases I have chosen do not discriminate in the way I assume, if building the grid first forces you to populate it with views nobody has ever defended, or if the honest finding is that my five axes collapse into three — say that plainly at the top and give me the nearest workable version. I would far rather be told that my framing is confused than be handed a tidy tree that quietly pretends otherwise.
