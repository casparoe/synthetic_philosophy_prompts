I am a fourth-year PhD student in philosophy of language and philosophy of AI, working on the semantics and ethics of ascribing honesty and deception to language models jointly with an ML collaborator, and I would like a diagnosis of an impasse rather than a verdict inside it.

The dispute, briefly. One camp holds that deception and lying are mental-state notions: they require that the speaker believe the content false and intend that the audience believe it, or at least warrant its truth. On this view, applying them to a model is either metaphorical or a substantive claim about machine psychology that nobody has yet earned the right to make. The other camp holds that these are dispositional or functional notions: a system deceives insofar as it reliably produces false beliefs in an audience, or produces outputs whose role in the wider loop is to mislead, and questions about inner states are either irrelevant or unanswerable from where we sit. Both camps agree on the ordinary cases — bald-faced lies, misleading truths, honest error — and both agree the distinction carries weight, since whether a deployed system deceived its overseers is supposed to matter normatively. Yet nobody has moved. The mental-state camp says the functionalists have quietly replaced a concept with a term of art that no longer licenses the moral conclusions being drawn from it. The functional camp says the mental-state camp defends a criterion that cannot be applied to any system whose psychology we cannot introspect, and is therefore useless as a guide to what to build or audit.

My own pilot data makes this concrete, and I think sharper than the published exchange. We trained a model to assert, with high confidence, a false claim about the medical efficacy of an inert compound, then interrogated it. Three findings: (1) the misleading assertion is stable across paraphrase and persists in contexts where no reward is present; (2) asked directly whether it takes the claim to be true, the model denies any such disposition and produces fluent, false self-reports about how it was trained; (3) in a subset of runs, its own chain of thought represents the claim as false in the very generation whose final output asserts it. Under the mental-state analysis, (3) is the paradigm lie and (2) a second-order lie; under the functional analysis, (3) is beside the point and (2) is simply more misleading output; under an analysis that drops the intention condition and appeals only to a norm of assertion, all three are lies. Both of my co-authors claim the data vindicates them, which I take to be the symptom.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis: the two camps are answering different questions — one about what our concept licenses, one about what an audit instrument must measure — and the appearance of metaphysical disagreement is an artifact of both insisting on the same word. Tell me whether that is right or whether there is a substantive disagreement underneath, and if so whether it is metaphysical (do these systems have belief-like states?), empirical (would better interpretability settle it?), or normative (what work do we want the concept to do?). In particular: would the dispute survive perfect mechanical transparency into the model's internals, or is interpretability precisely what ends it? Are the two definitions ever extensionally distinguishable for systems we actually deploy, and if they are not, does the debate retain any content at all? Finally, given your diagnosis, what would count as progress — a distinction, a formalization in signaling-game terms, a change of question, a class of experiments — or is this a case where no convergence should be expected, and why? Please engage the positions as I have stated them; I am not after a survey of the literature, and I have no need of citations.
