so, background: I'm four years into a novel and I've started to suspect my own rules don't fit together, and I'd rather be told that flatly than argue about it.

The book is about a river town. Real place, renamed Calder's Ferry. A covered toll bridge burned in August 1913 and a drifter named Ezra Vane was tried and hanged for it inside five months. The town retells it every year at the memorial. The Vanes — the family of the man who was hanged — still run a gravel pit outside of town, which is a strange thing to be.

I've been in the county archive three times. There's a quarry timekeeper's book that puts Vane nineteen miles upriver the night of the fire, initials next to hours, and there's an inquest where the mill agent's account changes between his first statement and his testimony. I'd say nine in ten that Vane didn't do it. Not that I say so in the book.

Here's what I've committed to, roughly in the order I committed to it:

1. Where the record and the town's memory disagree, the novel goes with the record. Not because I think archives are innocent — they were made by people with reasons — but because a hundred years of retelling has sanded the story into a shape that's good to say out loud, and getting out from under that shape is the whole project.

2. But the believing is part of what happened. The bridge burned once; the memory of it burned for a century and did work. It hanged a man, it decided which streets got the freshet relief money, it built the memorial. So the remembered version isn't local color to me, it's definately evidence about the past.

3. My device, and the thing I'm proudest of: I write both registers in the same flat third-person past tense. No italics, no "it was said that," no frame, no narrator flagging the seperate tracks. The remembered town and the documented town come at the reader in the same voice and the seam is theirs to feel. I've been calling it the unmarked register and I've told two interviewers it's the reason the book exists.

4. I don't think a novelist can be epistemically irresponsible. Fiction doesn't assert, so the Clifford business about not letting your belief outrun your evidence doesn't get purchase here. I'm not making claims. I'm building a room and letting people stand in it.

5. And yet I've also said, on the record, that I won't put something in a book that I'm confident is false and let it stand there as fact. That's a line for me.

6. The Vanes asked me to leave it unsettled. Not to bury it — to leave it open, so the story and the memorial and the family can all keep going. I said yes before I'd seen the timekeeper's book. I want to keep my word, partly because I think they have a real claim on it and partly, honestly, because it felt like being trusted.

7. So far I've kept that by omission. Nothing in the book pins Vane to the bridge and nothing nails the alibi down either. The quarry book shows up in a scene, the reader gets maybe two pages of it, then a chapter break.

8. No author's note, no afterword, nothing outside the text that says here's what actually happened. If the book can't carry it, a note at the back won't rescue it, and I'd just be using the town from a safe distance.

So: which of these are actually pulling against each other, and which only look like they are? I already suspect 2 against 1 and 8 against 1 might be fake problems, but I can't tell from inside it. Rank the real ones by how much damage they do, say what I'd have to give up or water down in each case and what that costs the book, and then tell me the one change that buys the most peace. And tell me whether it helps at all if I put a single sentence at the front — the town has two histories, this is both of them — instead of nothing, or whether that's just me building a hiding place.

I'm not going to say which one I'd like to survive. That's the reason I'm asking instead of deciding.
