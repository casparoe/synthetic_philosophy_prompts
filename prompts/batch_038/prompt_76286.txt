im TA-ing an intermediate decision theory seminar and next week we do Aumann's agree-to-disagree result. every textbook version i can find uses urns and coin flips and the students just nod along without ever feeling why the result is surprising. i want to hand out two constructed cases instead — ones that look like ordinary human disagreement and then let us point at exactly which hypothesis of the theorem broke. please build them to spec:

**Case A — honest, common prior, but nobody says a number.** Two agents who are truthful, common-knowledge-of-rationality Bayesians and who genuinely share a prior, and who after a long, substantive conversation still sit at different posteriors about some mundane empirical claim. The gap has to come from the fact that what they're exchanging isn't posteriors: bids, recommendations, hedged speech, coarse verdicts, actions taken on the basis of belief — your pick. Setting has to be realistic (a grants panel, two ER docs on the same shift, a pair of house hunters, a hiring committee), one or two paragraphs of scene, then a transcript of at least four rounds. Then show me what happens the moment one of them actually says the number out loud: the collapse should take no more than two rounds, and i want the arithmetic shown, not asserted. Keep the counts small integers so we can redo it on the whiteboard.

**Case B — same pair, now announcing exact posteriors, but no common prior.** Let them be perfectly honest and pool information as fully as you like, announce posteriors back and forth for as many rounds as you want, and have them *not* converge. The engine must be a prior difference that is itself common knowledge between them (different careers, different base rates internalized over the years), not stubbornness, not lying, not a communication breakdown. Show that no finite number of announcement rounds closes the gap, then name precisely which hypothesis of the theorem is doing the work and state what the theorem delivers the second you restore it.

after the two cases:

- a paragraph on the familiar objection that the common-prior assumption costs nothing, on the grounds that if two people learned they'd had exactly the same information their priors would have to match, so any prior gap must secretly encode a belief that the other side knows something you don't. say whether case B survives that objection, and if it does, what has to be true about the setting for it to survive.
- a short coda on the case where the priors aren't different but merely close to each other: what do you get in place of exact agreement?

ground rules: under about two pages total; every probability hand-checkable from stated counts and likelihoods; no exotic self-reference and no infinite knowledge hierarchies spelled out by hand; and — most important — the disagreement has to be *generated* by the priors, the likelihoods and the announcement protocol. if a case gets its verdict by stipulating that somebody's being obstuse or by having an agent make a quiet arithmetic error, it's a fail. finish by telling me which single feature of each case is load-bearing and what happens to the verdict if i delete it, since that's the thing i actually want to drill in class.
