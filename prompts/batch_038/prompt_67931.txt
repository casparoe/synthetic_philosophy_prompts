okay so I want a piece of satire, and I've got a pretty specific one in mind.

Write the launch landing page for a startup that has commercialized a logical inductor — you know the setup from the logical uncertainty literature: every sentence in some formal language gets treated as a share of stock worth a dollar if it's true, the reasoner's whole belief state is just a list of market prices that get revised day by day, and the guarantee on offer is that no polynomial-time trading strategy with finite risk tolerance can make unbounded money off that market over time. The company is selling this as coherence-as-a-service to people who are tired of geting Dutch-booked. About 650–750 words for the page itself, deadpan all the way through — no winking, no aside to the reader, the copy has to fully believe in itself.

The jokes have to come from the formalism being right, not from tech-bro boilerplate, so please dont lean on "we're like Uber but for beliefs" gags or on any joke about venture capital generally. The rule I care about: every claim the page makes should be an actual property of a logical inductor, just pitched as something a customer would plausibly want. Which means the page should be cheerfully selling coherence in the limit only, guarantees that hold exclusively against polynomial-time traders, a belief state that learns the far-out digits of pi one at a time and prices them at around a tenth each, self-trust that runs toward its own future beliefs rather than its current ones, prices for sentences about its own prices, and strict dominance over the universal semimeasure (in the limit). Make the mathematics come off as genuinely impressive and the productization as absurd — the target is the practice of shipping an asymptotic ideal object as a product, and secondarily the idea that coherence is a thing a bounded agent should be buying at all. Don't make the framework look dumb; make the business model look dumb.

Sections, in this order:

- **Hero** — headline, subheadline, one paragraph of copy (about 80 words)
- **What you're actually buying** — three bullets, each one a real property dressed up as a selling point
- **Plans** — three tiers, and the tiers should differ only in how far into the limit the customer is paying to get
- **FAQ** — five questions, one of which comes from a customer who was Dutch-booked on day n and wants to know whether that's a bug; the answer must not concede
- **Fine print** — two or three sentences of legal boilerplate defining what counts as a "trader," technically correct but written like a lawyer wrote it
- **One testimonial** — from a satisfied customer who is plainly not an ideal reasoner

Then, after the page, one section titled **What's true here** (about 150 words): for each joke, name the real feature of the logical induction setup it's exaggerating, and flag anything you invented wholesale. No citations, no bibliography, no page numbers — just the mapping.
