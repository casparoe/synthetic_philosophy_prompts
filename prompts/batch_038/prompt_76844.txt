I write as an executor rather than as a philosopher, though I have come to think that the difficulty I have run into is not a legal one. I am a solicitor; I deal in categories that have to be pinned down, and this one will not pin down.

Three months ago I took on the estate of a great-aunt who died last winter, alone, in the house she had lived in for sixty years. In the loft we found several hundred panels she had made by painting over the backs of wardrobe doors and offcuts of floorboard, mostly between 1970 and 2005. She said the paint stopped the damp showing. She never exhibited one, never signed one, and had no word for them other than "those things"; when a cousin once suggested she might show them, she said they were for the house and not for looking at. She left a note asking that they be burnt. We did not comply, and a small regional gallery has since asked to take forty of them.

What I want to raise is not whether that was the right decision. I have made it and I am not revisiting it. It is a classificatory problem that the vocabulary I work in does not reach, and it has two parts.

First, I cannot date the change. Nothing about the panels altered when the gallery wrote to me. They were, so far as I can tell, not works of art at any point in her life, and they now uncontroversially are; yet I could not tell you when that happened, or point to anything anyone did that made it happen. There was no decision and no ceremony — only a sequence of things: a nephew's photographs, a local historian's blog post, a curator's visit, an email to me. Whatever kind of status this is, it appears to have arrived gradually and by nobody in particular, and to be reversible in the same undramatic way; if the gallery loses interest, I assume they become junk again.

Second, and this strikes me as the stranger of the two, her refusal is now an asset. Everything the label will say about her makes use of the fact that she never intended these as art, never showed them, and asked for them to be destroyed. The disavowal is not an obstacle the new status has to be argued around; it is the reason the work is thought interesting. I do not know whether that is a regular feature of situations like this or an artifact of the particular market for them.

So: is there an established concept for either of these — a status that comes into being after the maker's death, without a datable act and without any authority from the maker, and a property of a work that consists in the maker's refusal of precisely that status? I am aware of a broad family of views according to which something is art because the art world treats it as art; I have not studied them, and I cannot tell whether they already absorb this case or whether the timing and the maker's protest put pressure on them.

Please tell me what the nearest existing concepts are, how each differs from what I have described, and which — if any — actually fits. If nothing fits, say so plainly and propose a term, with a definition and, more importantly, with edges: what would fall outside it; whether the same thing can happen to a living maker who disavows; whether it can happen in reverse, to someone who claimed the status and was refused; and whether the pattern is specific to art or would describe, equally well, a result credited posthumously to a scientist who never claimed it. I am not after a label and not after a reading list. I am after the carving.
