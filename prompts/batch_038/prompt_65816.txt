Ok so this is a weird one and I've been chewing on it for about a month now. I'm not a scientist — I manage a auto parts store two towns over — but I ended up on our county's conservation advisory board because nobody else wanted the job, and now I'm the one who has to write the actual language for a creek protection ordinance. Here's the situation. There's a little fish, call it the Coldwater dace, that only lives in about four miles of spring-fed creek up on the ridge. A state biologist came out last spring and told us something I still don't fully believe: every single one of them is female, and they can't reproduce on their own. They need sperm from the males of a related fish — a super common one that's all over the county, every warm sluggish creek, the kind anglers throw back on the bank — just to get their eggs started. But the male's DNA almost never actually gets into the egg. The babies come out as copies of the mother. So there's no real mixing. Except, she said, every so often — something like one egg in several hundred — the male's genome does get incorporated, and then those offspring can and do breed with the common fish downstream. So it leaks, one direction only, a tiny trickle. Apparently there's a fancy name for what our fish does and I can't remember it.

The ordinance has to say what we're protecting, and it has to say it in terms a code officer and a judge can actually apply. The developer who wants to put 200 houses above the headwaters has money for lawyers, and my understanding is that the whole thing hinges on whether our fish counts as its own species, because the state protections we'd be leaning on only bite at the species level. We have no genomics lab and no budget for one — we can afford some scale samples, a favor from a grad student, a hand lens, and counting fin rays. Honestly in the field you'd struggle to tell the two fish apart; ours are a bit deeper bodied and there's maybe something going on with the number of spots on the tail fin. Ours only live in the cold upper reach, while the common one is everywhere including right below the falls where the creek warms up and slows down.

So here's what I want from you. Below are six different ways people seem to decide what counts as a separate species, written in my own words because I don't know the jargon for half of them. For each one, do two things: (1) tell me what verdict it delivers on our fish — separate species, same species as the common one, or the question just doesn't get an answer — and (2) give it a score from 0 to 10 for how well it would work as the definition written into a county ordinance, where "work" means a non-expert can apply it, it's cheap, and it doesn't collapse the first time a good lawyer pokes at it. Put that in a table with one line of justification per row. Then tell me which one I should write in, which is the runner-up, and whether any of them should be thrown out entirely for a case like ours. My own instinct was to just say "things you can tell apart count as different," because that's the only one an ordinary person can check without a lab, and I have a feeling that's a mistake.

A. Can they interbreed — separate if the two groups don't swap genes in nature.
B. Family tree — separate if our fish sits on its own branch and doesn't share recent ancestry with the common one.
C. Tellable apart — separate if a trained person with a jar of specimens can consistently pick which group each one belongs to from looks alone.
D. Job in the ecosystem — separate if they make their living differently, in a place and a way the other one doesn't.
E. DNA clusters — separate if you plot the genetics and it falls into two clumps with a gap in the middle.
F. Skip the species question — protect it as a distinct population on the grounds that if it dies out nothing replaces it, and don't claim it's a separate species at all.

Two extra constraints on your pick. Whatever you choose has to be definable in a sentence or two of ordinance text, and it has to cope with the fact that our fish cannot persist without the common one's males turning up in the creek — so if your choice ends up requiring us to protect the common fish too, say that out loud, because that will be extremely unpopular with the landowners downstream. If you think two of the six are really the same idea wearing different clothes, merge them and tell me.
