I did jury duty last spring and got told off by the judge for googling the name of the street where the stabbing happened. Now I keep thinking about the whole bundle of rules around juries and it doesn't hang together for me, and I want you to reconstruct what picture of group knowledge it's actually built on.

Here's the bundle as I understand it: twelve strangers, chosen more or less at random; they're instructed not to look anything up, not to visit the scene, not to talk to anyone outside the room, not to consult any expert — all the information they get comes through one controlled channel run by two adversarial lawyers; then they're locked in a room together and made to talk until they converge; then in some places they must be unanimous, and in others a jury can come back 10-2 once it's been out long enough; and if they can't agree at all you just draw twelve new people and run it again. Meanwhile in the same courthouse the judge is allowed to read whatever she likes and can appoint her own expert.

So: what does this presuppose about how groups arrive at true beliefs? My instinct from reading about Condorcet's jury theorem is that it can't be that, because the theorem wants each voter to be better-than-chance and to be forming their judgement independently, and this system deliberately makes each juror dumber and then forces them to stop being independent — the two loudest people in my room did most of the deciding. But then why bother with twelve, why random selection, why the retrial? Maybe the point of the numbers isn't aggregation at all. What's my alternative hunch: that juries aren't a truth-tracking device but a legitimacy device, and every feature that looks epistemically stupid is doing political work. Is that right, or is there a coherent epistemology in there that I'm missing — one where unanimity in particular isn't majority rule at all but something else, like a rule about when a single dissent counts as evidence?

Tell me which of these features the system strictly needs its implicit theory for and which ones just happen to fit it, and be honest about where the practice is underdetermined — where three or four incompatible theories would license the exact same rules. Then: suppose I got my way and we built a maximally Condorcetian jury — jurors encouraged to go investigate on their own, forbidden from telling each other what they found, secret ballot, no deliberation whatsoever, maybe fifty of them instead of twelve, verdict by simple majority. What does that system lose that the current one buys, and which loss is the one that actually matters?
