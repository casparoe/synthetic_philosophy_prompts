I am chairing the forty-five-minute question-and-answer session that closes a two-day workshop on the philosophy of psychiatric classification. Our respondent is a philosopher whose recent book takes Hacking's account of looping effects — the way classifying people changes the people classified, and thereby changes the classifications themselves — and pushes it into a positive methodological program. Her position, as I understand it, has four parts, and I want the questions to engage all four rather than the popularized version of the thesis.

First, she insists that looping is not a self-fulfilling prophecy. In the prophecy story, a classification is false and then becomes true; in her story, the classification alters what would make it true or false at all, so the target moves rather than being hit or missed. Second, because the extension of an interactive kind shifts in response to its own publicity, she argues that a prevalence estimate is not a measurement of a standing quantity but a reading of a feedback system at one moment; she proposes replacing base rates with a measure she calls loop velocity, meaning how fast the extension of a diagnosis changes once the category becomes publicly available, and she holds that high loop velocity explains why some disorders rise and vanish within a generation, with the multiple personality epidemic as her case study, while others persist. Third, she claims that identity-entrenchment slows the loop: when the classified adopt a category as an identity and organize around it, the target hardens and measurement becomes possible again. Fourth, she treats the biological reclassification program in psychiatry, RDoC and its cousins, as an attempt to escape the loop by retreating to person-indifferent variables, and she thinks it cannot succeed because the behavioral anchors of those variables remain interpretable by the people studied. Throughout, she denies that any of this shows psychiatric kinds are unreal.

She is a careful thinker who will not accept straw men, and the room includes two epidemiologists who are skeptical of her program and one clinician who thinks it trivially true. Write me the questions I should ask, under the following constraints.

- Eight questions, ordered so that her commitments are pinned down before the pressure is applied, with a one-line note on why each sits where it does.
- Every question must be anchored in a mechanism or a case rather than a slogan. None may open by asking her to explain or defend looping effects in general.
- For each question, give me the answer she is most likely to give and the follow-up that removes that answer's leverage. The follow-up must be something I can deliver on the spot without having researched anything in advance.
- At least one question on each of the following: how she keeps her account distinct from Merton's self-fulfilling prophecy and from the double hermeneutic in interpretive social science; whether loop velocity can be defined at all without assuming that the kind at time one and the kind at time two are the same kind, and if that assumption is licensed, what remains of the argument against base rates; the challenge from publicity-sensitive quantities outside psychiatry — smoking rates, myopia, crime statistics, unemployment — which drift for structurally similar reasons without anyone concluding they are unmeasurable; whether the loop requires that the classified person know she is classified, and what she makes of diagnoses that are recorded but never disclosed; what her denial of anti-realism commits her to, and whether that denial is a substantive claim or a stipulation about the word real; and the ethical question raised by her third claim — if a category has become load-bearing for a community's self-understanding and politics, is hardening the loop a good thing, and does she have resources to answer that without appealing to the very hermeneutic facts she says defeat measurement.
- End with a single closing question that forces her to name one concrete, checkable prediction distinguishing her program from a purely historical narrative, plus the follow-up for the evasive version of that answer.
- Then tell me two questions I should not ask, because she will have rehearsed answers, and say what each of them would invite instead.
- Finally, write the two-sentence framing I will read aloud before opening the floor, stating her position accurately and without caricature, in language the epidemiologists will not find insulting.

Do not include a reading list or citations; I want the questions themselves.
