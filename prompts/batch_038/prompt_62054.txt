So I'm running a 100-minute workshop for about 20 second-year CS students who've done truth tables and a little natural deduction but have never heard the word "constructive" in a math class. I want an in-class game that makes them *feel*, rather than be told, why an intuitionist won't assert A ∨ ¬A for a statement that isn't settled yet. The standard demos never land with this crowd — Goldbach, or the √2^√2 trick with irrationals a and b — because they all just shrug and say it's still true or false, we're only too dumb to know which. So the job the game has to do is take that shrug away: arrange things so there genuinely isn't a fact yet, because the object in question is being produced as they play.

Here's the seed I had, feel free to rebuild it: class split into small teams, and the object under discussion is a long sequence of digits (or a graph, or a colouring — your call) that nobody has written down yet. It gets extended one step at a time by some arbitary process at the front of the room, and teams make claims about it and earn tokens when their claims get cashed out. Claims like "the sequence contains a 7" versus "it never will" should be assertable-or-not in a way that tracks what has actually been forced so far, and the token economy should hurt anyone who asserts a disjunction without being able to say which side they're on.

What I need from you: the full rules; the token/payoff scheme; the exact wording of the claim cards or whatever device replaces them; a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 100 minutes; what the facilitator does at the front and, importantly, how much of the sequence they should have decided in advance before anyone walks in — my instinct is none of it, and that none of it is the whole point, but tell me if that actually breaks the game and what to do instead. Then a debrief that maps specific moments in play onto the ideas: the constructive gloss on the connectives, why the monotonicity constraint (once something's forced it stays forced at every later stage) is doing the real work rather than any slogan about proofs, and where the double-negation gap comes from.

Two moments I especially want to happen, so please engineer them rather than hope for them. First, someone notices they can commit to "it is not the case that the sequence never contains a 7" while being completely unable to point at a position where a 7 shows up — and the payoff scheme should reward that commitment rather than treat it as a dodge. Second, a team tries to smuggle classical reasoning in, and the tokens punish them for it in a way they can articulate afterwards.

Also be straight with me about the failure modes: what predictably goes wrong the first time this runs, what a group does once they've figured out the trick early and start gaming it, and — the part I care most about — which feature of the view the game simply cannot reproduce, so I know I have to supply it myself in the debrief instead of pretending the activity covered it. My specific worry is that what they walk away with is "truth is just relative to what we know," which is an epistemic slide I don't want to cause. Tell me how to design against that, and if you can't, say so.

Finally, give me your own read on how confident you are that this produces the intended realization rather than a fun-but-hollow game — I want a sense of how committed you are to each part of the design, not a hedge that everything might work — and single out the piece you're least sure about and why.
