Since 2019 the city of Aldermouth has settled a narrow class of policy questions by conditional prediction market, under a charter that divides the work roughly along futarchic lines. The elected Council adopts and ranks the city's ends. The Conditional Markets Board does not decide policies; it decides whether a given policy question may be handed to a market that trades on how much each option would contribute to an end the Council has already adopted, and on what conditions. The Board treats its own votes as precedent, and its clerk's office — where I work — has to brief the members on whether the current docket item is consistent with the record.

The record so far:

1. 2019 — Which of three alignments the crosstown bus rapid transit line should take. End adopted by the Council: ridership per subsidy dollar. Resolved at 24 months on audited ridership and operating cost compiled by the regional transit authority, which held no position on any alignment. Approved, 6–1.

2. 2020 — Whether to close the Northgate overnight shelter, the city's only year-round facility of its kind, and move its budget to scattered-site tenancy support. Resolution metric: the twelve-month change in the north district's point-in-time street count — a count conducted by the city's own Housing Outreach Division, whose budget the decision would also alter. The Council's charter statement treats the absence of rough sleeping in the north district as a floor to be held, not a target to be maximized. Rejected, 6–1. Minute: the metric would resolve; the question would not.

3. 2021 — Whether to introduce a congestion charge in the Central Zone. Ends adopted: corridor travel time and central-retail viability. Resolved at 18 months on camera-derived flow times audited by the state transportation department and on vacancy rates published by the county assessor. Approved, 5–2.

4. 2021 — Whether to move the city's 2,400 administrative staff to a four-day week. Ends adopted: service responsiveness and payroll cost. Resolved at 12 months on median citizen-request response time and a payroll index, both audited by the state comptroller. Approved, 4–3, on the condition that no city employee, no supervisor of city employees, and no member of their households hold a position in the market.

5. 2022 — Which of four salary bands the City Manager should occupy. No end had been adopted for which the salary was to be a means. Resolved at 12 months on a management-performance index compiled by an outside consultancy that the City Manager's office retained, scoped, and paid. Rejected, 7–0. Minute: the resolution of the contract and the conduct of the office sit in the same hands.

6. 2023 — Whether the new harbour plaza should be named for a living former mayor. No end adopted. Resolved at 6 months on a resident-attachment survey designed and fielded by the Mayor's Office of Communications. Rejected, 5–2. Minute: with no end fixed, the market would merely have been pricing a name.

7. 2024 — Which of three winter road-salt regimes to adopt. Ends adopted: pavement condition and slip-injury burden. Resolved at 24 months on a pavement index published by the state transportation department and on emergency-department injury counts published by the county. Approved, 7–0.

The item now on the docket:

The Council has adopted an end — a 15 percent reduction in age-standardized pediatric asthma admissions by 2030 — and asks whether the choice of means may be settled by market. The options are (a) filtration and ventilation retrofits across the housing authority's stock, and (b) a contract with Meridian Health for in-home monitoring, nurse triage, and rapid transport. The resolution metric would be the county health department's quarterly count of asthma admissions among under-15s, drawn from hospital discharge records; the department confirms that it cannot revise the figures retroactively and has no stake in either option. Meridian, however, operates two of the three hospitals whose records generate the count, sets its own admission thresholds and coding practices, and would otherwise be free to trade. Meridian has offered to accept a trading ban covering itself and its affiliates, and to fund an annual state audit of its own coding. The pilot would run five years, outlasting the Council that adopted the end; every market the Board has approved to date resolved inside the sitting Council's term.

What I need for the brief:

(a) Lay out the candidate rules consistent with this record — a rule is consistent if it reproduces the recorded vote on all seven items — stated as procedures the Board could actually apply rather than as labels. I expect more than one, and I expect at least two of them to be single-condition rules that each explain every vote. Say, for each, which of the seven items is really doing the work of supporting it.

(b) Say what each candidate rule predicts for the asthma market, and where the rules' formulations become strained in applying it — in particular, whether a condition about who compiles a metric and a condition about who produces the data underneath it are the same condition or two.

(c) Predict the Board's verdict, with a rough sense of the margin, and give the argument a member of the majority would make. Then, separately, say what the Board ought to decide if fidelity to its own record is the standard: name the closest analog among the seven, and state precisely where that analogy holds and where it breaks.

(d) Attend to two features of the record that the vote tallies alone do not convey: that the 2021 four-day-week market was approved only under a trading exclusion, and that the 2022 rejection was unanimous while the 2020 and 2023 rejections were not.

(e) To the extent the record underdetermines the asthma case, say what principle the Board would be adopting by approving and what by rejecting — including what it would be committing itself to the next time a would-be provider is also the source of the measurement, and what becomes of the five-year horizon, which no precedent has tested.

(f) Describe one further docket item, in the same format as the seven above (options, adopted end, resolution metric, who compiles it, horizon, who may trade), that would discriminate the surviving rules, and state what each rule predicts for it.

Everything you need is in this prompt; please do not spend space on the general merits of prediction markets, and do not invent additional facts about Aldermouth beyond what is given. Throughout, tell me how confident you are in your main claims — the reconstruction of the rules, the prediction, and the recommendation may well warrant different degrees of confidence — and single out the point you are least sure of, saying what would change your mind about it.
