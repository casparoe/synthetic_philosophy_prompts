I have spent a term cataloguing Bernard Williams's opera criticism — the essays and reviews only, not the moral philosophy — and reduced it to a list of verdicts, as I read them. He was damning about a production that transposed a nineteenth-century work into a modern setting in order to stage a political thesis the score was not making. He praised a Ring cycle given entirely in concert, sung from the score with no staging at all, on the ground that Wagner's music drama could stand as music and lost nothing essential when the stage was taken away. He wrote with real enthusiasm about Janáček, and especially about productions plain and unshowy enough to let the orchestra carry the emotion instead of the acting. He was hostile to Mozart productions that winked at the audience about opera's artificiality, as though the convention stood in need of an apology. And he was cool about scrupulous period correctness when it was offered as a substitute for interpretation.

The new case: a company performs Die Walküre as a concert piece — orchestra on stage, singers at stands, no sets or costumes — but with a continuous video work projected above them, made by a visual artist, supplying a narrative of its own that reads the opera as a parable about ecological collapse. The music is untouched; the visual layer is the only staging.

Predict Williams's verdict and identify which earlier case is the closest analog, with your reasons. Then set out the candidate rules his catalogue admits — the autonomy of the music, hostility to a director's imposed thesis, the owning rather than excusing of operatic convention, and music's peculiar power to present emotion rather than report it — and say on which of them the new case actually turns. If the catalogue underdetermines the verdict, say which further production would discriminate between the rules, and what principle Williams would be committing himself to on each side of the choice.
