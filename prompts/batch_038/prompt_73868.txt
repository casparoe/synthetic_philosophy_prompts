I'm in a philosophy-of-economics seminar and this week is revealed preference plus the whole "what does utility actually mean" question, and I keep coming back to the New Coke disaster as a test case. Can you run the theory over it properly?

Facts, in case yours are fuzzy: by the early 80s Coke was bleeding share to Pepsi, Pepsi had been winning those blind sip-tests in malls on sweetness, so Coke spent a couple years developing a sweeter formula that won its own blind sip-tests, launched it in April 1985 as a replacement rather than a second variety, got hammered — tens of thousands of angry calls and letters, people hoarding cases of the old stuff, organized protest groups — and put the original back as Coca-Cola Classic within about three months, after which sales jumped. One detail that matters for what I want: Coke's own focus groups had turned up something like 10–12% of testers who said they'd feel angry and alienated and might quit Coke entirely, and management basically discounted that soft data in favor of the hard sip-test numbers.

So, first, the mapping. If choice is the only evidence we have about preference, and preference is all utility talk amounts to, then which choices here are the evidence — the mall sips, the focus groups, the boycotts and letter-writing, or the eventual stampede back to Classic? Say what the theory licenses you to conclude about what these people preferred and what was good for them, and where it goes quiet because there's no choice to read off. I specifically want to know what the theory does with the angry 10–12%, since they showed up as reported feelings and not as behavior, and what it does with the fact that a lot of people signed petitions against New Coke and then drank it anyway.

Second, I have a proposal I want you to stress-test rather than just agree with. My thought is that the sip-test and the market weren't measuring the same object at all: a sip is a preference over the first few seconds of a drink, while buying a case is a preference over a whole drinking life plus whatever the brand means to you, and sweetness that wins at second one becomes cloying by can three. If that's right, New Coke isn't a counterexample to revealed preference, it's a counterexample to the assumption that revealed preferences port across contexts. Call it context-portability. Does that distinction actually hold up, or is it a rescue device — i.e. can you always re-describe a reversal as a different preference measured in a different situation, and if so is the theory unfalsifiable, and does it matter if it is? Also tell me whether the identity-and-brand version of the story (people weren't choosing a flavor, they were defending a thing they belonged to) is better handled by the standard revealed preference framework or by the sort of critique that says choice isn't only about getting what you want.

Third, the verdict, both directions: which features of the episode does revealed preference genuinely explain, which does it misdescribe, and was this a case the theory ever anticipated? And was Coke management's own decision procedure — hard quantitative choices over soft reported attitudes — itself an application of the theory, or just bad marketing?

Last thing, and it matters for my paper: be clear about which parts of all this are settled among people who work on it and which parts are genuinely contested, and don't hand me a contested position as if it were the consensus. Flag it when you're siding with one camp against another, and say if I've mis-stated any of the theory.
