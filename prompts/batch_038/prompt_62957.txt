I spent forty-one years as an experimental physicist, most of it building and calibrating neutron instrumentation, and since retiring I have been reading philosophy of physics systematically for the first time. I am at home with the mathematics but a novice with the vocabulary, so please do not simplify the physics; do explain any philosophical term of art you need. I should add that I am not after a survey — I have read enough to know roughly what the theorem says. What I cannot work out is who wins which exchange in an argument I recently listened to, so I would like it treated as an adjudication.

Last term I sat in on a graduate reading group at a university near my home. Two regulars — a postdoc in quantum foundations, whom I will call Vera, and a doctoral student in philosophy, Owen — argued at length about what Bell's theorem actually establishes, and the rest of us listened rather than intervened. I have written the exchange out as faithfully as I can manage. Please go through it turn by turn, and for each turn say whether the argument made there is sound, partly sound but overstated, question-begging, or mistaken, with a sentence or two of justification. Where one of them has made a technical error, say so plainly rather than smoothing it over. No references are needed; I will supply my own reading.

Turn 1 (Vera). Any theory that assigns definite single outcomes to the two measurements and reproduces the singlet-state statistics must violate a factorisation condition: it cannot be that, given a complete specification of the physical state of the pair at emission together with both settings, the joint probability of the two outcomes equals the product of the two marginals. Since 2015 the experiments have been run with the two old loopholes closed and the violations confirmed. So the wings cannot be screened off by anything in their shared past, which means something at one wing is physically relevant to what happens at the other. The only escape left is to deny that the settings are statistically independent of the hidden variables — that is, to say the universe arranges source and experimenters to match one another — which she regards as scientifically disqualifying. Her conclusion: nature is nonlocal, and that is a theorem-level result, not an interpretive preference.

Turn 2 (Owen). Two objections. First, "disqualifying" is asserted rather than argued: statistical independence is not a locality condition at all but a condition on the relation between an intervention and a state, and a common cause linking setting to hidden variable is no more a conspiracy than a common cause linking a barometer to a storm; the word imports an agent the mathematics does not contain. Second, there is an option that keeps every influence inside the light cone — retrocausal models, in which the state at emission depends on the settings that will later be chosen, the dependence propagating backwards along the light cone. On that view the theorem does not show nature is nonlocal; it shows the past is not as sealed off from the future as we assumed. So Vera's conclusion is interpretation-relative.

Turn 3 (Vera). Retrocausality is not a third option but the same violation of statistical independence with the arrow drawn the other way: in both cases the hidden variable depends on the settings. And the price is specific, not rhetorical. If the hidden variable depends on the settings, the settings cannot be treated as independently variable, and one loses the ability to ask what the run would have shown had the other setting been chosen — precisely the inference on which controlled experiment depends. A theory that surrenders it can accommodate any result whatsoever. She adds that the superdeterminist cannot consistently argue for her own view: in making the case she must assume her listeners' assent is responsive to her reasons rather than pre-correlated with the hidden variable by the initial state of the universe.

Turn 4 (Owen). Both halves of that are too strong. On the first: the charge proves too much. Any deterministic theory entails that the complete state is correlated with everything, including what an experimenter will next do, and nobody concludes that Newtonian mechanics is unfalsifiable. Counterfactual reasoning under determinism simply means holding the laws fixed and letting the past vary, an entirely ordinary operation. On the second: the objection cannot separate the two positions Vera has merged. In a superdeterministic model the setting correlation is a brute feature of the initial state, and each further correlation you want costs another piece of fine tuning; in a retrocausal model the correlation is generated by a law, and laws can be constrained. Write down a toy model whose backward law yields exactly the quantum correlations and nothing more, and the anything-goes charge is refuted by construction. The live question is whether such models exist and are non-ad-hoc, not whether the programme self-destructs.

Turn 5 (Vera). Then take the local candidate everyone reaches for, the de Broglie–Bohm theory, and cost it out. It is usually described as nonlocal only in a harmless sense, and the description is made precise by splitting the factorisation condition in two: a requirement that the probability of an outcome at one wing not depend on the setting at the other, and a requirement that it not depend on the outcome at the other. Bohm is said to satisfy the first and fail only the second, and since only the first is thought necessary for peace with relativity, Bohm counts as acceptably local. But that description is available only if the hidden variable is taken to be the wave function alone. Take it to be the complete ontic state — wave function and configuration — and the theory is deterministic; and in a deterministic theory, once the complete state and both settings are given, the outcomes are fixed, so the second condition holds trivially and whatever breaks factorisation must be the first. Which is what happens: in Bohm the outcome at one wing depends on the distant setting. The harmless version of Bohmian locality is therefore an artefact of an incomplete hidden variable — and with an incomplete one, a failure of factorisation tells you nothing about locality at all.

Turn 6 (Owen). The technical content there is right, but it does not rescue Vera's opening. For one thing, it shows the two-condition taxonomy does no independent work: which condition a theory is classified as violating is settled by the choice of hidden variable, so such a taxonomy records bookkeeping rather than adjudicating metaphysics. For another, what survives the reclassification is a dependence of outcomes on distant settings given a state description — not a signal, not a transfer of energy, not anything an experiment could exploit. And there is a reading on which even that vanishes: if the state never collapses and evolves unitarily, there is no action at a distance anywhere, and the correlations concern how the branches stand to one another. What the theorem rules out is thus a package — local dynamics together with a separable state of the kind that screens off distant events — which is a considerably more modest claim than that nature is nonlocal.

Turn 7 (Vera). Those last two moves should not be run together. That no signal can be sent is a theorem of the quantum formalism itself; it holds with or without hidden variables, and it holds in every model that reproduces the observed violations, including the ones Owen prefers. It therefore cannot serve as evidence that an ontology is local — it is neutral between them. Nor does the collapse-free reading escape unscathed: on it the two wings are never in separately well-defined states, and what has gone is the assumption that the state of a composite is fixed by the states of its spatially separated parts. Call that nonlocality or non-separability, it is a loss a relativist cares about, and renaming it does not restore it. So the dispute is not about the physics but about what the word local is meant to track — signalability on one side, local causality and separability on the other — and once it is put that way, Owen's modest reformulation is just the signalability reading stated in full.

Which of them is right about what? Beyond the per-turn verdicts, I would like your view on these specific points: whether the conspiracy and fine-tuning charge in Turn 3 is a substantive objection or rhetoric, and whether Owen's Turn 4 answer to it succeeds; whether the distinction he draws there between superdeterminism and retrocausality is real or merely verbal; whether Vera's Turn 5 argument about the choice of hidden variable is correct, and what it does and does not establish about Bohm; whether Owen's Turn 6 conclusion follows from the premises he actually has rather than from the ones he would like; and whether Vera is right in Turn 7 that no-signalling is neutral between ontologies. Then say who is more right overall, and tell me what, if anything, both of them are missing — an option neither raised, or a shared assumption the whole exchange rests on that neither of them tested. I have deliberately not said which of them I found more persuasive; I would rather the arguments were judged as they stand.
