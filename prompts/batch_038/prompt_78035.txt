I'm running a 75-minute seminar next Monday for 16 second-years on a joint politics-and-philosophy degree. They've had two weeks of Arrow's theorem in lectures, none of them have ever watched a proof happen, and they're the sort who think they're above strategic voting. I want an in-class activity that walks them face-first into Gibbard–Satterthwaite instead of me just telling them it's true.

My rough idea, override it if you've got something better: three options the class actually has a stake in — I'm thinking where the module's budget for the end-of-term social goes, or which of three readings gets dropped — points-based payoffs so no real money is involved, and several rounds so they can learn between ballots and start to trust each other's rankings.

Constraints I care about:

- Nobody hears the name of the theorem, or the word manipulation, until the debrief. The first time someone lies their way into a better outcome it should feel like their own discovery rather than a plant.
- A middle phase where they're turned loose designing a voting rule that nobody in the room can exploit. They should end up either reinventing dictatorship or producing something they break themselves within five minutes. You'll need to force onto-ness into the game rules, because otherwise some bright spark will propose always picking the medium option and technically win the phase — tell me how to head that off without giving away why.
- A third phase where the same three options get quietly laid out along a line (cheap / medium / lavish) so that preferences are single-peaked and the median takes over. The point is that lying stops paying and they notice, and work out for themselves what the earlier chaos was depending on. Give me the facilitation moves for the group that doesn't notice.
- Be blunt about what will go wrong the first time I run it: the round where the points feel too abstract to care about, the student who lies in the single-peaked phase anyway and gets away with it, the pair who refuse to have preferences about a budget.

I also want an honest limits section. This is three alternatives and 16 known people with known payoffs, so it can't deliver a universal domain, and it can't distinguish "some voter can sometimes gain by lying" from "lying is common, or easy to find" — and there's a whole literature on how computationally awkward it can be to actually locate a beneficial lie, which cuts against the moral they'll draw. Say what I have to supply verbally and roughly how long that takes.

Format it as a materials list, then a minute-by-minute timeline per phase, then the debrief questions in the order I should ask them. Keep it to something I can genuinely print and run without extra prep.

End your reply with a single line beginning "Verdict:" giving one sentence on which of the three phases is most likely to collapse and why.
