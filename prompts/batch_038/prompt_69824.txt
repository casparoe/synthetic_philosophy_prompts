For some years now I have found myself persuaded by a broadly Berkeleian theory of money — the one Berkeley worked out in *The Querist* and in his earlier writings on paper credit, and championed, without success, when he lobbied the Irish parliament for a national bank empowered to issue and supervise a paper currency. I should like a rigorous assessment of what would have to be true for that theory to be false. Treat the following reconstruction as the object of assessment, not a straw man of it; where you think I have misstated it, say so and say why, but do not let disagreement with my formulation become a way of evading the question.

As I hold it, the view has four limbs:

(i) Money is not wealth. A nation's wealth consists in the necessaries and conveniences of life that its labour actually produces, and a stock of precious metal adds nothing to that stock save insofar as it sets productive effort in motion.

(ii) Money is a ticket, counter or token — a sign whose entire content is functional. To ask what money is worth over and above its capacity to circulate claims upon goods and labour is to commit a category mistake of precisely the kind Berkeley attacked in his metaphysics: it is to mistake a useful sign for a thing with an intrinsic nature behind it.

(iii) Because money is functional and conventional, its supply is proper matter for deliberate public management — ideally by a national bank issuing paper — rather than something to be abandoned to the accidental yield of mines and the course of trade. Hoarding, whether by individuals, merchants or a treasury sitting upon bullion, is a positive evil, since it withdraws the very stimulus that keeps industry moving.

(iv) The test of any monetary arrangement is therefore not convertibility, metallic backing or fidelity to some antecedent standard, but whether it excites and sustains industry in the community using it. Paper that fails this test is bad money though perfectly convertible; paper that passes it is good money though irredeemable.

What I want is a map of this view's exposure, sorted by how much damage each item would do.

First, the decisive cases. Which historical episodes count as genuine tests rather than mere illustrations — the collapse of the assignats and of the Continental currency; the British suspension of convertibility from 1797 and the bullionist controversy that followed; the struggle between the currency and banking schools that was resolved, at least on paper, by the Bank Charter Act of 1844; the German and Zimbabwean hyperinflations; the long-run stability of irredeemable fiat regimes since the 1970s, including the expansion of central bank base money after 2008 without the inflation its critics predicted? For each, identify which limb it bears upon, and whether it indicts the theory or only the competence and honesty of the administrators who applied it. Be willing to say which of these you regard as fatal and which as survivable.

Second, the conceptual objections. Private money — merchants' bills, goldsmiths' and later bankers' notes, deposit liabilities — has repeatedly arisen without sovereign design or backing, which sits badly with (iii); and for what it is worth, that is the objection I find hardest to answer. Consider also: whether (ii) can accommodate money's store-of-value function at all, or whether a theory that treats value as exhausted by circulation must treat saving and bequest as pathological; whether (iv) supplies any operational criterion for too much or too little money, or whether, lacking one, it reduces to approving whatever the state does; whether the view collapses into the quantity theory on one side or into a chartalist account of money as a creature of the sovereign's tax demands on the other, and whether either collapse would be fatal or a welcome clarification; and whether Berkeley's hostility to hoarding survives the modern observation that money held idle is not thereby withdrawn from the economy but lent onward through the financial system.

Third, the view's own predictions. What does this theory expect that a metallic or commodity account of money does not, and where has the expectation been borne out? If the record on balance favours Berkeley, say so plainly; an honest map of exposure should show where a position is well armoured as well as where it is thin.

Fourth, concessions. Which of these objections would a committed defender — say, a modern monetary nominalist or chartalist working in Berkeley's line — themselves concede as counting against them, and which would they dismiss as beside the point? Distinguish the two carefully, and give your reasons for each assignment.

Fifth, insulation. Has limb (iv), in particular, been qualified to the point where no evidence could tell against it, since any outcome can be redescribed as a failure of management rather than of principle? If so, is that a fatal defect for a theory of this kind, or a legitimate feature of a normative doctrine about how money ought to be governed? Give a reasoned verdict rather than a hedge.

I do not want a bibliography, a reading list or citations of any kind; argue from the content of the positions and the record of events. Please write in British English.
