so a friend in my social choice reading group has what he calls a two-minute proof that strategy proofness forces dictatorship even when there are only two alternatives on the table. I know that can't be right, but I've stared at it for an hour and can't find the crack, and I'd rather understand the crack than just be told the theorem's hypothesis says three. answer in British English please.

setup: A = {x, y}, n ≥ 3 voters, strict preferences (so each voter either prefers x to y or y to x), and f assigns one alternative to each profile of preferences. onto = every alternative is f's choice at some profile. strategy-proof = no voter can get a strictly better outcome for herself by reporting something other than her real preference.

his argument:

(1) let f be onto and strategy-proof.

(2) unanimity: at any profile where every voter prefers x to y, f picks x. why — by onto there's some profile q with f(q) = x; walk from q to the unanimous profile p one voter at a time; if at some step the outcome switched from x to y when voter j's report changed, then j, who really prefers x since p is unanimous for x, would have got a strictly better outcome by sticking with her old report, which strategy-proofness forbids.

(3) now write out the profiles p_0, ..., p_n where p_k has voters 1..k preferring y to x and voters k+1..n preferring x to y. by (2), f(p_0) = x and f(p_n) = y, so there's a first k\* with f(p_k\*) = y, and f(p_{k\*-1}) = x. voter k\* is the pivot.

(4) take any profile p at which the pivot prefers y. walk from p to p_k\* by changing one voter's report at a time. every move on this path is a single voter changing her report with everyone else held fixed, which is exactly what strategy-proofness governs, and no such move can turn an outcome the pivot ranks at least as high as x into x itself. since f(p_k\*) = y, f(p) = y too. same reasoning with the roles of x and y swapped when the pivot prefers x.

(5) so f picks whichever of x, y the pivot prefers, whatever everyone else does: f is dictatorial.

(6) nothing above needed A to have more than two elements — (2)'s proof works just as well if the outcome switches from x to some third thing, since the unanimous voters still rank x top — so onto + strategy-proof gives dictatorship for two alternatives as well as for more.

why I'm confident (6) is false: with an odd number of voters and two alternatives, majority rule is onto, anonymous, non-dictatorial, and its strategy-proof — you can only gain by lying if you're pivotal, and if you're pivotal your report is what decides, so lying can only hand the win to the side you like less. and when I run (1)–(3) on majority rule with five voters I get a perfectly ordinary pivot (voter 3, the median) with no contradiction anywhere, so (2) and (3) can't be where it dies.

what I want from you:

- name the step that fails and say precisely what goes wrong in it. I have a vague sense that (4) is applying strategy-proofness to a change made by somebody other than the voter the constraint is protecting, but I can't tell whether thats the real complaint or just me being impatient, since (2) does something that looks structurally identical and seems fine.
- if there's a reading of (4) on which every individual move really is licensed, say which later step collapses under that reading instead, and why.
- the theorem is true for three or more alternatives, so a cousin of this argument has to go through there. tell me what the third alternative is doing that x and y jointly can't, and why (4) gets repaired in that setting rather than merely dodged.
- since dictatorship is off the table for |A| = 2, what's the strongest thing you actually can prove about f from onto + strategy-proof in the two-alternative case? I suspect there's a tidy classification hiding here and I'd like it stated, with the reason it stops short of picking out one voter.
