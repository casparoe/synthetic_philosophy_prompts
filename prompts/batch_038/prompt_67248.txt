I did thirty-eight years of sensory physiology and retired into philosophy, which my wife considers a downgrade. Lately I've been chewing on the argument about whether anything has aboutness on its own account or only borrows it from us, and I've been trading cases with a friend from my reading group — ex-chemist, better at this than me. Here's his track record.

The road atlas in my car represents my town, but only because we treat it that way: derived. A smoke alarm really does mean there's smoke, but again borrowed from whoever built it. His labrador originally, non-derivatively believes the ball went under the sofa. A chess program: derived, same category as the atlas. And the molecular talk I used all my career he calls imported metaphor — tRNA doesn't read the codon, an antibody doesn't recognize the epitope, the ribosome doesn't proofread. Those states track things, he'll grant you, but tracking isnt meaning. That's also why he denies the bimetal-strip thermostat any beliefs.

The case I want to spring on him: a sea turtle hatchling that crawls up the beach toward the glow of a parking lot instead of out toward the moonlit water. Does he say its state is about the ocean and the hatchling is getting it wrong, about brightness and it's getting it right, or about the ocean only in the same borrowed way my atlas is? Predict his verdict, tell me which rules fit everything he's said so far — I make it at least three — and what each of them says about the turtle. Then give me the one further case that would force those rules apart for good, and if you think his verdicts really do underdetermine the turtle, say which of them he'd have to give up to settle it.
