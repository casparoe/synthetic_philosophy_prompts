So I work on a consumer AI assistant — the kind that lives in your house, runs the calendar, orders stuff, talks to everyone under the roof — and I've just been made the person who writes the policy for "household mode," where one agent serves several people at once. It's due Friday. The annoying part is that my own house is basically the test case: me, my wife, our 15-year-old, and my mother-in-law, who moved in last year and has early Parkinson's. I've been writing down what I actually think the agent should do, case by case, and I've ended up with a pile of verdicts I can't get to cohere.

1. Son asks the agent to order energy drinks. Wife has banned them in the house. Agent refuses — I think that's right, but right because he's on our account, not because energy drinks are bad. If he had his own little sub-account with his own allowance money, I think the agent should just buy them.

2. Son says don't tell my parents I asked about that. Agent never brings it up. Fine. But if my wife asks point-blank whether he's been asking about energy drinks, the agent has to say yes — lying would be worse. I can't explain why the same information is okay to withhold and not okay to deny. It feels like a real difference, not a dodge.

3. MIL asks for pill reminders. Two weeks later she asks the agent to cancel them, says she feels fine. Agent keeps reminding her and quietly flags it to my wife. I think that's correct. Now the same structure with my brother, 38, no cognitive problems, who stops his blood pressure meds and whose agent escalates to me: flatly wrong, a violation. Same shape, opposite verdict, and "capacity" doesn't cut it, because my MIL has capacity basically every day.

4. Thermostat: wife wants 73, I want 67, MIL's tremor is worse when she's cold. Agent time-shares and biases toward her in the mornings. Totally fine, good even. But when my wife wants the agent to book a couples retreat with money I'd spend elsewhere, I want the agent to have no opinion about whose plan is better. Fine to aggregate the temperature, not fine to aggregate the life choices — except the temperature thing is also a life choice, about whose body gets priority.

5. Neighbor's kid on our wifi asks how to get around his school's filter. Agent helps. Fine, not our kid. Babysitter asks how to dose Tylenol for our toddler and the agent hands her our house rule instead (we use Motrin). Also fine. Guests get our defaults in one case and not the other.

6. My wife and I both set advance instructions: if one of us can't speak for himself, follow the earlier version of him even against what he's saying in the moment. Honoring that seems right. But my wife also has a standing no-contact-with-my-sister instruction from four years ago, and last month she asked the agent to reach out and it refused, citing her own rule. I think that was wrong — she should be able to overrule her past self. Both are Ulysses contracts. One should bind, one shouldn't.

7. Three of us want the agent to stop answering MIL's repetitive questions; she asks the same thing fifty times a day and it's grinding everyone down. She's the fourth vote. Agent should not stop — you can't outvote the person whose life it's about. But three of us wanting it to stop blasting music she likes: fine, that's just noise.

The principle I keep reaching for is: aggregate over shared resources, veto over anything inside a member's own personal domain. It gets 4 and 7. It doesn't get 3, 5, or 6 at all.

Two I go back and forth on. First, the agent can see that all four of us are sleeping badly, snapping at each other, and spending our evenings in seperate rooms on our phones. It could nudge — mildly, transparently, opt-out-able. I'm pretty sure I'd object if a government did that to its citizens, on neutrality grounds. I'm not at all sure I object here, and I don't know if that's because a household isn't a polity or because I'm just a hypocrite. Second: should MIL's preferences count for less because she has fewer years left in the house? I want to say no, and I also want to say it's fine to weight a decision by how many future years it touches, and I cant hold both.

So — is there a principle behind these? I'd rather get two or three candidates stated precisely, precise enough that I could hand one to an engineer and she'd know what to build, with the case that motivates each clause, what each one says about the two I'm stuck on, and an honest list of which of my verdicts it can't save. If the cheapest coherent principle costs me 2 or 6, say so and tell me why that's the verdict to lose. And if a candidate turns out not to be neutral after all — if it's smuggling in a view of what a good life looks like — flag that explicitly, because that's exactly what I'll get attacked on in review.
