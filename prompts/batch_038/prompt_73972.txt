ok so I've been going down a rabbit hole on split-brain patients and something won't click for me. As I understand it, once the corpus callosum is severed the hemispheres can't pass information to each other anymore, full stop — the left hemisphere genuinely doesn't know what the right one just saw, which is why you get the patient pointing at the right object with his left hand and then swearing blind he saw nothing at all. So there are two seperate knowers in there, which is to say two streams of consciousness. Fine, I follow that.

But then the philosophers who insist these patients are still a single conscious subject lean on the observation that outside the lab, in everyday life, the patients look completely normal — one person, one set of intentions, no visible doubling. And that's the part I can't parse. If no information crosses, how does ordinary behaviour prove anything? Either something still leaks across underneath the cortex, or else the fact that they can move their eyes and head means the input gets smeared around so lateralisation stops mattering — but neither of those seems like it gets you back a genuinely unified mind, they just hide the split from casual view. Unless the unity people are quietly changing what counts as unified so that the split-brain evidence can never actually bear against them?

So: what would have to be true for one brain to host a single stream of consciousness despite being cut in half, and is there anything in the literature that could settle the question rather than just re-describe it? Where's the difference between a real argument and a redescription here?
