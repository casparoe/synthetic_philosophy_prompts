I'm prepping a seminar session on armed neutrality and I want a thought experiment that isn't the usual trolley-style stuff about killer robots. Here's the setup, which I've made up — please take it as given rather than trying to repair it.

A small permanently neutral European state (think Switzerland's or Finland's legal position, but just call it the Republic) abolishes conscription outright and replaces its army with what my co-author and I are calling algorithmic armed neutrality: a prepositioned, range-capped denial architecture. Sensor mesh along the border, self-emplacing obstacles, interceptor swarms. No human in the loop for any engagement inside its own territory; and by constitutional amendment nothing it owns can strike beyond its borders, and none of the hardware can be exported. Three claims get made for it: (i) neutrality becomes more credible, since there's no expeditionary capability and so no temptation and no plausible pretext; (ii) the state stops asking anyone to die for it; (iii) deterrence-by-denial goes automatic, so it can't be held hostage to the moods of a cabinet.

So: what would Rousseau have made of this? And I mean the Rousseau of the Social Contract's theory of the general will plus the constitutional advice he gave the Poles and the Genevans, not the sentimental nature-lover people reflexively reach for.

Things I want you to actually work through rather than gesture at:

- The Book II chapter on the right of life and death, where he's clear that the citizen isn't the judge of the danger the law requires him to run, and that when the sovereign says it's expedient for the state that you die, you die. Does claim (ii) look to him like the removal of an abuse, or the removal of the thing that made the pact a pact? Can a republic that is constitutionally incapable of asking anyone to die still be one where citizens have alienated themselves wholly to the whole?
- The representatives chapter of Book III, where he says roughly that a genuinely free people does everything with its own arms and nothing with money, and that paying men to serve in your place puts the state halfway to slavery. An algorithm is neither money nor a mercenary: it has no will, no ambition, can't be bought, and can't march on the capital. On his own criteria, does that make it better or worse than the professional volunteer armies he'd have despised? I want a definite answer here, I dont want a shrug.
- The Book I argument that war is a relation between states rather than between persons, and that the individual is an enemy only accidentally, as an instrument of the state. How does an architecture that engages on signature and can't accept a surrender fair against that? Bear in mind the Republic only ever fires inside its own borders, which I think cuts both ways.
- Sovereignty can't be represented, on his account. Is fixing the rules of engagement in a constitutional amendment an act of general will that then executes itself, or is it the worst sort of alienation — a people handing its executive over to something that can't be recalled, petitioned, or confronted in a square?

Then land somewhere. Would Rousseau sign up to this, would he prefer the everyone-a-soldier-but-no-one-a-soldier-by-trade arrangement the Swiss and Finns actually run, or would he think all three options are symptoms of a people that has already stopped being one? Pick one and defend it against the strongest case you can build for the other two.

One last thing, and it matters for how I use this in class: signal how confident you are as you go, and then name the single point in the whole thing you're least sure about — probably somewhere you're extrapolating from a text where Rousseau just wasn't talking about anything remotely like this. And if any of my attributions of views to him are shaky, tell me that directly rather than quietly working around them.
