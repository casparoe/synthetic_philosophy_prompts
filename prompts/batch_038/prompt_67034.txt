You are a philosopher of medicine with genuine expertise in the determination-of-death literature. Please think carefully before writing, and open with a brief brainstorming pass in which you list the distinct determinations that were actually in dispute in the case, before you analyse anything.

I am building a seminar session on brain death and want to run one real episode through an analytic apparatus of my own — partly to test the apparatus, partly to give students something harder than the usual survey. The apparatus, which I call the four-slot analysis, holds that every determination of death fills four slots that get run together in the literature: (1) criterion — what must be irreversibly lost for the organism to be dead; (2) test — the procedure by which the failure of that criterion is established; (3) modality — what "irreversible" is being taken to mean, i.e. impossible in principle, impossible with currently available technology, or merely permanent because support will not be continued; (4) addressee — to whom the determination is addressed and under whose authority it binds. My working hypothesis is that most celebrated disputes are misfiled as disagreements about slot 1 when they are really disagreements about slot 3 or slot 4.

The episode is the Jahi McMath case. The salient facts, so you need not look anything up: a thirteen-year-old was declared dead by neurological criteria in a California children's hospital in December 2013 following cardiac arrest after surgery for sleep-disordered breathing; a court-appointed independent examiner affirmed the diagnosis on the standard grounds — no electroencephalographic activity, no cerebral blood flow, no respiratory effort off the ventilator; a judge held that she was legally dead but ordered ventilation continued for a further period; the family rejected the determination, and she was released to their custody and transferred to a facility in New Jersey, whose statute bars a neurological death declaration where it would violate the patient's personal religious beliefs. She remained supported for roughly four and a half years, with reports of ongoing growth, wound healing, and the onset of menstruation, until a second death certificate was issued in June 2018 citing hepatic failure and haemorrhage.

Your deliverable is a case analysis running in both directions. First, sort the case into the four slots: identify precisely which slot each contested point occupied, and whether the family and the hospital were even addressing the same slot. Second, test the apparatus: which features of the episode does it explain, which does it misdescribe, and where is it simply silent — say so plainly rather than forcing it to speak. In particular, I want your verdict on three things: whether the New Jersey statute shows slot 4 overriding slot 1 and thus makes the criterion jurisdiction-relative, and what that costs; what the reports of sustained somatic function do to the organism-as-a-whole or somatic-integration critique, as against the whole-brain, higher-brain, and circulatory criteria; and whether the permanent-versus-irreversible distinction that does real work in donation after circulatory death can be imported here to dissolve the dispute or only relocates it. Finally, tell me whether a fifth slot is needed, and name it if so.
