A friend and I spent an hour last night arguing about the World Happiness Report, and I want a referee's judgment rather than another round of the same talking points. Her position: the national rankings are little more than a repackaging of GDP per capita and welfare-state generosity, so Finland's repeated first place tells you nothing about whether Finns are happier than anyone else, and no government should be trying to climb the table. Mine: the ladder question is an imperfect but real measure of something that matters, and the fact that rich countries dominate is a finding, not a fatal confound. Neither of us budged, and I suspect the argument is actually about which objections do the most damage and which are largely absorbed by the survey's design.

So: sort the standard objections to the happiness rankings into tiers by how damaging each one is to the claim that the rankings measure happiness, and say where you think the policy question — should a country be trying to top the table? — comes out once the dust settles. Cover at least the following, and add any two further objections you consider more serious than some of these:

1. the ladder asks people to evaluate their life as a whole rather than to report how they actually feel day to day;
2. cross-national differences in response style, scale use, and what the translated question is heard to ask;
3. hedonic adaptation, so that the long-term sick, the oppressed, and the very poor report better than their circumstances warrant;
4. the concentration of Nordic countries at the top, and what that does to the interpretation of first place;
5. the near-collinearity of the ladder score with national income and the six explanatory variables used in the report's own regressions;
6. the possibility that respondents answer with reference to their national circumstances, or to what they think the survey wants, rather than to their own lives;
7. the absence of any measure of distribution within a country — the ranking is silent on who inside it is miserable;
8. the fragility of the ordering among adjacent countries, given sampling error and year-to-year movement.

Return the result as a single JSON object with the following fields:
- "objections": an array of objects, each with "id" (a short slug), "statement" (one sentence, in your own words), "tier" (one of A, B, C, D, where A is decisive against the rankings and D is real but largely absorbed by the survey's design or fixable), "severity" (an integer from 0 to 10, where 10 means the objection defeats the rankings outright), "why_here" (one sentence of reasoning), and "what_would_move_it" (one sentence naming the empirical result or design change that would raise or lower this objection's severity).
- "ordering": an array of the ids, ranked from most to least damaging.
- "policy_verdict": one of "should_try_to_top_it", "should_track_but_not_maximize", or "should_ignore_the_table".
- "policy_reason": two sentences defending that verdict, and naming the single objection that does most of the work in getting you there.

Order the array itself by severity as well, so that a reader can check your tiers against your scores; if two objections genuinely tie, say so in "why_here" rather than inventing an order.
