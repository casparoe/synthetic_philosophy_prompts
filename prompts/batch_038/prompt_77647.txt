I'm a third-year phil grad student and in three weeks I'm giving a 20-minute paper at a small social choice / formal ethics workshop, with Q&A after. Rough thesis:

Everyone treats Harsanyi's aggregation theorem as an argument *for* utilitarianism, and then spends all their energy arguing about where the equal weights come from -- the veil, the 1/n assumption, interpersonal comparability. I want to say that's the wrong target. The theorem is a result about the *form* of social evaluation -- linearity in individual expected utilities -- and nothing in it pins down the weights. So the only moral views it can actually damage are ones that make a claim about form, and the big one that does is prioritarianism: a concave reweighting of individual well-being is exactly the sort of thing that stops social preference over lotteries from being a linear combination. Utilitarianism isn't the theorem's conclusion; it's what you get if you bolt on an equal-weights premise that the theorem doesn't supply and doesn't need.

My twist, and the part I most want pressure-tested: a distinction I'm making load-bearing between *aggregation-form* and *aggregation-weights*. Slogan -- a theorem can only refute views that speak its language. On this reading, the two stock objections ("it presupposes interpersonal comparability", "the equal-probability assumption smuggles in egalitarianism") both attack the weights side, so they never touch the theorem, while prioritarianism sits on the form side and gets flattened. And I want to claim the assumption doing all the real work is the shared-beliefs one: once people assign different probabilities, the consistency results go bad and the form result evaporates, which means the theorem's reach is narrower then its reputation -- it only bites on societies that already agree about the facts.

Audience, about fifteen people, and the composition is what matters here. Two formal social choice types: one works on aggregation under uncertainty and thinks the theorem is trivial and the philosophical fuss around it is embarrassing; the other works on state-dependent utility and Anscombe-Aumann representations and will want to know precisely which representation I'm leaning on. One moral philosopher, non-formal, a fairly commited prioritarian who's read Parfit and Adler and will insist the theorem is aimed at a strawman because it aggregates over lotteries rather than over outcomes, so ex post prioritarianism never enters the frame at all. One behavioral economist who'll say vNM rationality is empirically false anyway, so who cares what follows from it. Rest are grad students, mixed, including a couple of formal epistemology people who care about disagreement and priors.

Build me the prep sheet for the question period: the seven or eight objections I'll actually get from *this* room -- not the generic ones you'd hand any defender of Harsanyi -- ordered by how much damage they'd do if I fumble, each phrased the way that particular person would phrase it, jargon they'd reach for included. For each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes while standing up, plus one line on where the reply is soft or where I'd basically be bluffing. Then the question I'm praying nobody asks.

Two things before you write it. First, no diplomacy: if you think the form/weights distinction doesn't hold -- say because the equal-weights premise and the linearity result are more entangled than I'm pretending, or because "prioritarianism is a claim about form" is just false, since ex post prioritarianism is linear in something relevant -- tell me that up front, in plain terms, before the prep sheet, and don't cushion it.
