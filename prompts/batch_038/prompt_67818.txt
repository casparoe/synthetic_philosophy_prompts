I am setting the final examination for an upper-level undergraduate course on political concepts, and I need one essay question on whether the distinction between left and right is real. I want the difficulty to come from a concrete case that students have to work through, not from their ability to reproduce a survey of positions. Please write the question as students will see it, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The question must be built around the following table, which will be printed on the paper. It gives the platforms of five parties in an invented country on four issues. Reproduce the table inside the question; do not rename the parties, and do not add or remove rows.

| | (1) Taxation and transfers | (2) Public morality and religion | (3) Immigration | (4) Policing, surveillance, sentencing |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Party A | steeply progressive taxation; generous universal transfers | secular state; expand personal autonomy in family and bodily matters | open immigration | shrink policing, surveillance and sentence length; decriminalise most vice offences |
| Party B | low flat taxation; transfers limited to a minimal safety net | secular state; no enforcement of any moral code | open immigration, including free movement of labour | minimal coercion beyond the enforcement of contract and property |
| Party C | progressive taxation and generous transfers, restricted to citizens by descent | established religion taught in state schools; restrictions on abortion and same-sex marriage | small annual quota, with compulsory assimilation | expanded policing, longer sentences, broad preventive surveillance |
| Party D | low taxation and few transfers, but tariffs and subsidies protecting domestic industry | personally religious and traditionalist; opposes state enforcement of morality | limited to high-skilled and investor categories | tough on property crime and border enforcement; permissive about private conduct |
| Party E | steeply progressive taxation; generous universal transfers | formally secular, but willing to censor speech judged reactionary and to regulate religious bodies' public activity | restrictive, justified on wage and housing grounds rather than cultural ones | expanded coercion; heavy surveillance of political opponents; long sentences for corruption and sabotage |

The question should ask students to decide whether these five platforms can be placed on a single left–right line, and then — this is the real task — to say what that verdict does and does not establish about the reality of the distinction. It must not be answerable by recounting where the labels historically come from, by reciting the familiar two-dimensional economic/cultural map, or by announcing that left and right are obsolete. State to students what will count: explicit engagement with the pattern of agreements and disagreements in the table, and an argument rather than a taxonomy.

Deliverables:

1. **The question.** Self-contained, 45 minutes, 25 marks, no required readings and no named authors students would have to recall.
2. **A model answer** of about 700 words that commits to one determinate conclusion about the table and defends it through to the philosophical upshot.
3. **A marking scheme** of six must-hit points whose marks sum to 25, each with a one-line note on the error that forfeits those marks. At least three points must reward argumentative moves rather than content. One point must require students to confront the fact that five platforms can always be spread across enough dimensions to fit perfectly, so that a multi-dimensional redescription is not automatically a discovery: the answer must say what further evidence would be needed before it earns that name. Another point must require them to handle the relational character of the terms — a platform is left only relative to other platforms at a time — and to explain why the drift of content over the decades is therefore not by itself an argument that the distinction is unreal.
4. **An explicit statement of the A/B boundary**: which specific moves separate a first-class answer from a competent second-class one that has the right material in the wrong order.

Two responses I expect almost every student to reach for, and which the marking scheme must catch rather than merely tolerate: "left and right are dead, we need at least two axes," asserted as a conclusion rather than argued for; and the claim that Party A or Party E and Party C are secretly adjacent because they agree on immigration and state coercion. In each case, say what a student must actually do to earn the marks instead of repeating the slogan.

End your reply with a single line of the exact form `Verdict: <LABEL>`, where `<LABEL>` is whichever of these the model answer defends: REAL-1D (the five platforms do admit a coherent single left–right ordering), REAL-NOT-1D (the distinction is genuine but is not a single policy axis), COALITIONAL (the labels name a contingent historical coalition with no shared content), ARBITRARY (the distinction carries no explanatory content at all).
