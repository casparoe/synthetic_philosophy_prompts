I cover science and engineering for a general-interest magazine, and I've spent the past year on stories in which people argue about whether a latent property survives something that would stop it from ever showing up. I've come away with a set of gut verdicts I cannot fully reconcile, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

A tank of reactive monomer stored with an inhibitor that neutralizes it on contact with air: still reactive, I think — the inhibitor conceals the reactivity rather than removing it.

A high-voltage line wired to a device that drains the charge the instant anything makes contact: not live, I want to say, because everything an engineer would safely do with that line follows from the negative verdict.

A viral strain that would establish infection in almost anyone, in a person whose vaccine-induced immunity clears it before it takes hold: the strain is still infectious; the person is simply not susceptible.

A gene that would produce a disease phenotype, in a genome carrying a second mutation that silences it: here I want to say the gene is no longer a gene for the disease.

An intact glass that is not brittle, in a world where something would guarantee it shatters if struck: not fragile, despite the guarantee.

What I can't get straight is the pattern across them. In the inhibitor and charge-drain cases the preventer is extrinsic in both and acts only when the stimulus arrives, yet I keep the disposition in one and drop it in the other. In the viral and genetic cases I do the reverse: I keep the disposition when the preventer sits in the other party and drop it when the preventer is built into the bearer, as though where the preventer sits is precisely what matters. Both patterns cannot be right, and the glass case seems to rule out the simplest fix, since there the guarantee of manifestation fails to confer anything at all.

Is there a single principle that delivers these verdicts, or nearly all of them? Please state it in language I could put in print without jargon, identify which clause each verdict depends on, and say what it predicts for cases I haven't raised: a drug fatal to anyone who takes it but never dispensed without an antidote; a structural cable rated to fail under a load a redundancy system would always redistribute first; and a radioactive source sealed in shielding it can never escape. Close by naming the verdicts your principle cannot save, and tell me whether the cost of saving all of them is an account too baroque to be worth believing.
