I am at work on a novel whose second protagonist is a type theorist, and in preparation I have been reading as widely as a layperson responsibly can. My brother-in-law, who writes dependently typed code for a living and has been patient with my questions, keeps returning to a single formulation: a proof is a program, and a proposition is the type of the things that program produces. He does not offer this as a curiosity. He uses it as an argument, and the argument is that constructive mathematics is the only part of the subject that genuinely runs; that a classical existence proof is a promissory note with no cash behind it; and that the awe mathematicians report when they establish something non-constructively is nostalgia for a Platonism their own methods have already discredited. I want to give my character an honest interior life, which means not letting a clever outsider's slogan stand in for the mathematics.

So I would like the analogy audited rather than admired. Set out the correspondence explicitly -- what answers to the program, the type, the type-checker, the input, the act of running the thing, the bug -- and mark the points at which the mapping begins to strain. Pay particular attention to features of programming that the analogy quietly imports and that mathematics may simply lack. Then separate the disanalogies that actually bear on my brother-in-law's conclusion from the ones that are merely local colour, and tell me whether that conclusion survives: is a non-constructive proof really a claim about nothing executable, and is the intuitionist's advantage the one he says it is, or has the analogy been carrying the entire weight of his case? If it is the wrong instrument for his purpose, say what would serve better, or whether he should abandon the picture and argue plainly.

Please keep the whole response, correspondence list included, to a hard maximum of 700 words. I would far rather have a compressed and decisive audit than a tour of the territory.
