Been chewing on this for a while and I want a proper audit rather than a tour of the debate — assume I already know the stock objections to the hedonistic imperative, I've read them all. Here's my package, roughly:

1. Suffering is the only thing that's intrinsically bad. Pleasure isn't intrinsically good; its worth having mainly as ballast, a way of keeping people from being miserable in the first place.
2. Given (1) I'm an abolitionist in the strong sense: we should aim at re-engineering the biosphere so that nothing is capable of suffering above some very low threshold. Not just humans — I weight wild animals heavily, since theres so much more of them.
3. I'm against wireheading and against any version of the project that just cranks the pleasure dial. Part of what I want is for people and animals to stay hooked up to reality, responsive to how things actually are. A blissed-out population in a vat is a failure of the project, not its success.
4. Motivation has to survive, and I think it can. I lean on the wanting/liking dissociation and on pain-asymbolia type cases: you can have a state that carries the information "tissue damage, avoid this" and drives avoidance learning without any hedonic sting at all. So I don't buy "you can't take away the badness and keep the signal." I've started calling my version signal-preserving abolitionism.
5. But gradients. I'm into the set-point stuff — I want the baseline moved up so ordinary life sits somewhere mildly pleasant rather than mildly awful, and I think that's a real improvement on mere zero-suffering, not just a nicer route to it.
6. Consent: I would not support engineering a particular human child to have a raised set point without any way of asking them, but I have no problem at all with engineering dogs, mice, or eventually wild populations. What matters morally is the capacity to suffer, not the capacity to say yes.
7. I reject the doing/allowing distinction as morally basic.

The case I keep reaching for: a labrador that feels the needle go in but minds it about as much as you mind your shirt on your back. I'd make that dog tomorrow.

So — does this hang together? Tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other, which only look like they do, and for the real ones what has to go. Be surgical rather than comprehensive: I don't need every whiff of inconsistency flagged, and I especially don't want the ones that turn out to be nothing scored the same as the ones that are fatal. I'm not telling you which of these I'd be most willing to drop, because I honestly don't know yet.

Format: a table with exactly these columns, sorted by severity descending, and only include rows you score 2 or above:
- "pair": the two commitment numbers involved (or a number plus the labrador case)
- "verdict": genuine / apparent / none
- "severity": 1–5, where 5 means something has to be abandoned outright
- "mechanism": one or two sentences on why they conflict, or why the conflict dissolves on inspection
