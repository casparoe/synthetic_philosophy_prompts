A reading group of mine on the philosophy of pain keeps reaching for the line "pain is bad because it hurts." Last week a senior member used it to close down a discussion of pain asymbolia — the condition, typically following brain injury, in which people can still localize and describe painful stimulation yet seem unperturbed by it. He took the line to establish two things at once: that asymbolic pain is not really bad, and that we therefore have no special reason to relieve it. I suspect the sentence cannot carry both, but I cannot say where it slips.

Could you lay out the distinct precise claims this one-liner might be making? Vary the force of the "because" (analysis, grounding, causation, evidence), what "hurts" is meant to pick out (a phenomenal quality, a negative attitude, a disposition to withdraw or protest), and what kind of badness is in play (bad for the subject, bad simpliciter, reason-giving for others). For each reading, give a verdict — platitude, substantive but defensible, or false — and say whether it supports his two-step move, noting in particular which readings turn on whether asymbolics lack a felt quality or merely a capacity to care. Present the readings as a numbered menu rather than defending one of them.

Begin with your bottom line in a single sentence — can the line do the work he asked of it? — then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
