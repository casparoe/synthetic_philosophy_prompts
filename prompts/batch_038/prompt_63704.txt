ok so i TA a section that's half philosophy of law and half "how much can you trust intuitions," and every single semester somebody drags us back to the chairman case — you know the one, chairman says he doesn't care about the environment either way, just wants profit, learns the new program will harm it (or help it) as a side effect, shrugs, goes ahead, and then like four out of five people say he harmed the environment intentionally while under a quarter say he helped it intentionally. I want to make a handout for the last session that's actually funny instead of another summary slide.

Can you write me a parody of a pattern jury instruction manual — that flat, numbered, deadpan voice of those "Committee on Model Criminal Instructions" booklets, where every instruction is followed by a Committee Comment explaining the drafting choices in the most bureaucratically pleased tone imaginable — for a jurisdiction that has formally written the asymmetry into its law of mens rea. Call it 650-750 words, maybe seven or eight numbered instructions with comments, and invent the case names and committee members rather than using any real people or real citations. The funny parts need to be funny because the drafting is faithful to the arguments, not because legislatures are generically dumb.

Here's the stuff I want worked in, since the details are the point: an instruction covering side effects that are good for some parties and bad for others, where the committee has committed itself to some deeply embarrassing apportionment arithmetic; a brand-new offense category for praiseworthy side effects, drafted so carefully that it can never actually be charged; a defendant's motion, disposed of in one smug comment, arguing that the jury's "yes" in the harm case was never a conceptual verdict at all but conversational pragmatics — that people use the word to convey blame, and the statute has mistaken an implicature for a competence; a comment where the committee considers and rejects rival explanations, specifically that what matters is whether the agent actually weighed the effect (he entertained the harm and dismissed it, he never entertained the help) or whether the act expresses something deep about what the person cares about; a note on why the committee declined to just poll philosophy graduate students, since a member raised the worry that the whole edifice depends on which population you happen to ask; and a closing chapter extending the same valence-sensitive rule to whether the agent caused the outcome and whether he acted freely, because that's where the reductio really bites. Two hard constraints: never voice any of these as objections — the committee should be wholly sincere and think it's tidying up a messy area of law, and the recoiling is the reader's job — and keep the whole thing legible to a smart undergrad who's met the chairman case exactly once.
