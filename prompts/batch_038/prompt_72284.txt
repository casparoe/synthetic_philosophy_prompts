**Role.** You are a professor of philosophy of religion who regularly sets dialectical exercises for advanced graduate seminars. Your readers already know the standard Molinist apparatus and the standard open-theist alternative; write at that level and do not pause to define basics.

**Task.** Write a dialogue, of roughly 1,300 to 1,600 words, between two equally accomplished philosophers who disagree about divine foreknowledge and providence, and who work through a single constructed case together.

- **Dr. Adaeze Nwosu** defends Molinism: on her view God's knowledge divides into natural knowledge (all necessities and all mere possibilities), middle knowledge (the true counterfactuals of creaturely freedom — what any possible free creature would freely do in any complete circumstance in which it might be placed), and free knowledge (the actual world, given God's decree). Middle knowledge is logically prior to the decree; God does not make its contents true but consults them in deciding which world to strongly actualize, and by strongly actualizing some circumstances God weakly actualizes the free choices the true counterfactuals assign to them.
- **Dr. Peter Lindqvist** holds the open view: God knows all that there is to know, but what a free creature will do on an occasion that has not yet arrived is not yet anything at all, so it is not a gap in God's knowledge to say there is nothing there for Him to find. Providence on his picture is attentive, responsive, and genuinely risky, and petitionary prayer matters in a way it cannot if the future is already settled.

**The case: the reflexive petition.** Treat this as invented for the exercise; neither character should imply that it reports anything real.

Amara must decide, on a given day, whether to take on the full-time care of her estranged brother after his stroke — an undertaking she would find ruinous and resentful, and yet would not refuse if it were asked of her in the right way. Daniel, who loves them both, prays that God bring it about, by Amara's own free choice, that she takes the post. Now consider two counterfactuals of creaturely freedom:

- **CF1:** If Amara were, that day, in complete circumstance *C* — containing no indication from God about the matter — she would freely take the post.
- **CF2:** If Amara were, that day, in complete circumstance *C\** — exactly like *C* except that God has by then given her an unmistakable but non-coercive indication that taking the post is what is being asked of her — she would freely take the post.

The wrinkle: the circumstance in CF2 contains a feature produced by God's own decision whether to give the indication — a decision that, on the Molinist ordering, would have to be informed by CF2's truth value before it is made. (Lindqvist may sharpen this further: if the indication is to be unmistakable, it plausibly discloses not only what is wanted but that Daniel asked, so *C\** also encodes God's intention to act on that petition.)

**What each philosopher should do with the case.**

Lindqvist should argue that CF2 exhibits a vicious circle: its truth value would have to be settled in advance of the very divine decision that fixes part of its antecedent, and the antecedent is therefore indeterminate precisely where it matters. He should maintain that *C\** is not a complete circumstance at all, since the specification leaves open what else the indication reveals and how Amara takes it, and that no amount of further specification can close the gap without smuggling in the decree. He should also press the grounding worry for counterfactuals whose antecedents will never be satisfied. He should not stop there: he should acknowledge the feasibility constraint — that whether God can weakly actualize the sign-plus-compliance outcome depends on CF2's truth value, which is not up to God — and press the point that this makes God's providential options hostage to facts more brute, and less responsive to prayer, than his own view allows.

Nwosu should deny any circle, distinguishing logical priority from causal dependence: middle knowledge is presupposed by the decree without being produced by it, and the indication is simply one more feature of a creaturely situation, no different in kind from a letter Amara might have received that morning. She should insist that God knows, for every possible specification of *C\**, what Amara would freely do, including specifications in which the indication discloses God's providential intentions; that His deliberation is over which of the feasible worlds to strongly actualize, not over what the counterfactuals say; and that if God in fact gives no indication, CF2 is a true counterpossible-adjacent conditional about a merely possible situation, exactly like the countless others in His middle knowledge. She should also confront the objection that an unmistakable indication is not really non-coercive — that Lindqvist is caught between saying the sign removes freedom and saying it cannot be unmistakable — and she should address the charge that her counterfactuals have no ground.

**Rules.**

- Do not name, cite, or quote any real philosopher, theologian, work, council, or article, and do not invent scholarly attributions, page numbers, journal titles, or a bibliography. Real names may appear only as the labels of the two speakers. Where a character is tempted to appeal to authority, have her or him give the argument instead.
- Do not quote or paraphrase scripture, and have neither character appeal to proof-texts.
- Do not have the characters refer to real-world events, surveys, or history.
- Neither interlocutor may be a strawman. Lindqvist's position must not degenerate into an ignorance-and-panic caricature of open theism, and Nwosu's must not collapse into a determinism she would disown. Give each the strongest case available.
- The two must genuinely engage: each should answer the point the other has actually just made, distinguish the reading on which an objection bites from the reading on which it does not, press for a disambiguation when the other's formulation is slippery, and concede only what is forced — and then say what survives the concession.
- Use the technical vocabulary freely (middle knowledge, weak and strong actualization, complete individual notion, grounding objection, feasibility) without glossing it.
- Avoid alternating mini-essays. Allow interruptions, short rejoinders, restatements of the opponent's point to check it, and at least two moments where one of them is temporarily stuck.

**Required structure.**

1. **Planning notes** (about 150–250 words, before the dialogue): sketch each side's three strongest moves, ordered by force; identify two points at which the reflexive petition admits of more than one reading, since the debate will turn on those; and state what each character concedes, and at which point in the exchange.
2. **The dialogue** itself, with speaker names and a brief scene-setting line at the top.
3. **Postscript** (about 120–200 words): in the voice of each character in turn, one or two sentences stating what they now take the crux to be — the single question on which everything between them turns — and one sentence naming the strongest point the other made that they would need to answer if the exchange were repeated. Neither may declare victory.

Think carefully before drafting, and be prepared to revise the planning notes once you see how the exchange actually develops. Do not resolve the disagreement: end with it located more precisely than it began. Some narrowing is welcome — the two may discover, for instance, that they agree about more of God's providential exposure to risk than the opening positions suggested, or that Lindqvist's unknowability claim is narrower than Nwosu first assumed — but only where that discovery costs neither side their position.
