I'm drafting the final essay for a medical ethics module and would value a worked model of how to argue one particular version of the advance-directive problem through to a definite conclusion, rather than a survey of the debate.

Our readings have covered the familiar confrontation. On one side sits the view that a competent person's earlier refusal continues to govern because her life has a narrative integrity that survives her incompetence — the line that treats her enduring critical interests as authoritative over her present experiential ones. On the other sits the view that what morally matters is the welfare of the person now in the bed, whose experiential life may be entirely good and who no longer holds any of the values the directive expresses. I don't want to re-run that argument in the abstract, because the case I want the essay to turn on is designed to short-circuit it.

The case: a woman in her late fifties, diagnosed with early-onset dementia, draws up an advance directive refusing antibiotics for any future pneumonia and refusing artificial nutrition. But she adds a clause her solicitor warned her was unusual. It says, in substance, that if a time comes when she is no longer troubled by her losses — when she is content, warmly attached to the people around her, and evidently no longer identifies with the values that produced this document — then the document is void, and she is to be treated as the person she has become rather than the person she was. Seven years later that time has come. She is cheerful, sings in a day centre, greets her daughter with pleasure, has no recollection of her professional life, and has developed pneumonia. She plainly lacks capacity to make treatment decisions. Her daughter wants the clause honoured and antibiotics given; her son says his mother foresaw exactly this moment of sentimental weakness in the family and that honouring the clause is a betrayal of the person who wrote it.

Write an essay that decides what should be done, and defends the decision. It needs to address at least three things: whether a directive that authorises its own override is coherent at all, or whether it is more like a self-untying Ulysses contract that cannot be simultaneously binding and self-cancelling; what the clause does to the usual identity-based objections to precedent autonomy, given that the earlier self has already conceded the point about her later self's values; and who, if anyone, the clause actually delegates to, since the person it names as decision-maker has no decisional capacity — whether that makes the clause empty, or forces a reading on which surrogates must apply a substituted judgement indexed to the new self rather than the old. A conditional verdict is welcome if the conditions are stated precisely and defended; a shrug is not.

Please write in British English. Where you take a stand, distinguish clearly between what is genuinely settled among people who know this literature and what remains contested, and don't let a minority position travel as the consensus view — that's the mistake I'm most likely to make with this material.

After the essay, give me a single JSON object with exactly these fields: "verdict" (one sentence stating what should be done); "conditions" (array of strings: circumstances under which the verdict would reverse, empty if none); "load_bearing_claims" (array of objects, each with "claim" as a string and "status" as one of "settled", "contested", or "original_to_this_essay"); "strongest_objection" (string); "reply" (string); and "effect_on_the_debate" (one or two sentences on whether the self-voiding clause ultimately strengthens or undermines the case for the authority of the earlier self).
