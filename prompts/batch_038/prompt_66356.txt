I write about the behavioural sciences for a general-audience magazine, and I am working on a piece about how the handicap principle travelled out of mate choice and into accounts of religious ritual, luxury goods, and political credibility. One of the people I interviewed sketched the following chain for me, and it ends somewhere I can neither print nor refute.

(1) A receiver can rationally trust a signal only if a sender lacking the advertised quality would not want to send it.
(2) Such a sender would not want to send it only if sending costs that sender more than being believed is worth to them.
(3) A cost borne equally by all senders cannot do that work: it subtracts the same amount from the impostor's payoff and from the honest sender's, and a subtraction that makes sending unattractive to one makes it unattractive to the other.
(4) So reliability requires a differential cost, one that bites harder on the low-quality sender than on the high-quality one.
(5) A differential cost of that kind is what Zahavi had in mind by a handicap.
(6) Therefore every reliable signal is a handicap, and the more it wastes, the more it can be trusted.
(7) The same logic governs human institutions: a degree, a large donation, a public fast, a vow of celibacy are credible in proportion to what they cost the person least able to bear them.
(8) But if reliability can always be purchased with more waste, displays should escalate without limit, until the ruin they cause destroys the very quality being advertised and the system collapses.
(9) Hence (1) is false, and in any population with conflicting interests no signal can be trusted.

(9) flatly contradicts a large empirical literature about signals that demonstrably do work, so something in the chain is wrong; I cannot tell whether it is invalid or merely built on a false premise. Tell me which step or steps to reject, and why each one looked acceptable when I first met it. Then price the alternatives: what do I lose if I deny (4) instead, or (6)? And what becomes of the chain under each of the four positions I keep running into — Zahavi's original claim about waste, Grafen's formalisation, the index-signals account on which reliability comes from physical constraint rather than cost, and the result that honest signalling among relatives can be cost-free? Relatedly, does the finding that costly signalling can be stable yet worse for everyone than no signalling at all bear on (6) and (8)? Finally, if forced to choose I would rather abandon (7) than the biological core; is that the right sacrifice, and what is the most I can safely tell a general reader without being wrong?
