You are a poet with substantial training in analytic philosophy, preparing a piece for an anthology of verse about collective action problems. Before drafting, think carefully about the mechanics of the form you are using: sketch the recycling map (which lines return, and where), and for each returning line state what it will mean the second time it is heard, since the argument of a pantoum lives entirely in the shift of context rather than in any single line. Show that map briefly, then give the poem.

The subject is the unilateralist's curse: the way an initiative becomes more likely to be carried out the more independent people each judge it net-beneficial and are each willing to undertake it themselves, even though nearly all of them would have voted against it had they been made to decide together. Every actor in your poem is well-meaning, informed, and not especially reckless; the harm arrives through the number of hands rather than through any one hand.

Constraints:

- A pantoum of five to seven quatrains, closing in the traditional way: the poem's first line returns as its final line, and the third line of the opening stanza returns as the second line of the closing stanza.
- The recycled lines carry only what a single actor can see — the local calculation, every part of it respectable. The fresh lines carry what no single actor can see: the other hands, the tallying, the aggregate.
- The scaling must be made visible rather than asserted. A reader who knows the argument should be able to point to the specific lines where the number of actors does the work, and to the structural reason the harm outruns each individual's estimate of it.
- By the closing stanza, a careful reader should be unable to locate the decision anywhere in the poem — there is no line in which anyone chooses badly — while still recognizing the returning first line as the moment the thing is done.
- Do not name the concept, do not moralize, do not allow any stanza the consolation that nobody meant to, and do not let the closing stanza offer a remedy. Keep the diction concrete and ordinary; no personified abstractions.
