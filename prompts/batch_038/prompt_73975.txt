DRAFT

Everyone agrees the snapshot view of doing vs allowing has to say something about the shape of the act at a moment: what the agent's body is doing, and whether the fatal sequence was already underway before she touched anything. Both of the standard moves — the act-shape move and the causal-origin move — try to settle the question from that single time-slice. I want to argue the time-slice is the wrong unit. What matters is the history of the agent's involvement in the protective arrangement she is now withdrawing.

Call an agent a custodian of a barrier when (i) the barrier stands between a person and a threat, (ii) the agent's continuing activity is what keeps it standing, and (iii) the person's own capacity to survive without the barrier has been worn down by the agent's having supplied it. (iii) is the part I'm adding, and note that it's diachronic — it's a fact about a period, not a moment. On this account, withdrawing support from someone whose independence you eroded isn't letting die. It's a killing stretched out over the whole period of the arrangement, and the withdrawal is only the last installment.

The case I want to run it on: an aid agency drills a borehole and puts a pump in a village. Before the pump, the village drew water from three shallow wells it maintained communally, and the siting and lining skills were held by a dozen people. Over twelve years the wells silt up and nobody re-digs them, because it's pointless labour; the people who knew how to line a well die or move away; the population roughly doubles, because water is now reliable. In year thirteen the agency's funding ends and it pulls the pump out. People die of thirst and of waterborne disease. On the snapshot view the agency introduced no threat, removed nothing that was ever the villagers' own, and left in place a fatal sequence — drought, contaminated surface water — that was there the whole time. So it allowed. On mine, it killed, and the fact that the fatal move was an omission is beside the point. (I should say here that I'm not relying on any doctrine of double effect machinery, which anyway makes permissibility turn on whether the harm is intended or merely foreseen plus whether the good is big enough. And I'm not relying on the trolley either — Foot's version, where the bystander has to decide whether to shove a heavy man off the bridge, is a case about using someone, and mine isn't.)

The account is supposed to cut both ways, and this is where I most need pressure. A rescue swimmer tows an exhausted person for fifty minutes and lets go twenty metres from shore because her own legs have gone. Snapshot-causally, she was the barrier, and her letting go is what causes the death. My view says: allowing, and plainly so — she never wore down anything of his, she improved his position relative to where he started at every moment of the tow, and the fatal sequence was always independent of her. Same for a technician who deactivates a pacemaker at a competent patient's request: the conduction disease is entirely independent of the technician, and there was no cardiac capacity of the patient's left to erode. So the classification doesn't track how deliberate, skilled, or tool-mediated the final act is. Two withdrawals that look identical at the moment of withdrawal get different verdicts, and only a historical account can say why.

Objection one, which I think I can answer: this is just the assumed-duty / special-obligation account in disguise, since lifeguards have duties because they took the job. But the duty account explains why you may not withdraw; it doesn't explain why the withdrawal is a killing rather than a failure to rescue. My claim is about the kind of wrong, not its weight, and (iii) can obtain with no promise, no contract and no role — the agency never undertook to stay forever.

Objection two, which I'm less sure about: your view makes every improvement hazardous, since if helping anyone can ever reduce their independence then you have a standing reason not to help, and that's absurd. My reply is that the requirement doesn't come out as 'don't withdraw' but as 'build redundantly' — help in ways that leave the other party's own capacities intact — which is roughly what good development practice says anyway.

Then I want to end by conceding that none of this is usable in law, because criminal liability needs a time-slice: act or omission, fixed at a date. A diachronic test makes the classification turn on a decade of history a jury can't pin down, so doctrine has to fall back on duties of care. I can't decide whether that paragraph is an honest cost or whether it gives the whole thing away.
