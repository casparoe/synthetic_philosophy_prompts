I convene a graduate seminar on explanation in the historical sciences, jointly with a colleague in economic history who has been admirably tolerant of my philosophical pestering, and I am currently rebuilding the reading list for Michaelmas term around the Great Divergence and the older formulation of the same puzzle that goes by Needham's name. Working through that material has left me with a dissatisfaction I have not been able to articulate, and I want to know whether the thing I am circling already has a name in the methodological literature, or whether it needs one coined for it.

The pattern is this. A contrastive question is posed: why did sustained industrialisation get going in England rather than in the Yangzi Delta? An answer is offered as a list of features the first case had and the second lacked — accessible coal, a distinctive wage-and-energy price structure, the ecological windfall from the New World, certain property rights, a certain scientific culture. A critic then observes that the feature was present in the other case as well, merely elsewhere or earlier: China had coal too, in the north; the Dutch Republic had high wages, cheap fuel, dense markets and a vigorous natural knowledge, and did not industrialise; the same institutional arrangement can be found in Song China. And here is the move I want named. The answer is not withdrawn and the feature is not given up. Instead the comparison is quietly narrowed until the feature differentiates again: not England but Lancashire; not China but the Lower Yangzi; not coal but coal within economical haulage of a navigable waterway; not the Dutch Republic but the Dutch Republic together with its lack of a continental hinterland. No narrowing is announced as a revision of the question, and each one is individually unimpeachable — of course we should compare like with like, and of course the sensible unit is a region rather than a civilisation. Cumulatively, though, the effect is that the comparator is fixed once the outcome is already known, and then re-fixed whenever the nominated difference turns out not to be a difference, so that the answer is never genuinely at risk. I saw the identical shape in a student essay last term on why a particular empire collapsed rather than merely contracted: each differentiator was met with the observation that other empires had it too and did not collapse, and each time the comparison class shrank, from empires to agrarian empires to agrarian empires without a maritime frontier, until the differentiators were once again differentiating.

What I want from you is not a label but a carving. Begin by telling me plainly whether this has an established name, and if it does not, say so and supply one, with a definition and with what it rules out. I suspect the honest answer will be that I have been describing a composite of several things I only half-know — choosing cases on the strength of the outcome, the tidiness that hindsight lends a story, the limits Mill himself put on the method of difference where the cases cannot be made to agree in all other circumstances, and whatever the philosophical literature on contrastive explanation says about who is entitled to fix the alternative against which a fact is explained — but I want each of those identified precisely, told how the pattern above differs from it, and told which comes closest and why. I also want the boundary drawn on the other side, since there must be cases where narrowing the comparison is not a rescue at all but simply the right refinement, and I should like a criterion that separates the two, plus one or two genuinely hard cases that sit on the line. Please give me your bottom line in the first sentence and the reasoning after it, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
