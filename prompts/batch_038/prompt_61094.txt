Just spent three hours arguing with my friend Theo about Good's theorem and I'm pretty sure we were mostly talking past each other, so I want a third party to sort out which of us is confused.

Theo's take: the theorem — expected value of information is never negative for a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer — basically settles whether an AI assistant should ever withhold stuff from its user, and the answer is never. His reason is that the real content of the theorem is the fallback: you can always just act as though you hadn't learned anything, so the informed menu of options contains the uninformed one, so learning weakly dominates. On his view anything that looks like a counterexample is really just a cost of getting the information, and costs get handled by subtracting some c from the informed branch, not by claiming the theorem fails.

I pushed back with two cases and he wouldn't budge.

One: a user has a standing instruction to be told everything the assistant learns about their grandmother's condition. The assistant finds out the prognosis is bad and can also predict that on hearing it the user will drop out of their program and move home — a choice the user would rank badly right now but will rank highly once they know. So the two branches are being scored by different utility functions, and the person who has to decide whether to set up that information flow in the first place is the ignorant one, whose utility only gets overridden *because* they made that decision. I said that's circular and the theorem just doesn't get a grip. Theo said Im confusing a change in preferences with a cost.

Two: a user is mid-negotiation and the assistant's message lands in a thread the other side can read, so the learning is itself a move that changes what the counterpart does next. Theo said fine, that's your c, it's just a big one. I said no — the set of acts available afterwards is literally different from the set available before, so behaving-as-if-uninformed isn't on the menu anymore, and you're not comparing a max of averages against an average of maxes on the same matrix. There's no constant to subtract because what it costs depends on what you found out and on what they infer from the fact that you found out.

Can you explain the actual conceptual issue here? Concretely: which assumption in the standard proof each of my two cases strains — I know there's the invariance condition on utilities across learning events, something about the act set staying fixed, and something about the future self conditionalising and maximising, but I can't tell which is doing the work where; whether Theo's subtract-a-cost maneuver is a legitimate general way to absorb apparent counterexamples or whether it's quietly redefining the theorem until it's trivial; whether the you-can-always-ignore-it gloss is genuinely equivalent to the inequality or smuggles in an availability assumption; and what has to be true for it to even make sense to ask whether ignorant-me or informed-me is better off, given that they don't share a utility function. If there's a principled line between information that's merely expensive and information that changes the decision problem, I want it stated crisply enough that I can use it next time we pick this up.
