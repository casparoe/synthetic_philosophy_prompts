Okay so I've been circling this for a while and I want someone to actually try to break my position, not just steelman the other side by vibes.

The position: what a person values is *fully given* by what they'd do. Not "behavior is the best evidence we happen to have" — fully given, nothing left over. My reasoning is that every rival source of evidence about preferences (what people say they want, what they'd endorse after a week of calm reflection, their self-image, their "higher" values, the stuff they're ashamed of wanting) has to reduce to one of: a claim about what they'd choose in some other situation, a claim about what other people would choose, or something that isn't a claim about preferences at all. So if you've got a good enough model of the person's situation and they're roughly competent at getting what they want, the trajectory is a sufficient statistic for the values. This is basically the IRL frame taken seriously, plus the old revealed-preference intuition that talk is cheap and choice isn't.

What would count against it? Ranked, fatal at the top. In particular:

- the formal problems. I know IRL is ill-posed in some sense, and there's something in the Samuelson/Afriat tradition about how pretty much any finite set of choices can be rationalized by *some* utility function. Is that a problem for me or is it actually on my side?
- preference change and dynamically inconsistent humans. Does hyperbolic discounting hand you two agents where I'm claiming one, and if so does that break the sufficient-statistic claim or just tell me to index the values to time?
- the reformulation where the human is uncertain about their own utility function too, so the system's job is to help them figure out what they want. Is that a rival view or just my view with an extra state varible?
- stuff that has already happened: the people actually building aligned systems ended up collecting comparative labels on generated text rather than observing what users do, and that apparently beats inferring from behavior. How hard should that hit me?
- whether I've quietly insulated the position so that literally nothing could count against it, and if so whether that's a real problem given I'm using it as an engineering stance and not a scientific theory.

And for each one, tell me whether a committed defender would concede it as counting against them or swat it away — I'd rather know upfront which ones I'd have to swallow.
