I'm a pricing analyst at a mid-size UK home insurer. Product teams send me maybe twenty proposals a quarter for new rating factors — heat pumps, home batteries, e-bikes stored in sheds, flood defences — and right now I just pick whichever comparator pool has enough rows in it and hope nobody asks. Each one ends up in the rating engine, so it moves thousands of premiums, and a qualified actuary signs it off purely from the one-page rationale I write. I want a decision procedure I can actually run on a proposal in about forty minutes, grounded in whatever the philosophy of probability says about choosing reference classes: the tension between going as narrow as you can and only using classes you've got decent statistics for, the homogeneity requirement, the idea that you should be able to place a case in the class without already knowing how it turned out, and the awkward fact that the most statistically relevant partition is sometimes the one you're not allowed to use (gender stopped being usable for individual motor pricing after the European ruling, for instance). No reading list, just the thing I can follow.

Please use these sections, in this order: "What the procedure assumes" (about 100 words); "The procedure" (numbered steps, each saying what question to ask, what counts as a sufficient answer, and which answers kill the proposal outright); "Judgement calls" (about 150 words on the steps that look like procedure but are really me making a value choice); "Worked examples"; and "What this can't do" (about 100 words). For the worked examples, run it on three live ones: a heat pump factor where we have eleven claims of our own but nine hundred if I lump in all low-carbon heating; a postcode-level factor that predicts really well but is close to a proxy for ethnicity; and a telematics factor built out of the drivers who stayed with us after we last changed the price, so our own desicion made the pool. At least one of the three should come out against me — they're the ones I want to approve.
