I'm in a third-year phil of mind seminar and this week we did the frame problem, including Dennett's example of the digger wasp — the one that paralyzes its prey (a cricket or a caterpillar depending on the species), drags it to the mouth of its burrow, leaves it right at the entrance, goes inside alone to check that everything's in order, then comes back out and hauls the prey in. If you drag the prey a few feet away while it's doing the checking, it comes out, finds the prey, pulls it back to the entrance, goes in to check again, comes out, pulls it back... forever, or at least until whoever's running the experiment gets bored. Dennett has a word for this, sphexishness, and the moral he draws is that intelligence isn't a magic ingredient but a matter of having the right machinery for coping with a world that doesn't play along — cognitive wheels, he calls them.

Here's where I've gotten stuck and I want to make sure I'm not about to write something dumb. My draft thesis is that the wasp story shows there's no such thing as free will on Dennett's own terms. Reasoning: the wasp isn't broken or malfunctioning, it's just a physical system running the routine it was built to run, and every one of its moves is fully caused. But Dennett says we differ from the wasp only in having more and cleverer subroutines, which makes our flexibility just sphexishness with extra wheels on it. And you can always find the pertubation that loops any agent — put a person in a sufficiently novel situation, don't let them sleep, hand them the right trigger, and out comes the fixed routine. If every agent is sphexish under some perturbation or other, then sphexishness is universal and freedom is a difference that makes no difference.

So: is that a fair reading, or am I missing something structural about what sphexishness is actually supposed to be picking out? I'd especially like to know whether the "there's always some perturbation that loops you" premise is doing the work I think it is, and whether the regress move — that adding representations to handle relevance just creates a fresh relevance problem about the new representations — genuinely strengthens my case or is a cheap trick my prof will see straight through. Semi-unrelated: I vaguely remember reading that later entomologists redid the wasp experiments and found the wasps recover their prey more often than the classic story admits, but I can't track down the source anywhere, so treat that as a hunch rather than a fact. Does any of it matter for the philosophy, or only for the ethology? Draft is due Friday and my study group is evenly split on whether I'm onto something real or just conflating two arguments that never touch.
