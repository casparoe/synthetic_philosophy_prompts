Three drafts into a paper on when AI assistants should refuse or override, and I've hit a wall. Can you read the argument below and tell me where it actually breaks, rather than where its merely thin? No reading list — I've got the obvious ones and I can't look anything up right now. I just want to know whether the thesis survives.

**Working title: which self gets the keyboard?**

1. The debate is stuck on a threshold question: how bad must the outcome be, and how confident must the system be, before interference beats deference. Everyone draws a line somewhere, nobody agrees on where, and the disagreements look unresolvable because they're disguised as disagreements about numbers.

2. I think that framing hides a prior question. Refusal isn't mainly a weighing of harm against autonomy — it's an assignment of authority between different temporal slices of one user. Standing instructions ("don't help me message my ex after midnight", "never help me place a bet", "if I ask you to help me write a resignation letter at 1am, stall me") are issued by a self that is, by hypothesis, not the one speaking when the instruction triggers.

3. The default resolution rule — latest instruction wins — is always presented as the neutral one, the one that just respects the user. But it isn't neutral. It decides every Ulysses case in favour of the compromised self, and it does so by quietly reclassifying the earlier instruction as a mere preference the user has since changed. That's precisely what the earlier self was trying to prevent. So precommitment to an assistant is structurally unstable: the commitment has to be enforced against the very thing it was made against, by an agent whose tie-breaker already picks the other side.

4. The mirror rule — earliest instruction wins — has an obvious disaster attached: the user can never cancel a block, and gets trapped in a configuration set by a depressed, a manic, or simply a younger self.

5. So you need a meta-rule: instructions about which instructions win. But those are revocable by the latest self too, so you regress. My claim is that the regress terminates in exactly two places, neither comfortable. (a) Some instruction is made irrevocable — which is paternalism by construction, not by weighing, and the construction is what does all the justificatory work. Or (b) authority defaults to a third party, i.e. the developer's settings — in which case the "user's own values" that everyone agrees should set the threshold are partly the vendor's, and the neutrality claim in 3 fails at the next level up instead.

6. Stacked on top, an evidence problem. With a human paternalist, the ward's protest disciplines the practice: you find out, you object, the practice corrects. With an assistant, overrides that were right are invisible — the user never learns they were steered, so endorsement never arrives — and overrides that were wrong tend to surface as confusion rather than protest, because the tool seems broken or the user thinks they misremembered asking. So the paternalist receives no signal when correct and a corrupted signal when mistaken. Which means you can't repair 5 by learning which slice was right; there's nothing there to learn from.

Conclusion sketch: policy has to be fixed in advance and defended as a rule, not tuned case by case, and the right question to ask of any such rule isn't how much harm it prevents but which self it disenfranchises.

**What I want comments on:**

- Is 3 right that "latest wins" is doing hidden substantive work, or is there a formulation of it that really is neutral?
- Does the regress in 5 terminate only at (a) and (b)? I suspect an escape I'm not seeing — maybe something about revocation requiring a reason rather than just a request, or about a cooling-off period being neither revocable nor irrevocable.
- Where does the whole thing collapse if the slices aren't distinct selves at all but one person with noisy, context-dependent preferences? Does that rescue "latest wins" or destroy it?
- Is 6 load-bearing or decoration? If I cut it, does the paper still stand on 3 and 5 alone?
- Give me one counterexample that would sink the argument, ideally not the obvious acute-crisis one.
