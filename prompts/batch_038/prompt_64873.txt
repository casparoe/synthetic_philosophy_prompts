I'm giving a 40 minute talk next month at an interdisciplinary workshop on emotion — the room is mostly affective scientists and psychologists who work within dimensional models of affect (valence and arousal as the axes, intensity ratings treated as real measurements), plus maybe six philosophers of psychology who are broadly naturalist and pretty skeptical of first-person authority, and two or three ethicists, at least one of whom is an attitudinal hedonist. Nobody there is a well-being specialist. My background is ethics, not psych, so I can't out-experiment them and shouldn't try.

My thesis is that the heterogeneity problem for hedonism — the question of what the pleasure of a cold drink, the pleasure of a good argument, and the pleasure of a warm bath all have in common — is based on a false presupposition, and that giving up the presupposition costs hedonism less than people think. Sketch of what I'll argue:

1. The problem assumes that if two experiences are both pleasures there has to be a felt quality they share.
2. Every candidate for that quality fails. Pleasantness-as-such isn't independently pickable-out across cases, and no functional profile — approach, wanting more, tension-release, immunity to distraction — applies to all pleasures while applying to nothing else.
3. The common rating scale is an artifact of the language, not a readout of one dimension. Within a modality the scale is stable; across modalities, "more pleasant than" verdicts flip with framing, contrast and adaptation, and whether people are remembering the peak or summing the episode.
4. So pleasures form a disjunctive class held together by overlapping similarities and by the work the concept does in explaining and guiding behavior, not by a shared intrinsic mark.
5. Hedonism can survive this if the thing doing the normative work isn't "pleasure" as a natural kind but a claim about which episodes matter to a person: the ones that, while occurring, present themselves to the subject as worth continuing. That presentation is local and first-personal, and it needn't feel the same way in each case.
6. I concede commensurability across episodes goes, and I try to sell that as a feature — local trade-offs only, no aggregate utility-of-pleasure.
