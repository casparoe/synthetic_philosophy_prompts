Taking a philosophy-of-history seminar that keeps waving at "the archive" without ever getting into the weeds of how one actually works, and something from my summer job at a county archives is bugging me.

Two practices. First: appraisal. We keep maybe 4% of what comes in, and the retention schedule is essentially driven by what the creating office says it needs for legal/fiscal/administrative purposes — so the directors' policy correspondence survives and the individual case files get pulped. Second: arrangement and description follow provenance and original order, which in practice means the catalogue entry inherits the agency's own file titles and vocabulary. For the records of the county poor farm, that means people show up in the finding aid under whatever heading the superintendent wrote on the folder (inmates, defectives, that sort of thing), because the archivist isn't supposed to editorialize. We describe ourselves as neutral custodians. Meanwhile we just launched an active collecting push to solicit materials from communities that barely appear in the holdings at all.

So what theory of what a record *is* does the first pair presuppose — that records are evidence of the creator's activity rather than evidence about the people named in them? Which rival account does that quietly rule out, and is neutrality here even a coherent stance or just a label for whose viewpoint wins by default? And does the collecting push run on the same theory or a different one — do they contradict eachother, or can a single framework license both? Finally, what would concretely have to change in appraisal and description if you swapped in the rival view?
