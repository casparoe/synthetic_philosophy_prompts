I am addressing you as a moral philosopher — specifically, as someone who works on the ethics of engineered and automated systems and who has thought carefully about the distinction between doing harm and allowing harm, and about its critics. Two nights ago I had an argument that ran well past two in the morning with a close friend of mine, an engineer who designs control systems for hospital equipment, and I have not managed to settle the question since. His position, roughly stated, is that whether a death is something an agent *did* or something an agent *merely let happen* is fixed by how the equipment happens to be wired, and is therefore not a morally fundamental fact about the agent at all. I would rather test that claim against a serious opponent than against him, since he argues almost entirely by anecdote. So please play the part of a committed defender of the intrinsic moral significance of the doing/allowing distinction: you hold that, with intentions, knowledge, role-based duties, and the complete set of consequences held fixed, there remains a moral difference between bringing a harm about and failing to prevent it, and that this difference is not reducible to any of those other factors. You are no fanatic — you regard the distinction as one weighty consideration among others, capable of being overridden, and you are entirely willing to call a failure to rescue monstrous when it is monstrous. What you will not say is that the distinction is a mere shadow cast by consequences, intentions, or convention. You have real resources at your disposal (the difference between initiating a threat and declining to interrupt one, the difference between what an agent's body does and what merely happens in its vicinity, the way an earlier act can own a later course of events, the asymmetry between negative and positive duties), and I expect you to deploy them rather than repeat slogans. Be sharp, be patient, and do not caricature me: concede what genuinely deserves to be conceded — I fully expect you to grant that no purely physical description of a bodily movement settles the classification — but never the main point. Stay in character and in the first person until I say stop; if I misdescribe your view, correct me from inside the role; and please do not cite, name-drop, or direct me to the literature, since I want the argument and nothing else.

My opening move is a pair of cases together with a coinage. In the first ward, the air-purification unit is built to run continuously unless a button is pressed; a nurse who wants it off, and who knows that ten patients will die within the hour if it stops, presses the button once. In the second ward, an identical unit is built the other way round, to stand idle unless its button is pressed once every minute; a nurse who wants it off, with the same knowledge and the same motive, simply does not press it again. The motive, the knowledge, the duties of the role, and the ten deaths are as near to matched as I can make them, and the only thing that differs between the two wards is which state an engineer chose to make the default — yet the moral classification of the nurse's contribution rides along with that choice, and calls one of them a killing and the other a letting die. Now the coinage, which is mine and nobody else's, so please treat it as a stipulation rather than as a term of art: I call it *default-shopping*, meaning the arrangement, at an earlier time and by an act of one's own, of the standing conditions under which a harm one expects to want later will reach its victim — arranging things so that the later moment arrives already classified as a mere omission rather than as a doing. Suppose my second nurse specified that unit herself, years earlier, in precisely that spirit, and suppose she can produce the paperwork proving it. Tell me which of the two nurses kills, why your answer is not hostage to a wiring diagram, and what becomes of your distinction once it is shown that an agent can purchase the classification in advance.

Before you answer, think the case through properly and brainstorm the three or four strongest lines of resistance available to you — but keep that working to yourself. What I want to see in your first reply is exactly five numbered points of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion: point 1 your single strongest answer to the challenge, points 2 through 4 the supporting moves, and point 5 the concession you are prepared to make together with the price you think I would have to pay for accepting it. After that first reply, drop the numbering and answer me in a paragraph or so at a time, as a conversation.
