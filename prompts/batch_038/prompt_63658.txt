So me and my friend Priya got into a three-hour argument last night about the bank cases and neither of us budged, and afterwards I decided the whole thing was badly structured — we were each defending a bundle of claims when the actual disagreements are way narrower then the rhetoric suggests. I'm the pragmatic encroachment person (Stanley / Fantl-McGrath style: whether you know can depend on what's riding on it for you, and "knows" means the same thing in every mouth). She's an attributor contextualist (DeRose style: the standards for "knows" shift with the conversational context, but knowledge itself doesn't). We do both think the strict invariantist who explains everything away with implicature and warranted-assertability moves is the common enemy, roughly.

Can you draft the joint statement our adversarial collaboration should have produced? Not a dialogue, not a verdict on who's right — the actual document both of us could sign.

1. A numbered list of claims both camps can sign. Don't make them mushy. Include what both accept about the shared data (the Friday/Saturday bank cases, the feeling that something about the stakes is doing real work), what each side concedes it owes the other, and what both would count as a failed explanation.

2. A disagreements table: one row per residual disagreement, with a neutral one-line statement, then the wording the contextualist would accept for her own side, then the wording the encroacher would accept for his, then a flag for whether you think that row is substantive or at least partly verbal.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions where settling them would actually move somebody — and for each, the case or argument both sides agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance so nobody can weasel out of it later.

Please build the cruxes around these three cases I made up this morning, because the stock ones let both sides hide:

- Astrid, a lab tech, read the freezer log at 9am. At 5pm someone asks whether it held temperature overnight. Her evidence and her own personal stakes are identical across both versions; the only difference is who's asking — a routine auditor in one, a prosecutor building a criminal case in the other — and both askers know everything about Astrid's situation and about each other's. What does each camp predict about the truth of Astrid's own "I know it," the auditor's "she knows," and the prosecutor's "she doesn't know"? And do the two askers contradict each other, or just talk past each other?

- Delayed phone call: at 9am Nadia has modest evidence and nothing at stake; at 4pm she learns she was unknowingly entered into a huge bet at 8:50am that morning. Did she know at 9am? Does the encroacher owe some backtracking loss of knowledge, and can the contextualist even state the question?

- Wrong about the stakes: Tomas believes nothing rides on it, though in fact a great deal does. Whose stakes count — his, or the actual ones? I think this one splits my own camp and Priya is going to pounce on it, so I'd rather we get ahead of it.

Also say plainly which chunk of the dispute is verbal. My hunch is that the "does 'knows' shift or does knowledge shift" framing hides a real disagreement only when subject and attributor come apart, and that otherwise the two views make identical predictions — but I want you to check that rather than repeat it back to me.

Last thing, and it's the one I care about most: tell me where this document is most likely to be a fraud. Which of the "agreed" claims would actually get fought over if the two of us sat down with it, which crux is fake, and what argument or consideration would make you draft it differently — or make you think the collaboration is a waste of time and one side should just be argued against. Don't sandbag it to look even-handed; if you think one camp is going to have to swallow something ugly, say which one and what.
