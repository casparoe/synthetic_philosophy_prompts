I retired three years ago after a career in atmospheric physics, and I have been working through the ethics of long-run climate policy on my own account. Before I commit my conclusions to a seminar paper, I should like an audit of whether they hang together. My position is as follows.

(1) Discounting a person's welfare merely because it lies further in the future is a bias of the same kind as discounting it because it lies further away in space. The pure-time-preference term in the social discount rate should therefore be set to zero.

(2) The social discount rate nonetheless has a second term, arising from growth in consumption per head combined with the diminishing marginal utility of consumption: because our descendants will be richer, a unit of their consumption does them less good than a unit of ours does us. I regard this as a substantive moral consideration rather than a bias.

(3) For the applied appraisal of long-lived projects, I take the discount rate from observed market returns on capital, on the ground that public investment displaces private investment which would have earned that return.

(4) Where there is uncertainty about the appropriate discount rate itself, I accept that the certainty-equivalent rate falls as the horizon lengthens, and I use such declining rates for anything beyond roughly a century.

(5) I keep the risk of civilizational collapse out of the discount rate altogether, treating it separately as an explicit probability over outcomes.

I cannot see for myself where, if anywhere, the trouble is. Please tell me which of these pull against one another and which merely look as though they do, ordering the genuine tensions by severity, and for each saying what would have to give and what the cheapest repair would cost me. I am not after a survey of the wider dispute about discounting the future, only the audit. Please keep the whole answer under 550 words.
