I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the past year I have been reporting a feature on the state of the species concept: a field station in the American West, a museum collections drawer, a population-genetics lab, a back-breeding project in the Karoo. Before I write it I need to know what I myself believe, because "the experts disagree" is turning out to be my way of not having a position. So here is my honest list of verdicts, arrived at by gut rather than by argument.

1. Ensatina. The salamanders that ring California's Central Valley: every population breeds with its immediate neighbor all the way around, but where the two ends of the ring meet in the south they behave as though the other did not exist. My verdict: one species — though I say it unhappily, and I suspect I say it mainly because the ring is famous.

2. The flycatchers. Two small birds that nobody could reliably tell apart in a drawer until it emerged that they sing different songs and do not respond to each other's. My verdict: two species, without hesitation. If a curator cannot separate them, so much the worse for the curator.

3. The elephants. Two kinds of African elephant long written up as one, differing in build and habitat, now carrying separate names in the conservation literature, hybridizing where their ranges touch. My verdict: two species, and the hybrid zone does not trouble me at all.

4. The Italian sparrow. A bird with its own appearance and range across most of Italy that originated as a hybrid between two other sparrows and has held its own ever since, without human help. My verdict: a real species, and one of the most interesting facts in the entire debate.

5. Pizzly bears. Polar bear by grizzly, fertile, increasingly common as the ice retreats. My verdict: not a species — a symptom. I would not want an illustration of one captioned as a newly discovered species in my own magazine.

6. Dogs and wolves. Freely interfertile, one descended from the other, and so unlike each other that almost no one outside biology would call them the same kind of animal. My verdict: one species. I hold this firmly, which surprises me, because I cannot immediately say why it feels more firm than my verdict in 2.

7. Bdelloid rotifers. No males that anyone has ever found, lineages separate for a very long time, clustered into forms that behave like species ecologically and morphologically. My verdict: they are species, and any account I settle on that makes interbreeding essential is refuted by them on the spot.

8. Neanderthals. They and my ancestors exchanged genes, and I carry some of the result. My verdict: separate species, emphatically. I worry this verdict is mostly drama and mortality — that I would say something quite different about the same two populations if both were still walking around.

9. Bacteria. My verdict: no species, properly speaking. The word is bookkeeping, and it is harmless bookkeeping that has been mistaken for metaphysics. This sits badly with 7, and I do not know what to do about it.

10. The gulls. The other textbook ring, the one that goes around the Arctic, which I have since read is not really a ring once you look at the genetics. My verdict, such as it is: that losing the example has cost me no confidence in the idea it illustrated — which strikes me as suspicious in itself.

My tentative principle, which I cannot defend and would like tested: two populations are separate species when each would remain itself on its own, with nothing else holding them apart. It seems to get 4 right, since the sparrow holds itself apart. But it also seems to imply that the bears of 5 are on their way to becoming a species, since nothing but ice and distance separates them and both are going away — a prediction I do not want. And it appears to endorse 6 while condemning 2, unless song somehow counts as help from outside.

What I need from you:

Do not write the survey. I have read the tour of the biological, phylogenetic, ecological, genic and recognition concepts with their standard objections, and so has any competent feature writer. Names may appear only at the very end, as a check on where I have independently landed — never as the organizing principle of the answer. Build the principle out of the verdicts above. Where a clause exists solely to handle one case, say so and name the case; where a clause is doing unearned work, cut it. Make it precise enough that I could hand it to a colleague and get the same answers back: lineage, independence, and cohesion are the words I keep catching myself using loosely, and they are the ones I most need pinned down. And where my verdicts cannot all be kept, tell me plainly which one goes and what the loss costs.

Please use exactly these sections, in this order:

1. Bottom line (about 150 words). Which of the ten verdicts a single principle can keep, how many you have to break, and the principle itself in a sentence or two.

2. The principle (700–900 words). Clause by clause, each clause followed in parentheses by the numbered case that forced it on you, plus a short gloss on what the operative terms are to mean.

3. The two hard pairs (about 400 words). Case 4 against case 5 — a stable hybrid lineage and a hybrid that is not — and case 2 against case 6 — indistinguishable birds that are two species and wildly different canids that are one. Show me the mechanism that separates them, or concede that there is none.

4. What I have to give up (about 300 words). The verdicts your principle cannot accommodate, why each felt compelling, and what a second principle would have to look like if I insisted on keeping them.

5. Candidate B (about 350 words). That second principle, stated to the same standard, with its own price. Say which of the two you would defend in print and why.

6. New cases (about 400 words). Your principle's verdict on four cases I have not listed: a population that split recently and looks nothing like its parent yet is freely fertile with it; a widespread organism whose local forms intergrade everywhere except on one island; a captive lineage that has bred apart from its wild relatives for a century and would not recognize them if released; and an extinct population known from a handful of specimens whose DNA places it inside a living species. Then, briefly: the South African project that is selectively breeding plains zebras toward the extinct quagga's reduced striping. If it succeeds, is the resulting animal a quagga — and is that a question your principle must answer, or one it is entitled to decline?
