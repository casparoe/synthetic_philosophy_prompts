I work on the perception and planning stack for a company doing autonomous long-haul trucks, and lately I've been the one who has to sign off on what the truck does when there's no accident-free option left. Concretely: there's a class of cases where the truck's own lane is going to be blocked no matter what — a van has spun out across it, say — and the software has maybe 200 milliseconds to pick between steering onto the shoulder, where the lidar says there's *something* it can't classify well enough to tell whether it's a person or a pile of debris, and holding its line, where the people inside the van are very likely dead. The thing that keeps me up isn't the split-second case. It's the whole setup around it. Our lawyers push hard for the hold-your-line behavior, because you get sued for what your product *does* to people, basically never for what it fails to prevent. So the legal incentives quietly favor a truck that just doesn't act, even if across a fleet of 40,000 trucks over ten years that choice kills more people than the more aggressive version would. Meanwhile the state highway authority is floating a rule that would require collision-avoidance systems to minimize expected fatalities, full stop, with no credit given for whether the vehicle caused the harm or merely failed to stop it. And nobody in the debate seems to have thought about whether that rule is even coherent.

So here's what I want. Suppose we could sit Philippa Foot and Judith Jarvis Thomson down in a conference room with this exact mess — the 200ms decision, the sensor uncertainty about who's on the shoulder, the liability asymmetry that makes passivity the safe legal bet, the fleet-level statistics, and the proposed regulation. Give me what each of them would actually say. Where would they land against each other, and which of them is going to think the whole framing I've just described is confused?

I know what the easy answer sounds like: it's a trolley problem, everyone says so, move on. But I don't think that settles anything, because the interesting bits here are ones that didn't exist in 1967 — the fact that the "doing nothing" option is itself something we engineered and chose months in advance, deliberately, in writing, with lawyers present. Does the doing/allowing line survive being relocated into a codebase? Or does it collapse into a straight numbers question once inaction stops being a natural default and becomes an authored behavior?

Argue it out. I'd rather get the two of them disagreeing in detail than a survey of what they published. And please don't hand me a reading list — I want the reasoning, worked through on this specific case.
