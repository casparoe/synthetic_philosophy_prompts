I want to screen Denis Villeneuve's Arrival in a philosophy of language seminar that has just covered Twin Earth, the causal-historical theory of reference, and Putnam's division of linguistic labor, and I would like the film treated as a case to argue about rather than as a colourful illustration. Please write a reading that takes both the film and the philosophy seriously and addresses three things. First, the mistranslation: the logogram the various teams gloss as weapon, and which Louise comes to read as tool, is not settled by anything in the interpreters' heads — the Chinese gloss is shaped by their having communicated through a competitive game, and the eventual resolution turns on facts about what the visitors will need from humanity three thousand years hence. Is this a Twin-Earth-style case of internally indistinguishable interpretive states with different content, and does the film's resolution favour a backward-looking causal history of the kind Kripke and Putnam describe, or a teleological account of what fixes reference? Second, the message is deliberately split across the twelve craft, so no single community possesses the whole content: Putnam's division of labour assumed experts within one linguistic community, whereas here the would-be experts are twelve governments that stop talking to one another. Does that extend externalism, or strain it past usefulness? Third, where must an honest critic concede that the film resists the lens — its own view of language is broadly Whorfian, locating the transformation inside Louise's skull, and the ambiguity is finally resolved by a private insight rather than by evidence about the world? Please end with what the film contributes to the question of how reference gets fixed that the thought experiments do not.
