My perception seminar prof handed around a description of an unpublished study from another lab and said it basically settles the cognitive penetration debate. I'm not so sure and I can't tell where my doubt should be aimed. Roughly the setup:

Training phase: two novel, meaningless glyphs, A and B, matched for luminance, contrast, spatial frequency and physical size. A was always followed 800 ms later by a burst of white noise loud enough to make people flinch; B was always followed by a soft chime. Test phase: continuous flash suppression. A or B was presented to one eye while colorful masks flickered in the other, and people pressed a key the moment any part of the suppressed glyph became visible. Breakthrough times for A came out about 90 ms faster than for B. Two null controls: people who got the same A/B pairing but were told to ignore it and did a concurrent task showed no difference, and people who were only told in advance that A predicts noise and B predicts chime, with no training at all, also showed no difference.

Write an essay on whether this shows cognitive penetration of perception. Take the standard criteria seriously (Pylyshyn's, and the deflationary line that most apparent top-down effects dissolve into attention shifts, response bias, memory, or demand) and say which of those alternative explanations can and can't get a grip on a CFS breakthrough measure, where the dependent variable is a detection latency rather than an appearance judgement. Then commit to a verdict -- genuine penetration, penetration of some watered-down kind, or no penetration -- and say what work the two null controls are doing for you.
