In three weeks I give a colloquium talk in a personality psychology department — not a philosophy venue — arguing a broadly situationist thesis in the tradition of Doris and Harman: the robust, global character traits that Aristotelian virtue ethics is built on do not exist. My case runs in three steps. Cross-situational consistency coefficients for morally relevant behavior cluster around .30. The classic demonstrations — obedience, bystander apathy, trivial mood manipulations — show incidental features of the situation swamping the person. And the standard psychological repairs (aggregating across occasions, if-then signatures, traits as density distributions of states) do not rescue the virtue-ethical trait, because what they describe is a distribution or a bundle of local conditionals, not the stable disposition that reliably surfaces across the range of situations virtue ethics requires.

The audience: mostly trait psychologists, several working on Big Five structure, one behavioral geneticist who studies rank-order stability across the lifespan, one who has published on whole trait theory and regards the person-situation debate as settled by a synthesis, and a methodologist whose whole interest is effect sizes, reliability, and the replication crisis. Nobody in the room has read Aristotle, and several will hear me as re-opening a question they consider closed by people who did the actual work.

Prepare me for the question period. Give me the objections in roughly the order they will come, phrased the way these people would phrase them rather than the way a philosopher would, and rank them by how much damage each does. For each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes and a note on where that reply is soft. Tell me what I should concede in my opening five minutes so I am not painted as ignorant of the field, and identify the one reply that has to land for the talk to succeed. Finish with the question I am hoping nobody asks.

Please write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a long email to a colleague — no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables.
