For a novel I'm writing, one of the central characters has spent a decade as a hearings officer on a municipal board that reviews price ceilings and wage floors. The rulings I've given her are invented, but I want the pattern of reasoning to be faithful to how such bodies actually decide, so that the character is right more often than a lay reader expects. Her rulings to date:

- A ceiling on renewal rents for older apartment buildings, set at documented operating costs plus a modest return, upheld.
- A ceiling on what fuel-delivery companies may charge, struck down as a permanent measure bearing no relation to any supply disruption.
- A cap on what a pharmacy may add to the wholesale price of a drug in short supply, upheld, but only with an expiry date and strict record-keeping.
- A ceiling on the price of a patented drug for which there is no substitute and no insurance coverage, struck down, the board reasoning that buyers there had neither an alternative nor an exit.
- A freeze on home-heating prices during a three-week pipeline failure, upheld; the same freeze proposed as permanent a year later, struck down.
- A cap on late fees and application charges levied on tenants, upheld as confiscation rather than payment.

Her next case: a permanent ceiling on residential moving and storage, set at audited operating costs plus a fixed return, in a market where firms may refuse new customers but where a customer already holding a unit faces steep costs to leave.

Predict how she rules. Then tell me which principle her record actually supports — cost-tracking, duration-plus-documented-emergency, the buyer's alternatives, the seller's legal duty to serve, or some other rule that fits every ruling equally well. If two or more fit, say which precedent is closest, why, and what the board would be committing itself to by ruling either way. Finally, name the next case I should put in her docket that would force the candidate rules apart.
