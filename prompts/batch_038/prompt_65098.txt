I help run a small hardship fund in my town — money that a few local businesses donate, we hand out two or three thousand a year to people who've gotten flattened by something. I'm a dispatcher at a trucking outfit, no education past high school, but two years ago they asked me to sit on the committee and I ended up writing most of the criteria we use. The main one was that we help people who got hit by something they couldn't have prevented. Everybody nodded when I read it out. Now I lie awake about it.

Thursday we're voting on two cases. Dana's furnace died in January, her pipes burst, and she had to move her dad into a care place not long after — nobody at the table thinks she brought any of that on herself. The other is Marcus. He drove for one of the outfits we contract with, paid by the mile, and he blew through his mandated rest breaks constantly because he needed the miles, and he took the cash-in-lieu option instead of the health plan for the same reason. He wrecked in April, broke his back, can't work now, has nothing coming in. Half the committee says he picked that life and if we cover him we're telling everyone who played it straight that it was for nothing. Two weeks ago I'd have voted with them. Then I did the intake visit and it got strange. He took the cash option because when he was 24 he was driving his mother to chemo three days a week and couldn't afford to lose hours. He drives the way he drives because his father did. And going the other direction, I realized Dana bought that house cheap on a street the whole town knows floods, and she skipped the furnace inspection two winters running to save ninety bucks. So when I try to sort the two of them into "their fault" and "not their fault," each one lands in both columns depending on how far back I go. Go back far enough and everything is circumstance; stop early and everything is a choice somebody made. There doesn't seem to be a natural place to stop, and I'm the guy who wrote a rule that assumes there is one.

Two of us on the committee are basically not speaking now. One says we should scrap the fault test entirely and just rank people by how badly they need it, since we can never actually settle who's responsible for what. The other says the fund is dead the day we do that, because donors want to feel like they aren't rewarding wrecklessness, and she thinks the test can still be made to work if we're careful about which choices count and which don't. So: is she right that there's some version of "couldn't have prevented it" that doesn't dissolve the second you poke at it? If there is, what would it actually look like written on an application form that a volunteer can apply on a Tuesday night without a philosophy degree? And if there isn't, is the needs-only version really as bad as she says, or is she just attached to a sentence we wrote two years ago? Then the practical part, which is the one I most need help with — Thursday, Marcus, do I vote yes or no? I want to know if there's a way to decide that isn't just punishing a man for his father, or handing out money we don't have, or pretending the rule says something it can't.
