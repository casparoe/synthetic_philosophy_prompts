Write a dialogue of about 1,500 words between Augustine of Hippo and Consentius, set in the bishop's study at Hippo, in which Consentius has come for approval of the sort of stratagem he admires in the monk Fronto: send an orthodox agent among the Priscillianists posing as a sympathiser, win their confidence by professing their errors, and thereby expose the sect from within. Consentius should not be a cynic but a partisan of the Church, arguing that a group which has raised dissimulation to the level of doctrine and is quietly corrupting the faithful must be met on its own ground, and that the concealment Augustine concedes to be lawful differs from the lie he forbids only in the assurance with which it misleads — a distinction of technique, not of moral kind. Augustine must answer on the merits rather than by appeal to authority: why the line between hiding the truth and speaking against one's own mind does real moral work, and what is destroyed when the tongue, the very instrument by which hearts are joined, is turned to producing a belief the speaker does not hold. Let both men be equally acute and equally sincere, each answering the other's actual argument, and let Consentius land at least one blow that Augustine cannot wholly parry — for instance, that a refusal to lie coupled with scrupulous concealment purchases the agent's own integrity at the cost of the souls the heretics are ruining, and that Augustine's rule therefore treats purity of heart as the only good that may never be traded. Do not resolve the exchange by converting either party: end where the argument genuinely stands, with the hard case still open and both men aware of what their position commits them to. Keep the piece free of anachronism and of scholarly apparatus; if Scripture is invoked, paraphrase it rather than quoting verbatim. Please write in British English.
