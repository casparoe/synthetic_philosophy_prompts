I help run research oversight at a children's hospital and I'm redrafting our assent policy, and the more I stare at the current one the less I can tell what it thinks it's doing. Roughly what we do now: for anything a kid undergoes as part of their actual clinical care -- a lumbar puncture, a chemo regimen, a central line -- the parents consent, the 14-year-old is asked, and if the kid says no we write it up in the chart, we "document and consider" it, we send in a child life specialist, maybe we delay a day, and then the procedure happens anyway. For the optional stuff bolted onto research -- an extra 5ml during a stick that's happening regardless, a questionnaire arm, a genetics sub-study -- the same 14-year-old's no ends it on the spot. No override, no appeal, no ethics consult, we just don't do it. We also administer a maturity/capacity screen to everyone over 12 and score it, and for what its worth I have never once seen that score change a decision. And then, separately, the same institution will treat a 16-year-old as having sole authority to consent to contraception or STI treatment under state law, which cuts against the age-line logic everywhere else in the building.

So what theory of consent is this practice actually running on? I want you to reconstruct the implicit view rather than tell me the rules are messy -- what has to be true about consent, competence, and whose say-so counts for the split between the lumbar puncture and the extra blood draw to make sense? Which of the obvious candidates is it committed to: consent as authorization to use someone's body, consent as protection of the vulnerable, assent as a way of building a kid's developing agency, or assent as trauma reduction and institutional cover? And which rival views does the practice quietly rule out -- say a straight capacity view where the threshold is decision-specific competence rather than age and benefit, or a view on which the wrong is the non-consensual use of the person at all, or a view where a child's refusal matters exactly as much as their agreement?

Also: is the split tracking the child's interests, the child's will, or our exposure, and does the fact that the capacity score never bites show that we don't really believe capacity is the relevant thing? Tell me which parts the policy strictly presupposes versus which it merely fits comfortably with, and where it's underdetermined so that several different theories would license the exact same document. Then, if we swapped in the capacity view -- one assessment per decision, no age tiers, no benefit-based carve-out -- what would concretely have to change, and what would we be giving up to to get it?
