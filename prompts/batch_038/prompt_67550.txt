Got into a three hour argument with my roommate last night and my head is still spinning, so I want a third party. He's a bio undergrad, I'm the philosophy one, and we were trying to sort out whether the history-based way of assigning function to a trait (a heart's function is to pump blood *because* hearts were recruited into lineages for pumping blood) is actually any different from the causal-role way (a heart's function is whatever it currently does that figures in some explanation of the containing system that we care about). And basically the entire argument ran through one analogy: artifacts.

His version goes like this. A screwdriver's function is to turn screws because somebody made it for turning screws. A hammer's function is to hit nails. Nobody thinks that's mysterious. So — his words — "selection is just the designer with the intention rubbed out," and the history account of biological function is nothing but the artifact case wearing a lab coat. Which for him settles it, because the artifact case is the one everybody already has intuitions about, and the causal-role people are the ones with the weird consequences. He gave two: (a) on the causal-role view my heart has the function of making a thumping sound, which he says is obviously absurd, and (b) it can't distinguish a trait that's just a side effect from one that's genuinely for something. Then he turned the analogy on exaptations: he said think of the spare bedroom everyone in the house has converted into an office — it's still a bedroom, function-wise, it was built as one — so a trait that arose by drift and only later got co-opted for something useful does not thereby acquire the new use as its function, and the history account is vindicated again.

My pushback, which I articulated badly: with artifacts there are two agents in the story, a maker and a user, and maker-intention and current-deployment can come apart — the bedroom/office case is exactly that coming apart. In biology there's only the maker slot and nothing occupies it, so the analogy isn't neutral between the two accounts, it's smuggling an agent in through the back door and then presenting the result as an intuition. He said I was just repeating "no intentions in nature" like a mantra and that this is a difference in mechanism, not in logical structure: both are cases of a thing being there because of what it does. We went around this circle about six times.
