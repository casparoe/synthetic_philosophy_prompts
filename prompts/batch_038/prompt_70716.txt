I am a third-year statistics doctoral student, and for the past two years I have co-run an informal reading group that is half statistics students and half philosophy students. Our end-of-term session this year is on the no-free-lunch results — the 1997 Wolpert–Macready paper on optimization and the earlier results Wolpert derived for machine learning — read alongside Hume on induction. Instead of assigning another expository paper, I want to hand out a piece of short fiction that the group can argue about, and I would like you to write it. The story should run roughly 1,800 to 2,600 words, in a plain literary register, and it should be self-contained: nothing in it may depend on the reader already knowing what a no-free-lunch theorem is.

Here is the setting I have in mind, which you should feel free to reshape. A mid-sized coastal city has for eighty years maintained a public forecasting office, known locally as the Ledger, whose published predictions determine when the storm barriers are raised; anyone may lodge a prediction method with the office, and each year the method with the best record over the preceding decades is promoted to official use. A reformer on the city council proposes an amendment to the office's charter, arguing that scoring methods against the single actual history of the city is parochial and that the only impartial rule is to score each method by averaging its performance over every possible sequence of weather compatible with the historical record, each weighted alike. Once the amendment passes, the office discovers that under the new rule every lodged method scores identically — the method that copies yesterday's weather, the method that inverts it, the method that always predicts the worst, and a genuinely skillful dynamical model all tie — and that the tie is not a defect to be fixed but exactly what impartiality of that kind costs. The second act should involve an engineer who responds by building a procedure that selects among the competing methods on the basis of their past selections, and then, inevitably, a procedure that selects among the selectors; the tower should not terminate in a vantage point free of assumptions, and the story should make the reader feel why it cannot. The third act should turn on someone realizing that the entire content of the charter is the weighting over possible worlds, that the weighting cannot be defended by any argument inside the office that does not already presuppose a weighting, and that this is Hume's uniformity-of-nature assumption wearing formal dress. I would like one scene in which a character walks a layperson — a council member, or a child — through a miniature version of the problem, something like a world that exists for two days, each day either clear or overcast, so that there are exactly four possible histories, and in which any rule that profits on one history loses on another. Please keep that scene in natural dialogue: no character may deliver anything resembling a lecture of more than a few sentences, and the concept must be load-bearing rather than decorative, meaning the plot should turn on it and not merely mention it. The ending should be a decision with consequences — someone signs the amendment, or refuses to, or the barriers go up on the wrong day — rather than an epiphany delivered by exposition.

Two things I want you to be scrupulous about, because they are where most popular writing on this theorem goes wrong. First, the story should not rest on the gloss that no method is better than any other so all science is arbitrary; the results are about what happens when performance is averaged across all possible target problems, and there is a real and important difference between that bookkeeping device and a claim about the world we happen to inhabit. Second, the two camps in the actual dispute — those who take these theorems to encode a deep insight about the price of inductive bias, and those who take them to be true but largely irrelevant to practice, since we never face a uniformly random environment — should both be represented by characters whose positions are defensible as written, and the story should not settle the dispute by fiat. After the story, please add an afterword of about 250 words stating which reading of the theorem the narrative in fact presupposes; where you compressed or distorted for the sake of the story, including whether you collapsed the search version and the learning version into one, whether you treated the objective as fixed or as changing during the search, and whether you relied on identical mean performance or on the stronger claim that the whole performance distribution is identical; which side of the real dispute the story ends up favoring, and why that is a choice rather than a fact; and whether the identification of the choice of weighting with Hume's problem of induction is licensed or is a philosopher's overreach. Finally, and I mean this seriously: if you think the commission rests on a confused premise — if the only dramatically satisfying version of this story is the popular misstatement, or if a narrative of this shape must personalize the theorem in a way that falsifies it — say so plainly, either before writing or instead of writing, and tell me what would have to change. I would much rather receive the objection than a polished story that teaches my reading group something false.
