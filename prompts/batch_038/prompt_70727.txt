I've spent the better part of a year working through the neo-Roman revival in the theory of liberty — Berlin's two concepts, MacCallum's rejoinder that there is only one triadic concept of freedom whose rivals merely disagree about how to fill in the variables, and then the republican response that what Berlin's negative concept misses is not a further quantity of freedom but a different relation to power. I've ended up with a version of republicanism that I don't see defended in quite this shape, which leaves me unsure whether I've landed on something or simply talked myself into a muddle, and my supervisor has asked me to write a chapter that states the view and then attacks it as hard as I can manage. The view is this. Domination is not a matter of how much another agent is able to interfere with you, nor of whether they do interfere, but of whether your position rests on arrangements you can point to and invoke rather than on the continuing disposition of someone else. Call it the invocable-standing view. Its three signature commitments are: (i) a slave with a genuinely kind and predictably indulgent master is unfree, not because the master is likely to interfere, but because whatever security she enjoys is something she cannot claim — she cannot prove it to a third party, contest its withdrawal in advance, or cite any standing arrangement that obliges the master to be as he is; (ii) law that constrains you does not thereby dominate, provided the constraints are publicly ascertainable and their content is answerable to the interests of those constrained, whereas the identical rule applied at an official's unstructured discretion does dominate; and (iii) the policy upshot is that the aim is not to minimise interference but to replace discretionary beneficence with invocable standing, which is why I think means-tested benefits delivered at caseworker discretion, at-will employment, and ad hoc emergency powers are dominating even when they are, in fact, generously and rarely exercised. I hold that this is not merely negative liberty with a reliability premium bolted on, because what does the work is the source and answerability of power rather than its extent.

So: what would count against it? I want a map of its exposure, not a defence of it. Work through the arguments, intuitions, cases, and conceptual results that would tell against the view, and separate the ones that would be genuinely decisive from the ones that would be merely awkward or merely verbal. In particular I want you to take seriously the collapse worry in both directions — that invocable standing is either a covert way of talking about the probability-weighted extent of non-interference, in which case Berlin plus an expected-value calculation already has everything I claim to have added, or else that it is a purely procedural and legalist notion, in which case my view is committed to saying that freedom exists only where law has constituted it. What would have to be true for either collapse argument to go through, and what would defeat it? Relatedly: if the stateless person, the informal-sector worker, and the member of a close-knit community governed by nothing but custom and mutual knowledge are all maximally unfree on my account, is that a cost I must swallow, and does the anarchist objection — that every expansion of law expands freedom on this view, which sounds less like a theory of liberty than a theory of jurisdiction — bite? And what about the worry of expansiveness: if discretionary beneficence is dominating, then parents, teachers, physicians, employers, and spouses dominate by default, and if domination is ubiquitous it cannot be a guide to anything.

I'd also like an honest audit of whether the view risks anything at all or has been insulated by its own vocabulary. "Arbitrariness" defined as failure to be forced to track the interests of those affected, and "tracking" cashed out in terms of whatever survives contestation, looks suspiciously like a pair of definitions built to absorb every counterexample — and if that is right, I want to know whether it matters, since plenty of respectable normative concepts are elaborated rather than tested. Which empirical findings would discriminate my view from a negative-liberty rival, and what would it mean if they came out the other way: given that I claim the slave's unfreedom is objective rather than experienced, would survey evidence that people in arbitrary-but-benign conditions report feeling free be decisive against me, irrelevant to me, or a sign that I have made my view untestable by construction? Finally, everything rests on the intuition about the contented slave, so tell me what happens if that intuition is pressed — for instance, a master permanently incapacitated, or bound by an irrevocable, self-enforcing arrangement that he could not breach even if he wanted to. Is that slave still dominated on my account, and is either answer defensible? For each item, say what a committed defender of the invocable-standing view would concede as counting against them and what they would dismiss, rank the defeaters from fatal to merely irritating, and note where my view makes predictions about actual institutions — basic income versus means testing, private arbitration clauses, family law, emergency powers — that a negative-liberty or a positive-liberty account does not make, and which of those predictions is most exposed. Please argue rather than catalogue, and reach a verdict on whether this is a view worth defending. I don't want a bibliography or a list of further reading; refer to individual thinkers only where a position is really inseparable from them, and if you're not confident of an attribution, describe the position without naming anyone.
