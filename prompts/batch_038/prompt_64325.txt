I have been assembling my own verdicts about disposition ascriptions, and I want to know whether a single principle underwrites them. I am not after a survey of the positions on offer; I want a principle stated precisely enough that I could apply it to cases neither of us has thought of, together with an honest accounting of what it costs me.

Two background assumptions, which you may challenge but should know I hold. First, fragility and its kin cannot be read off the corresponding subjunctive conditional, since the conditional comes out false for some things that are fragile and true for some things that are not. Second, in each case below the question is whether the ascription is true of the object now, as it is now arranged. I have deliberately avoided the standard labels for these arrangements, because the labels seem to carry verdicts with them.

1. A wine glass packed in a crate of foam. If it were struck, the foam would take the blow and it would not break. Verdict: fragile.
2. A mug of unbreakable polymer on a shelf rigged so that any sharp impact detonates a charge that pulverizes the mug. If it were struck, it would fly to pieces; strike it with the charge removed and it is unmarked. Verdict: not fragile.
3. A copper wire attached to a monitoring device that kills the current the instant anything conductive touches the wire. The wire's own structure is untouched; the device works on it from outside. Verdict: not live. This is the verdict I hold least firmly.
4. A glass built with a lattice of microscopic dampers distributed through its own walls, which turn an impact into heat before a crack can start. No packing, no apparatus, nothing external: the blocking is done entirely by properties the glass has in itself. Verdict: fragile.
5. The device in 3, miniaturized, embedded in the wire's own jacket and powered from the wire itself, so that wire and device are now one object with one permanent structure and nothing outside it intervenes. Verdict: not live.
6. A steel block that would shatter if struck by a sledgehammer swung hard enough — as, on that description, almost anything would. Verdict: not fragile.
7. The glass in 1, except that the foam is impregnated with a compound that on the first impact both absorbs the blow and permanently anneals the glass. Verdict: fragile right up to the moment of the first strike, and not fragile a moment later, although the crate, the foam, and everything I would say about the arrangement look exactly as they did before.
8. A variant of 4 in which the dampers work by locally melting and resolidifying the surface layer: the blow is absorbed, and the layer that absorbed it is permanently altered. Verdict: I keep going back and forth. At present I lean toward fragile, but I could not defend the lean.

The pair I cannot make consistent is 4 and 5. Both are single objects whose intrinsic structure simultaneously grounds a capacity and prevents its exercise; the only difference is that in 4 the blocking is done to the incoming stimulus and in 5 to the capacity itself. If the principle turns on whether the interference comes from inside the object, 4 and 5 get the same verdict and I lose one of them. If it turns on whether the grounding structure is still there, 5 comes out live and I lose the other. Case 2 pulls against both: there the conditional is true and the grounding structure absent, which suggests the structure is doing all the work — yet 3 has the structure and, on my verdict, no disposition.

My working principle, which I expect is broken in an instructive way: an object is disposed to M when S just in case it has some property B such that, in the nearest situation in which it is S and nothing interferes with B that is not already part of its own structure at the time, B brings about M.

What I would like back:

(a) The best principle you can give me, clause by clause, each clause paired with the case above that forces it — and, wherever you use terms such as intrinsic, basis, or interference, an account of how you are using them that does not presuppose the very disposition ascriptions the principle is meant to explain. I suspect that is where the real difficulty lies, and I would rather you say so than paper over it.

(b) Whether it delivers both 4 and 5, and both 3 and 4. If it cannot, say which of those verdicts goes and what I have to stop believing in order to accept that.

(c) Its verdicts on two situations I have not adjudicated. A wire that is live, and whose insulation is such that conducting for more than a second burns it out permanently, so that the first second of conduction is also its last. And a glass whose foam protects it against impacts but not against thermal shock: does it come out fragile simpliciter, or only fragile relative to a stimulus, and what does that imply about whether ordinary ascriptions ever pick out a single disposition at all?

(d) A plain statement of which of my eight verdicts the best simple principle overturns. If saving every one of them requires something baroque, I want to be told that too, and told which single verdict is cheapest to abandon.
