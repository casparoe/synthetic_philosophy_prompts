Been going in circles on the absentminded driver thing (the Piccione–Rubinstein puzzle: two exits that look identical from the road, 1 for getting off at the first, 4 at the second, 0 if you blow past both, and the guy can't tell where he is when he's sitting at a junction). I've read the usual material on plan-optimality vs action-optimality and the self-locating beleif stuff it shares with Sleeping Beauty, but here's a twist I don't remember seeing anyone work through properly. Suppose that before setting off he can buy an app that will ping him exactly once, at whatever exit he happens to reach, and tell him which one it is — for a fee that comes straight off his payoff.

Ex ante the app looks worthless: his best plan already nets 4, which is the ceiling, so no information can improve on it. But sitting at a junction running the naive credence math, it looks worth a couple of units and he'd happily pay that. Both numbers can't be right in the same currency.

Write an essay on which one is right, i.e. what he should actually pay for the app. I want the full case both ways: (a) the ex ante calculation governs and the action-time willingness to pay is just an artifact of confused credences about where he is, versus (b) the action-time calculation is the real one and something is broken in the standard way we value information under imperfect recall. Don't split the difference and don't end on "it depends which theory of rational choice you prefer" — pick a side, and say explicitly what has to be given up in order to keep it.
