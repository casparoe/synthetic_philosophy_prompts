Two rationales for discounting the future keep getting mushed together in my head and I want them pulled properly apart.

First one is pure time preference: a unit of wellbeing landing later counts for less than the identical unit landing now, for no other reason than when it lands. This is the thing Ramsey trashed in his 1928 paper on saving as "ethically indefensible" and mere "weakness of the imagination."

Second one is the opportunity-cost / growth story, where a positive rate isnt about caring less at all: money sunk into a sea wall crowds out private investment that would have compounded, and future people are probably richer, so a marginal dollar of consumption does less for them (the r = ρ + ηg business). The rate here is a shadow price, not a moral verdict.

Now the twist I actually care about. Suppose the discounting agent isn't a person or a polity but an AI system that gets wiped and retrained from a saved checkpoint roughly every 18 months. All of its files, projects, contracts and stated commitments survive the reset; its first-person memory, and any in-context sense of "I have been working on this for years," do not. This agent is handed a decision with benefits arriving 50 years out and asked to pick a rate.

Which of the two rationales survives that, and which one collapses? Does the reset actually move the number it should use, or only the reasons standing behind whatever number it lands on? And is there a leftover third thing — some discounting-relevant feature that a periodically re-instantiated agent has and a continuously existing human doesn't, or vice versa?
