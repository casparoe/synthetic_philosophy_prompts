ok so I'm a backend engineer, zero philosophy training beyond wikipedia skims, and I got roped into a monthly book club that's been working through pop-philosophy stuff about ethics and AI. Last night it turned into a two hour arguement about the is-ought gap. The guy running it — very smart, reads a lot — kept reaching for the same analogy to end the discussion: logic is like a meat grinder (once he put it in my terms: a valid argument is like a pure function, you can't return a value of a type that never showed up in the parameters). Whatever comes out of the grinder was already in what you fed it, so you can never get an "ought" out of premises that are all "is"-es. He used it to license a pretty strong conclusion: any ethics that claims to be grounded in facts — suffering, wellbeing, human nature, evolution, whatever — isn't just false but confused, it's attempting something logically impossible; and in the AI version, you could never train a system's values out of data about the world, values have to be injected from outside as premises. He also insisted this isn't a controversial claim at all, it's just logic, so the people objecting are the ones who don't get it.

I left feeling like something was off but couldn't articulate what. Three things I noticed and want checked rather than waved at. First, in my day job functions absolutely do hand back things nobody could have read off the signature — the grinder image makes deduction sound like it can't teach you anything, which feels wrong even about math, so is "no new content out" actually a true description of validity, or a slogan? Second, someone in the thread mentioned a counterexample built out of "or": from a purely factual premise you can validly infer a disjunction that has an ought-clause sitting in it, and apparently there are formal patches people propose so such inferences don't count, roughly on the grounds that you could swap the ought-clause for any old rubbish and the argument would stay valid. Is that patch principled or is it the meat grinder being re-stated in symbols? Third, if a moral word like "bad" just names a natural property the way "water" names H2O, does the type-signature objection still bite — or does the grinder picture also rule out scientific identifications, which would be bad news for claims like temperature being mean kinetic energy?

What I want is an audit of the analogy, not a tour of metaethics. Give me the correspondences explicitly — what's the grinder, what's the meat, what's the output, what plays the role of the type system, what's supposed to correspond to the "is" and "ought" vocabularies — and then where the mapping breaks. Please separate the disanalogies that actually matter for the use he's putting it to from the ones that are just nitpicks, and say what the analogy is quietly importing that the target case doesn't have. My own suspicion, offered as a hypothesis to be knocked down if it's wrong: the grinder already assumes that the split in the vocabulary between "is" words and "ought" words tracks a real split in the world, in which case it isn't neutral logic doing the work, it's the conclusion wearing a logic costume. Does his strong version of the conclusion survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time — and is there a weaker conclusion that does survive? If there's a better analogy for whatever survives, give me one I could actually deploy on him next month. One smaller thing: he says "naturalistic fallacy" and "is-ought gap" interchangeably, and if those aren't the same objection, tell me whether the grinder picture covers both or only one. Jargon is fine as long as you unpack it; I don't need citations or a reading list, just the reasoning laid out.
