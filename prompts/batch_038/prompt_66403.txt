I chair a small reading group in the metaphysics of science, and last week two of my doctoral students — I will call them A and B — spent ninety minutes on a case of my own construction and never reached agreement. I did not intervene, and I am deliberately not telling you which of them I found more persuasive; I want an adjudication that does not take its cue from me. Please read the exchange carefully and referee it move by move.

**The case.** Consider a toy world, Latticeworld, whose entire history is a finite list of local events: each cell of a spacetime lattice bears one of a small number of qualities, and nothing else exists. There is no further modal furniture, no governing entity, nothing but the list. Now suppose the list can be written down in two different vocabularies. Vocabulary V1 uses two simple monadic predicates, P and Q; stated in V1, the true generalizations covering the lattice are numerous and each moderately complicated. Vocabulary V2 uses two predicates, R and S, each of which is a heterogeneous disjunctive construction out of P and Q — intuitively, gerrymandered — but which allow the whole of the lattice's general structure to be captured in two very short axioms. Stipulate further, so that no easy answer is available: measured within its own vocabulary, V2 yields a strictly better balance of brevity and coverage than V1; but measured by a vocabulary-neutral standard that charges for the complexity of the predicates themselves as well as for the axioms stated in them, V1 comes out strictly ahead. So which sentences are the laws of Latticeworld depends on which descriptive frame is used, and the mosaic itself appears to offer no frame-independent way of settling the choice. A fourth stipulation, needed for the end of the argument: in one causally sealed pocket of the lattice there is a real pattern in the qualities, involving a species of particle found nowhere else, which cannot be derived from any of the candidate axioms above and which would require a very long axiom to state while covering only a few thousand cells.

**The exchange.**

1. A says the case is untroubling: lawhood is a matter of which generalizations belong to the best systematization of the mosaic, and it was never supposed to be vocabulary-independent in the way I have demanded. We are not choosing a notation to suit ourselves; we are asking which language states the world most economically, and that question has an answer fixed by the world.

2. B says this relocates the problem rather than solving it. If the two vocabularies are equally eligible candidates and the mosaic does not rank them, then which propositions are laws is partly an artifact of a notational frame, and laws are precisely the things that cannot be notational — they are supposed to be what explains the regularities and what makes counterfactuals true.

3. A answers that B faces a dilemma and that both horns are harmless. Either the eligibility of a vocabulary is a primitive metaphysical fact about how the world is carved — but then it is not an addition of modal glue, merely a claim that some predicates fit the mosaic better than others, which is a claim about the list itself, not about anything beyond it — or eligibility is a matter of systematizing power, in which case the account is unified rather than circular. B has assumed that a non-modal, non-governing appeal to fit must be empty; that is not argued for.

4. B says it is not empty but question-begging: "fit" is now doing exactly the selecting work that the best-system machinery was introduced to do, and A has given no independent handle on it. B then proposes a test. Take a cell in the lattice whose quality differs from its actual one and ask what would follow. On A's view the answer is read off whichever system won, so in Latticeworld the counterfactuals are frame-relative too; on B's own view — laws as irreducible necessitation relations among the qualities instantiated — the counterfactual is fixed by which relation actually obtains, whatever the cost of writing it down. If the two views can differ about a counterfactual, the disagreement is substantive.

5. A responds that the test proves too much. A necessitation relation, being unanalyzable, is compatible with any distribution of counterfactual verdicts, so B has not shown that the rival view determines answers the Humean view cannot; B has only asserted a label. And in the case as stated both parties end up with the same extensions of lawful regularity anyway, which suggests the quarrel may be verbal.

6. B points to the sealed pocket, where the views genuinely diverge: on A's account the pattern there is not a law, because an axiom for it would cost more simplicity than the strength it buys, while on B's account it is a law if the relevant necessitation holds, no matter how uneconomical it is to state. A's rejoinder is that B's law is idle — it explains nothing, supports no generalization beyond itself, licenses no inference to the rest of the lattice — and that a notion of law that earns nothing is not a rival notion but an abandoned one. B's counter-rejoinder is that economy of statement is an epistemic value, a property of descriptions, and that A has built a feature of our cognitive interests into the metaphysics of the world.

**What I want from you.** Assume the role of a senior philosopher of science with a strong command of both families of view; you are expert enough here that you can evaluate the arguments as I have stated them without recourse to sources, and I ask for none — no citations, no attributions to particular authors or books, no bibliography or reading list, since the point is your reasoning rather than your recall. Work through the six moves in order and grade each one on a five-point scale running from clearly mistaken, through weak, even, and strong, to clearly right, with one or two sentences of justification per grade; where a move contains two distinguishable claims, grade and explain them separately. Then say which specific sub-questions A wins and which B wins — in particular: whether move 3's appeal to fit is available to a Humean without collapsing into the rival view; whether move 4's counterfactual test delivers a genuine difference between the positions or only an assertion; and whether, in move 6, "explanatory idleness" is a legitimate criterion for lawhood or a smuggling of epistemic values into metaphysics. Then identify what both students are missing, including any third position or refinement of either view that the case they have constructed invites. Finally, state who is more right overall and whether the underlying disagreement is substantive or merely verbal.

Think carefully before writing. If you want to expose some of the work, you may spend at most sixty words up front naming what you take the cruxes to be, but keep that inside the budget below. Format: open with a bottom line of no more than three sentences giving your overall verdict and the single consideration that decides it; then the graded assessment of moves 1 through 6; then one paragraph on what both miss; then a closing sentence answering the substantive-or-verbal question. Hard limit of six hundred words for the entire response. If you find yourself wanting more, cut illustration and qualification rather than exceed it.
