For a seminar I am taking on the economics and ethics of price controls, our cohort has split into two factions that have spent weeks talking past one another on rent ceilings. I would like you to draft the joint statement that a good-faith adversarial collaboration between them would produce.

The first camp holds that a ceiling on rents misallocates the existing stock, blunts maintenance and mobility incentives, and, through the supply response, ultimately falls on future tenants. The second camp holds that urban land and housing markets are characterised by highly inelastic supply, localised landlord market power, and a good that is not fungible with other consumption, so that the competitive partial-equilibrium model is the wrong benchmark and the burden of proof sits with those who invoke it.

To stop them arguing about different cities, stipulate the following: a mid-sized European city in which roughly forty per cent of dwellings are privately rented, fifteen per cent are social housing, the vacancy rate is about one per cent, new construction requires discretionary planning approval, and the proposal on the table caps rent increases for sitting tenants at inflation plus one percentage point, resetting to market level when a tenancy ends.

The statement should contain: (1) a numbered section of claims both camps can sign, including claims about what the standard model predicts, about which of its assumptions are actually in dispute, and about the ethical considerations both regard as relevant even where they disagree about weight; (2) a table of residual disagreements, each row phrased as its own side would accept it, with a column marking the row substantive or merely verbal — and where you judge that part of the dispute (for instance over what counts as "supply", or over whose counterfactual is the relevant baseline) turns on words, say which part and why; (3) the cruxes, understood as the sub-questions whose resolution would genuinely move at least one camp; and (4) for each crux, the design of a study, natural experiment, or argument both camps would accept as bearing on it, together with each camp's prediction recorded in advance, including direction and rough magnitude where a magnitude can sensibly be given.

Please make one of the cruxes the question of whether the monopsony-based challenge to the competitive prediction in the minimum-wage debate transfers to housing, and be explicit about what would have to be true of landlord market power, tenant search costs, and supply elasticity for the analogy to carry. Describe evidence by its design rather than by author, and do not include a reference list. Please write in British English.
