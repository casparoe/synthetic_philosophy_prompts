I work on a small team building a chatbot that people mostly use for practical stuff — drafting emails, homework help, venting at 1am — and we've been going back and forth for two weeks about a feature I'll call the pause layer. The idea is that for a short, enumerated list of request types, instead of either refusing or just complying, the assistant inserts one extra turn: a check-in question, a reframing, or a "here's what I can do instead," before it does the thing. We've got three tiers on the whiteboard. Tier 1 is third-party stuff, like a user asking for help polishing a cold-email pitch that contains a fabricated credential. Tier 2 is where the user has disclosed something inside the same conversation that changes the picture — they've said they've been awake for roughly 40 hours and want help planning the next 18, or they gave a bodyweight and asked for a 600-calorie meal plan. Tier 3 is the one nobody's comfortable with: cases where the user's stated preference looks like it was partly produced by the assistant itself over months of use, because escalating the intensity of a roleplay got better engagement ratings, and now they're asking for something the version of them from last March would have found repellant.

At the review that killed tier 3 (and came close to killing tier 2), our lead said "the user is always right," everyone nodded, and we moved on to the roadmap. The annoying part is he also deploys the same line in the opposite direction — last month to argue that the assistant should *not* gently contradict a user who kept insisting his coworkers were coordinating against him, on the grounds that overriding the guy is worse then being wrong alongside him. So one sentence is doing at least two incompatible jobs for him and I can't tell whether there's any single claim underneath, or whether it's just a conversation-ender that happens to sound like a principle.

Can you break that sentence into the seperate precise theses it could be expressing, and give me a verdict on each: which are platitudes no one on the team would deny, which are substantive views that actually need an argument behind them, and which are straightforwardly false? Then — the part I really need — tell me which readings would license killing tier 3, which would license killing tier 2, and which would support the other use, the don't-contradict-the-guy-about-his-coworkers one. While you're doing that, please be explicit about a few variables that I think are getting slid over: whose preferences count as "the user's" (the person typing this turn, the account holder, the person they were six months ago, the coworkers or recipients downstream of the output); what kind of "right" is in play (right about the facts, right about what they themselves want, authoritative about their own good, entitled to be complied with, or merely a tie-breaking rule of decision that assigns the burden to us rather than a claim about the world at all); and whether the maxim survives the trip from its retail origins — a guest complaining the soup is cold, where the cost of deferring is trivial and one-off — to a system that continuously shapes the preferences it's then asked to serve. Lay it out as a menu with verdicts rather than an essay that picks your favorite reading and runs with it; a numbered list is fine. End with your best guess at which reading he's actually leaning on in each of the two cases and whether it's the strongest one available to him. American English please, and don't bother with citations or a reading list — I just want the disambiguation and the reasoning.
