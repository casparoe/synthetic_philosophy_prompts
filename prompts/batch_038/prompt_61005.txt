I'm on a small-town school renaming commission and last night we burned three hours on a building named after a Virginia planter who owned a couple hundred people. The longest-serving member says "he was a man of his time" every single time somebody brings up what he actually did, and she says it like it ends the conversation, and honestly I dont think she even knows which claim she's making. I've caught her using it to mean at least three different things in one evening, and when I try to nail down which one she means she tells me I'm being pedantic.

Can you give me the full menu of precise claims that sentence could be making? Start at the bottom with whatever version is trivially true of every human being who has ever lived, work up through the substantive ones, and end with the most radical thing anybody could seriously mean by it. For each one: what exactly it commits you to, whether it's a platitude or a real thesis, whether it's true in this case, and — the part I actually need — whether it licenses the move she's making with it.

Because that's the thing I can feel but can't argue. She's using it to stop the renaming. But renaming isn't a verdict on whether the man was wicked, it's a decision about who our kids walk past every morning and who we're willing to hold up as ours. I have a strong suspicion that even if every one of her readings came out true, not one of them gets her to "leave the name up," and that there's a silent step in there about the difference between blaming a dead person and choosing what to celebrate. If that's right, say it plainly and show me exactly where the gap is.

Also flag the readings that backfire. The ones where the same principle applied to us right now would deliver something she'd hate, since somebody in a hundred years is going to say the exact same thing about us and whatever we're doing today. And the ones that just die on the facts of this case: the guy wrote at length about how the whole institution rotted both master and slave, floated gradual emancipation plans more than once, said he dreaded a just reckoning, and freed a handful of people out of hundreds. Does that kill the "he couldn't have known" reading outright, or only one version of it?

One more wrinkle. Some of these readings are going to turn on how typical he was, and I think "typical" is getting smuggled in two ways — typical of his peer group, versus typical of anyone who had the relevant information available to them. The peer group is doing quiet work there, because you can make almost anybody look typical just by shrinking the group enough. Say which readings depend on that and how much.

Numbered list I can hand out, plain english, no throat-clearing, and be exhaustive: I'd rather bring a list then a vibe.
