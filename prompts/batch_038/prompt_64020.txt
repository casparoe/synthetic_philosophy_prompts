I write literary fiction and I've talked myself into a pretty hardline view: if a forgery is genuinely indistinguishable from the original on the page or the canvas — no tells, no anachronisms, nothing a scholar with the archive could catch — then provenance can't matter aesthetically. It's the aesthetic-empiricist line, basically. Friends think its naive, and I'll admit the view feels a little too tidy.

So: what would count against it? I want the arguments, intuitions and possible cases that should actually move me, not just the ones a hostile critic would reach for. Three I especially care about: (a) cases where provenance changes what a work *says* rather than how it looks — a forger's "lost" novel in a dead writer's voice that readers take as that writer's own late reckoning with his marriage, or the chapter of mine a friend ghostwrote years back that nobody ever spotted; (b) the claim that a perfect forgery is impossible in principle because style is evidence of a hand and a period, so my "genuinely indistinguishable" clause never gets a real case to bite on; and (c) whether detectability-in-principle smuggles in something aesthetic that survives going undetected in practice.

Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a defender like me would honestly concede and which I'd explain away, and give me a straight verdict on whether the indistinguishability clause insulates the view past the point where it risks anything — and whether that insulation is a defect or a legitimate way to hold a position like this.
