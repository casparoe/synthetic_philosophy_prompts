I am the marker for a final-year undergraduate paper in the philosophy of physics, and I would like an independent second reading of one candidate's examination script. Please write your assessment in British English throughout.

The question set was as follows:

"In the Everett interpretation, every outcome of every quantum measurement occurs. Explain why this is thought to create a problem for probability. Distinguish at least two distinct versions of that problem, and assess one proposed solution. (25 marks)"

Candidates were allowed forty-five minutes for the question and were not permitted to consult any materials. The script is reproduced in full and uncorrected:

---

Everett keeps the linear dynamics and drops collapse, so a measurement on a system in superposition leaves the total state as a superposition of system, apparatus and observer, each term containing an observer with a definite record. Every outcome therefore occurs, in the sense that each is real relative to some branch. The difficulty is that probability seems to have no work left to do. If it is certain that some observer sees spin up and equally certain that some other sees spin down, then a claim such as "the probability of up is 0.36" cannot mean that up is likelier to happen, since nothing fails to happen. Nor can it be an ordinary frequency claim, because every frequency one might want to count is realised somewhere in the multiverse.

Two problems should be kept apart here. The incoherence problem asks whether an Everettian agent can have anything deserving the name of uncertainty at all: she knows the wavefunction, she knows it evolves deterministically, so she seems to know exactly what will happen, and it is unclear what her credence could be about. The quantitative problem is the second step: grant that some notion of uncertainty or of rational betting can be made coherent, and ask why the numbers it yields should be the squared amplitudes rather than something else, for instance one over the number of branches.

Everett's own response was a typicality argument. One puts a measure over the branches, or over sequences of outcomes in repeated measurements, and shows that the sequences failing to display the Born frequencies have measure zero, so an observer should expect to find herself in a branch where the statistics look normal. This plainly assumes much of what is wanted, or at least it introduces a measure without explaining why that measure should be read as probability.

The most developed answer is decision-theoretic. Deutsch and Wallace argue that a rational agent who accepts the Everettian picture, and whose preferences over rewards distributed across branches satisfy certain structural conditions, is compelled to act as though the branches were weighted by squared amplitude. The theorem is a representation theorem of broadly Savagean form: given the axioms, the agent's preferences can be represented as though she had credences, and those credences must be the Born weights. Two features are usually emphasised. First, the axioms mention neither probabilities nor amplitudes, so the familiar charge that the derivation presupposes the Born rule cannot get started, since there is nothing in the premises through which it could be smuggled. Second, the argument proceeds by fine-graining the state into ever more branches until unequal weights can be treated as ratios of equal pieces.

That second step is shared with a different line of argument. Zurek's envariance argument begins from symmetries: if a joint state of system and environment is left unchanged by swapping two equally weighted components, the probabilities of those components must be equal; Wallace then extends the argument by fine-graining the environment until any amplitude ratio becomes a ratio of counts of equal pieces, yielding the general rule. A third proposal, due to Sebens and Carroll, locates the missing uncertainty in self-location: after the measurement interaction but before the observer has inspected the record there are several copies of her, and she does not know which of them she is. Because the wavefunction never collapses, this ignorance is ignorance about the future state of the universe, which is just the kind of ignorance that credences standardly track; the claim is then that self-locating credences, conditionalised in the usual way, must come out equal to the Born weights.

Two further points. Gleason's theorem shows that any probability assignment over the outcomes of measurements in a Hilbert space of three or more dimensions, provided it is additive over mutually exclusive outcomes, must take the Born form for some density operator. Since the Everettian keeps the entire Hilbert space structure and adds nothing to it, the quantitative problem is therefore already settled at the level of the formalism, and what remains is only to show that uncertainty is coherent. Relatedly, branch counting cannot be the right procedure in any case, because decoherence does not select a unique decomposition of the state into branches and so there is no fact about how many branches a given measurement produces; had there been such a fact, counting them would have been the natural thing to do.

Even so, one reservation seems fair. The decision-theoretic result, if sound, shows how an Everettian agent ought to bet; it does not show that the world contains objective chances. Nothing in the arguments above closes that gap.
