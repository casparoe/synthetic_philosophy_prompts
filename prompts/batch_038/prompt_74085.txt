Our first-year seminar closes with a mock tribunal: one student plays the Athenian Stranger of the *Laws* and must hand down a ruling on a case the class has not discussed, defending it solely from Plato's prior rulings on drink. The record we have compiled runs as follows. At the *Symposium* the party elects a president, drinking is left to each man's own wish with no compulsion, and Socrates drinks everyone under the table, then bathes and walks off at dawn unaffected. In the *Republic* the guardians are barred from drunkenness outright. In the first two books of the *Laws* the Stranger argues that wine should be managed rather than prohibited; he proposes as a thought experiment a drug that would induce terror, so that courage could be tested and trained at no real risk, and then treats wine as exactly such a drug when a sober overseer directs its use; he withholds wine entirely before eighteen, permits it in moderation until thirty, and after forty admits it freely at the common mess as Dionysus's remedy for the sourness of age. In the *Gorgias* the life of maximal appetite and unlimited satisfaction is rejected.

Our case: a magistrate of forty-six drinks alone at home each evening, a fixed and modest amount, in order to sleep. He never loses command of himself. There is no company, no president of the feast, no training purpose, and no civic occasion — only rest.

Predict the ruling. Lay out the candidate rules that fit the record equally well, identify precisely where they come apart on this case, argue which has the strongest claim, and name the single further case that would separate them. Finally: which prior ruling is the closest analog here, and what principle would the Stranger be committing himself to by ruling either way?
