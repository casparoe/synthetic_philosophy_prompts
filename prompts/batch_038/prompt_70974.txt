You are a philosopher of mind who works on the epistemology of consciousness. Please think carefully and work through the following as a rigorous audit rather than a survey; I want to know exactly where my position creaks.

Context: I am a property dualist of the epiphenomenalist variety, and I have been sharpening my view in preparation for a workshop where it will be attacked. Here are my commitments, stated as precisely as I can manage:

(1) Phenomenal properties are caused by neural processes but are themselves causally inert. They produce no effects on the physical world and none on other mental states — not on judgment, belief, reasoning, or behavior.

(2) Knowledge is not merely reliably produced true belief; it requires sensitivity in roughly this sense: S knows that p only if, were p false, S would not believe that p.

(3) I accept the standard common-cause reply about self-knowledge of pain. When I believe I am in pain, some neural state N causes both the pain and the belief, and the belief is about the pain in virtue of sharing a cause with it. I regard this as entirely adequate, and I am unbothered by the following consequence: had the psychophysical laws been different, so that N produced a mild itch rather than a pain, I would still have formed the belief that I was in pain, since the belief comes from N.

(4) I nevertheless maintain that when I am in pain I know that I am in pain, and I treat this as a paradigm of knowledge rather than a lucky true belief.

(5) The principal evidence that there are phenomenal properties over and above the physical is testimonial and behavioral: the sincerity and ubiquity of people's reports, the fact that a science of consciousness has something to investigate at all, and the evident impression that any purely physical description of a person leaves something out.

(6) Epiphenomenalism is a conservative addition to a physicalist worldview: it leaves every physical fact, law, and prediction exactly as it was, and a world from which phenomenal properties had been removed would be physically indistinguishable from ours in every respect.

What I want from you:

- Begin by brainstorming. For each pair of commitments that looks as though it might conflict, sketch the mechanism of the conflict before deciding whether it is real.
- Then separate genuine tensions from merely apparent ones. At least one pair here looks like a serious problem and is not; identify it and explain precisely why it dissolves under my own stated glosses.
- Rank the genuine tensions by severity, from worst to mildest, and for each state what would have to be abandoned or reinterpreted and what that costs me.
- Finally, name the single revision that would resolve the most trouble, and describe what the position looks like afterward.

Please do not give me a general introduction to epiphenomenalism or recite the standard objections to it; assume I already know them, and assume I know what physicalists will say. Do not suggest readings or cite sources. If my package turns out to amount to a recognized problem with a name, you may say so in a single sentence at the very end.
