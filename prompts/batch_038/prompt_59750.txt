I write a weekly column on science and ideas, and I am currently reporting a piece about consent in large, long-running health databanks. One of the researchers I interviewed — a bioethicist with a legal training — offered an analogy he clearly leans on a great deal, and I should like it audited before I put it in print.

His claim is that research consent is fundamentally a contract, and that critics who ask for more are demanding something the concept cannot deliver. On his telling there is an offer (a participant information sheet setting out the terms), an acceptance (a signature, or a click), a price (the participant's samples and data, against whatever benefits the study promises), a presumption of capacity in adults, and duties of disclosure rather than duties to secure comprehension. What binds, he insists, is the transaction and not the state of mind behind it — much as English law has long held that a signature binds whether or not the signatory read the document. From this he draws two conclusions: that a plain-language summary followed by a single click is valid consent, provided nothing was concealed or misrepresented; and that consent, once given, fixes entitlements at a moment, so a participant's later change of heart cannot unravel uses already made in good faith. When critics object that people did not really grasp what they were agreeing to, he says they are smuggling in a romantic picture of consent as inner endorsement, which contract law sensibly abandoned.

The setting that makes this hard to swallow is the one my piece is actually about: samples held for thirty years, onward sharing with researchers and companies who were not identifiable at the outset, recontact impractical, and a right of withdrawal that exists in the governance documents but is hollow once the data have been pooled, analysed and published. A patients' advocate I spoke to offered a rival analogy in reply — consent is like lending a house key: you specify who may enter and for how long, you can ask for it back, and handing a copy to a stranger's friend is not covered by the original loan, however plainly that loan was worded. She uses it to argue that unspecified onward transfer is a distinct act requiring fresh permission, and that a right of withdrawal which cannot be exercised is no right at all.

Could you assess both analogies? Specifically: a correspondence table for each (for the contract, what plays the part of offer, acceptance, consideration, capacity, disclosure, the thing transferred, breach and rescission; for the key, the key, the borrower, the door, the term, the copy); the disanalogies, separated into those that genuinely bear on the click-through conclusion and those that are merely decorative; what each source domain quietly imports that this kind of consent lacks — negotiability, symmetry of stakes, the ability to walk away, damages as a remedy, the discreteness of the moment of signature, the separability of the thing lent; and whether either conclusion survives once that is all on the table. If both analogies fail at the load-bearing points, tell me what source domain would carry the work better, what it would license, and what it would forbid.

I do not want references or a reading list, only the analysis. Please write in British English, open with your verdict in two sentences, and keep the whole thing to 650 words, tables included.
