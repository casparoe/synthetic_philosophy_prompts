I'm a junior, and this term I'm in an after-school seminar on ethics. For our unit on honesty, our teacher handed out a primary source rather than a modern article, and I've hit a wall with it. The text is Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologiae*, Second Part of the Second Part, question 110, article 1 ("Whether lying is always opposed to truth?"), in an old English translation I found on a free website — I believe it is the version by the Fathers of the English Dominican Province. I can follow the general shape of the article (the objections, then "On the contrary," then "I answer that," then the replies to the objections), so I don't need help with the format. What I need help with is the argument itself, and specifically how it bears on a question our class has been circling without progress: whether deliberately misleading someone with statements that are all literally true is really different from outright lying.

Here is the stretch that has me stuck, taken from the body of the article:

> Accordingly if these three things concur, namely, falsehood of what is said, the will to tell a falsehood, and finally the intention to deceive, then there is falsehood—materially, since what is said is false, formally, on account of the will to tell an untruth, and effectively, on account of the will to impart a falsehood. However, the essential notion of a lie is taken from formal falsehood, from the fact namely, that a person intends to say what is false … even if what one says be true, yet inasmuch as this is a voluntary and moral act, it contains falseness essentially and truth accidentally, and attains the specific nature of a lie. That a person intends to cause another to have a false opinion, by deceiving him, does not belong to the species of lying, but to perfection thereof …

And then, in the reply to the first objection:

> it is more in opposition to truth, considered as a moral virtue, to tell the truth with the intention of telling a falsehood than to tell a falsehood with the intention of telling the truth.

Four things would help me most. First, what work is each of "materially," "formally," and "effectively" doing here? My teacher insists these are not three ways of saying the same thing, but I cannot see how a single act could have all three or only some of them, or what changes morally if one is missing. Second, if the intention to deceive belongs only to the "perfection" of lying and not to its "species," does Aquinas mean that a person can lie without trying to deceive anyone at all — and if so, why does he bother listing that intention among the three things that concur? Third, and this is the one I most need settled, my classmate argues that on this account a literally true but carefully arranged statement is still a lie, because "the essential notion of a lie is taken from formal falsehood" and that has to do with the speaker's mind, not with the sentence's truth value. Consider the case I want to test this on: the editor of our school paper, asked directly whether the paper is funded by the administration, answers that no check from the principal's office has ever been deposited in the paper's account, while knowing perfectly well that the district prints the paper for free and pays the hosting bill. Does the passage above classify that answer as a lie, as a case of hiding the truth without lying, or as something Aquinas simply does not have a category for? Please point to the exact phrases that decide it. Fourth, the reply to objection 1 defeats me: why would telling the truth while intending to tell a falsehood be *more* opposed to truth than telling a falsehood while intending to tell the truth? I would like an explanation of what "more in opposition to truth, considered as a moral virtue" is meant to convey, plus one concrete example of each of the two situations described, because I have not been able to construct a case in which a person both tells the truth and intends to tell a falsehood. Where any of this cannot be settled from the passage I have quoted — because it would turn on material elsewhere in the question or in the following question on the kinds of lies — please say so plainly instead of guessing at what Aquinas argues there.
