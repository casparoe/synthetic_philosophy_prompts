I serve as the non-physician member — a clinical psychologist — of the human research ethics committee at an academic medical center, a seat I have held for two years. Our terms of reference contain a single sentence that has done all the work until now: protocols involving interventions on healthy volunteers fall within our remit only where the intervention addresses a disorder, and protocols properly described as enhancement are referred elsewhere. That sentence has stopped functioning. In the past twelve months we have seen a trial of transcranial direct-current stimulation to accelerate procedural-skill acquisition in healthy surgical trainees; a placebo-controlled trial of a wakefulness-promoting agent in healthy residents rostered to night shifts; a trial of app-based working-memory training in healthy school-age children whose parents are worried about academic standing; and, most recently, a request to give healthy volunteers a drug already licensed for a sleep disorder in order to shorten recovery from jet lag in airline crew. We approved the first two, rejected the third, and split on the fourth, and I cannot reconstruct a principle that would explain our own record. The inconsistency runs in a direction I find personally hard to sit with: I cast the deciding vote against a protocol for stimulant use in healthy residents, and three months later learned that our employee-health service has been dispensing the same drug off-label to staff on request. My vote protected no one, and it cost a research team its funding.

I would like a decision procedure my committee can actually run, rather than another account of why the distinction is hard. Specifically:

1. It must be operable by seven people in forty-five minutes — two physicians, a pharmacist, a lawyer, a statistician, a patient representative, and me — under a chair who is not a specialist in this literature and who must be able to explain the outcome to a principal investigator who disagrees with it. It must also generate the text that goes into our minutes, which are capped at three hundred words of justification per protocol.

2. Every protocol should exit the procedure with exactly one of these labels: APPROVE, APPROVE-WITH-CONDITIONS, DEFER (with the missing fact named), REFER (the question is one of institutional values rather than fact, and belongs to the board, not to us), or REJECT. Tell me the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure terminates in a label, and where it deliberately stops and hands the matter to a human being.

3. Ground it in what the therapy/enhancement material actually supports, and be candid about which of the familiar distinctions survives contact with practice and which does not. At minimum, address: normal-functioning baselines, and how they behave when the baseline is contested — a trained athlete, a chronically sleep-deprived resident, a child measured against her classmates; prevention as a third category rather than a species of therapy, and whether it inherits therapy's permissibility; positional or competitive goods, where a participant's benefit depends on others not having it; interventions that are hard to distinguish from education, nutrition, or coaching; and the appeals we keep hearing from speakers — authenticity, giftedness, a child's open future — identifying which of these can be converted into a question a reviewer can answer from the protocol documents in front of them, and which cannot and should be marked as such.

4. Do not require information a protocol would not contain, and do not require us to settle contested philosophy in the minutes. Where a step embeds a judgment call wearing the clothes of a rule, say so in the step itself.

5. Run the procedure on three cases. (a) The working-memory app in healthy children, where the outcome measure is a standardized academic test and the sponsor is a consumer company. (b) The jet-lag drug in healthy airline crew. (c) A protocol we received last week for a commercially available implanted neural stimulation device in healthy adults, aimed at sustained vigilance, with defence-related funding and — I want to be fair about this — a consent process and long-term follow-up plan that are better than anything else on our docket. Apply the procedure to (c) honestly, even if the answer is one I will dislike. If it comes out as APPROVE, say so plainly, and then tell me what the procedure was structurally unable to see in that case, and what would have to be added to see it.

Finally, please tell me how confident you are in the load-bearing parts — in particular, whether the distinctions you build the procedure on are ones the literature genuinely supports or ones you are supplying because they make the procedure runnable, since I will be defending this to people who know it better than I do. Then single out the one step you are least sure of and explain what would make it fail.

End your response with a line beginning "Verdict:" followed by exactly one of the five labels for case (c), and nothing else on that line.
