I work in reliability engineering — my days are spent comparing predicted failure rates against observed ones — and for the past two years I have been auditing an evening philosophy-of-science seminar, mostly because I wanted a vocabulary for arguments I keep having with colleagues about which of two wrong models is the better one. The seminar has given me names for a few things and no help at all with the thing I actually care about, so I am writing down my own judgements and asking whether there is a principle behind them, or whether some of them have to go. Every case below involves claims that are strictly false, and in each the question is which of two false claims is nearer to how things are. I am not after a reading list or references; I am after the principle itself, stated so precisely that I could apply it to a case neither of us has thought of.

Here are my judgements. (1) Our curing process takes 6.0 hours. One supplier's data sheet said 4 hours, another said 9. In hours, 4 is the nearer miss; but 4 is six multiplied by two-thirds and 9 is six multiplied by one-and-a-half, so each is off by exactly the same factor, one downward and one upward. When I said "equally wrong," a colleague said "no, four is nearer," and neither of us could identify what we were disagreeing about. I want to say we were both right, and I cannot see how. (2) Two false guesses at the attendance of an event whose actual count was 268: "between 300 and 400" and "between 1,000 and 1,200." I rank the first clearly nearer. But as far as I can tell, neither claim's true consequences contain the other's — each entails truths the other fails to entail, and falsehoods the other fails to entail — so an account that ranks claims by the truth and falsity they contain ought to refuse to rank them at all. I take that refusal to be a defect in the account rather than a vindication of it. (3) "Either it will rain tomorrow or it will not" is, so far as I can see, the only claim in my entire list that carries no falsehood whatsoever. If nearness were a matter of containing no error, that claim would win outright. I rank it last. (4) A newsletter that was flatly wrong about an election starts appending a dozen indisputable sentences about the weather and the phase of the moon to every issue. It now entails a good many truths it did not entail before, and I cannot find a single falsehood in the new version that is not either an old falsehood or an old falsehood with one of the new truths stapled to it. On any tallying account the padded version looks at least as good as the unpadded one; I do not share that verdict, and I notice that what I mean by "the truth" in this case is "the truth about the election," though I cannot say what fixes the topic, since the padding leaves the election content exactly as it was. (5) Two wrong maps of a metro system. Map A gets every line and every interchange right but distorts all distances, squashing the network into a shape no surveyor would accept. Map B has all the distances right but shows an interchange that does not exist and omits one that does. For a passenger trying to get across the city, A is nearer the truth; as a picture of the city, B is nearer. I want both of those verdicts, and I also want to deny that either map is nearer, full stop. (6) A load limit that is really 9.6 tonnes. "Ten tonnes" is nearer in the arithmetic than "nine tonnes," and yet I would send a truck across on the strength of the nine and not on the strength of the ten. I do not want to say that safety is a separate subject from closeness; I want to say that here "nearer than" is not a comparison between two claims but between two claims and a use. (7) A stuck thermometer reads 20.0 °C in a room that is 20.0 °C; a working one reads 19.5 °C. The stuck one's reading is exact, hence nearer, and I would still take the working one without hesitation, because its readings are near not only here but in the next room and tomorrow. I appear to slide between ranking claims and ranking the habits that produce them without noticing that I have changed the subject.

I dimly remember from an undergraduate course that someone once tried to define nearness to the truth in terms of the truths a claim entails and the falsehoods it entails, and that the definition collapsed under objections I no longer recall; several of my judgements above look to me like ways of feeling that collapse rather than independent reasons to accept it. So: is there a principle — a single measure, or a small family of measures together with a rule for choosing among them — that delivers these verdicts, or nearly all of them? I would like each clause of it tied back to the case that motivates the clause, and I would like it applied to a case I have not listed: two competing estimates of a machine's remaining life, the first right about the mean and wrong about the spread, the second right about the spread and wrong about the mean. Then I want an honest accounting of which of my seven judgements the principle cannot accommodate, and which single one I should be most willing to surrender. If the honest answer is that no one relation is doing the work here — that I am using two or three relations under a single name — I would much rather hear that than be handed a tidy label; but in that case please say exactly where the boundaries lie between the relations you end up positing, which of my cases falls on each side of each boundary, and whether the relations can ever conflict in a single case, since (1), (5), (6) and (7) all look to me like places where they do.
