Taking a course on electoral systems this term and I'm supposed to be able to defend some position on reform, but my own intuitions are a mess, so I'd like to know what principle they actually add up to — if any. Here are my verdicts, mostly from a problem set we did with made-up countries:

- Party A gets 44% of the vote, party B 43%, and A wins 60% of the seats. I don't feel any injustice here, it's just how single-member districts work.
- Same 44/43 result, except the map was drawn so that A would have won 60% even on 38%. That one feels like a genuine wrong. Is it a *different* wrong from the first?
- In the problem set, a party with 12% of the vote spread evenly everywhere wins one seat, while a party with 5%, all of it in one region, wins 56. I called this a scandal in seminar and I still think it is.
- But when a regional party wins nearly all the seats in the region where it's actually popular, I think that's a feature and not a bug.
- Hung parliament: the largest party gets shut out of government by a coalition of the second, third and fourth parties. Even with perfectly proportional seat totals my gut says the election was stolen.
- I voted tactically for my second choice to block someone I really dislike. Didn't feel wasted, but I also don't feel represented by my MP.
- A district decided by one vote out of 40,000: I think the losers' votes should count for nothing *and* that the winner should get the seat.

The principle I keep reaching for is "seats should mirror votes", which I'm pretty sure doesn't survive #1 or the hung parliament. Can you state the real candidates precisely, say which of my verdicts motivates each clause, run them on cases I haven't thought of (a party that only stands in half the districts; 30% turnout in one seat and 80% in another; measuring against votes cast vs. eligible voters; a gerrymander in the minority party's favour), and just tell me straight which of my intuitions have to go?
