I am preparing a session of a graduate reading group on nineteenth-century responses to mechanism, and I have run into an exegetical snag in a sentence that is quoted constantly but, I suspect, rarely read closely. It comes from Thomas Henry Huxley's 1874 essay "On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata, and its History":

"The consciousness of brutes would appear to be related to the mechanism of their body simply as a collateral product of its working, and to be as completely without any power of modifying that working as the steam-whistle which accompanies the work of a locomotive engine is without influence upon its machinery."

Here is the snag. I want to know what work the whistle is actually doing in the argument, because the standard use of this sentence — as the founding statement of epiphenomenalism — seems to slide past a distinction the sentence itself makes available.

Let me lay out the distinction, since it is my own coinage and I would rather you attack it than go looking for a precedent. Call a relation between two processes interventionist when one can be used to alter the other, and covariational when the two merely track one another. A steam whistle, whatever else it is, is an excellent covariational device: its pitch and loudness shift systematically with boiler pressure and with the state of the valve gear, so that someone standing trackside can read off a good deal about the engine from the sound alone. It is, by hypothesis, interventionally inert with respect to the machinery. So the analogy, read carefully, appears to offer tracking without intervention — and that pairing is exactly what a modern epiphenomenalist should want, because it looks like a ready-made answer to the most serious objection to the view.

The objection is this. If conscious states have no causal purchase on the physical world, then every neural event underlying my utterance "the red of the afterimage is spreading toward the edges" is fully accounted for without mentioning the afterimage; the utterance would have had the same cause, and been produced just as reliably, in a creature with the neural state and no afterimage. So either what I say about my experience is true of it only by a standing miracle of parallel variation, or — worse — my reports are not about the experience at all, and I am systematically misdescribing my own case with no way in principle of detecting the misdescription. That is the paradox of phenomenal judgment. My contention is that the covariational half of Huxley's analogy is precisely the resource needed to defuse it: what makes a report about the afterimage is not that the afterimage caused it, but that afterimage and neural correlate are locked together in the same kind of systematic co-variation that locks the whistle's pitch to boiler pressure.

Three questions, in descending order of importance to me.

First, is that resource really in the sentence? Note that Huxley writes "as completely without any power of modifying that working," and that he describes consciousness as "a collateral product of its working" rather than as an accompaniment to it. I want to know whether "completely" and "product" together close off the covariational reading or leave it open, and what turns on the answer. In particular: does a whistle that is produced by the working of the engine — that draws steam from the boiler and imposes back pressure on the cylinders — remain completely without power of modifying that working? And is a whistle the right sort of thing to invoke at all, given that a locomotive's whistle is sounded deliberately by the driver through a valve rather than emerging automatically from the engine's operation? If you think that last observation sinks the analogy, say so plainly; if you think it is a quibble that leaves Huxley's point intact, say that instead.

Second, suppose the covariational reply does go through. Does it rescue the epiphenomenalist, or merely relocate the difficulty? My worry is that co-variation between experience and neural state is itself something that, on this view, nothing explains and nothing could have brought about — whereas whistle and boiler pressure covary because someone designed and plumbed them to. The analogy may therefore smuggle in a designer the epiphenomenalist has no right to invoke. I want your judgment on whether that asymmetry is decisive or can be answered.

Third, a smaller point I do not want to lose. What is the hedge doing? "Would appear to be" strikes me as doing something other than Victorian politeness, and I want to know whether it licenses reading Huxley as uncommitted — in which case the paradox of phenomenal judgment never gets a grip on him, since he asserts nothing — or as committed but cautious, in which case it does.

I am asking for a committed answer rather than a survey. On each of the three questions, give me your own verdict and the reasons for it; if you think the covariation/intervention distinction is the wrong tool for this sentence, say so and say what tool would cut better. Where your reading rests on a premise about steam locomotives rather than on the text, flag that dependence explicitly. And where you think I have asked the sentence to bear more weight than it can, cut it down to what it will support. Please work entirely from the passage quoted above and from general reasoning; I am not looking for pointers to the secondary literature.
