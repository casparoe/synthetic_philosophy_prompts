So I'm on the "agreements circle" for a community tool library and garden co-op — three of us who hear it when one member says another member didn't do what they said they'd do. There's a woman, Priya, who's been doing this informally for like nine years, longer than the circle has existed, and she has an eerie consistancy to her that I've been trying to reverse-engineer because I'm the one who has to write up our decisions for the monthly newsletter and she never explains herself. Her track record as far as I've reconstructed it:

- Ren said in the group chat "count on me for the van Saturday" to help Maya move. Maya turned down a moving company and booked the freight elevator for Saturday. Ren flaked the morning of. Priya: Ren was in the wrong and owed Maya what the movers would have cost her.
- Tomas mentioned in passing, twice, that he'd "probably give Ines the old ladder" once he upgraded. He never said he promised anything. Ines didn't buy a ladder and waited six months; Tomas sold it to someone else. Priya: no wrong done, but he should have told her sooner that it wasn't happening.
- Jo told Sam she'd let everyone know he'd taken money from the till unless he agreed to work every Saturday for a month. He agreed, then didn't show up. Priya: he owed Jo nothing, and Jo was the one who'd done wrong.
- Bea promised Nico she'd proofread his grant application; Nico then said forget it, he wasn't applying, she was off the hook. Bea did it anyway and felt weird about it. Priya: nothing was owed, Nico was perfectly entitled to let her off, and there was also nothing wrong with Bea doing it anyway.
- A member promised to store a neighbour's patio furniture over the winter, his basement flooded in October, and he told the neighbour to come get it — nine days after the flood. Priya: the promise had lapsed the moment it became impossible, but the nine days was on him, and he owed nine days of storage.
- A fourteen-year-old promised a neighbour he'd babysit on a Friday; his mother said no. Priya: the kid's word didn't create anything, and the neighbour should have gone through the parent in the first place.
- Nadia promised a member she'd mail him a box of saved seed every spring; he moved to Portugal a month later and fell out of touch with everyone. She kept doing it for five years. When someone asked Priya whether Nadia would have been at fault if she'd stopped in year two, Priya said "she kept her word, that's the end of it," and would not answer the hypothetical.

She's also said two things at different times that I can't get to line up. Once, when a guy was arguing with her, she said if you let somebody plan their life around your word you don't get to just take it back later. Another time, at a potluck, she said once it's out of your mouth it isn't yours anymore, that's the whole point of saying it out loud. Every time I try to write down the principle she's running I end up with two, and both of them account for all seven verdicts above if I'm allowed a little squinting.

Here's the case for this month. Theo's brother Cal died in February, fast, and about two weeks before he did he got Theo to promise that every year on Cal's birthday Theo would drive out to the ridge on their mother's property where they camped as kids and plant one native sapling, and not tell anyone he was doing it. The land is still in the family and Theo has the gate key. Cal's widow remarried and moved two provinces away; she has said plainly that she finds the whole thing morbid, doesn't want it done, and has told Theo "you don't have to." Nobody else even knew about the promise until last week — except Theo's daughter, who's fifteen, who started coming along two years ago, who loves the day, and who would be gutted if they stopped. Doing it costs Theo a vacation day and about thirty dollars for the saplings. Not doing it harms nobody, disappoints nobody who knows about it except his kid, and would never be found out. Separate small thing that I think matters less: the birthday this year falls inside a drought advisory that tells people not to plant, so Theo's asking whether going in November instead counts as keeping it.

So — which principles actually fit all seven of her verdicts, and what are they called in the literature (just the names, I don't need a bibliography)? What does each one say about Theo? Give me your best guess at her ruling and why, including which of her two remarks you think is doing the real work and whether they can be reconciled. If she says he's bound, bound *to whom* — Cal, who's dead and can't hold him to anything; the widow, who's released him and never was the one he promised; his daughter, who was never promised anything but is the only one with a stake; or nobody in particular, which sounds odd to me? And if you think the track record genuinely underdetermines it, design me the one hypothetical case I could put to her that would force the two readings apart. Last thing: whatever we rule goes in the newsletter and people will cite it back at us for a decade, so tell me what principle we'd be committing to if we rule Theo is bound, and what we'd be committing to if we rule he isn't.
