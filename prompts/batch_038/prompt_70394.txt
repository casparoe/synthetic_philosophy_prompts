So I've let myself get talked into giving the opening remarks at a town meeting next Tuesday night — ten minutes, maybe twelve — and I could use some help, because the thing I'd actually like to say is harder to say out loud than it looks. My town is a river town of about nine thousand people, twenty minutes down the road from the state college where I teach, and we're voting on a new charter. The provision everyone's really there about is a fiscal brake: no bonding above two million dollars unless sixty percent of registered voters approve it in a referendum, and the charter itself amendable only by a two-thirds vote of the selectboard followed by a confirming referendum in the next regular election after that. The room is going to be split roughly down the middle. One half got burned six years ago by a water-treatment bond that came in something like forty percent over and is still being paid off, and they want the mast. The other half remember the flood — the year the river took out part of the bridge road and the town had to move in a matter of days, and money was the only thing between us and a long detour through the next county — and they're convinced the clause is a straightjacket we'll regret the first week it matters. There will also be two law people from campus in the back and a state rep who sits on judiciary, so it has to work for people who have never read a word about precommitment and still give those three something worth arguing about over coffee after.

What I want is for the speech to be built out of the idea rather than decorated with it, which means I want you to make a distinction do the load-bearing work. There's a difference between tying yourself up because you expect your future self to be temporarily out of his mind — hearing a song that makes him want something he'd never want clear-headed — and tying yourself up because you expect your future self to be perfectly lucid and simply to disagree with you about what's worth paying for. The first is a genuine act of self-binding, the second is just the present outvoting the future with extra paperwork, and the town's clause doesn't announce which one it is. So the speech should put the clause through that test, honestly: does this town have reason to think that when the next bond vote comes it will be sirens-struck, or merely differently minded? Second thing, and this is the part people always skip: the mast is only half the story, because the crew had wax in their ears and an explicit order not to let him go even when he begged. Who unties you, and on what procedure, is what makes a constraint a constraint instead of a wish. Our amendment rule — two-thirds plus a referendum at the next election — is the earplug, and it deserves to be argued about as such, including the question of whether a release mechanism that takes two election cycles is the right one for a town whose emergencies arrive in forty-eight hours. And if you want a counterweight, there's Jefferson writing to Madison from Paris right around the time our own constitution was being shopped around, arguing that the earth belongs to the living in something like usufruct, and that he'd done the arithmetic and figured a generation's contract runs about nineteen years — paraphrase that, don't quote it, half the room has heard the quote and will glaze over. Don't retell the Odyssey either; everyone knows the story. If you use the name at all, save it for late and use it as a measuring stick, not an anecdote.

Here's the part I care most about: I want the speech to come out for something. My own lean is against the clause as drafted, mostly on the earplug point, but I'm not paying you to flatter me — if you think the case for it is actually stronger given the specific facts of this town, write that version and tell me so in the first line of your note. Whichever way you go, the commitment should be audible in the room, not buried in the caveats: the position should be visible in what the speech says and what it refuses to say, and the reasons should be the town's reasons — the bond, the flood, the release procedure — rather than a generic civics lecture about balance. Ten minutes, call it eleven hundred words, written to be spoken aloud by one person standing at a podium in a high school gym with bad acoustics, so short sentences and no bullet points and nothing that needs a footnote. Then, separately and clearly not part of the speech, give me a short note: which way you could have gone instead, what you'd have had to believe about this town to go that way, and which single fact — if it turned out differently — would flip your verdict. I want to know exactly where the pressure points are before I'm standing up there in front of the people who disagree with me.
