So: write the spec. Give me the list of requirements any adequate theory has to meet — the verdicts it must return on particular cases, the distinctions it mustn't collapse, the features of how we actually praise and blame that it has to preserve — numbered D1, D2, and so on, with the case or consideration behind each one, and say for each whether it's a hard constraint or just one camp's pet preference wearing a requirement's clothes.

Fix the purpose so the list has a target: I want a theory good enough to underwrite praising and blaming particular episodes, so moral worth has to come out as the sort of property you can locate in a single act and say "this one counts." Anything that only makes sense as a property of whole lives or characters needs to be flagged as failing that, or defended as redefining it.

One case I want the list to be built to handle, and I don't think it's in the usual canon: someone opts into a neural compliance module — no coercion, they wanted it — that makes it psychologically impossible for them to knowingly stiff a server, lie to a client, blow past a red light. They do the right action, and reflectively they endorse having the module; they're glad it's there. Three separate questions the theory has to face: does the act have moral worth; does the module itself have moral worth or disworth, given that installing it was an act; and does the answer shift if they got it specifically to spare themselves a temptation they knew they'd otherwise give in to. I want the requirement that forces a principled answer here stated explicitly, not smuggled into another one.

Also tell me which subsets of the requirements can't be jointly satisfied — if the list is impossible in places, that's the most interesting part of the deliverable. My hunch is that demanding the theory track the agent's own reasons and demanding it give the verdicts a well-placed observer would give pull apart, but I want to know whether that's the real tension or whether there's a worse one I'm missing.

Keep the scorecard short: one paragraph at the end placing the motive-of-duty view, a pluralist view that lets several right-making features confer worth, and the view that locates moral worth in dispositions or character rather than acts. The thinking should be in the criteria.
