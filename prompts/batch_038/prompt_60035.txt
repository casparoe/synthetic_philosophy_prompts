I'm a novelist, and I sit on the advisory board for a university's special collections. Mostly ceremonial, except twice a year we get an access petition that actually has to be decided. Here's the one landing Thursday.

A writer died twenty-two years ago. Her will deposited forty years of correspondence under a fifty-year seal. A biographer — not the estate's chosen one — wants one specific bundle: the letters between the dead novelist and her younger sister. Her claim is that the sister drafted a large chunk of the book everyone takes to be the novelist's masterwork. The sister is 91, alive, and has written to the board begging us to hold the seal; she says the claim is simply false and that she never wanted any of it known. The policy we have to apply says we may release material early "for compelling scholarly reasons consistent with the reasonable expectations of privacy of the persons named in the papers." Nobody's ever had to decide whether a living person who didn't write the letters gets to invoke that clause.

Two facts that complicate it: a magazine profile from the late '90s quotes one line out of the bundle secondhand, so its not airtight; and the biographer says she'll write the book regardless, with or without us.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before the meeting. I want a bottom line — seal, release, or release under some specific condition — plus the mechanics of how it would actually run and by when, so we can vote on it. And I want the reasoning to do the thing I can't do myself: work out whose privacy is even at stake here. Is the sister's objection about information getting disclosed, or about being turned into a character in somebody else's story without her say-so? Those feel like different injuries to me and I suspect they point at different verdicts. Take her "it isn't true, so why hide it" line seriously rather than batting it away — if that argument fails, say exactly why it fails and what it would license if it worked. Then give the losing side its strongest version.

Last thing: tell me what would change your mind. Which consideration, if it turned out true, flips your recommendation, and where do you think your own reasoning is shakiest. No citations or reading lists please, I cant check them and I'd rather have your judgment.
