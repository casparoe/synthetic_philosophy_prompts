I am a fourth-year doctoral student in philosophy of science, and this term I am serving as the teaching assistant for a graduate proseminar on the metaphysics of scientific classification. The instructor has delegated the take-home assessment to me. The cohort is mixed — mostly philosophy, a few from history and sociology of science, two from the biology department — and all of them will have covered the standard ground on natural kinds: microstructural and essentialist accounts, cluster-plus-mechanism accounts of kindhood, the deflationary and pragmatic reactions to both, and the standing dispute over whether the predictive and explanatory success of a scientific taxonomy licenses any metaphysical conclusion. I do not want an item that can be answered by rehearsing that dispute in the abstract. I want one that forces the students to apply the concepts to a case where the usual diagnostics pull against each other, and to commit to a verdict they then have to defend.

I have drafted the case below. Please write the complete assessment item around it: the question as it will appear on the paper, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

**The case (to be embedded verbatim in the question, or lightly tightened by you):**

Researchers working in seasonally waterlogged, arsenic-rich paddy soils have isolated a set of bacterial organisms that share a tight cluster of properties: a distinctive membrane lipid profile, anaerobic arsenate reduction, a shared repertoire of stress-response proteins, and a characteristic colony morphology. For convenience, call this the ARP cluster. (Everything stipulated here is for expository purposes; ARP is not a real taxon, and no claim is being made about any actual organism.)

The clustering is not accidental. Two mechanisms maintain it. First, all members occupy the same microhabitat, and that environment selects for the cluster. Second, and more consequentially, the organisms exchange genetic material promiscuously with one another by horizontal transfer, and the transferred material is precisely what carries the clustered properties: the properties co-occur because they travel together.

Now the complication. The same transfer mechanism does not respect the cluster's boundaries. The mobile elements carrying arsenate reduction move readily into organisms outside ARP — organisms with different lipid profiles and different morphology that happen to share the habitat — and those recipients acquire the property without acquiring the rest of the cluster. Conversely, some organisms that match ARP in every morphological and lipid respect have lost the arsenate determinants entirely. Phylogenetic reconstruction from a conserved marker gene yields a tree that cross-cuts the phenotypic cluster; ARP members are not each other's closest relatives. The mechanism that holds the cluster together is therefore also the mechanism that leaks it, and there is no threshold of transfer frequency at which the leakage stops.

One member of the research group proposes, in response, that ARP membership should be fixed not by any set of shared properties but by a *transfer profile*: an organism belongs to the kind insofar as it stands in relations of sufficiently frequent genetic exchange with the others, where "sufficiently frequent" is calibrated by how well the resulting partition predicts which organisms will acquire arsenate resistance under a specified selection regime. On this proposal, the boundaries of the kind are explicitly vague and explicitly interest-relative, and the kind is said to be no less natural for that.

**What I need from you:**

1. The question proper, with the case embedded, a word limit of 2,500 words, and a stated completion window of 72 unproctored hours. The question should require the student to decide whether ARP is a natural kind and to defend that verdict against at least two of the accounts of kindhood they have studied. It should also require them to say what, if anything, the transfer-profile proposal adds: specifically, whether a kind whose membership conditions are fixed by predictive success relative to a selection regime is a joint in nature or a well-engineered instrument, and whether the difference matters for the realism the research group is claiming. Please state in the question itself that it is self-contained and that no particular secondary literature is required.

2. A model answer of 900–1,100 words that argues for a definite verdict rather than surveying positions, and that squarely addresses what I think of as the leaky-mechanism problem: how an account that grounds kindhood in a homeostatic mechanism should respond when the mechanism in question is exactly what makes the cluster's extension indeterminate. I want the model answer to refuse the two easiest exits — declaring the case a mere pragmatic taxonomic convenience, and declaring the question ill-posed — and to explain why each exit fails rather than simply declining it. It should also say something definite about the cross-cutting phylogeny: why it does not straightforwardly vindicate a common-ancestry account, given that the same horizontal transfer that disrupts the phenotypic cluster also disrupts the marker-gene tree.

3. A marking scheme out of 25, organized as five must-hit points with the marks each is worth, and for each point a one-line note naming the characteristic way a competent but uncomprehending answer botches it — for instance, treating the cross-cutting phylogeny as though it simply refuted cluster accounts, when it is equally damaging to any account that grounds kindhood in shared descent.

4. A short list of three or four attractive-but-wrong theses; an answer that asserts any of them should be capped at a stated mark however well it is otherwise written. One of these must be the claim that vagueness or interest-relativity of extension is incompatible with a kind's being natural, since I expect a third of the cohort to reach for it.

Finally, please write the marking scheme so that a student who reaches the opposite verdict from the model answer can still earn full marks: the points should be phrased in terms of moves the argument must make, not conclusions it must reach, and that openness should be built into the wording rather than left to the marker's discretion at the second-reading stage.
