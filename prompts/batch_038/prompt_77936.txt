So I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine and I've been going back and fourth on a sidebar for a piece I'm doing about predictability as a weapon — why it can pay to be the sort of agent other people can count on to do the apparently dumb thing. My editor wants a short, sharp comparison of the two best-known versions of this idea, and honestly I've read both of them and I still cant tell whether they're saying the same thing in different accents or actually disagreeing. For this one I want you to play the part of a philosopher who works on decision theory but who also has a genuine feel for Cold War strategic studies. Treat me as an educated lay reader, but please don't sand off the edges of the argument to make it friendly — the whole point of the sidebar is that the disagreement is technical.

Version one is Parfit's hitchhiker, from Reasons and Persons (1984). Your car dies out in the desert, a driver comes along and will take you into town, but he's been burned by ungrateful passengers before and only picks up people who'll mail him a reward afterward — and he happens to be an extremely good reader of sincerity, able to tell from your face and your answers whether you actually intend to pay. Once he's dropped you in town he's gone, there's no enforcement, nobody will ever find out, and sending the money is a pure loss, so paying is not the act that maximizes your expected utility. And yet it was rational, back before you climbed into the truck, to be someone who pays. Parfit's move is that the level of "what kind of person should I be?" and the level of "what should I do right now?" can come apart, and the first level can win. Version two is Schelling's material on commitment in The Strategy of Conflict (1960): bargaining power often comes from voluntarily wrecking your own options — burning the bridge, surrendering the wheel, handing the decision over to chance, making a threat you'd hate to have to carry out — so that the other side believes you. His twist is that a convincing display of unreasonableness can be the rational move, and the party who cannot help himself is the one who gets the deal.

Here's the case I'd like you to run both theories through; I made it up myself for the piece, so it's a clean room and you shouldn't go looking for it anywhere. Call it the tollhouse. You operate the only winter road into a mountain village, and every October you post a single rate that you promise to hold for the whole season. Two separate audiences are watching you. First, the village council sends its roadmaster to sit with you for an afternoon; he's reputed to be uncannily good at telling whether you intend to keep the posted rate, and if he reports that you don't, the council runs a convoy over the pass instead and you earn nothing. Second, a resort developer one valley over is thinking about building a rival road, but he'll only put up the money if he's convinced you'd start a price war the instant his road opens — and he doesn't care what you say you'd do, he's a finance person, he only believes moves that can't be taken back: a lease that obliges you to keep traffic running, a public mortgage that defaults if revenue falls, snowplows sold for scrap. So the council wants a disposition it can read, and the developer wants an option you have destroyed. My suspicion is that these pull against each other: someone who has truly handcuffed himself looks evasive and fanatical to the roadmaster, while someone with a settled, legible character looks far too negotiable to scare the developer off.

A few things I specifically want out of you:

- Which framework, if either, gives better advice in the tollhouse, and is there a single policy that satisfies both audiences at once? Show your reasoning, don't just pronounce.
- A friend of mine who's midway through a PhD insisted the difference is that Schelling changes your action set while Parfit changes your utility function — one is about what you can do, the other about what you want, and everything else follows from that. Does that gloss survive the tollhouse, or is it too tidy?
- Give me a real comparison along the axes where the two recipes come apart: how accurate the other side's detection is, whether the mechanism itself is observable or only the resulting behavior is, one-shot versus repeated interaction, and whether the cultivated irrationality is meant to be shed once the deal closes or kept on as character.
- The bit I most want nailed down: do Parfit and Schelling actually share that two-level structure, where it's rational to be someone who does X even though doing X isn't rational? Or does Schelling get all his results without ever conceding that point, so that the resemblance is superficial?
- Bottom line, and pick a side: is Parfit's rational irrationality a special case of Schellingian commitment, a rival to it, or an orthogonal discovery that happens to rhyme? Then tell me where the comparison starts to strain — Parfit's payer comes out both better off and better, whereas Schelling's committed bargainer comes out better off and considerably harder to live with. Does that asymmetry do any work, or is it decoration?
- One aside if there's room: in political economy people use "rational irrationality" for something else entirely, where it's cheap for individual voters to hold sloppy beliefs. A sentence or two on whether that third sense is a cousin of the two above or a straight homonym would be a nice closer.

Think carefully before you write. Start with a short brainstorm — a handful of bullets sketching the candidate answers to the bottom-line question — and then commit to one and argue for it in the main comparison. Please skip any bibliography, footnotes, or citations; I have no way to check them and I'd much rather you not guess. American English, and roughly 1,000 to 1,400 words for the main comparison.
