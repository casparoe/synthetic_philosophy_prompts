I have been asked to circulate a position paper to a workshop on penal policy, and before I do I would like a consistency audit of the package rather than an overview of the field. Here is the package, in roughly the order I would defend it.

1. The only thing that can justify maintaining a penal system at all is its contribution to reducing future harm: preventing offences by incapacitating those who would commit them, deterring would-be offenders, and changing the people who pass through it. I do not believe anyone deserves to suffer. I take desert to be a label for a family of reactive attitudes that a decent society should be trying to educate away, and I would rather not appeal to it even in a merely limiting role.

2. The punishment is the loss of liberty and nothing further. Anything imposed on a prisoner beyond the restriction of movement needs its own separate justification, and I think most of what is actually imposed in American prisons does not have one. I therefore endorse the normalcy principle on the Norwegian model, of which Halden is the best-known instance: life inside should resemble life outside as closely as security permits, and health care, schooling, and library services inside should be supplied by the same public agencies that supply them outside rather than by the prison itself.

3. No person may be subjected to an intervention designed to alter their values, desires, or neurochemistry without free and informed consent. This holds for detainees, holds when we are confident the intervention works, and holds when we believe the person would consent if he were thinking clearly.

4. Release should track risk rather than elapsed time. Someone who no longer presents a serious danger should be out, and someone who does should not be out merely because the calendar has run down. I accept that this makes the length of confinement indeterminate in principle, and I accept renewable extensions on grounds of continuing dangerousness.

5. Life without parole and whole-life orders are unjustifiable and should be abolished everywhere; there must always be a realistic prospect of release.

6. Capital punishment is impermissible without exception, including for a person who is permanently dangerous and cannot be safely detained.

7. It is impermissible to punish someone known to be innocent, whatever the social benefit: no scapegoating, no exemplary punishments, no holding relatives as leverage.

8. Victims and affected communities should have standing in the determination of sentences — not because they are owed a matching quantum of suffering, but because a system that persistently offends the community's sense of what is owed loses the reporting, the testimony, and the general compliance on which its harm reduction depends.

9. Method: where the package collides with a strong intuition I am willing to give up the intuition, but only if the principle doing the work is earning its keep somewhere else. I do not want to end up as someone who simply bites bullets.

Two cases I use in the paper to test the view.

Case A. A man convicted at twenty-one of a serious violent offence serves a determinate ten-year term. At thirty-one, the instruments the board relies on place him at moderate-to-high risk of serious reoffending. The only programme shown to reduce risk in his category combines group work with a pharmacological component; he refuses it as a violation of his bodily and mental integrity; the town he would return to has organised against his release. On my commitments, may he be released, and if not, how long may he be held — and is there any date at which the answer changes?

Case B. A therapeutic-community wing run on a deliberately non-normal regime — no private space, mandatory daily confrontation sessions, staff acting in clinical rather than custodial roles — is shown by a well-designed comparative study to cut reoffending substantially more than a normalcy-model wing of the same security classification, at the same cost per prisoner. What does my view tell the administrator to build?

What I want back is an audit, not a lecture on penal theory. Specifically: identify the points of mutual pressure and order them by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list, saying in one line what makes each one severe; for each, state the smallest change that removes it and what that change costs elsewhere in the package, including whether it forces me to retract something I have said is merely an attitude to be educated away; separate out the pairs that only look inconsistent and give the reading on which they are compatible; work through Cases A and B from the commitments as stated and say whether the view yields a determinate answer, and if it does not, name the commitment that runs out; and point out any commitment that is silently doing work of a kind I have officially disowned. If two of the tensions share a single root, say so and treat them as one item instead of padding the list.

I do not know in advance which of these I would give up, so please do not assume that the consequentialism is the expendable element, and do not assume that any of the absolutes is either. Close with the single revision that would repair the most, describe what the package looks like after that revision, and be explicit about what I would no longer be entitled to say. If you think one of my commitments is simply false rather than inconsistent with the others, flag it separately; I would rather not have that mixed into the audit. No bibliography, reading list, or citations — I am asking about my own commitments, not about the literature.
