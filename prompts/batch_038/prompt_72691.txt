Ok so I got steamrolled at a house thing last weekend by a guy who kept saying 'separation of church and state' like it was a verdict rather than an argument, and I want to come back at him properly.

Context for what the slogan was being made to do: I'd said something mildly in favour of state funding for faith schools (I'm English, half our schools are church schools, this is normal here) and he shut me down with the slogan, plus something about Jefferson's letter to the Baptists in Danbury and it's wall, plus the flourish that the phrase isn't actually in the Constitution but the principle obviously is. Then my German friend pointed out that basically none of what he was describing is what other countries call separation: Germany has the state tax authorities collect a church tax from registered members on behalf of the recognised churches and runs religious instruction in state schools; France bars pupils in state schools from wearing conspicuous religious symbols but still pays clergy in Alsace-Moselle under a surviving concordat; England has bishops sitting in the upper house and a monarch who is head of the church; and the US gives churches tax exemptions and has been funding religious schools indirectly through voucher schemes. All four get routinely described as secular states with church and state separated. My head spun, I mumbled something, and I lost the argument anyway.

So: can you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims 'separation of church and state' could be making? I want the axes seperated rather than mushed together — separation of what from what (institutional? financial? jurisdictional? symbolic? personnel?), whose duty it is (the state only, or the churches too), what the wrong consists in (coercion, endorsement, funding, entanglement, influence over minds), whether it's an absolute bar on contact or a matter of degree and proportionality, and whether it's being used as a legal rule, a constitutional principle, a moral ideal, or a description of a social trend, because people slide between those four without noticing.

For each reading, tell me: is it trivially true of every liberal democracy (and therefore useless in an argument), substantive but defensible, or just false? And which of the four cases above would it condemn and which would it leave untouched — I specifically want to know whether there's any coherent reading under which England's bishops and Germany's church tax are violations while France's symbol ban is not, or vice versa, since the guys on both sides of that table assumed there was.

Then finish with the bit I actually care about: which reading did my opponent need in order to get from 'separation' to 'no state money for faith schools', and does that reading survive the fact that countries which fund faith schools heavily also tend to have perfectly strong legal protections against religious coercion? I want to know if there's a version of his point that's true, a version that gets him his conclusion, and whether those turn out to be the same version or not.
