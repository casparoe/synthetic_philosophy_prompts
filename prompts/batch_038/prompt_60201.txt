I run a small monthly reading group that has been working through medieval political thought on Discord for about two years — four of us, none of them professional medievalists, one a canon-law-trained Catholic who reliably takes the other side of anything I argue. We have been circling a question I would like you to settle properly: does Aquinas actually permit tyrannicide, and if so, under which description? The material is genuinely tangled rather than merely obscure. In *On Kingship* (the chapter numbers shift between editions, which is part of why people talk past each other) he appears to grant that a ruler who seized power without title may be removed, even lethally, by those subject to him, while warning that private initiative is dangerous because the attempt may fail and invite worse cruelty, and because a bad successor may be worse than the tyrant; he also says that where the right to appoint a ruler belongs to a community, that community may depose or restrain him, and that this is not rebellion — the tyrant is the one disturbing the peace — whereas where the right belongs to a superior, the remedy must be sought from that superior, and where no human remedy is available, from God, tyrants being permitted to chastise the sins of subjects. His early *Sentences* commentary seems to run the same permission in a slightly more permissive key. Meanwhile the *Summa*'s question on sedition says that disturbing a tyrannical government is not sedition, with the explicit caveat that it becomes culpable when the disturbance costs the subjects more than the tyranny does, and the *Summa*'s questions on killing confine lethal force to public authority, ground the killing of dangerous offenders in a part-to-whole argument about the common good, and allow a private person only proportionate self-defensive repulsion of an ongoing attack. Add John of Salisbury's much blunter endorsement of slaying tyrants as background, and you have the ingredients of a real dispute: is Aquinas's restriction the usurper/oppressor distinction (tyranny from defective title versus tyranny from oppressive exercise, as later scholastics put it), or is it the harm-comparison clause, or is it the authority requirement? My suspicion is that these three are usually run together when they are not equivalent, and that the framework's actual center of gravity is the third, with the first doing decorative work.

To force the issue apart, work through this case, which I constructed for our last session and which my canon-law friend and I could not resolve. A ruler comes to power by unimpeachable hereditary title — no defect in the source — and governs oppressively for a decade. Over that decade he has not merely ruled badly; he has dismantled the organs through which the community could act: the ordinary courts are suspended, the assembly that historically confirmed his predecessors is dissolved, the bishops who might have censured him are exiled or bought, and the army is composed of mercenaries owing allegiance to him personally rather than to the crown. There is, in short, no constituted public authority left anywhere in the polity that could depose or judge him. Now suppose a minor household functionary — commissioned by no one, but privately trusted by a plurality of the towns and by several surviving members of the disbanded assembly — has exactly one reliable opportunity to poison the ruler. Grant the following counterfactuals, so that the case turns on principle rather than on empirical guesswork: the attempt succeeds; it triggers roughly two years of civil war costing on the order of twenty thousand lives; the war ends in a broadly just restoration under the legitimate heir, who is independently known to be moderate and to intend to reconstitute the courts and the assembly. Does Aquinas's framework permit this killing, forbid it, or require it? Which of his commitments controls, and what happens to the others? Specifically, I want the essay to adjudicate three sub-questions rather than gesture at them: whether the community's residual right to provide for itself survives the destruction of its institutions, and if so whether a private agent can act on a presumed or counterfactual mandate (a tacit commission, as it were) without anyone having actually deliberated or appointed him; whether the harm-comparison clause, if it is genuinely load-bearing, makes Aquinas's position a proto-consequentialist calculus with an agent-relative authority constraint bolted onto it, and whether that hybrid is stable or merely unresolved; and whether the theological counsel of patience — tyrants as instruments of chastisement, repentance as the remedy — is doing independent quietist work such that removing it collapses the rest of the structure, or whether it can be bracketed as an add-on without disturbing the political argument.

The deliverable is an essay of roughly eighteen hundred to two thousand five hundred words that takes a side and defends it. Open with the thesis stated in one or two sentences, including your answer to the constructed case, so I can tell immediately whether you have committed. Then reconstruct the strongest version of the reading you reject — I want the restrictive and permissive interpretations each at their most compelling, not strawmanned, and I want you to be explicit about which of the three candidate restrictions does the most explanatory work across the whole corpus of texts, not just in one of them. Address directly the objection that Aquinas has a competence vacuum, i.e. that his permission lapses precisely when the need for it is greatest, and say whether that makes his view coherently quietist, contingently conservative, or internally inconsistent; if you think it is the first, explain why a Thomist should accept a framework engineered to fail in exactly these circumstances. Do not pad with a bibliography, footnotes, or a list of secondary commentators — I have no library access and neither do you, and invented citations would be worse than none. Paraphrase Aquinas rather than quoting him; I care about the structure of the argument, not the philology. Finally, close with the two or three considerations that would most change your verdict, phrased as things that could actually be discovered or argued rather than as vague appeals to further nuance: which textual point, if it went the other way, would flip your answer to the case, and which of the three restrictions you would demote first if forced to rank them by explanatory work.
