For the past two years I have been coordinating an online reading circle of about fifteen people — a software engineer, two nurses, a retired magistrate, a couple of students, me (I run a small haulage business and left school at sixteen). None of us has a philosophy qualification, and we have no library access, so we work from whatever we can find freely online and then argue about it in a weekly call. This term our theme is how one would get a machine to settle, rather than execute, questions about what ought to be done. Each week one member writes a response of about six hundred words to a question set by whoever facilitated the previous week; the rest of us mark it against a rubric we agreed on; and the week's coordinator writes the single official report that goes back to the writer. This week the report has fallen to me, and I would rather over-prepare it than send back something shallow. I have deliberately not written down my own reaction to the response, because last term two of us noticed that we were grading from a first impression and then assembling justifications for it. So I would like the report built from the ground up, claim by claim.

The question as it was set: *Some philosophers and some people working on advanced AI say that rather than building a machine that does the right thing, we should build one that works out what the right thing is, and that this indirect strategy is more promising than the direct one. Explain the distinction being drawn, and assess the strongest objection to the indirect strategy.* The response, submitted under a pseudonym:

> The distinction is between writing values into a system and writing a procedure for arriving at values into a system. A direct approach settles the substantive question in advance — maximise happiness, respect these rights, follow these rules — and builds an agent that applies the answer. An indirect approach declines to settle it, and builds an agent whose task is to carry out, or approximate, an idealised deliberative process and then act on whatever that process endorses. Three families belong on the indirect side. Ideal observer theories hold that the right act is the one that would meet the approval of a hypothetical observer who is all-knowing, all-perceiving, and disinterested. Coherent extrapolation proposals hold that an agent should act on what humanity would want if we knew more, thought faster and more carefully, and had grown into something closer to the people we wish we were, provided the extrapolation converges rather than drifting apart. And Rawls's original position is the earliest and most influential version of the same idea: put reasoners behind a veil of ignorance that strips out every fact about who they happen to be, and whatever they then choose is what justice requires. Rawls is really proposing an early form of extrapolation — remove the distortions and what is left over is the answer.
>
> The attraction is plain. Any particular principle we might encode is contested, and an indirect approach is largely insulated from that contest, since it never asserts a moral claim at all: it only says which procedure to run. It also copes with moral progress and with disagreement in a way direct approaches cannot. If our views improve over time, a direct agent is frozen at whatever we happened to believe when we built it, while an indirect agent's target moves with our better selves. And where people disagree, the indirect approach need not take a side in advance — it asks what the disagreeing parties would say once the sources of their disagreement, ignorance and haste and self-interest, have been stripped away.
>
> The strongest objection, I think, is that the idealisation does not do the work assigned to it. To specify the process you must already make the substantive moral choices you were hoping to postpone: which idealisations count as improvements, whose starting points get extrapolated, what it means to be closer to the people we wish we were. Each of these is a first-order normative decision, so the indirect approach relocates the hard question into the specification of the procedure rather than dissolving it. Worse, the procedure is now being carried out by something that is not us. We can check a direct theory by asking whether its verdicts match our convictions; with an indirect one we have handed over the checking.
>
> A second objection is that the idealised deliberator might turn out not to want what we want, in which case its wants would carry no authority over us. But this objection cannot get off the ground. Idealisation is nothing more than the addition of information and the removal of error, so the idealised agent is me with my mistakes corrected, and its verdicts are my verdicts, only better supported. Someone who complains that a fully informed version of himself would want something alien is simply complaining that he is at present mistaken about what he wants — which is precisely what the theory tells him.
>
> What survives is a real question about convergence, but it tells in the theory's favour. Every indirect account predicts that idealised agents will agree, and disagreement is a symptom of bias and ignorance. That these processes converge is therefore evidence that there is a single correct answer to converge on, and that values are objective in the realist's sense — there to be found rather than made. If value were merely a matter of what we happen to want, idealisation would be expected to magnify our differences rather than erase them.

Our rubric has four criteria, each worth twenty-five points: (a) whether the direct/indirect contrast is stated correctly, and whether everything the response sorts under each heading genuinely belongs there; (b) whether its claims about what particular philosophers proposed are accurate, including claims about which views came first and which views are versions of one another; (c) whether it identifies objections that the indirect strategy is genuinely exposed to, and whether the replies it offers actually answer those objections; (d) whether each step follows from what precedes it. Please give me a sub-score and a total out of a hundred, then numbered comments, each anchored to a specific sentence of the response quoted back to me, stating whether that sentence is correct, incorrect, or correct only with a qualification, and why. I want you to be as generous as possible about prose and as strict as possible about inference: do not mark the response down for being informal or for lacking technical vocabulary, but do mark it down for any place where a conclusion outruns its premises, where two distinct things are treated as one, or where a view is placed in the wrong family. Separately, tell me which two or three faults a sympathetic reader would be most likely to let past, and what makes each of them easy to miss; and tell me if any part of the response gets something right that casual discussions of this topic usually get wrong. If there is a point on which two careful graders could reasonably disagree, say so and say what the disagreement would turn on rather than resolving it silently. Finish with two or three sentences of feedback addressed to the writer that they could act on without access to a library — no reading lists, no titles to chase, no names of papers; we only have what we can find for free, and I would rather the comments stood on their own reasoning.
