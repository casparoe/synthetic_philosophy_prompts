I am trying to work out what my own commitments about common knowledge actually amount to, by looking at how I judge a set of cases. Below are the verdicts I have collected — some from puzzles I believe I understand, some from ordinary life. I should like to know whether a single principle, or a small set of explicitly stated clauses, generates all of them; what that principle implies for cases I have not yet considered; and which of my verdicts, if any, cannot be preserved.

**Case 1: three muddy children.** Three children come in from the garden. All three are muddy, each can see the other two but not himself, and none has any other way of determining his own condition. The father announces to all of them at once that at least one of them is muddy, then asks each in turn whether he knows his own condition. First round: no one does. Second round: no one does. Third round: all three do. My verdict: I accept the reasoning without reservation. What troubles me is the announcement itself. Before it was made, each child already knew that at least one child was muddy, and each already knew that each of the others knew it. The announcement supplied no new fact about the world; nor, so far as I can see, did it supply any particular additional rung on the ladder of knowing that one knows that one knows. And yet the difference between the situation before the announcement and the situation after it is precisely the difference between the deduction being unavailable and being available. I note that in the two-child version of the puzzle the same announcement does supply a rung the children genuinely lacked, while in the three-child version it appears not to. So my verdict is that whatever the announcement accomplishes is not to be located at any finite level of iteration.

**Case 2: two armies.** Two armies, each on its own weaker than the enemy, must attack simultaneously in order to win. They can communicate only by messengers who may be captured. My verdict: the pessimistic conclusion is correct, and I regard it as established rather than as a paradox. No finite exchange of messages, however many confirmations and confirmations of confirmations it contains, can put the two commanders in a position to know that the other knows that he knows that the attack is on; therefore they can never rationally attack.

**Case 3: the corridor.** Two colleagues pass one another in a noisy corridor. One says, "three o'clock, my office," and the other nods. Neither can confirm that the other heard correctly, and neither will see the other before three. Both turn up at three. My verdict: this coordination is entirely unremarkable, and each was rational to expect the other. Nothing further was needed. I want to say that in this case they had whatever Case 2 says is unattainable — and I notice that this is in tension with Case 2, since the structure of the communication is the same.

**Case 4: the email game.** Two players face a coordination problem in which the best outcome depends on which of two states obtains. Only the first player is told the state directly. Her machine then informs the second player's machine, which sends an acknowledgement, which is acknowledged in turn, and so on down the chain; each transmission independently has a small chance of failure, and a failure ends the exchange. The result I have in mind is that, however small the chance of failure and however long the chain of successfully exchanged acknowledgements that a player has observed, the players' behaviour does not converge to the behaviour they would exhibit if the state were common knowledge; the unique equilibrium is the one they would have reached had no message been sent at all. My verdict: I accept the mathematics and I refuse the conclusion. I cannot bring myself to believe that real agents who have observed a thousand successful acknowledgements behave exactly as though nothing had been said, and yet I do not think such agents would be irrational if they did behave that way — so I cannot dismiss the result as a modelling artefact either. I hold both attitudes simultaneously and should like to be told which to abandon.

**Case 5: agreeing to disagree.** Two colleagues, both careful Bayesians who began from the same training and the same prior, have read one another's published analyses, corresponded at length, and know each other's current opinions in considerable detail. Each knows the other's posterior probability for a contested policy claim, each knows that the other knows it, and so on down the chain. They continue to disagree, and neither thinks the other has made an error. My verdict: nothing is amiss. The disagreement is rational and does not violate the agreement theorem, because the theorem's requirement of common knowledge of the posteriors is not genuinely satisfied by reading papers and exchanging letters. I hold this verdict firmly, though I recognise that it sits uneasily beside Case 3, where I was perfectly willing to let an informal exchange in a corridor count as the full thing.

**Case 6: the backfire, and the siren.** Two people standing side by side hear a car backfire loudly. Each heard it, each can see that the other heard it, and each can see that the other can see this. My verdict: common knowledge of the noise is genuinely present here, though nothing was announced, and it is present because the event was mutually manifest rather than because anyone spoke. I take this as a datum: shared perception can generate common knowledge on its own. But now a siren sounds across a village in the middle of the night. Every villager in fact hears it; each also suspects that some neighbours may have slept through it, and has no means of checking. My verdict: here it is not common knowledge that the siren sounded, even though every villager knows that it did and knows that every other villager knows that it did. The two cases feel different to me, and I cannot say in virtue of what.

My own tentative formulation, which I expect will not survive, is that common knowledge obtains exactly when a fact is produced by a process whose operation is itself mutually manifest to the group. I cannot tell whether this does any work or merely restates the datum in Case 6.

What I should like from you is the principle, or a short list of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without further interpretation. In particular:

1. For each clause of the principle, identify which of the cases above motivates it, and which of my verdicts would be lost if that clause were deleted.
2. Say what the principle yields for these cases, on which I have no settled view. An all-staff email announcing a change of policy, sent with no delivery or read receipts — is the change common knowledge among the staff, and does it matter if the same text is also posted on a door everyone passes? A group message that reaches every member of a team except one, where the one who misses it does not know that he is the exception. A traffic signal that is green for me, where I can see that you can see it and you can see that I can see it — do I need common knowledge of its colour in order to proceed, or would something weaker suffice, and if weaker, how much weaker? Two traders who see the same headline at the same instant but have no way of knowing whether the other has seen it.
3. Tell me honestly which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate. If some principle does save every one of them, set out what it costs in complexity or in independent plausibility, and say whether you judge that price worth paying compared with the simpler principle that overturns one or two of my judgements. Where you think a verdict of mine must go, say which and why, and say what should replace it.

I am not looking for a survey of the literature, a list of names, or references; I want the principle itself, in a form I could use.
