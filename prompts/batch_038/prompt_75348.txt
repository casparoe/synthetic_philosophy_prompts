I am a second-year graduate student writing a seminar paper that is supposed to adjudicate between Lewis's counterfactual analysis of causation and Woodward's interventionist one by asking which handles a body of cases better. Before I commit to a side, I want to know what my own verdicts already commit me to, because I suspect they do not cohere. Here they are.

1. Two gunmen fire simultaneously; either wound alone would have been fatal, and both strike. I do not think either shot caused the death — or rather, I cannot find any sense in which each caused half of it.
2. One gunman fires and kills; a second, poised behind him, would have fired a moment later had the first missed. Here I am confident that the first shot caused the death, even though the victim would have died regardless.
3. A nurse omits to add a stabilizer to an infusion, and the patient suffers a reaction. The omission caused the reaction.
4. A custodian at another hospital, unconnected to this patient and ignorant of the infusion, likewise omits to add a stabilizer to it. His omission caused nothing. Cases 3 and 4 strike me as the same counterfactual fact about two different absences, and I cannot say what I am leaning on to separate them beyond the nurse's job description, which does not sound like a causal consideration.
5. A relay's contacts close, completing a circuit whose surge destroys a motor. I am happy to say the closing caused the destruction, though what the relay did was release a catch rather than supply any energy.
6. A legislature repeals an emissions standard, emissions continue, and a hurricane decades later does the damage it does. Same shape as case 5, yet I want to say the repeal merely failed to prevent the damage rather than caused it.
7. The moon's mass causes the tides. This is a paradigm, but I cannot describe an intervention on the moon's mass, even a notional one, that leaves anything else recognizable intact. If interventionism must be read as saying what would happen were there such an intervention, I accept the reading — but then I am unsure the account tells me more about the actual world than the counterfactual dependence I already had.
8. A slightly denser region of the early universe caused the galaxy cluster now found there. I want this to be a genuine causal claim and not mere correlation, yet here even the counterfactual seems to require altering the laws or the initial state wholesale, and I cannot say what is to be held fixed.
9. A reactor's catalyst loading caused an impurity spike. But the only way the operators can vary the loading is by varying the feed rate, which affects impurity on its own. I want to say this is a fact about the world that our data cannot settle, not a fact that is unsettled: the claim has a truth value that the interventions available to us cannot reach.
10. My grandmother's smoking caused her emphysema; the individual cigarette she smoked on a given Tuesday did not cause anything in particular. I accept singular causal claims at one grain and refuse them at another, and I cannot say what fixes the correct grain.

Two requests. First: is there a single principle — a condition on when one thing caused another, with an identifiable clause doing the work in each case above — that comes out right on all ten, or on all but some specified few? State it as a test I could apply to a case I have never considered, not as a summary of my intuitions; say which case motivates each clause; and tell me honestly which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate and would have to overturn. If no single principle will do, give me the smallest set of candidates and say what each one buys and what it costs. Second: my own tentative rule has been that X caused Y just in case Y would not have happened the way it did had X not happened the way it did, where the "way it did" clauses oblige me to hold fixed everything that is not itself a consequence of X. Test that rule against the ten cases, identify exactly where it breaks, and say whether any repair leaves it recognizably a counterfactual analysis rather than a restatement of the interventionist one.

Finally, tell me what the position you end up recommending says about two cases I have not listed: whether a referee's missed call caused my team's defeat, and whether a gene causes a trait that appears only in an environment no member of the species has ever encountered.
