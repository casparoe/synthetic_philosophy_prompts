For two years I have sat on a university panel that hears misconduct complaints, and the work has made a distinction I once treated as purely academic uncomfortably practical: whether to deal with the person across the table as someone to be held answerable, or as a risk to be managed. Lately I keep returning to Norway's handling of the attacks of 22 July 2011 — the car bomb in the government quarter and the shootings at the youth camp on Utøya, 77 people killed — as a real episode in which a whole society had to make that choice in public, and I would like you to work through it with me as a test of Strawson's account of the reactive attitudes.

I will supply the facts I take to be relevant, so that nothing turns on your recall of the details. Two court-appointed psychiatric reports were commissioned one after the other and reached opposite conclusions: the first, issued before the trial, diagnosed psychosis and supported a finding of criminal insanity; after sustained criticism a second team examined him and found him legally accountable; in August 2012 the Oslo District Court accepted the second, found him sane and guilty, and imposed preventive detention — twenty-one years, extendable for as long as he is judged dangerous. He pleaded not guilty on grounds of necessity and insisted throughout that he was sane and had acted deliberately. One lay judge was removed early in the proceedings when it emerged that he had publicly advocated the death penalty in the days after the attacks. And much of the official rhetoric in the first weeks had promised that the answer would be more openness and more humanity, rather than a return of the harsher register.

I want the analysis to run in both directions. First, map the episode onto Strawson as precisely as you can. Where exactly was the objective attitude adopted, and by whom — the psychiatrists, the court, the state in its public voice, or the panel that removed the lay judge for the visibility of his outrage? Were the two reports claims about the man's psychology or claims about whether resentment and indignation toward him would be fitting, and does the difference matter to how we should read the court's choice between them? And what was at stake for the bereaved and survivors in the prospect of an insanity finding: is it plausible that many experienced the likely withholding of the reactive attitudes as a further injury, so that being held responsible is a standing that can be denied rather than a description of a mental state the offender happens to occupy?

Then turn the case back on the theory. Does this sequence confirm Strawson's suggestion that the objective attitude cannot generally be sustained, because it costs too much in the way of ordinary human relations? Does it expose a gap in his treatment of the vicarious or impersonal attitudes — indignation on behalf of others — and more sharply in the question of which institution is entitled to allocate them? Or is it simply a situation his paper never contemplated: a legal system attempting to administer the objective attitude deliberately, as declared policy?

Finally, judge a claim I have been nursing. My thought is that the shift from the first report to the second was never really a change of psychiatric opinion but a change in the community's determination about whom it was prepared to resent, and that if so the eventual verdict tracked what Strawson says responsibility consists in. Does that survive, or does it collapse the distinction between being psychotic and being unfit for resentment — a distinction Strawson himself needs when he treats insanity among the recognized excuses?

Please be explicit about what is settled and what is genuinely open among people who work on this: how far Strawson's framework licenses the objective attitude in legal settings, whether his reply to the determinist succeeds, how much weight the argument that the objective attitude cannot be sustained can bear. Do not present a contested position as though it were the consensus. And write to me in continuous prose, as one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, no tables.
