I am an Everettian about quantum mechanics: unitary evolution only, no collapse, every outcome realized on some branch. I take the decision-theoretic derivation of the Born rule — the one that begins from rational preferences over quantum acts (prepare a state, measure an observable, award a payoff on each resulting branch) and concludes that a rational branching agent must weight branches by squared amplitude — to be the best available answer to the probability problem. A colleague has built the following argument against it. I am confident it fails, but I cannot say where, and I would like a diagnosis rather than encouragement.

The axiom he targets is branching indifference: an agent is rationally indifferent between two acts that differ only in that one of them splits some branch into several sub-branches all carrying exactly the same payoff.

(1) Branching indifference is indispensable to the derivation: it is what licenses the proof's subdivision of branches into equal-weight copies, and hence what carries the argument from the equal-amplitude case to arbitrary superpositions.

(2) As stated, branching indifference is a thesis about value rather than about physics. It says that when each of several successors receives the same payoff, and each of them is your future self in the same respect, their number adds nothing of value.

(3) That same description fits a case containing no quantum mechanics. A duplicator in a laboratory has two settings: one produces a single exact copy of you in an adjoining room who receives payoff X; the other produces a thousand such copies in a thousand rooms, each of whom receives X. Nothing in (2) refers to superposition, amplitude, decoherence, or measurement.

(4) So branching indifference, applied as (2) states it, requires indifference between the one-copy and the thousand-copy settings.

(5) That is not a matter of indifference. On the thousand-copy setting there are a thousand people who will go on living your life, remembering your childhood, and standing to you in every relation that makes a person you; and if each of them were given a lethal injection after receiving X, we would call that a catastrophe rather than a neutral rearrangement. A principle of rational value that cannot distinguish one continuer from a thousand has been drained of exactly what it was meant to govern.

(6) Therefore branching indifference is false.

(7) Therefore the derivation fails at its first substantive step; and since, once that step is gone, no other route from the Everett ontology to squared-amplitude weights remains, the interpretation cannot account for the probabilities by which quantum mechanics is confirmed. Branch number is the only weighting the branching structure itself supplies, and it yields one half for every two-outcome measurement whatever the amplitudes, which is contradicted by the observed statistics.

I would rather keep (1) than (5): (1) is a claim about a proof I can inspect, and (5) is the only step that reads like common sense. But I suspect (5) is doing more work than it looks like, and I want to know precisely what.

Which step do you reject? Explain why it seemed acceptable in context and what the argument needs it for; say what the Everettian pays for rejecting it, and in particular whether the rejection leaves the derivation intact or merely relocates the difficulty. Then tell me whether the conclusion could be recovered by repairing that step, and what the repair would cost. You need not cite anything or name the literature; label the principles however you like, so long as I can tell which of my numbered steps you are addressing.
