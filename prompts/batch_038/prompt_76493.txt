so I keep reading stuff about peer disagreement — conciliationism vs. the steadfast camp — and every piece of it just defends one side against a couple of toy cases (splitting a restaurant bill, a horse race, a hung jury). Before I commit to a side I want the spec. What does an account of "how should I respond when someone I take to be my epistemic peer disagrees with me" actually have to deliver to count as adequate? Give me the desiderata, not the arguments.

Purpose, since I think adequacy is relative to it: I'm writing a short internal guidance doc for a research team that uses models as reviewers and red-teamers, so the account has to cover disagreement with an LLM as well as disagreement with a human colleague. I suspect that second case breaks some of the standard machinery, which is why I want the requirements laid out before I pick a horse.

For each desideratum: the case or consideration behind it (an actual scenario, not a slogan), and a mark for whether it's non-negotiable or is really just one camp's preference smuggled in as a constraint. Things I'd expect on the list, plus whatever I've missed: what makes two parties peers at all and whether that's binary or comes in degrees; what the account owes to cases where you can't inspect the other side's evidence; how it handles a party whose apparent view shifts when you reword the question; whether it has to explain away or accommodate the intuition that you're entitled to trust your own reasoning from the inside; what it says about disagreements you can't resolve even in principle.

the part I care about most is three features of model interlocutors that I dont think the existing debate has any handle on, and I want requirements that speak to them explicitly:

1. Replicability. I can spin up thirty copies of the same reviewer and they agree with each other nearly every time. Is that thirty peers outvoting me, or one peer counted thirty times? An account that lets the number of instances do epistemic work has to say which, and an account that says it never does has to explain what's different from thirty genuinely independent human experts who happen to agree.

2. Editable assent. I can change the other party's apparent mind by editing its input — telling it I'm a specialist, or asking it to steelman my position. Its agreement is, in a way human agreement isn't, at my disposal. Does a conciliationist account have to rule that kind of assent out as evidence, and if so, on what grounds that don't also rule out ordinary persuasion, which also changes minds by changing inputs?

3. Asymmetric legibility. It has plausibly read more of the relevant material than I have, but I can't tell understanding from fluent reconstruction. Human peerhood leans on track record and visible reasoning; neither is available in the same shape here, so whatever I substitute has to be stated as a requirement.

Then: is some subset of the desiderata jointly unsatisfiable? I have a suspicion that you can't simultaneously hold (a) that the rational response depends only on the evidence you actually have, (b) that you may take your own first-order reasoning into account from the inside, and (c) that recognized peer disagreement as such always calls for moving toward the other side. If that's right — or if the real tension is elsewhere — say which requirement gets dropped and why. And tell me which single requirement, once you commit to it, knocks out the most otherwise attractive views. I want to know where the fork in the road actually is rather than a catalogue of everything everyone would like a theory to do.
