I'm a clinical nutrition researcher. I design n-of-1 and crossover feeding trials, and I think in terms of baseline covariates, individual treatment-effect heterogeneity, adherence, effect sizes, and nocebo response. I have no philosophy training, but I've read the diet material in Ecce Homo and I keep thinking Nietzsche is a crude precursor of what I do — he formulates the question of questions, in one English translation, as "How precisely must thou feed thyself in order to attain to thy maximum of power."

Restate the claims doing the work in his dietetics, one at a time, in the vocabulary I actually use: that nutrition matters more than any theological question and is properly posed as a question about one's own maximum of power; that there are no universal dietary rules, only a regime fitted to a particular constitution and discoverable by experimenting on oneself; that a people's cookery explains its intellectual condition, and that philosophical outlooks are symptoms of metabolic states; and that health is not a condition one has or lacks but something one wins back and can push past the normal range.

Then give me the residue report. For each claim, say whether it becomes something my framework can measure or test, whether it survives only by being distorted, or whether it has no expression at all — and where you had to supply a charitable proxy, flag it instead of passing it off as translation.

Finish with the hard question: is Nietzsche's denial of universal dietary rules the same claim as individual treatment-effect heterogeneity, or did something go missing in that identification? Tell me whether the residue points to a concept my framework cannot express, or to a way of talking that looked like a claim and wasn't one.
