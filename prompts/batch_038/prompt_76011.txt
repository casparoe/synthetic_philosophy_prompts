I'm reading Darwin's Dangerous Idea on my own — no degree, I drive a forklift for a living and I've always liked this kind of stuff — and I've been stuck on chapter 3's crane and skyhook thing for about two weeks now. My friend who did a philosophy degree says I'm wasting my time, because the whole distinction is an analogy Dennet never has to defend, and if you actually spell out what's being compared to what it falls apart. His version: a real crane is a machine somebody built, standing on ground that has to be able to bear the load, rigged by a person, lifting pre-made material up toward a height somebody decided on in advance. So when he calls natural selection plus language and culture a "crane," he's borrowing a designed machine with a goal and pointing it at a process that has no designer and no goal. And a "skyhook" is just the same picture with the arm shortened until it looks like a miracle. Which makes crane-vs-skyhook a dial you can turn rather than a line you can find: anything gets branded a skyhook if you're suspicious of it and promoted to a crane if you're not.

Is he right, or is he just attacking the packaging? I'd like a real audit, not a summary of what the book says. Lay the mapping out explicitly — what in the metaphor is the ground, the foundation, the base of the crane, the load, the lifting, the height, the operator, and what the skyhook version of each of those would be — then go through where the mapping breaks and tell me which of those breaks actually hurts the argument and which are just the usual sloppiness you get from any metaphor. Two things I especially want answered: (a) is there a checkable criterion hiding behind crane vs. skyhook, something that tells you an explanation has paid its way instead of borrowing sophistication it hasn't accounted for, or is it purely rhetoric? and (b) does "greedy reductionism" name a distinct mistake — skipping levels you could have used — or is it just a putdown for reductionists who disagree with him about which levels are real? Also here's my own thought from the job, tell me if it's dumb: a crane doesn't get you anything a slow winch wouldn't get you eventually, it just saves time, and if biology has all the time in the world, what work is the crane actually doing? Same question for the later chapters where culture and language get turned into cranes — how would you even tell a crane from a slow skyhook once you're that far up?

And please be straight with me about what's settled here versus what's a live fight among people who work on this stuff. My friend talks like everybody agrees the metaphor is empty and I doubt that's true, so flag the spots where you're giving me your own read rather than a consensus, and say so if there's no consensus either way.
