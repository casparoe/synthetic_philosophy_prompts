We covered sunk costs in my practical rationality seminar this week — the standard line being that past expenditure is normatively inert and only the future prospects matter. But going through my own life I noticed my verdicts don't line up with that at all, and I want to know whether there's a principle underneath them or whether some just have to go.

- $15 movie ticket, half an hour in and it's unbearable: walking out is obviously right.
- A ten-year marriage, no abuse, just flat, and we both suspect we'd be happier apart: "we built this thing" feels like a real reason to keep at it, not a bias.
- A two-year relationship with exactly the same flatness: leave.
- A country that keeps fighting a war so the dead "didn't die in vain": paradigm case of the fallacy, genuinely repulsive. Except it looks structurally identical to the marriage one.
- My grandparents' farm, which I could sell tomorrow for good money and take an easier life: not selling feels right, and I don't think it's because of any forecast about my future happiness.
- A startup, four years in, product failed, but those four years left me with skills and contacts I wouldn't otherwise have: that investment reads as an asset, not a sunk cost.
- Same startup, four years in, product failed, nothing transferable learned: quit.
- A free ticket handed to me by a friend, bad movie: I feel more entitled to leave than with the $15 one, which I'm pretty sure is just the bias showing up in me.

My tentative idea is that past investment generates a forward-looking reason only when it's the kind of thing you can't hand back — nobody takes the movie ticket back, but ten years of a marriage can't be unlived and the farm can't be un-inherited. The war case breaks that and every patch I've tried feels like cheating. I also wondered whether what matters is that someone invested *alongside* me, but the dead soldiers invested too, so that doesn't separate them either.

Is there a principle that gets all or most of these? What does it say about a case I haven't listed — say, staying at a job for six more months purely because a pension vests at ten? And if I can only save the rest by giving one up, which verdict should go?
