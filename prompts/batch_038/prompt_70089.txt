I want a careful comparison of two frameworks that appear to cover overlapping terrain from opposite directions, together with an evaluation of a reductive thesis of my own that I have not seen defended anywhere.

The first is the tightness–looseness construct in cultural psychology, associated with Michele Gelfand and her collaborators. Tightness is defined as the strength of a society's social norms together with its low tolerance of deviant behavior; looseness is weak norms and wide latitude. The construct is meant to be a descriptive dimension of cultural variation rather than a moral appraisal, and the literature is explicit about a trade-off: tight societies tend to be orderly, well coordinated, and low in crime and social dysregulation, but also lower in openness, creativity, and institutional innovation, while loose societies are more inventive and tolerant of difference but more prone to anomie, under-coordination, and norm erosion. Tightness is explained largely by ecological and historical threat — population density, resource scarcity, disaster frequency, external conflict — and the framework is applied at national, regional, and even situational levels.

The second is the moral/conventional distinction from developmental psychology, associated with Elliot Turiel. On that account, normative judgments divide into those concerning harm, fairness, and rights, which are treated as authority-independent and generalizable across settings, and those concerning conventions — dress, forms of address, decorum, procedural rules — which are understood as contingent, alterable by authority, and not wrong in themselves apart from sanction or agreement. Richard Shweder and, later, Jonathan Haidt pressed the objection that the boundary is itself culturally variable: in many societies violations of community, purity, and deference norms are experienced as fully moral rather than as breaches of mere etiquette, and the developmental evidence for a universal, early-emerging boundary is weaker than the framework assumes.

Here is the thesis I want tested. Tightness is not an independent dimension of cultural variation at all, but a redescription of two features of the moral/conventional boundary: its location and its enforcement. A tight society, on this reading, is one that has annexed much of the conventional domain into the moral domain and polices the annexed territory severely; a loose society is one that has left most convention where Turiel says it belongs — contingent, negotiable, and not a matter of wrongdoing. Whatever the tightness construct treats as a global cultural quantity would then be a function of how much of ordinary life a society has moralized and how hard it sanctions moral failure.

Compare the two frameworks in detail and tell me whether the reduction succeeds. In particular:

1. Does tightness capture anything the moralization-plus-sanction account leaves out? Candidates I have in mind are the degree of normative consensus (tightness seems to require agreement about what the norms are, not merely that they are moralized), the density of monitoring and sanctioning institutions, and the enforcement of norms nobody treats as moral — etiquette, queuing, dress — which tight societies may police hard without moralizing.

2. Does the reduction smuggle in a normative bias? Turiel's framework already carries a liberal evaluative commitment, since classifying something as conventional demotes it from the domain of genuine moral concern, whereas the tightness construct was designed to be neutral. If so, is my thesis covertly the claim that tight societies commit a category error, and can it be restated in a value-free way?

3. How does each framework handle a culture that is tight in some domains and loose in others — severe about sexual conduct, permissive about speech? The domain picture is content-specific by construction; tightness is usually scored globally. Which handles the patchwork better, and what does that suggest about whether tightness is a single property or a bundle?

4. Finally, take up the practical question the tightness literature invites: how much rule-following should a society demand of its members? Is that question intelligible on the domain picture, or does that picture dissolve it into the question of which norms deserve to be moralized at all?

Please argue rather than cite: give me the analysis, the strongest case for the reduction, and the strongest case against it, and say plainly whether you take the two frameworks to be rivals, complements, or descriptions of one thing at different levels. Do not provide a bibliography or reading list.
