I'm sitting in on a phil-sci seminar this term (I'm in physics, this is my one elective) and in the first week the instructor handed out a two-page orientation sheet on structural realism so everyone would be on the same page before we started on the actual papers. It reads a lot more confident than the papers I've tried to skim, and a couple of things in it sit badly with what I thought I knew, but I don't want to make a scene in section over something that turns out to be standard. Here's the central paragraph, pretty much as he wrote it:

"Structural realism starts from the pessimistic meta-induction: theories that were wildly successful turned out to be false, and that is a standing problem for realism about the unobservable world. Worrall's insight was that in the shift from Fresnel to Maxwell the equations for light carried over while the ether they were originally equations about did not. What accumulates across theory change is mathematical structure, not furniture. The structural realist therefore holds that the relations are what is real, and the relata at the ends of them are at best bookkeeping devices. That is why this is not a retreat from realism but realism correctly aimed, and why the meta-induction turns out to be harmless: it only ever bites on the parts of a theory that structural realism has already conceded are dispensable, and everywhere else the successful structure is retained — which is a prediction the view makes and history keeps confirming. Newton's law of gravitation is another instance: nobody believes gravity is an instantaneous pull between bodies anymore, but every trajectory we fly is still computed from the inverse-square law. Note also that the structural realist is not saying we know nothing about the unobservable world. We know its structure — and structure, in any case, is all a theory ever says. Where the view really pays off is quantum mechanics, where what an electron is has no settled answer but the Hilbert space structure is not in doubt." Then the sheet concludes that the old fight between realism and empiricism mostly dissolves: the empiricist is right that we can't know the nature of things, the realist is right that we know more than the phenomena, and there's nothing substantive left to argue about.

Two things bug me. First, I thought there were two versions of this — an epistemic one that keeps the relata around but says we can't know their intrinsic natures, and an ontic one that says there aren't any relata at all — and I thought the epistemic one came first and was supposed to be the moderate option. But the sheet only ever describes the radical one, while presenting it as the moderate one, and it never mentions that anyone distinguishes them. Second, I have this nagging hunch that if all you know is that the world has some structure or other, you know basically nothing; and if you know which structure it has, then you seem to know a lot more than the view wants to admit. I genuinely can't tell whether that's an objection people actually press or just my own confusion, and the sheet doesn't gesture at anything like it.

So: can you go through that paragraph sentence by sentence and tell me what a structural realist would happily sign off on, what they'd want reworded, and what is actually wrong? I'd especially like to know whether the Fresnel case can bear the weight being put on it — including whether "the equations carried over" is quite right given what happened to how those equations were interpreted afterwards — and whether the Newton example is doing the same kind of work or sneaking in something weaker, since an inverse-square law that survives as a limiting case seems like a different thing from a structure that's just retained. Also whether the line about the meta-induction being harmless is a fair claim or one that makes the position impossible to test, and if it's the latter, whether the structural realist has any way of being wrong about a future theory. And whether that last claim — structure is all a theory ever says — is something the position is entitled to, or something it needs in order to get off the ground and doesn't actually have. Then tell me where my hunch lands: is it a recognized problem with a name and standard replies, or is it a misfire, and either way is my ESR/OSR worry the right thing to bring up in section or am I splitting hairs?
