Bit of an odd request. I'm three weeks into deterrence theory for a dissertation chapter and it's become obvious that everyone in the field smuggles in a private list of what an account of deterrence has to do, then scores themselves against it and declares victory. So: write the list out properly first, before I let anyone score anything.

Give me the desiderata — the requirements any adequate theory of nuclear deterrence has to meet — and for each one, the puzzle, argument or situation that makes it a requirement rather than a flourish. Pitch it at a theory that has to do work for a mid-sized power with a small, vulnerable, second-strike-only force and a declared no-first-use posture, not for a superpower dyad, because that's the chapter and the literature generalises from the Cold War without ever arguing for the right to.

Things I expect to turn up somewhere, though don't just nod along to them because I named them: how a threat that would be catastrophic and arguably insane to execute can be made credible by someone who is in fact sane; what backward induction actually does to threats that are optimal off the equilibrium path, and whether that's a fatal objection or a misdescription of what the agents are playing; the stability–instability problem; how much of the observation that there's been no great-power war since 1945 a theory is entitled to claim as confirmation; and whether the account still works when the adversary reads it, since anything published stops being a private calculation.

Then the part I actually care about. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which turn out to be the local taste of one camp — I strongly suspect a couple of the game-theoretic ones are the latter, and I'd like to be told which. If some subset of them can't be jointly satisfied, say so flatly and say which give: if rational credibility and a genuinely vulnerable no-first-use force simply cannot be had together, that's the finding of my chapter, not a problem with it. And tell me which single requirement knocks out the most candidates.

Be committed about all of this — take a definite line on the negotiable/non-negotiable split and give your reasons, rather than laying out the camps and leaving me to choose. Numbered list is fine. Hard limit of 800 words, and none of them spent on sources or a reading list; I've got the bibliography, what I don't have is the specification.
