I cover evolution and the history of science for a magazine — ideas desk, mostly profiles and disputes. Last week I sat with a researcher who's firmly in the anti-adaptationist camp, the spandrels-of-San-Marco lineage, and he walked me through what he calls the retrofit problem: his argument that calling something an adaptation isn't an explanation at all. I wrote it down on the train home and it went roughly like this.

1. To call a trait an adaptation is to claim it was shaped by selection because it did some job — call the job F.
2. That claim is about populations nobody observed, in environments nobody can visit, starting from ancestral forms of the trait nobody can recover, competing against demands nobody measured.
3. So for any trait and any F you like, somebody can produce a selection story that lands on it: pick the right ancestral environment, the right starting form, the right competing demands, and the trait comes out favored for F. Nothing in the story is pinned down by the trait, because the trait is exactly what's up for explanation.
4. An account you can always produce, whatever turns up, is an account nothing could contradict.
5. And a claim nothing could contradict isn't explaining anything — it's just handing the outcome a nicer name after the fact.
6. So "adaptation for F" never explains anything, and we should stop saying traits are adaptations.

He's aiming at 6 and I mostly buy the spirit of it — his favorite example was the human chin, which he insists is a byproduct of how the jaw gets reshaped as the face shrinks rather than anything selection built for a purpose. But now that I'm trying to write the piece I keep hitting the fact that the argument doesn't seem to know where to stop. Swap in his own preferred explanation — the trait is a consequence of how the structure next to it grows — and 3 through 5 seem to run on it just as smoothly: for any trait I can nominate some other thing it's a consequence of, and if you push back I can retune which other thing. If it runs on both sides of a fight, it isn't showing that one side is empty, it's saying something much bigger about infering the past from the present. Which is not the piece I want to write, and I dont think it's true, because that same inferential move is basically the whole rest of my beat — attributing a warming trend, working out what killed something off, reconstructing who's related to whom.

So: which step do you cut, and what does cutting it cost? I'd rather keep 2 — I like that it's about the past being unavailable — but tell me honestly if 2 is where the damage is and I'm just fond of it. I also want the two sides' own concessions: which of these steps a working adaptationist would admit is a real problem (I suspect there's a narrower version of 3 he'd sign onto about a lot of published papers, especially the ones that gesture at optimality without measuring anything), and which steps the anti-adaptationist has to disown to keep his byproduct explanations in play as science rather than as the same story told backward. And if the honest answer is that the argument survives but 6 needs a much smaller blast radius than my source thinks, say that and tell me how much smaller — I can absolutely write "the retrofit problem is real but local" if that's the truth. No bibliography, I've got the literature; I need the diagnosis.
