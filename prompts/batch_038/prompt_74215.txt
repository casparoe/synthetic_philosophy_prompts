I'm a freelance science-and-ideas writer working on a longread for a general-but-smart outlet about Solomonoff induction, Kolmogorov complexity and the whole "is there a mathematically correct way to do induction?" thing. Not a book review, no anniversary peg, just: here's a formal theory that claims to settle what counts as the best inference from evidence, here's why it's beautiful, here's why nobody can actually run it, here's what's still unresolved. About 4-5k words. I've got the outline problem though, which is that I have roughly nine candidate open questions and room for maybe three to be handled properly.

Here's what I've been kicking around:

1. Is the uncomputability objection fatal, or is it a red herring? You can't compute K(x) in general — there's no program that outputs it for every input — and my understanding is you also can't even prove most statements about it from any fixed axiom system. So is Solomonoff induction just an unreachable ideal the way a frictionless plane is, or is something actually broken?

2. The prior-choice worry. The invariance theorem only gets you equivalence up to a constant factor, and with a finite amount of data the constant is doing real work. So does the theory secretly depend on picking your reference machine, and does that matter for finite data even if it washes out eventually?

3. "Completeness." Solomonoff induction dominates every other enumerable prediction scheme — but only if the world is actually generating the data from some computable measure. What breaks when it isn't, and is anyone taking that seriously as a live worry rather than a technicality?

4. Whether it vindicates Occam's razor or actually undercuts it. The pop version is that the shortest hypothesis gets the highest prior so simplicity wins. But it doesn't pick the shortest one, it averages over all of them weighted by 2^-K. Which is it, and how much of what scientists and ML people mean by Occam's razor is a folk theorem the math doesn't support?

5. Whether any of this is live in modern ML. Compression shows up everywhere now — MDL-flavored arguments, minimum description length bounds, the generalization gap being predicted by how well a net compresses its training set, the whole "deep learning is compression" line. Is Solomonoff induction actually an ancestor of that work, or a cousin that gets name-checked?

6. Hume. Does any of this answer the problem of induction, or does it assume induction is legitimate and then prove theorems inside that assumption? The convergence guarantees seem to be conditional on the data coming from a computable source, which is sort of the thing in question.

7. The Levin-complexity route. Swapping time-bounded complexity for plain Kolmogorov complexity makes things computable-ish but drags in the runtime of the program as part of the description. Does that fix the practical problem or just relocate it?

8. Berry's paradox as a worry about the whole enterprise. "The smallest positive integer not definable in fewer than twenty words." If K(x) isn't well-defined in an absolute sense, only up to a constant, and the constant is enormous compared to the strings anyone actually cares about, what's left of the claim that there's a unique right prior?

Rank these for me — which three or four get real space, which get a paragraph, which get cut. I want the reasoning more than the list: what's upstream of what, which ones would actually move the field if someone settled them versus which just sound good in a pitch, and how tractable each one is for a writer who's good with the concepts but is not going to derive anything. And flag any of the eight where I've got the framing wrong, because I'd rather find out now than get dinged in copy edit. If there's a ninth question I'm missing that beats several of these, say so and tell me which ones it knocks out.
