I have covered science and ideas for a general-interest magazine for the better part of a decade, mostly working the seam where quantitative forecasting meets public decision-making, and I am applying for a one-year nonfiction fellowship that carries a stipend, no reporting budget to speak of, and a requirement that the year end in a substantial series of essays plus a book proposal. I would like you to help me draft the two-page proposal itself. My half-formed idea is this. The familiar coherence argument in formal epistemology holds that non-probabilistic degrees of belief are defective because they expose the believer to a sure loss: a bookie who knows your prices can assemble a package of bets that costs you money no matter how the world turns out, so incoherence is not merely inelegant but expensive. The argument quietly assumes a single agent who can accept or refuse the whole package and who bears the loss personally. Almost every institution I report on fails that assumption in an interesting way. Their implicit probabilities are distributed across documents issued by different offices on different schedules: a coastal city's climate adaptation plan, the risk language in its bond offering, the rate its flood insurer charges, and the recurrence intervals its emergency management office uses for the same stretch of shoreline can imply numbers for the same event that differ by a factor of several. No individual is incoherent; each office is internally scrupulous. And yet an arbitrageur with the patience to hold contingent claims against all of them could in principle construct a package that profits whatever the water does. I have built a composite case out of three municipalities from my own reporting notes, and I would rather the proposal work from that composite than from any single city, since one of my questions is whether the phenomenon is structural rather than local. There is a second half to the idea: a great many of the claims that matter most are forecast on horizons that will not resolve within the lifetime of anyone who accepts the bet, and a bookie who cannot collect has no leverage at all, which makes me suspect that the sure-loss story does real normative work only for short-horizon, individually owned bets and does something else entirely elsewhere.

What I need from you is a proposal, not an essay defending a conclusion. It should state the question precisely enough that a reader could tell me what would settle it, and I want the question stated in the form I actually care about: when the agent who would be Dutch-booked is a diffuse collective with no single person authorized to accept or refuse the package, and when the package would not resolve within the relevant decision horizon, does the sure-loss argument lose its normative force, or does it reveal that coherence was always a norm on unified agency rather than on belief? I would like the proposal to say why the existing positions leave that open. I am already acquainted with the two standard replies — that the collective case is a category mistake because only individual agents have degrees of belief, and that sure-loss results can be generalized from individuals to groups whose judgments are aggregated — and neither one tells me what to go and do with a notebook for twelve months. Then the method, month by month, and here I want you to be concrete about the two kinds of work and how they interlock: the conceptual work of pinning down what counts as an institution having an implicit probability, whether a document that never uses a number can commit an agency to one, and what a sure loss looks like when the loser is a budget line rather than a person; and the reporting, which would involve assembling the composite case, getting a friendly actuary or two to price the implied claims, and sitting with the people who write adaptation plans and bond disclosures to find out whether they experience the inconsistency as a problem or as a division of labor. Say why this method rather than the obvious alternatives — a straight history of the idea, a round of interviews with professional forecasters about calibration, another think piece on prediction markets — and be candid about which of those I am tempted by and should resist.

Finally, tell me what a result would look like and what would count as failure, and I mean failure in the specific sense: if all the project establishes is that public institutions state inconsistent probabilities, I will have spent a year proving something a county clerk already knows, and the fellowship committee will suspect as much. I want the proposal to name the findings that would actually change how a philosopher frames the argument and how an editor frames a story about institutional forecasting, and to be honest about the possibility that the honest finding is a negative one — that the coherence norm simply does not travel to collectives, and the interesting deliverable is a clear account of where it stops and why. Include a realistic scope assessment for twelve months of one person's time with no research assistant, and flag the parts where a journalist is out of her depth and would need to bring in a formal epistemologist rather than bluff. My editor has limited patience for anything containing a Greek letter, so the proposal should indicate how the technical material would be carried by reported scenes rather than exposition; readers responded far better, in my last piece on this beat, to a trader explaining how he sizes a position than to any of the arithmetic I put beside it. No bibliography and no literature review by citation, please — I will supply the references myself once the argument has a shape.
