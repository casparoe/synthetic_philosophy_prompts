I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine — a weekly column plus a few reported features a year — and I have a recurring problem: Zeno. Every couple of months somebody pitches me a story about how his paradoxes have finally been solved, or resurrected, or turned out to matter for quantum computing. It's usually a physicist or a mathematician, they're genuinely smart, and the pitch email always reads beautifully. Then the piece runs and my inbox fills up with philosophers explaining that I conflated three different questions and never actually touched the paradox. My editor has no philosophy training and has to sign off before I commission anything, and I dont have time to read a paper before a commissioning meeting — so whatever you build has to be runnable by a smart non-specialist in about fifteen minutes, tops.

What I want is a triage procedure, not an essay about Zeno: ordered steps I can literally walk down a pitch. Ground it in whatever is actually solid about why these paradoxes resist the obvious fix — in particular the distinction I keep blowing past, which is that "can an infinite series sum to a finite number" is a different question from "can anyone actually perform infinitely many acts," and that there's a third register people slide into, which is a claim about whether space and time are physically discrete or continuous. My suspicion is that nearly every bad pitch moves between those three without noticing. If that's the right diagnosis, make catching the slide the first move in the procedure. If I've got the taxonomy wrong or incomplete, say so and give me the correct one instead.

For each step I want: what a sufficient answer looks like, what an evasive or hand-wavy answer looks like, where the procedure kills the pitch outright, where it stops and hands the call to a human because the remaining question is about newsworthiness rather than correctness, and which steps are secretly judgment calls dressed up as procedure. It should also catch pitches constructed so that no counterexample can touch them.

Then run the procedure end to end on four pitches:

1. A lab reporting that repeatedly probing a trapped ion suppresses the transition they're watching, pitched to me as "we watched the pot and it didn't boil — Zeno was right."
2. A mathematician arguing that a nonstandard/hyperreal treatment shows there's no such thing as completing infinitely many steps, so the paradoxes are just notation confusion.
3. A philosopher of time arguing that instantaneous velocity is a real property of a moment rather than a limit in disguise, and that this changes how we should talk about determinism.
4. A computer scientist pitching a machine that halves its step time each round and claims to settle the state of a lamp switched infinitely many times.

At least one of these should come out as "genuinely interesting story, but not the story it's pitched as," and I want the procedure to say what the alternative story is and how to reframe the ask. Finish by telling me what the procedure can't do — the failure modes that will still get me burned after I've been using it for a year. No reading lists or citations, please; I can't check them and neither can my editor.
