I teach a graduate seminar on risk, uncertainty, and public policy, and in three weeks we screen Kubrick's *Dr. Strangelove or: How I Learned to Stop Worrying and Love the Bomb* as the opening text for a unit on precautionary reasoning. My students have read the standard materials on the precautionary principle and on expected-value reasoning, and they arrive expecting the film to be a satire of nuclear deterrence, which it is, but I want the screening to do harder work than that. I am trying to get them to distinguish three ways of ranking risky options, and I want the film used to test the third, which is the view I actually want to defend and which they have not met before.

The three views are these. First, ordinary expected-harm reasoning: rank options by probability-weighted outcomes, the default of cost-benefit regulation, which works when probabilities are well estimated and degrades when they are not. Second, worst-case or maximin-style reasoning about catastrophic outcomes under uncertainty too deep to assign probabilities, which some legal theorists argue is the correct posture when the stakes are ruin and the odds are not merely unknown but unknowable; on this view you compare worst cases and ignore likelihoods. Third — the one I want the film to interrogate — what I will call, for want of a better label, the recoverability view: rank options by whether errors about them can be corrected, by whether there is a point past which a mistake cannot be walked back and no further information can change your mind. On this view the object of precaution is not the probability of harm but the structure of one's options after one turns out to be wrong. It is not maximin, since an option with a grimmer worst case can outrank one with a milder worst case if the error is recoverable. I would also like the students to work with a constraint I attach to it: a safeguard is genuinely precautionary only if those exposed to the risk can know about it, so a protective arrangement that has to stay secret in order to function, or that eliminates the possibility of being told the threat has not materialized, fails to count as precaution on this view rather than merely being badly designed.

With that in hand, write the critical reading I can circulate. Treat the doomsday machine as the central exhibit: the machine is described in the War Room as a device whose deterrent effect was supposed to rest on everyone knowing of its existence, which it does not, and whose triggering is deliberately made automatic precisely so that no human being can hesitate or be talked out of it. Ask what each of the three rankings says about building such a machine and about the Soviet leadership's decision not to announce it, where the recoverability view locates the actual error, and whether the film's irony lands on the maximin principle itself or only on the institution that adopts it. Then work through General Ripper's unilateral launch and the recall machinery that fails around it — the code prefix meant to authenticate orders, the base sealed against communication, the recall transmitted but incomplete, the crew that discards messages it cannot verify — and say whether the film's diagnosis is that probability estimates were bad or that the arrangement left no point at which being wrong could be discovered and reversed. Take up the mineshaft argument at the end, where the surviving decision-makers are urged into a vast protective program on the ground that a rival's protective capacity must not be allowed to exceed one's own, and tell me whether that scene satirizes precautionary reasoning or dramatizes the recoverability view's point that safeguards which cannot be disclosed become instruments of rivalry.

Two further things I need, because I would rather the reading be honest than tidy. Tell me what the film's actual target is if it is not the expected-harm calculus: my suspicion is that it is the distribution of discretion and automaticity across an institution, which is a design question rather than a principle of choice under uncertainty, and if you agree, say what work the recoverability view is then doing and whether it earns a place in the unit. And tell me where the film resists this reading — including the awkward fact that it is a farce, in which every character is a caricature and the catastrophe arrives almost as a punchline, so that it may be evidence about incompetent institutions rather than about any principle; if the reading forces the film, I want to be told so before my students discover it themselves.

Give me exactly five numbered points, no introduction and no conclusion, each two or three sentences, in this order: the doomsday machine, Ripper's launch and the failed recall, the mineshaft argument, what the film's real target is and what that implies for the recoverability view, and the strongest resistance to the reading. Paraphrase the film rather than quoting it; I will supply the lines myself.
