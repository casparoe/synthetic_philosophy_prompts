Right, so. I retired four years ago after 33 years in a lab — analytical chemistry, latterly doing method development for the people who test drinking water. Boring, reliable work, and the one thing I never once questioned was the maths underneath it: calibration curves, error propagation, the lot. Since retiring I've been reading philosophy, badly and enthusiastically, and I've stumbled into the idea that the standard axioms of set theory might themselves be inconsistent, and that we couldn't prove otherwise from the inside even if they weren't. That's taken the wind out of me a bit, so I want a number rather than a mood.

The proposition, as tightly as I can state it: ZFC — Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory with choice, in its usual first-order form — is inconsistent. That is, there exists a finite derivation of a contradiction from those axioms, whether or not anybody ever actually writes it down. I want your credence in that, as a probability, and I want the derivation of the number rather than just the number.

Please break it into pieces, with a figure and a couple of lines of justification against each, then show me how you combine them. Here are the pieces I'd like you to handle, plus any you think I've got wrong or missed out:

- The ladder. You can order theories by strength: Peano arithmetic, then Zermelo set theory without replacement, then ZFC, then ZFC plus an inaccessible or two. Give me a credence at each rung, and tell me whether the decline from rung to rung is smooth or whether there's a particular one where it falls off a cliff. I read somewhere that Gentzen got consistency for arithmetic by allowing induction up to a rather enormous ordinal, which makes me think the real question at each rung is how far you trust the well-foundedness of whatever ordinal buys you that rung. Is that the right shape for the decomposition, or am I mangling it?

- Reachability. Suppose ZFC is inconsistent — does it matter how long the shortest contradiction is? My suspicion is that the honest thing to hold a credence in is something like "ZFC is inconsistent AND the shortest such proof is short enough to be produced by people or machines within, say, a few centuries", with the two factors kept seperate. Tell me whether you agree, and what you'd put the second factor at.

- Blast radius. Strictly outside the proposition, but I want it anyway: conditional on ZFC being inconsistent, what's the chance that the bulk of usable mathematics survives — that most of what working analysts and scientists actually do could be rebuilt on something weaker? I gather a lot of classical mathematics turns out to need surprisingly little.

- Near misses. Back in 2011 a Princeton mathematician announced he'd proved arithmetic itself inconsistent, and it collapsed within days when somebody spotted the flaw. I also vaguely remember a prominent logician predicting an explicit contradiction in ZFC would turn up within the century and setting out to find it himself — I may be garbling that, don't worry about the names. Should either kind of event move a number like mine, and by how much? And does having a large body of mathematics formally checked in a proof assistant count as evidence of anything, given that the checker is trusted precisely because the theory underneath it is?

My own figure is about 0.04, which feels simultaneously rash and cowardly. A mate of mine who did maths for a living thinks the question is a daft one and puts it at effectively zero, though he wont say why in any detail. So: your number, the working, which single term your answer is most sensitive to, and which of these pieces you think he and I are actually disagreeing about. Then tell me what evidence or argument would move your number furthest, and in which direction. Cheers.
