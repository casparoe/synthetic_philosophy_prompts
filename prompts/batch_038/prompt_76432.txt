Our town is dedicating a new wing of the county history museum in about six weeks and somehow I've been asked to give the address. Some background on me: not an academic. I taught high school history here for 33 years, and in retirement I transcribed the county's quarter-sessions and poor-law records from roughly 1740 to 1830, mostly by hand, because almost none of it had been indexed since it was written down. The wing is paid for by a donation from the Vardeman family — their ancestor Elias Vardeman owned the rope-walk and the two mills, there's a bust of him in the old lobby, the family is very proud of him, and several of them will be in the front row. There'll also be about forty eighth graders bussed in, the town council, and maybe three or four real historians from the state university who've seen some of my transcriptions and are coming to find out whether I embarrass myself. The centrepiece of the new wing is a wall panel with names on it that I typed up myself, mostly out of a pauper burial register.

Here's my problem. Everything I know about anybody who isn't Elias Vardeman comes from documents his businesses and the county court produced in order to count them, tax them, prosecute them, or bury them at public expense. My favourite person in the entire archive is a woman named Sarah Doan, who turns up eleven times between 1798 and 1811 — linen theft, receiving, a bastardy proceeding, once for hitting a constable with a chamber pot — and across all eleven she has maybe forty words of recorded speech, every one of them a denial or a plea, all written down by a clerk who spelled her name four different ways. I also have a burial register where about a third of the entries read something like "child, no name, of the workhouse." And I have a 1796 assessment that lists one loom and one cow against the name of a weaver who appears nowhere else in any record that survives.

So: I want a speech, eight to ten minutes read aloud, that makes this material do what a dedication is supposed to do — people should leave feeling they owe something to particular dead people — without pretending I've recovered anybody's inner life or "given voice" to anyone, because I haven't. What I have is their friction with institutions, and that's a real thing and it's not nothing, and I'd like the speech to be honest that it's exactly that much and no more. It should say at least four of these names out loud, it should be scrupulous about the difference between what a document actually states and what I'm inferring from it, and it has to handle the fact that the only reason we can say any of these names is that Elias Vardeman kept outstanding accounts. Which means the family paying for the wing is also, in a sense, the reason the wing contains anything besides him. That cannot land as an accusation from a podium, and it cannot be skipped, and it has to be survivable for them to hear in front of their grandchildren.

Constraints: no name-dropping historians, and please don't use "history from below," "giving voice to the voiceless," or anything about history being written by the winners — the eighth graders will switch off and the academics will roll their eyes. No citations or references needed, it's all my own material. I'd also rather it wasn't solemn for ten minutes straight; the quarter sessions are very funny in places and Sarah Doan in particular is funny, and a dedication that can't laugh at her is talking down to her. End on something the people in the room can actually go do, but not a call to donate. And at the bottom, tell me roughly where you'd cut if it runs long on the day.
