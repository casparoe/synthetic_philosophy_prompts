Last term I took part in a debating evening at my college on whether independent schools should lose their charitable status, and the speaker on the opposing side closed with the formula that he was entirely in favour of equality of opportunity but opposed to equality of outcome. He said it three times in five minutes, the chair accepted it as though it disposed of the motion, and yet it seemed to me to change meaning between each repetition: at one point it amounted to an objection to quotas in admissions, at another to the claim that anyone who wants to compress the income distribution is merely dressing up envy as justice, and by the end it was being used to suggest that the children of the comfortably off cannot have been treated unfairly, since nothing was taken from them. I could not pin any of this down on the night, and I would rather not go into my supervision next week unable to say what the sentence means.

Could you lay out the distinct theses that 'equality of opportunity, not equality of outcome' can be pressed into expressing — running from the ones that are close to tautology, through the substantive and defensible, to any that are straightforwardly false or that no serious person has actually held (I suspect 'equality of outcome' is often a position nobody occupies, which would make the slogan's second half a denial of nothing)? For each reading, I would like you to be explicit about what 'opportunity' amounts to — the absence of formal legal barriers, a level playing field in the competition for positions, some capability-based measure of what people can actually do and be, equality of starting resources — and what is being denied under 'outcome': identical incomes, any patterned distribution at all, a ceiling on the ratio between top and bottom, or relative social standing. Say too which readings coincide with familiar positions in the literature, such as the luck-egalitarian insistence on compensating brute luck while leaving option luck alone, or the distinction between merely opening careers to talents and genuinely equalising the chances of those with similar native endowments and similar willingness to use them.

I am especially interested in whether there is a reading on which the slogan is self-undermining — the thought that unequal outcomes in one generation become unequal opportunities in the next, so that anyone who takes the opportunity half seriously must end up constraining outcomes after all — and in how a defender might resist that without collapsing into holding one of the trivial readings. Then, having given me the menu, tell me which of the readings would genuinely have supported his case about charitable status and which would not; which one he most likely meant, given the use he put it to; and two or three short questions I could have asked from the floor that would have forced him to specify which reading he was committed to. A line-by-line list I can work through, with a verdict attached to each reading, would be far more useful to me than a general attack on or defence of the slogan.
