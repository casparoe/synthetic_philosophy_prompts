I serve as vice chair of the board of a regional land trust — eleven volunteer trustees, most of them not professionals of any kind connected to conservation finance. In three weeks the full board votes on whether to exercise a right of first refusal on 410 acres of riverside woodland. The owning family has offered it to us at $1.15 million, roughly thirty percent below what a builder has already put in writing; a state habitat grant provisionally covers $690,000 of that, and we have $310,000 of the remainder committed. The offer lapses in seventy-five days.

The complication is a survey. A hydrologist who walked the property with me last month thinks there is a spring-fed fen on the north parcel, and with it a real possibility of a state-listed mollusk. He is careful to say this is an impression, not a finding; he has not published anything and does not want his name attached to a guess. A formal survey sufficient to settle the question would take eight weeks and cost $18,000 out of the acquisition fund. To do it before the offer lapses we would need the family to extend by sixty days, which they say they will do only against a $40,000 deposit — credited to the price if we close, forfeited if we walk.

Here is why the board is split. If the survey confirms a listed species, we must report it under the terms of the state grant, and the grant then requires a state-approved management plan. That has happened on the trust's two other preserves, and in both cases the approved plan meant closing the land to the public access our neighbors actually use: a fishing trail, dog walking, a fourth-grade stream program. Our membership drive is built on that access, and two of our three largest individual donors have said plainly that it is why they give. So confirmation would not stop the purchase; it would change what the purchase is worth to the people who fund us.

Against surveying, one trustee points out that two members have already told her privately they will vote to buy whatever the survey says, because the alternative is a subdivision, and that a third wants the land for the trail. On her account, the survey cannot change the vote, so it is pure downside: either we spend $18,000 and learn nothing, or we spend $58,000 and four months and learn the one thing that costs us the access program. In favor of surveying, another trustee says we cannot buy land blind, that more information is always worth having, and that if we skip it and the fen turns out to be there, we will spend the next thirty years wishing we had known. A third says the real problem is not the money but our own bylaws, which commit the trust not to knowingly act against its conservation purpose — if we commission the survey and it comes back positive, we will have known, and we will have chosen the trail anyway, and we will not be able to tell ourselves or anyone else that we were ignorant. If we do not commission it, nobody can hold us to anything.

Two things happened since our last call. A reporter from the county weekly phoned the office and asked whether we had had the site assessed, which means the fen rumor is circulating locally whether or not we generate data. And the family's agent emailed to say the builder has asked for an extension of his own offer.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the trustees ahead of the meeting. It should:

- Recommend a concrete course of action for the next seventy-five days, with dates and amounts — what we do about the survey, the deposit, the extension, and the vote — rather than laying out the considerations and leaving the choice to us.
- Give reasons trustees could actually argue back against, and be explicit about what would have to be true for your recommendation to be wrong.
- Treat the strongest consideration on the losing side at full force, not as a straw man; whichever way you come out, one of the two camps above is going to feel dismissed, and I need the memo to be good enough that they don't.
- Address the reporter. Say what our position should be if the question is put to us directly, and explain how the fact that the rumor is already loose bears on the decision about the survey.
- Close with a short standing rule the board could apply the next time an acquisition turns on a fact we could go and look up.

Aim for roughly 900 to 1,300 words. Trustees should be able to follow every step without any prior training: write in ordinary language, and where you rely on a general principle, state what it is and why it governs this case rather than naming it or gesturing at a literature. Do not pad it with caveats about consulting counsel or the state agency; assume we have done that and it is not the obstacle.
