I am in my final year at school, writing an extended essay on whether a great many very small harms can together outweigh one grave harm, and I have decided to test the question against the history of leaded petrol rather than against thought experiments. The facts I intend to rely on are these: tetraethyl lead was introduced as an anti-knock fuel additive in the 1920s; five workers died at a new plant in New Jersey in October 1924, the state closed it within days, and production resumed two years later once a federal inquiry concluded there was no practical substitute; the diffuse harm — marginally raised blood-lead levels in children, each below anything a parent or doctor would notice — took a further half century to act on, with sales for road vehicles ending in the United States in 1996 and on British forecourts in 2000; retrospective estimates put the annual cost in the hundreds of millions of lost IQ points.

Here is the position I want to test, which I have framed myself and would like you to treat as given: threshold aggregationism holds that a harm too small to register in anyone's experience cannot count in the moral calculus in its own right, but that the sum of such harms can count, provided the sum is itself something some identifiable person can be said to have suffered. Please tell me, first, whether the lead case satisfies that proviso at all, and what it would mean if the aggregate — a shifted distribution of ability across a generation — has no victim to whom it belongs. Second, how to keep apart the two aggregation problems the episode contains: many tiny harms done to many people, and many tiny contributions made by many motorists, none of which makes a difference on its own. Third, whether the argument that actually moved regulators was aggregationist, or whether it sidestepped the difficulty by pointing to the tail of the distribution, where the number of children crossing a clinical threshold genuinely rises.

I would also like an honest account of what this framework cannot explain about the 1924 contrast — why one substance produced a harm recognised within days and a harm denied for fifty years — and of where threshold aggregationism falls silent, rather than being made to speak. Please write in British English.
