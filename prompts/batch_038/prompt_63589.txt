So I've been sitting in this odd corner of the resistance-theory literature and I keep coming back to July 20, 1944 — Stauffenberg, the briefcase bomb under the table at the Wolf's Lair, the Valkyrie coup that fell apart the same night. I want that episode run through Aquinas's account of tyrannicide specifically. Not just war, not double effect, not the generic "is assassination ever permissible" stuff everyone reaches for — the actual Thomistic conditions.

Here's the apparatus I want used, as I read it, from the treatise he wrote for the King of Cyprus and from the secunda secundae:

1. Initiative against a tyrant doesn't belong to private persons. Where the ruler is a usurper who seized power illegitimately, Aquinas is much more permissive and points approvingly at scriptural figures who killed such a ruler. Where a legitimate ruler degenerates into tyranny, he's far more restrictive.
2. Even in the permissive case, what does the controlling work is a harm comparison: disturbing a tyrannical government isn't the sin of sedition, since such a government isn't ordered to the common good — but if the disturbance is such that the subjects suffer more from the ensuing disorder than they did from the tyrant, the action is off the table. (In the sedition question he even lets an objection stand that delivering a multitude from tyrannical rule is praiseworthy; the qualification shows up in the reply.)
3. Where there's no human authority left to appeal to, his counsel is to have recourse to God, who turns the ruler's heart or removes him. Human beings don't get to occupy that gap.
4. Underneath all of it: political authority is an office defined by its end, the common good, so a ruler who governs for his own private benefit has undercut the very thing that makes obedience owed to him.

If you think I've garbled any of that, say so, but please work with the conditions as stated — I don't have the texts in front of me and I don't want citations or a reading list, I want the analysis.

What I'm after:

(a) The mapping, condition by condition. Was Hitler a usurper or a legitimate ruler turned tyrant? He was appointed through something like constitutional procedure and then ruled tyrannically, which seems to shove him into the category where Aquinas is most restrictive — but does the total absence of any ordering to the common good, especially once the extermination program is running, change the classification rather than just the degree? And what do you do with the oath: the army oath was reworded in 1934 to swear personal unconditional obedience to Hitler. Does anything in Aquinas on oaths and on commands that exceed a superior's authority bear on that, or is it a dead end?

(b) The authority question, which I think is the real crux and which most treatments skip. Stauffenberg was chief of staff in the Reserve Army's organization, and Valkyrie wasn't an invention of the plotters — it was a standing contingency plan of the German state itself for restoring order during internal unrest, which they had quietly amended. Does that make him an organ of public authority acting under a lawful procedure he hijacked, or a private person wearing a uniform? Is there anything in Aquinas that could decide that, or is he simply silent about the situation where the only institutional capacity left standing is inside the tyrant's own army?

(c) The harm comparison, and whether it runs ex ante or ex post. The coup failed within hours; thousands were arrested and thousands executed, Freisler's People's Court made spectacles of the trials, relatives were sent to camps, and the war ran another nine months. Does that settle it on Aquinas's own terms, or is the harm comparison supposed to be about what a reasonable actor could expect? If it's the latter, what could Stauffenberg reasonably expect in mid-1944, given how often the plan had already been postponed and how thin the garrison support was? And here's the part I can't make fit: Tresckow apparently argued (paraphrase, don't trust the wording) that the attempt had to be made even if it failed, so that the world would see there were Germans who acted. Does Aquinas's harm comparison have any room at all for an act whose value is partly testimonial, or does that consideration have to be smuggled in from outside his framework?

(d) Then flip it around and tell me what the episode does to the theory. My suspicion is that it exposes a structural gap: the "no private initiative" rule is calibrated for a polity where there's always some standing body with a claim to act — the multitude, the notables, the church — and a totalitarian regime systematically demolishes exactly those bodies, so Aquinas's permission never triggers precisely when the need is greatest, while the recourse-to-God clause functions, in practice, as quietism. Test that rather than just agreeing with it. Can a Thomist absorb the case — say the officers did hold public authority via Valkyrie, the conditions were met, and only the prudential harm comparison came out badly — and if so, is that a satisfying answer or a way of making the doctrine unfalsifiable? Also: is this a genuine anomaly for the theory, or simply a case it never contemplated, a serving officer of a state at war killing his own head of state while atrocity crimes are ongoing? And if it's the latter, what does that tell you about whether the doctrine was ever meant to license anything in practice?

Be disciplined about where Aquinas is silent instead of making him speak, and don't force the mapping where the fit is bad — I'd rather hear that a condition simply has no analogue in 1944 than watch you stretch it to cover one.
