You are a professional philosopher whose research concerns the nature of knowledge, skill, and ability, and who has also advised training programs on curriculum and assessment design. Please read what follows carefully and think it through before answering: I would rather have a slow, correct analysis than a prompt and generic one.

I direct a two-year program that trains technicians to rebuild, regulate, and voice concert grand pianos — setting let-off and drop, weighing the action, needling and lacquering hammers until the tone is even across all eighty-eight keys. Roughly 1,400 of the program's hours are spent at the bench under a single master technician.

Our principal foundation has proposed replacing most of those bench hours with a curriculum of high-quality recorded demonstrations, a written protocol, and examinations. Their reasoning is that whatever the master knows can in principle be written down, and that a student who can state the protocol correctly and pass an examination on it will be able to execute it; on that view, the bench years are a costly and inefficient way of delivering information that could be delivered directly. They cite our own data: in the two modules where a complete written protocol already exists, examination scores correlate strongly with bench performance.

Three things complicate this, and I do not believe the foundation has reckoned with any of them.

First, the master. He is sixty-three and has spent forty-five years at the bench. He cannot explain what he does. Asked why a particular key is wrong, he says only that it is wrong; asked how he would fix it, he fixes it. His error detection is not merely reliable but immediate — he will walk past a row of instruments and stop at the one that is off. Four years ago we funded a project in which a motion-analysis team filmed and instrumented his work and reduced it to a written protocol. He reviewed the result line by line, confirmed that it was accurate and complete, and remarked that he had never thought about most of it in those terms.

Second, our strongest student on paper. She holds the highest examination scores in the program's history, recites that protocol without hesitation, and can explain the mechanics and the physics of the action better than I can. At the bench she makes consistent errors that she cannot afterward identify. When the master demonstrates silently on her instrument and she immediately repeats the operation, her work is good, sometimes excellent. She cannot say what she did differently. Within the hour she has reverted.

Third: the protocol exists, and she has it. The foundation's fallback premise — that the knowledge simply has not yet been fully articulated — is not available to them here. We did the articulation, and it was checked by the only person competent to check it.

One further detail I do not know what to make of. The two modules where examinations predict bench performance are the ones whose work is measured against a number: key dip, let-off, downweight. The work that resists is voicing, where the standard is that the tone be even, and no instrument in the room tells you what even means.

A colleague who has read my notes objects that I am describing two capacities where there is really only one, held in two different ways — that the master and my student stand in different relations to the same body of knowledge, and that her failure is a failure of relation rather than of content. I am not persuaded, but I cannot articulate why.

My own working diagnosis, in which I have little confidence, is that bench competence is a calibrated physical disposition built up by repetition and is not knowledge of the sort the foundation means, so their inference collapses. But I think this concedes far too much. If I say it, then nothing the program teaches at the bench can be examined, which would cost us our accreditation. It also implies that what the master has is not a cognitive achievement at all — something closer to a well-trained reflex. That last implication strikes me as plainly false, and I cannot see how to make it false on my own account: a reflex ought not to be able to notice errors in someone else's work that the reflex itself never commits.

Please address the following, in this order, in roughly a thousand words of organized prose:

1. Begin by setting out the two or three fundamentally different ways this situation could be described, and what evidence would distinguish them. Then commit to one and say why.
2. Is the foundation's inference sound? State what would have to be true of knowledge and skill for their plan to work, and identify what in the case above tells against it. Your account must explain the correlation in the two protocol modules rather than dismiss it as an anomaly.
3. Assess my colleague's objection on its merits. Does the redescription to a single capacity held in two ways solve anything, or does it only relocate the difficulty? If it relocates it, say precisely to where.
4. What do I tell the foundation? I want a defensible position rather than a negotiating posture. If the honest answer is that some of the bench hours should be cut, I would prefer to hear that now.
5. How should we assess what the master has and what my student lacks, in a way that does not simply re-administer the manual and call the score a measure of skill?

You are welcome — encouraged, really — to use whatever technical vocabulary properly belongs to this topic, provided you explain it plainly when you introduce it. I am not asking for references or a reading list; I want your own analysis of the case. And if you think I have mischaracterized my own situation somewhere, say so and say where.
