I host a podcast out of our cognitive science department, and next month's guest is a psychologist who's spent about twenty years running lab studies in support of linguistic relativity — color discrimination in speakers whose language has two basic blue terms instead of one, grammatical-gender effects on how people describe inanimate objects, and spatial frames of reference in communities that talk in absolute cardinal directions rather than left/right. She's warm and funny and shes extremely good at absorbing criticism by agreeing with it: her stock move is to say the effects were never supposed to be large, so any complaint about size just shows you read the straw-man version of the hypothesis.

Give me ten to twelve questions in the order I'd actually ask them, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. No biographical softballs — nothing about what got her interested in language, nothing she could answer from a TED talk.

Pressure points I want covered, roughly one question each:

- the verbal-interference result: when a concurrent verbal task wipes out the cross-linguistic difference, she treats it as evidence that language is being recruited online, and skeptics treat the same result as evidence that the task was a language task all along. I need a question that makes her choose a horn instead of holding both, plus a follow-up for whichever way she goes.
- effect size: what has to be true for a few tens of milliseconds to earn the word "relativity" rather than just "people are quicker with labels they use every day"? Where's the line between a real Whorfian effect and a mundane familiarity effect?
- what level the claim is actually at — perception, memory/coding, or something closer to "thinking for speaking" — and whether narrowing it down that far leaves anything the historical Whorf would have recognized as his own view.
- the confound that speakers of two languages also differ in schooling, writing systems, and how comfortable they are being tested; what design genuinely controls for that rather than assuming it away.
- causal direction: maybe the lexical split is downstream of some culturally salient perceptual difference, not upstream of it. Ask her what evidence could distinguish those two.
- one flat-out question about what result would make her abandon the view, and one that lets her name the single finding she thinks is the most robust thing she's ever produced.

Under each question add one line for me: what a good answer sounds like, and what the evasive version sounds like, so I know when to push back live. Also mark which of the questions a hostile audience member is most likely to jump in on, so I can get there first.
