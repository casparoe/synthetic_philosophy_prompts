I am a junior, and I am writing an essay for my school's philosophy journal about the argument that if every truth could be known, then every truth already is known. I follow the argument: suppose some truth p is unknown; then the conjunction "p, and no one knows that p" is itself true; but no one could know that conjunction, since knowing it would require both knowing p and knowing that no one knows p, which cannot be done at once; so there is a truth that could not be known; so not every truth is knowable. What I cannot settle is which of my own judgments about knowability to trust, because they do not seem to fit together. They are as follows.

(A) The number of dust motes hanging in the air of one particular room at one particular instant two centuries ago, in a house that has since burned and left no record. True, never known, never to be known — but knowable: someone with suitable instruments standing in that room could have known it, and nothing about the fact itself stands in the way.

(B) What a particular long-dead person was silently thinking at a moment when they were alone, told no one afterward, kept no diary, and left no trace of any kind. Here I want to say the truth was not knowable even in principle, and I cannot say why, because on paper it looks exactly like (A): a past fact with no surviving evidence and no possible future route to it.

(C) A machine printed the sentence "No one has ever read this sentence" on a slip of paper and then incinerated the slip before anyone or anything observed it. The sentence was true, and it could never have been known, because the only route to knowing it would have made it false.

(D) The ten-to-the-hundredth digit in the decimal expansion of pi. Knowable, I think, even though no physically possible machine will ever carry out the calculation — which means that here I count a method that could never actually be executed as good enough, while in (B) I do not.

(E) The conjunction from the argument, built out of (A). I accept that no one could know it, but I do not feel that this teaches me anything about which truths are knowable, because the impossibility seems to come entirely from the second conjunct, which is about knowledge rather than about the world.

Underneath all of this I suspect an ambiguity I have been exploiting: sometimes by "knowable" I mean knowable by a person like me, and sometimes knowable by any possible being whatsoever, however well equipped. I do not know whether shifting between those senses is legitimate or merely convenient.

My tentative principle is that a truth is knowable just in case some possible knower has some method of arriving at it that would not destroy it. Please test that principle against (A) through (E) and tell me what I should put in its place.
