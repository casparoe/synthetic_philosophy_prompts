So I'm a 2L and this semester I hold the law-student seat on my school's Student Conduct Appeals Board, and one case has quietly eaten my entire week. Facts, as neutrally as I can manage: back in February, four students who were then the majority coalition in the undergraduate Student Senate drew on the Senate's discretionary initiative fund to pay for a chartered bus and two nights of hotel so about forty students could go to a protest at the state capital. The funding rules then in effect said the money had to be spent for the "benefit of the student body" and separately barred using the fund for campus election campaigns; the protest wasn't tied to any campus election, and there was genuinely nothing in writing either way about off-campus political travel. That October a different group had asked for bus money for a comparable trip and been turned down, but nobody got punished for asking. Then in April a rival slate won the Senate elections, and in May the new Senate passed a resolution defining exactly this sort of expenditure as prohibited partisan political spending, made it reach back to the start of the academic year, and directed the student judiciary to claw the money back and suspend the four signatories' funding privileges for two semesters. The judiciary did both. Two of the four are appealing to us. Our code says conduct can only be sanctioned if it violated a rule "in force at the time of the conduct," but it also says the Board may "consider the equities of the case" and may modify any sanction, and the general counsel has told us in writing that ex post facto limits don't bind us directly because we aren't the state.

The reason this is living in my head rent-free is that I just turned in a seminar paper on the retroactivity fight over Nuremberg and Tokyo — the nullum crimen sine lege objection, the fact that the crime-of-aggression count was new and that both tribunals were staffed entirely with judges from the winning powers, and the other side's answers: that aggressive war had already been renounced by interwar treaty so the law wasn't being invented on the spot, and that the acts were monstrous whether or not any statute said so. Half my seminar group says that's our case in miniature — a retroactive rule plus a panel seated by the faction that just beat the people its now judging. The other half says comparing a student senate bus fund to war crimes trials is grotesque, and that if the comparison shows up in something we sign, it discredits us.

What I actually need is the opinion: a short memo I can circulate to the other four board members before Thursday, recommending one concrete disposition — reverse, affirm, or affirm on liability but vacate or cut the sanction — and saying what the Board should do about the retroactivity clause given that the October denial exists and given that the fund chair at the time pushed the disbursement through at the tail end of a very long meeting without the usual line-item review, which the other side will absolutely argue shows everyone involved knew it was improper. It has to take the strongest case against your recommendation seriously rather than strawmanning it, and I think that case is roughly this: the underlying conduct was self-dealing, and letting it go on a drafting gap hands the old coalition a win and tells every future Senate it can raid the fund so long as nobody has written the rule down yet. I also want your judgment on whether and how the Nuremberg/Tokyo analogy belongs in the document — I'm torn between making the reasoning legible to people who've thought about these problems and not writing something that reads like I'm calling my classmates war criminals, so maybe it gets one paragraph, maybe it stays out and only shapes the analysis underneath; argue for whichever you pick. Keep it around two pages in plain language, and if you think the Board should also say something prospective about what "in force at the time" requires — like whether an unpublished past denial can count as fair notice — build that in too.
