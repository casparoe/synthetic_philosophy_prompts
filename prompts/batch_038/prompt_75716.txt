I'm in a philosophy of time seminar this term, and for the closing session each of us brings in a non-philosophical text and argues that it either dramatizes or botches one of the positions we have covered. I have chosen Vonnegut's *Slaughterhouse-Five*, and I want to read it against Gödel's rotating-universe argument rather than against the usual block-universe talking points.

Let me state that argument as I have it, so you can flag where my reconstruction is sloppy. Gödel produced a solution to Einstein's field equations describing a universe filled with rotating matter in which every point lies on a closed timelike curve, so that a traveler could in principle reach any region of their own past. His inference was not that we live in such a universe — he took the observed absence of cosmic rotation to rule that out — but that the mere coherence of such a solution shows an objective lapse of time not to be a necessary feature of a relativistic world, so that our confidence in becoming rests on nothing in the physics. My seminar also stressed, and I would like you to test this, that Gödel did not think the loss of absolute simultaneity in special relativity did the work on its own, since a Minkowski spacetime can be foliated as happily as a Newtonian one; the force of his case comes from the closed curves.

Against that, I want four things. First: is the Tralfamadorian picture actually Gödel's conclusion, or a different thesis in its clothes? The aliens see all moments at once and hold that a corpse is merely in a bad condition at that particular moment — that reads like a claim about how things are experienced, not about whether the manifold admits a global becoming, and the novel's recurring three-word refrain at each death seems to trade on the conflation. Second: Gödel's argument turns on the possibility of *acting* on one's own past, and he treated the resulting absurdity as a mark of how unlike ours such a universe must be. Billy is carried through his life involuntarily, knows in advance when and how he will die, and alters nothing. Is the novel adopting a self-consistency constraint Gödel would have counted as a reductio, or is it simply indifferent to the question? Please be explicit about the gap between "the past cannot be changed" and "there is no objective passage." Third: does the shuffled chronology, and the telegram-style books the Tralfamadorians write, do argumentative work, or only illustrate a thesis the novel states outright?

Fourth, and where I most need help: where must the reading be surrendered? The text leaves genuinely open whether Tralfamadore exists at all or was produced by Billy after his breakdown, with his wife dead and his daughter treating him as a child. If it is a symptom, I am not sure the book can be made to carry Gödel's view at all. Tell me whether that ambiguity weakens or sharpens the comparison, and where the analogy would mislead an audience.

Finally, say what the novel contributes that the argument does not. My hunch is that it offers something like a phenomenology of living without becoming — mourning Dresden in a world where nothing passes — and that this is a datum for the debate rather than decoration. I don't expect quotations, since you cannot consult the text: describe scenes as you remember them and mark anything you are unsure of.
