I teach a philosophy of mind seminar where, every year, I hand out those letter-string puzzles from the Copycat work — abc goes to abd, so what does ijk go to, and what does xyz go to — mostly to get students arguing about what a rule even is. And every year the same thing happens, and I've never been able to find a word for it.

Students who meet ijk first lock onto "change the rightmost letter to its successor," then hit xyz, where the alphabet runs out, and almost always reject wyz as cheating. Students who get xyz cold, with no easy warm-up, produce wyz without hesitation and are genuinely annoyed if you suggest it isn't the answer. Same people, same problem, different order. So far that's just context-dependence, fine. What I keep coming back to is what happens next. Ask the second group to state the rule they used and they state it already-slipped — you go the other way, from the left, because the string sits at the end of the alphabet — and they give reasons for it, reasons about the source pair, that they did not have a minute earlier and would have rejected a minute earlier. Nobody experiences any of this as adjusting anything. Push them and they say the rule was like that all along and they were just the ones who noticed.

Second instance, same shape. On mrrjjj, once the letter-level reading fails to yield anything, the unit slips from letter to run of repeated letters, and then from the run to how long the run is — which is how you get mrrjjjj. Afterwards students point at features of the source string, it's obviously built out of runs, that were equally visible before and were never mentioned before.

So there are two halves and I think they belong together. Whether a given conceptual adjustment happens is set by whether the unadjusted reading produces an answer at all, rather than by how far apart the two concepts are. And once it has happened, the adjustment gets re-founded — retroactively, with reasons, sincerely.

Here's what I dont think it is. Not motivated reasoning or post-hoc rationalization: there's no desire in play and nothing being defended; they aren't attached to wyz and would drop it in a second for something better. Not the principle of charity: they do it to puzzles they invented themselves. Not reflective equilibrium: nothing gets weighed and the whole thing is over before they notice. Not Kripke's rule-following skeptic either, and this is the one that bugs me, becuase Kripke's point is that nothing determines which rule was meant — whereas here the direction of the move is about as determined as anything in cognition, and everybody goes the same way. A colleague over in the law school says it sounds like the absurdity canon, courts reading a statute so it stops producing an unacceptable result and then presenting that reading as the natural one, only done out loud; but that's an institutional practice with a doctrine behind it, and I'm describing something that happens inside one head in about four seconds.

So: is there an established concept for this, in the Copycat literature or anywhere else — philosophy of mind, semantics, psychology? If the nearest fit is one of the above, say which one and say exactly where the fit breaks down. And I'm not after a bare label, I want the edges. Does it need the blocked alternative, or would it fire any time two readings compete? Does the sincerity matter — a student who knowingly picks the answer-yielding reading and says so out loud, same phenomenon or a different one? What about the cases, which are most of them, where the retroactive reasons are actually good reasons? If nothing in the literature covers it, coin me a term, define it, and tell me which of my instances fall outside the definition.
