A friend and I spent the better part of a weekend arguing about when a story stops being legend and starts being history, and he afterwards sent me his position written out in full. It reads:

"The Trojan War is history. Schliemann dug up Troy; the city is real, the destruction layers are real, and the Late Bronze Age setting matches, so the war happened even if Homer dressed it up. King Arthur is legend, and the difference is the paperwork: nothing composed near the time names him, and the earliest accounts we have were written centuries later by people who needed a national hero. The Exodus sits much closer to Arthur than to Troy. The Egyptians kept meticulous records and never mention a mass departure of Israelite slaves, and when a bureaucracy that documented everything stays silent about an event, that silence is evidence the event did not happen. So the rule is simple: archaeology confirming the setting makes a story history, and the silence of the sources makes it legend."

I came away unconvinced but unable to say exactly where, and I suspect I conceded far more than I should have. Go through his summary sentence by sentence and tell me what a historian working on each of these three cases would accept, what they would find overstated, and what is simply wrong. In particular: does establishing that a place and a period are real do any of the work he thinks it does, and what would it take to get from a confirmed setting to a confirmed event? Are the three cases even governed by the same kind of evidence, given how differently texts and material remains survive in each? And is his claim about silence a legitimate inference, or does it quietly assume something about the completeness and the concerns of the Egyptian record that he has not argued for? Finally, tell me whether his rule survives once the errors are corrected; if it does not, sketch what a better rule would look like and what that rule would then imply about the Trojan War, which I suspect is not the verdict he expects.
