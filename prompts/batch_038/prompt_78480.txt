I run a three-person office that acts as neutral broker for two air-ambulance charities whose patches overlap across a mountainous region. Both now run automated dispatch software that decides whether to scramble a helicopter when a call comes in, and in the overlap belt they've been tripping over each other for years — two aircraft turning up to the same road accident, crews burning their monthly hours, and at least one genuinely bad outcome where each system assumed the other was handling it. So they've started bringing us joint protocol proposals: pre-committed rules both dispatch systems would follow, signed in advance, before anybody knows what the next emergency will be.

What I need is a screening procedure my team can actually run on one of those proposals in about half a day. It's me, a policy analyst and a software engineer; between us we have maybe one first-year game theory course, so nothing you write can require us to model anything formally. We get six-ish proposals a year, the joint board meets quarterly, our legal counsel signs off on anything we recommend, and the stakes are the obvious ones, so I'd rather the procedure be slow and conservative than clever.

We've taken to calling what we want a safe Pareto improvement, and I want to be explicit about what I mean by that, because I don't think everyone means the same thing: a protocol that guarantees each charity ends up at least as well off as it would have been running its own default dispatch policy, in every scenario and not just on average, with the joint outcome strictly better in some scenarios, and where that guarantee doesn't depend on either charity trusting the other's model of the situation. The whole point of "safe" is that nobody has to bet on how the other side's algorithm will behave.

Five things keep going wrong for us, and I'd like the procedure built around them rather than around the ideal case. One: the two charities never agree about what the default even was, so "safe relative to your default" can't be checked — each side's analysts produce a different counterfactual and both insist theirs is the honest one. Two: legal wants worst-case safety in every branch, the finance people are happy with expected safety plus a side payment, and I don't know whether the procedure should force one of these or let it be a board decision. Three: when several protocols are all safe, the fight over which one is purely about who gets the bigger share of the gains, and I need the procedure to recognise that moment and stop being technical. Four: once an emergency is actually unfolding, one side sometimes has an incentive to ignore the protocol, and if that's foreseeable, arguably the protocol was never safe to begin with. Five — and this one nobody wants to admit — "better off" is ambiguous for us, because each charity cares both about patient outcomes and about its own metrics (transports per pound, which is what its donors see), and those two pull in different directions on the same protocol.

Also assume we usually can't get numbers. What we get is each side's own simulation output, run on its own assumptions, and the two aren't comparable. The procedure needs a branch for that: what do we do, and what do we demand from a proposer, when safety can't be quantified at all?

Then I'd like you to run the procedure on three live-ish proposals, and be blunt about what comes out.

The first is a stand-down rule: both systems share a live readiness map, and in any sector where one charity's nearest available aircraft is more than four minutes closer to the incident, the other's software doesn't scramble. Both sides' analysts say this saves wasted scrambles and leaves response times unchanged or better. I'm suspicious because the four-minute comparison uses each charity's own self-reported readiness, and because in some branches the standing-down charity's aircraft would in fact have arrived first for reasons the other side can't see.

The second is a rotation rule for the overlap belt: a shared queue assigns each call to whichever charity is next in rotation, closeness ignored. Both finance directors say expected cost per patient falls about fifteen percent and expected transports rise for both. But if an avalanche or a coach crash lands on one charity's home side, that charity loses patients it would have treated under its default and its crews sit idle, and the rotation can't be adjusted mid-event without breaking the commitment.

The third is the one I've been pushing and I expect you'll kill it. Before signing, each charity deposits its full dispatch decision rules with my office in escrow; we compute, scenario by scenario, what each would have done with no agreement at all, and the protocol is written conditional on that computed default. I like it because it seems to end the baseline argument for good. I can already half-see the objection — that depositing your default lets the other side tune against it, and tune how its own default looks before escrow — but tell me straight whether there's anything left of the idea or whether it's self-undermining.

For each proposal I want the procedure's verdict in one of four forms: pass, fail, cannot be assessed with what we have (plus what to demand next), or not a technical question, hand it to the board because it's about the split. And across all of it, mark where the procedure is really a judgment call wearing a procedural costume, and say what it structurally can't settle.

One thing I'd ask you to be careful about: keep clearly separate what's settled in this area from what people who actually work on it disagree about. I don't want a contested choice smuggled in as the textbook answer. If your procedure takes a side on something genuinely disputed — I suspect worst-case versus expected safety is one, and what the right reference point for "the default" is may be another — say that you're taking a side, and say what the other position would answer instead.

Format: please write the whole thing as continuous prose, like an email or a letter. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. I'm going to paste it into a note to the two chief executives and I'd have to strip all of that out anyway. Ordering the steps and numbering them inside sentences is completely fine.
