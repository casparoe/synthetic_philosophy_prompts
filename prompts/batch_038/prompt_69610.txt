I am preparing a position paper for an interdisciplinary workshop on the foundations of AI safety, and before I circulate it I should like a consistency audit of the commitments I intend to state publicly. What follows is my position in something close to final form. The question I am putting to you is narrow: does it hang together?

(1) Safety, properly so called, is a universal property. A deployed system is safe only if it satisfies its specification on every input it may encounter; the proportion of inputs on which it satisfies it is beside the point. A system that fails once in a million inputs is not nine hundred ninety-nine thousand nine hundred ninety-nine parts safe. It is unsafe.

(2) The set of inputs a system may encounter is not fixed in advance. Much of what makes a general system worth building is that it gets put to uses nobody anticipated, and the environment it operates in shifts under it.

(3) Benchmarks matter. Average-case performance over a representative distribution of tasks is the best evidence we actually possess about any system, and I would not discount it merely because it is not a proof.

(4) Under uncertainty the correct rule minimizes expected cost. I reject maximin as a general principle of rational choice, and I am no Rawlsian about distributive justice.

(5) Some outcomes are not compensable. Where a failure would be irreversible and would foreclose what comes after it, I do not accept any nonzero probability of it, however small, and however favorable the expected cost of accepting it.

(6) Two case verdicts I want to be judged by. First: given a filter certified to block every category on a list I have compiled, and a filter with no certification but excellent measured performance, I deploy the certified one and regard the uncertified one as simply unsafe, whatever its score. Second: I will not accept a design whose safety depends on being kept inside an operating envelope enumerated in advance. A system that is safe only within the limits of my imagination is not safe; my imagination is not a safety mechanism.

(7) Inductive warrant does not travel. A claim about how a system will behave that rests on observed frequencies has no force in a domain where the future need not resemble the past. A safety claim without a guarantee is a prediction, and a prediction of that sort is not evidence of safety.

(8) I accept the no-free-lunch results in learning theory, and I take them to establish that generalization is always purchased with assumptions about the domain: no method succeeds across all possible environments.

(9) A methodological requirement I impose on my own view: it must be action-guiding. It should tell a regulator what to permit, not merely what one might wish were true.

Please audit the set. I want to know which of these genuinely pull against one another and which pairs only look inconsistent but survive inspection — I expect several apparent conflicts to dissolve, and I want to be told plainly which ones do and in virtue of what. Rank the genuine conflicts by severity rather than listing them side by side as equally damaging. For each, identify the cheapest revision that restores consistency and say what that revision costs me, including which of the surviving commitments it weakens. Then tell me which single revision would do the most work overall. I am not asking for a survey of the literature or for references of any kind; I want the reasoning laid out, and I want it to engage these formulations as stated rather than a generic restatement of the debate between worst-case and average-case standards.
