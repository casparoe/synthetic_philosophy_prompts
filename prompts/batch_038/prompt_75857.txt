I am putting together the opening handout for a graduate seminar on the foundations of statistical mechanics, and my brief is to produce a single sheet that says, before we read any candidate accounts, what such an account has to be able to do in order to count as an explanation of the direction of time rather than a redescription of it. The seminar keeps foundering because people argue past one another: one camp says an account that posits a special low-entropy beginning has merely relocated the puzzle, the other says an account that does not has failed to explain why we are not living in a fluctuation, and a colleague of mine who works in condensed matter says the whole dispute is idle because in the laboratory the arrow is just a matter of boundary conditions and driving. Please take up the role of a philosopher of physics who has thought hard about what would count as a genuine explanation here. I want the desiderata for a theory that could be defended in a viva as a complete account of why physical processes are temporally directed — not merely a self-consistent story about why entropy rises in this or that system, but one that also underwrites the ordinary asymmetries we lean on: that we have records of the past and none of the future, that we can intervene on the future but not on the past, that causes precede their effects, that radiation goes out rather than converging in.

Two constraints on the list. First, it must be usable against accounts that refuse to postulate a privileged initial condition for the whole universe as well as against accounts that embrace one, so any requirement that can only be failed by a theory on one side of that divide is not a desideratum but a sectarian demand, and I want those labelled rather than smuggled in. Second, the requirements must bite on the standard objections and on the messy cases, not only on the textbook ones: velocity reversal, recurrence on absurdly long timescales, the fact that fine-grained entropy is constant under Hamiltonian evolution so that any increase is a function of how we describe the system, the question of which macrovariables make a coarse-graining the natural one, and the behaviour of small systems observed for short intervals, where fluctuations are not negligible but routine. Where you believe two or more of the five cannot be satisfied together, say so inside the items themselves rather than in a separate discussion.

Give me exactly five numbered desiderata and nothing else: no preamble, no summary, no parting remark, and no suggestions for further reading. Each item should be tight — at most three sentences — naming the requirement, giving the concrete consideration or counterexample that shows it to be a real demand rather than a taste, and closing with a square-bracketed tag reading either non-negotiable, negotiable, or camp prejudice; add a second tag, decisive, to the single item you think eliminates the most candidates. You may reason privately as long as you like, but the deliverable is the list alone. On the epistemic side I would much rather have five well-motivated requirements with an honest account of their status than a confident-looking checklist: where practitioners who know this material genuinely disagree about whether a demand is legitimate, say so plainly in the item and do not write as though your own camp's requirement were consensus, but where something is settled — that recurrence is a theorem whose timescales make it empirically irrelevant, that the constancy of fine-grained entropy forces any account of increase to say something about description or about initial conditions — treat it as settled and do not hedge it. Write throughout in British English.
