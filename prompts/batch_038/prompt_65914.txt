I TA a 75-minute intro ethics section, 24 students, mostly first-years, and next Tuesday is the utilitarianism-and-its-discontents day. They've all read the couple of pages where Nozick floats the utility monster, and when I tried to just talk it through last semester it went exactly how you'd expect: about half the room waved it off as a fake creature, like the ontological argument's evil twin — "nobody actually gets a hundred times the pleasure from a cookie, so the case is rigged and we can move on." I want them to stop being able to say that. Not because I think the objection is decisive, but because the interesting question is what you do once the case is granted rather than dissolved, and they never get there when they can dismiss the setup. So I'd like an in-class game that makes them live inside a fair procedure that produces the monster's allocation, where the dismissal isn't available to them.

Here's the shape I've got in mind, but please push back on any of it that won't work. Six groups of four, four rounds, each group dividing up a pot of chips every round (I have a jar of poker chips I borrowed from my roommate and a pack of index cards, so materials should stay in that register; points tracked on a ledger that has zero effect on their grade). Two design constraints matter a lot to me. First, the allocation rule has to be chosen by the students themselves at the start, behind a veil — everyone gets a sealed envelope containing their private utility schedule for all four rounds, they read their own, and only after the rule is fixed do they learn who's holding the monster card. I want the deck built so the monster role is spread around the room rather than landing on the same three loud kids, because my hunch is that the dismissers are precisely the people who've never held the card. Second, honesty has to be auditable: any player can pay points to force the round's monster to reveal its schedule, and the reveal always confirms it, so "he's lying about how much he wants it" gets closed off at a real cost rather than by fiat. Tell me whether you think the audit should be cheap or expensive and what each choice does to the lesson, because I can't figure out which way it cuts. Also — and this is the part I care about most — I want the pot to shrink each round while the monster's marginal utility per chip stays flat. The ordinary players should have diminishing returns so that in round one handing over most of the chips looks almost reasonable, but the version of the objection I actually want them to feel isn't the infinite-monster one, it's that the monster's share keeps growing even as there's less to go around and everybody else's total is sliding toward nothing.

So: full rules, the envelope deck (enough detail that I could actually build 24 of them Sunday night), the payoff ledger, a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75 minutes, and a debrief that maps specific moments from the rounds onto the machinery underneath them — sum-ranking versus distribution, whether interpersonal comparison of utility is really doing anything, the each-counts-for-one slogan, and the escape routes (cap the monster, deny comparability, bite the bullet) with a line on what each one costs. Don't let the phrase appear on any handout until the debrief, and no lecture beforehand. Then the two honest bits: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — I'm already worried about guilt-driven redistribution, the monster chickening out and giving chips back, groups short-circuiting the whole thing by agreeing to split evenly in round one, and the audit never getting used because nobody wants to spend their own points — and how I should handle each of those in the moment without breaking the illusion. And finally, which feature of the thought experiment this activity flatly cannot reproduce, so I know what I'm going to have to supply with my own mouth afterwards.
