The thing I keep running into:

Malthus. 1798, he argues population multiplies itself while the food supply only grows by addition, so sooner or later subsistence has to bite down on population. Two centuries on, world population is up maybe eightfold, calories per person are way up, nobody in Britain is being culled by famine. And the standard defence I hear is not "he was wrong." It's: the mechanism he identified was real, it just got masked by things that didn't exist yet and that he couldn't have known about — Haber-Bosch, semi-dwarf wheat, fossil-fuel-powered yields — and the mask is finite, so the prediction still stands, just later than advertised.

Same shape with Ehrlich. He loses the famous 1980 metals bet to Julian Simon outright — all five prices fall over the decade, he mails the check — and the response from his side isn't retreat, it's: metals were the wrong index, prices don't capture ecological damage, and anyway that particular window happened to be one where the temporary offsets were still working. The mechanism stands.

Here's what I actually want named, and its narrower than "wrong people won't admit they're wrong":

1. The rescuing factor is *real*. Not an invented entity, not an unverifiable force. Haber-Bosch happened. Fracking happened. You can point at it. So the usual complaint — that the auxiliary assumption has no independent support and is pure ad hocery — just doesn't land.
2. But the factor is identified only after the predicted failure, and it was never in the theory. Nobody said in advance "this will be offset by X for a while."
3. The core claim never gets amended, at least in the version I hear. Only the expected onset date moves. The theory gets less specific about *when* and no less specific about *what*.
4. And the deferral is booked as a credit, not a debit. Defenders talk like the theory is now *more* impressive for having seen through the boom to the constraint underneath it, and like the skeptics are the naive ones — they mistook a temporary offset for a permanent escape. Every missed date seems to make the theory look deeper.
5. Strictly it isn't unfalsifiable. You could falsify it in principle, by showing the offset is self-renewing. But in practice the burden flips onto the skeptic to prove a negative about the future, which nobody can do, so the theory never actually faces a test it can lose.

So: does this configuration have a name? I know the neighbourhood — I know about auxiliary hypotheses and the Popper/Lakatos material on theories protecting themselves, and I've heard "moving the goalposts" and "self-sealing" used loosely. But none of those seem to capture the part that makes this one interesting to me, which is that each individual rescue is true, is responsive to evidence, and looks like good scientific practice when you hold it up alone. It's the pattern across many of them that's pathological. That's the bit I want carved out.
