I sit on the ethics committee of my university's teaching hospital, and we have been asked to review the brief-intervention protocol governing how clinicians talk with patients about behavior change: tobacco, alcohol, medication adherence, opioid tapering. The protocol is built on motivational interviewing. In outline, clinicians are trained not to argue for the change, not to recite risks or statistics the patient has not asked about, and not to treat patient pushback as an obstacle to be overcome but as a signal to change tactics. The documented aim is to elicit and reflect back the patient's own avowals in favor of change — what the training literature calls change talk — on the working assumption that a patient moved by reasons she articulates herself sustains the change longer than one persuaded by the clinician's. The same institution, however, runs informed consent and patient education on something close to the opposite principle: a patient who has been given accurate risk information and remains unmoved is recorded as non-adherent, the remedy is more information restated more forcefully, and the clinician's instinct to correct the patient is treated there as a professional duty rather than a reflex to be suppressed.

I want a philosophical reconstruction of what each practice presupposes about practical reasons, not a description of either. Does the counseling practice presuppose internalism, and if so which version: the crude one on which a reason must attach to some existing element of the agent's motivational set, or the Williams-style one on which it suffices that the agent would be moved after a sound deliberative route from that set? The difference is not academic here, since evocation looks like an attempt to run that deliberation on the patient's behalf. Does the consent practice presuppose externalism, or only a thesis about how information produces motivation? Can one institution house both without incoherence, or is there a real contradiction about whose reasons count and when — and if the latter, which practice has to give?

Then consider the deflationary reading on which neither practice is committed to anything in the metaphysics of reasons, both being applied claims about what causally works — reactance, rapport, self-perception — and tell me whether that reading is genuinely available or whether it concedes the interesting question. Finally, the protocol fixes the target behavior before the conversation begins. Does evoking a patient's reasons toward an end the clinician has already chosen amount to manufacturing internal reasons, and what does that presuppose about whether reasons can be produced by reflection rather than only discovered?

Throughout, please separate what the practices strictly entail from what they merely sit comfortably with, mark the places where they are underdetermined so that rival theories would license them equally, and close by saying what would have to change in the protocol's wording, and in the consent practice, were we to adopt the rival account in each case. I am after a defended reconstruction with the reasoning exposed, not a survey of the positions and not a bibliography.
