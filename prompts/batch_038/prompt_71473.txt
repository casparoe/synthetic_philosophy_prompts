I teach a third-year/MA philosophy of science course on evidence and confirmation and I'm writing the final. The raven paradox is the one topic where I can predict what every answer will say before I open a single script: Nicod's criterion plus the equivalence condition, Hempel's own diagnosis that we're smuggling in background knowledge, the Bayesian line that the white shoe confirms by a negligible amount. All of it true, all of it worthless as an assessment, because none of it requires the student to have thought about anything.

So: write me one essay question, about 50 minutes of a two-hour paper, worth 25 marks, built around an argument I made up. Present it to the students as an anonymous argument — don't attribute it to anybody real, and don't add references anywhere in the question, the model answer, or the marking scheme. I dont want a question that's only answerable by someone who's read the secondary literature, and I don't want scripts padded with citations. It has to be fully self-contained.

The argument goes roughly like this; polish the wording but keep the shape:

1. A report like "this is a white shoe" carries information about ravens only if the thing reported on was a candidate raven in the first place — only if I've already narrowed the population I'm sampling from to birds, or to things that could plausibly be ravens.
2. A shoe isn't in that population, so the report carries no information about ravens.
3. Yet "all ravens are black" and "all non-black things are non-ravens" are logically equivalent, and they tell you to sample from different populations: the first says go look at ravens, the second says go look at non-black things.
4. So evidence can't simply attach to the content of a hypothesis. Two logically equivalent hypotheses can differ in what counts as evidence for them. The equivalence condition is false.

I want the question to make students do three things, and I want it obvious to them what those three things are:

(a) Locate the mistake. Step 4 denies the equivalence condition, but what steps 1–2 actually establish is something else — something about the gap between "E confirms H" and "E is a sensible way of testing H." They should say precisely which principle would have to be denied for the conclusion to follow, and why the sampling point doesn't deliver that denial.

(b) Build a case where shoe-style evidence does the heavy lifting: a concrete scenario, described so a non-philosopher could follow it, in which walking around inspecting non-black non-ravens is the best available way to establish that all ravens are black, and the confirmation is anything but negligible. They should name the background premise that's doing the work.

(c) The hard part. Build a scenario in which observing one more black raven *lowers* your confidence that all ravens are black. Then explain why that doesn't vindicate the anonymous argument — why it isn't a case of positive instances failing to confirm — and what it does show about Nicod's criterion. (There's a classic result along these lines, I think it's Good's, but I'd much rather they constructed one from scratch than recited it, so don't name it in the question or the answer.)

Then give me a model answer of around 750 words and a marking scheme totalling 25, with the marks distributed across named must-hit points. The part I care about most: a short list of moves that should cap a student at a B or below even when the script is fluent and well organised. I'm thinking of things like invoking "background knowledge" as a label instead of identifying which premise is doing the work, or claiming that Bayesianism dissolves the paradox rather than relocating it into the priors — but come up with your own, and make each one specific enough that two different markers would apply it the same way. Same for the deductions: tell me what costs marks and how much.

Keep the exam question itself in the register of a real exam paper. A student who's done the reading but hasnt thought about it should come out with a mediocre mark; a student who has thought about it should be able to do well without remembering a single name.
