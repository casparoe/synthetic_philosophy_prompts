I chair the acquisitions and ethics subcommittee of a mid-sized regional museum, and in ninety days our board votes on a collections policy governing what we may buy, hang, and retain once an attribution turns out to be wrong. Our registrar keeps forwarding me the usual clippings — van Meegeren's Vermeers, the German couple who supplied the modernist fakes, and now the argument about whether the output of a generative model working in a dead painter's manner is a forgery at all — and my committee is split three ways. One faction holds that the whole question is about fraud and that everything beyond fraud is sentiment; a second holds that a work's value lies entirely in what hangs on the wall and that provenance is the art market's private superstition; a third wants known fakes exhibited beside the originals with an explanatory placard, on the ground that the comparison teaches visitors to look. Before we adjudicate between them I want the specification rather than the arguments. Take the purpose of the theory to be narrow but demanding: it must tell a curator, case by case, whether and why an object's standing changes when its history of production is revised, and what that change does to the reasons for acquiring, conserving, and exhibiting it.

Write the desiderata. I want a numbered list of what any adequate account of the value of originals has to deliver, and for each item: (a) the requirement itself, (b) the concrete case that motivates it, and (c) whether you regard it as non-negotiable or as a demand that in fact belongs to one camp's prejudices. The list must handle at least the situations my committee keeps tripping over, which are these:

1. A forgery whose surface is, on the best connoisseurship available, indistinguishable from the original — together with the awkward fact that nothing perceivable changed when the fraud was exposed, although our assessment of the object did.
2. A forgery that is better than the originals it imitates and that fooled experts for decades.
3. An honest studio copy executed by an assistant as part of ordinary workshop production, which we mislabel as autograph three centuries later — the misdescription is ours, and no one at the time deceived anyone.
4. A bronze cast from the artist's own plaster, made forty years after his death under licence from his foundation, in an authorized edition of twenty-five.
5. A reconstruction of a destroyed painting assembled from surviving written descriptions and one old photograph, where there is nothing left to compare it against.
6. A canvas bearing a sixteenth-century ground, a nineteenth-century repaint, and a twentieth-century cleaning campaign, so that most of what a visitor now sees was not laid down by the artist's hand.
7. A work genuinely made in the right period and the right circle, with no intention to deceive, which our own documentation now wrongly reports as a modern fake — and which everyone consequently treats as one.
8. An artist who forges his own early work, deceiving the market about date rather than authorship.
9. Model-generated work trained on a painter's corpus, produced with no intent to deceive and no claim of authorship, yet indistinguishable from a lost original.
10. A forged manuscript in a composer's hand, passed off as an earlier draft — and, for contrast, a performance of the resulting score that nobody would dream of calling a forgery. The theory must explain the asymmetry.

Tell me also which distinctions the theory may not collapse: nominal authenticity against the expressive or self-true sense of the same word; the work against the object that carries it; aesthetic standing against market value against documentary interest; forgery against copy, pastiche, reproduction, and homage. For each, say what false verdict the collapse produces somewhere in the list above. Then the part I most want: if some subset of these requirements cannot be jointly satisfied — if, for example, demanding that discovery changes what the work is worth aesthetically, while also demanding that no perceivable property changed and that the objection cannot be reduced to fraud, is asking for too much of any single theory — say so plainly and name the requirement that ought to be sacrificed. Close with two or three sentences each on how the two most frequently invoked answers fare against the list: the account on which the original's advantage lies in the achievement it manifests and a forgery misrepresents that achievement, and the account on which the difference stays aesthetic because knowing that the two can be told apart tells us there is a distinction to be learned to see. That is all the scorecard I need; the committee will do its own. End your reply by naming the single requirement from your list that, in your judgment, rules out the largest number of otherwise respectable theories, in the form \answer{R# — short label}.
