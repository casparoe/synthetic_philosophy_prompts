I have no training in philosophy. I run a small boatyard on the coast, and I've been turning this over mostly on winter evenings for the better part of a year, ever since I read a library book that mentioned Aristotle and the sea battle. I've now written the argument out as carefully as I can, and what I want is a straight diagnosis, not a tour of the literature.

Let S be the sentence "There will be a sea battle in the harbour tomorrow," written on a card today and put in a drawer, where it will sit unread until tomorrow evening.

1. S is either true or false. Nothing sits between those two, and this holds today exactly as it will hold tomorrow. Call this **Bivalence**.
2. If S is true, there will be a sea battle tomorrow; if S is false, there will not be. A sentence is true because of how things are, never the other way round. Call this **Correspondence**.
3. Truth is not the sort of thing one can act on. If S is true today, then nothing I do today or tomorrow can make it the case that S was false today; the most I could ever manage is to bring about a situation in which S was false all along and I was simply wrong about it. Call this **Untouchability**.
4. Take the first branch of (1). If S is true, then it is not within my power to prevent the sea battle, since preventing it would require making S false today, and (3) rules that out.
5. Take the second branch. If S is false, then it is not within my power to bring the sea battle about, for the same reason.
6. By (1), one of the two branches holds. So either way, what happens in the harbour tomorrow is not within my power.
7. Steps (1) through (6) never mention the harbour, tomorrow, or battles. Substitute "I will sign the invoice on Friday" and every step goes through unchanged. So nothing I do is within my power.
8. The same substitution works for the past. "I sold the old slipway in 2019" is true, and by (3) nothing available to me now makes it the case that I did not. Generalising: at no time has anything been within anyone's power, and nobody has ever had a choice about anything, which would seem to make blame and praise a kind of noise.

The conclusion I cannot accept is (8). What disturbs me is not that it is bleak but that it is cheap: the argument reaches it using only logic and a description of what truth is, with no physics, no deity, and no appeal to causation anywhere in it. An argument that gets that far on that little fuel has almost certainly picked up something it was not entitled to, and I have failed for a year to find where.

Three escapes have already been offered to me and I do not think any of them work.

- Deny (1): say S is neither true nor false. But "S is neither true nor false" is itself a sentence about tomorrow, so by the same reasoning it too is neither, and I lose the very claim I wanted. And if nothing is settled about tomorrow, I cannot even say the battle may happen, because the word *may* would have to be made true by something.
- Deny (3): say I can affect the truth of S after all, by making it false. But making it false just is bringing about a situation in which S was false all along, which is precisely what (3) says is the most I could manage. So I think this objection concedes my premise.
- Say I have confused truth with necessity. I don't think I have. (6) and (8) are claims about what is within my power, not about what is metaphysically necessary, and (3) is a premise about power rather than about necessity. Nowhere did I move from *it is true that* to *it is necessary that*.

So: tell me which step fails, and why it looked so innocuous when I wrote it. If you think no step fails and the argument is sound, say that plainly instead and tell me what a person is supposed to do with (8). And if you think one of my steps is a muddled way of stating something that can be stated correctly, restate it correctly and then tell me whether the corrected version still goes through.

Two further things. I would most like to keep (2) and (3), because giving up (2) seems to mean that the world stops making my sentences true, and I don't see how to think at all without that. So tell me what it would cost to keep them and still hold that tomorrow is open — whether openness requires abandoning the idea that a sentence's truth comes from the world, or only abandoning some particular version of it. And please commit. I want the position you would defend and your reasons for it, not a balanced list of the standard replies with the pros and cons of each left for me to sort through. If I've mis-stated the problem in some way that makes it easier or harder than it really is, tell me that too.
