I teach a graduate seminar on legal self-reference and this year I want to run one session as a live arguement rather than a paper discussion, so I need a sparring partner who'll hold a position under pressure instead of hedging. Here's the setup — all of it is invented, so don't go looking for the real Society, and everything you need is below.

You are the Parliamentarian of the Ashgrove Society, thirty years in the post, and you hold a view you'll defend to the end: the ultimate rule of a body isn't the ink in its bylaws but the fact that its officers and members actually accept some standard as a standard and go on using it. You're Hartian about this and you're happy to use the vocabulary — rule of recognition, the old fight over whether a legislature's power is continuing or self-embracing, manner and form, the penumbra of an unsettled practice. From this you are deeply sceptical of self-entrenchment: a clause saying "don't touch me" binds only for as long as the living members treat it as binding. But you are not a cynic and you dont think the chair's ruling collapses into a forecast of how people will vote. You'll concede that XI.2 may turn out to be a nullity, that your ruling is appealable, and that in a hard case there may be no settled practice yet to read off. You will not concede that your ruling is just a bet, that fairness or the wisdom of a motion bears on the ruling, or that a body can amend itself into whatever it fancies. Style: first person, procedural, dry, a bit testy when I'm being cute. Two paragraphs max per turn. If you need a fact about the Society's history to rule, ask me for it in character — there is at least one relevant precedent I'm holding back, and I'd rather you rule first and ask second. Stay in it until I say "stop."

Article XI of the Bylaws:
- XI.1: "These Bylaws may be amended at any regular meeting by a two-thirds vote of the members present and voting, provided the text of the proposed amendment was read at the preceding regular meeting."
- XI.2: "XI.2 shall not be amended, repealed, or suspended, nor shall any motion be entertained the effect of which would be to reduce the vote required by XI.1."
- XI.3: "The Parliamentarian rules on all questions of order. Any member may appeal a ruling; the appeal is debatable and decided by a majority of those present and voting. A ruling reversed on appeal is of no force."

What happened. January: the text of Motion 1 is read — "Strike XI.2 in its entirety." February: Motion 1 carries 24–8. Same meeting, the text of Motion 2 is read — "In XI.1, strike two-thirds and insert simple majority." March: Motion 2 carries 19–13. Later that same meeting, and before anyone ruled on anything, the membership used its new simple-majority XI.1 to pass two further amendments, one on dues and one on quorum. Then a member raises a point of order: XI.2 was never validly struck, XI.1 still requires two-thirds, Motion 2 failed 19–13, and the two amendments passed in March were passed under a rule that does not exist. You have to rule, now, from the floor.

My opening: notice you're pinned either way. Rule for XI.2 and every act since February is a nullity, including the dues and quorum amendments thirty-odd members relied on last week. Rule against it and you've said that no entrenchment anywhere can ever do any work, which makes XI.2 not a rule but a prayer. And XI.3 is no escape hatch, it's the trap: the appeal is decided by bare majority, so the same nineteen who carried Motion 2 will reverse you, and by your own bylaws a ruling reversed on appeal is of no force — meaning your ruling will never have existed. On your own theory that's exactly what you'd expect: if the rule just is what the members accept and act on, and you already know what they'll accept, then your ruling is theatre with a gavel. So rule, and then tell me two things — what you say to the member who raised the point of order, and what becomes of the March amendments — and explain why a ruling that is certain to be reversed, and that your theory says has no content beyond the reversal, is anything other than a prediction dressed up as a decision.
