For nineteen years I have worked at a bakery and café on our high street, and this spring the owner — who is seventy-eight, tired, and has no one to sell to — is handing the business over to the people who work in it as a worker co-operative. I have been asked to give the opening remarks at the launch supper on the fourteenth, before the vote on the draft rules. I should say plainly that I have no training in any of this: I left school at sixteen, and the only reading I have done is the draft bylaws at my kitchen table, several times, in the small hours. What I keep getting stuck on is something nobody in the room seems to treat as a difficulty. The draft says the co-operative will determine its own membership, which sounds sensible until you notice that the people determining who belongs are entitled to do so only because they happen already to be inside. It is like the first page of a rulebook having to be written by people the rulebook has not authorised. Whatever we settle on the fourteenth will have been settled by a group no prior rule empowered to settle it, and once it is settled the only route back is to petition the very people the rule favours. I do not yet see how to say that out loud without sounding like a man complaining about his own luck, because I come out ahead under two of the three options on the ballot.

The room will be about twenty-five people: everyone who works here, the two van drivers who are on the agency's books rather than ours and who have been asked along as guests, the founder, our solicitor, the development officer from the regional federation who has shepherded a hundred of these conversions and who knows the standard principles — open membership, one member one vote, democratic control by those who do the work — well enough to spot instantly if I invoke them without following where they lead, and a handful of long-standing customers. On the paper there are three options. The first gives one vote to everyone over eighteen on our own payroll with twelve months' service, which leaves out the two drivers, leaves out the sixteen-year-old who has done our Saturdays for three years and starts full-time in September, and leaves out the woman who built the wholesale side and left four years ago to open her own place. The second gives a vote after three months to anyone working eight hours a week for the business, through the agency or not, and admits the girl at eighteen. The third restricts membership to those who have signed the personal guarantee on the bank loan and weights their votes by hours, which brings the founder in heavily — he is guaranteeing it personally and will not work a single shift — and pushes everyone else out. Two of these options would bind people who are sitting at the tables that night, and who will still be driving the vans and still be working Saturdays next week, to rules they had no hand in making.

What I want from you is the speech itself: about seven hundred words, written to be read aloud from the page by someone who is not a natural speaker, in short plain sentences, with no jargon, no named philosophers, no quotations from anybody, and no toastmaster's cheer. It has to do three things at once. It must move us into the vote, ending by stating the three options in a sentence each in the order they appear on the paper and then saying what happens next. It must say the thing everyone would rather I left alone — that two of these options leave people in this room bound by a decision they were not allowed to take, and that our good feelings about them do not repair that — without deciding the question for anyone, since I have promised the committee I will not indicate which way I am voting. And it must offer something better than consolation: I want to propose that whatever boundary we draw on the fourteenth is reopened after two years by a review in which the people it excluded hold seats and votes, and I want the speech to admit without flinching that those people have no power whatever to demand such a review, so it has to be conceded tonight or never. Do not let the speech call us a family, tell anyone they are valued while explaining that they cannot vote, or suggest that a vote once taken makes the outcome finally legitimate. Please mark two or three places where I should stop and let the room catch up, and afterwards, separately, give me two sentences I can say if one of the drivers or a customer asks from the floor whether I am telling them to vote for their inclusion — an answer that is neither evasive nor a decision made on everyone else's behalf.
