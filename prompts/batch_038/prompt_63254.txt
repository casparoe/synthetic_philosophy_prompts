I've been in a reading group for about a year now — me (software, longtime LW/SlateStarCodex-adjacent lurker), one actual archaeologist who does survey work, and two history grad students — and we've ground through the whole societal-collapse canon: Tainter's version where complexity is a problem-solving investment that hits diminishing returns and gets shed, Diamond's version where societies environmentally commit suicide and the difference is how they respond, and then the Questioning Collapse camp saying that the whole frame is broken because societies don't fail, they reorganize, and the word does real damage when it's pointed at places where the descendants are still alive. Nobody has moved an inch in twelve months. My archaeologist friend and the grad students read the same site reports and come out with opposite conclusions, and every time one side produces a case the other side says that case is exactly why the word should be retired. So I want a diagnosis of the disagreement itself, not a survey of the positions and not a reading list — I've read the standard stuff and I don't want you guessing at page numbers.

Here's the thing I built that made me want to ask, because it might be a clue or it might be me rationalizing. I got the group to code fourteen cases (Late Bronze Age eastern Mediterranean, the Classic Maya lowlands, Rapa Nui, Chaco, Cahokia, Angkor, the western Roman provinces, Norse Greenland, Tiwanaku, Wari, Great Zimbabwe, a few others) on six seperate proxies, and for each proxy I recorded a rough onset date and a trough date: settlement counts / population estimates, last dated monumental construction, presence of long-distance imports, dated texts or inscriptions, number of tiers in the settlement hierarchy, and whether a successor polity claims the same capital or legitimacy. What came out is that the proxies almost never bottom out inside the same 50 years. Texts and monuments often stop long before the population proxies dip. Imports vanish while local settlement holds steady. In a couple of cases the hierarchy flattens but the total occupied area doesn't move at all. Which makes me suspect 'collapse' isn't a natural kind but an index — a weighted average of proxies over an arbitary time window, applied to an unstated unit of analysis (a dynasty? a city? a pottery tradition? a language community?). If that's right, the two camps mostly differ in weights and units, neither side states theirs, and the argument is unfalsifiable-by-design because it's really a stipulation fight dressed up as an empirical one. My second hypothesis, which I like less but can't shake: there's a genuinely non-empirical stake underneath — the word assigns blame and denies continuity to living people — and the proxy fight is just where that stake gets laundered into something that looks like a technical dispute.

So: which is it, or is it a third thing I haven't thought of — e.g. Tainter answering why complex societies lose complexity while the critics answering what we should say about the past given who's listening, with the shared word 'collapse' doing all the confusion? I want reasons drawn from what each side actually argues, not vibes, and I want an honest answer about what would count as progress: a formal criterion, an agreed unit of analysis, some new class of evidence, a change of question, or nothing at all because one side is doing ethics and the other is doing measurement and they will never meet. And please flag how confident you are in each of the main claims you make, and single out the one you're least sure of. Especially push back on my index idea if it looks too neat — I have a nagging worry that I designed the spreadsheet to produce the answer I already wanted, since desynchronized proxies would let me dismiss both camps at once, which is obviously a suspiciously comfortable place to land.
