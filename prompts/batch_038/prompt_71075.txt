I'm trying to write down what an account of emergence has to actually deliver before I trust anyone's claim that X is emergent, and I want the spec, not the candidates. The case driving it: a lab grows sheets of cultured heart cells. One cell fires erratically; a sheet of a million settles into a stable reentrant spiral. Zapping any single cell does nothing to the spiral, but killing 5% of the sheet relocates the spiral's anchor point and it stays there for hours. Nobody can currently derive the spiral from single-cell channel kinetics plus geometry, and the group that simulates it admits they hand-tune a coupling constant to get it.

Give me the requirements list: what a theory of emergence has to get right about this case and about the concept in general — the distinctions it can't collapse, the verdicts it has to be able to return (including "not emergent, and here's what would show it"), the results from physics or biology it can't contradict — with the consideration behind each, and a mark for which are non-negotiable vs. which are just the hobby of one camp. Tell me which single requirement does the most damage to the popular accounts, and whether any subset is jointly unsatisfiable; I suspect demanding that emergence be gradable and demanding a clean epistemic/ontic cut can't both be met, so argue with me if Im wrong. Crucially: what would count as evidence *against* an emergence claim like the spiral one? If your desiderata can't say, say so plainly — I think that's a hole in most of the literature and I want to know whether it's fixable. Then in a couple of sentences each, how a multiple-realizability-based nonreductive physicalism, a strong emergentist view with real downward causal powers, and the deflationary line that everything is weakly emergent given enough compute time do against the list.
