Please take on the role of a professional philosopher who works across classical Indian epistemology — Nyāya in particular — and contemporary analytic epistemology, and who regularly supervises undergraduate theses. I am a third-year student in a seminar on the pramāṇa literature, and before I commit to a thesis I want to find out what principle, if any, actually underlies my own verdicts. Please think carefully. I would like you to begin with an explicit brainstorming or case-mapping stage — sorting my cases by which knowledge source they seem to involve, and isolating the pairs of verdicts that pull against each other — and only then deliver the polished analysis.

Here is my material. I have been recording verdicts about when a true belief amounts to knowledge, trying as far as possible to phrase them in the vocabulary we have been reading: perception, inference, comparison, and the word of a reliable person, together with the ideas that a source can operate under a defect, that inference requires a genuine pervasion between sign and signified, that error is a matter of something real being presented otherwise than it is, and that memory is not itself a source but only revives a cognition whose status was already settled when it was first produced.

Testimony. (1) My building's manager tells me the boiler is inspected every March; she keeps the invoices; it is true; I have no independent way of checking. That strikes me as knowledge. (2) A stranger at the bus stop, who says outright that he is only guessing, tells me the 42 turns at the corner; it does. Not knowledge — and yet I also think it would have been perfectly reasonable for me to wait rather than walk, so I appear to be separating knowledge from something else I approve of, and I do not know what that something else is. (3) My cousin, who is scrupulous about facts, tells me a restaurant is closed on Mondays because he checked its website; the page was three years out of date, and the restaurant happened to be closed that Monday for a private booking. Not knowledge. (4) The case I keep returning to: I buy a battered paperback from a used bin believing it to be a pulp adventure novel. It is in fact a carefully sourced popular history, and I come away with a correct account of a border dispute. My belief about the source is false, the source is in fact reliable, and what I end up believing is true. I want to say I know. But (3) and (4) look identical from where I sit and opposite from where the world sits, and I cannot tell which vantage point is supposed to be decisive.

Perception. (5) At dusk I take a coiled hose for a snake: not knowledge, no puzzle, the belief is simply false. (6) I take a post at the end of a lane for a person; someone is in fact standing a few feet behind the post, so what I believed was true. Not knowledge. The person was real, just not where I put him. I like this case because it seems to have exactly the shape our reading describes, but I cannot tell whether my verdict turns on the belief's truth or on the mismatch. (7) A radiologist glances at a chest film and sees a nodule. Knowledge — though it also looks like inference from a trained sign, and she can be right without being able to say why at that moment. I do not know which source to file it under, and I suspect my verdict would flip if it emerged that she was right for a reason she would herself disavow. (8) I look at a friend's face and cannot say what expression she had; shown six photographs afterward, I reliably pick out the one that matches, and I apologize appropriately. I want to call this knowledge even though I cannot articulate its content, and that seems to sit badly with any requirement that perceptual knowledge be determinate and reportable.

Inference. (9) Smoke on the hill, fire on the hill: knowledge. (10) Birds burst out of a hedge; I infer a cat; there is a cat in the hedge, but the birds rose because of a passing truck. Not knowledge, even though the generalization is mostly true and the conclusion is true.

Comparison. (11) A friend tells me an ibex is like a goat with enormous curved horns; months later I see one on a ridge, and thereafter I pick ibexes out of photographs. Knowledge — though I cannot say whether what I gained was knowledge of the world or of my own vocabulary, and I suspect it was both at once. (12) Told that a jackal looks like a thin stray dog, I identify a thin stray dog as a jackal. Not knowledge.

Memory. (13) I believe the Amazon empties into the Atlantic. I cannot recall ever learning it, and nothing in my present situation supports it. Knowledge. (14) I believe a childhood friend's bicycle was blue; my brother says it was red; I have no way to check, and my confidence sags. Not knowledge, or at any rate not anymore. The trouble is that (13) and (14) are indistinguishable until somebody raises an objection, which suggests my principle is not about pedigree at all but about whether anyone has challenged me — and that would make my knowledge depend on social luck.

A defeater. (15) On a hot road I see standing water, and there really is water, spilled by a tanker. But a friend I trust told me this stretch throws up mirages all summer, so I believe my eyes are unreliable here. I lean toward not knowledge, and I lean that way even though nothing whatever is wrong with the causal chain from the water to my belief.

My own tentative principle, which I expect is broken: a true belief counts as knowledge only if (i) it was produced on that occasion by one of the recognized sources, (ii) that source was functioning without defect at the time, and (iii) the link between the source and the fact is one that would have held in nearby situations of the same kind, so that the belief could not easily have been false given how it was formed. My suspicion is that clause (iii) is doing all the work and clauses (i) and (ii) are decoration — or else that (iii) gets cases (4) and (15) wrong, since in both the belief could not easily have been false and yet I am reluctant to grant knowledge in one and eager to withhold it in the other.

What I would like from you, in this order:

1. A diagnosis: which of my verdicts genuinely conflict, and the smallest set of dimensions that separates the conflicting ones.
2. One principle, or a small number of rival candidates, stated precisely enough that someone else could apply it without consulting me. For each clause, identify the case that motivated it.
3. Applications to cases I have not listed. Please also construct two new cases of your own — at least one designed to expose whether your principle is externalist about pedigree or owes something to the knower's own perspective — apply the principle to them, and say whether the resulting verdict is stable or ad hoc.
4. An honest reckoning: which of my verdicts your preferred principle cannot accommodate, and an argument that I should give them up. If a simpler principle overturns exactly one of my verdicts, present it and tell me whether that trade is worth making; case (2) and case (8) are the ones I would most regret losing, so please address them specifically.
5. An assessment of how much of this can be said inside the Nyāya framework's own resources — defect, the reliability of the speaker, pervasion, reflective second-order cognition, the distinction between indeterminate and determinate perception — and whether my clause (iii) requires importing something that framework would reject as a category mistake rather than a heresy.

Please write in American English. Do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind; I have the primary and secondary material, and what I need is argument, not references. Gloss any Sanskrit term on first use, keep quotation of source texts out of it entirely, and aim for roughly 1,500 words of focused prose.
