You are a philosopher of science with a working knowledge of statistical ecology. Treat this as a careful reconstruction problem, not a survey: think it through first, and open with a short brainstorming passage in which you list the candidate accounts of confirmation that the practice below could be read as presupposing, before arguing for your own reconstruction.

The practice. Citizen-science bird databases — eBird is the best known — separate two kinds of record. An *incidental* record reports a species someone happened to notice; it says nothing about how long they looked, over what area, or what else was present. A *complete checklist* reports every species the observer was able to detect in a stated place, over a stated duration, under a stated protocol, and the observer certifies that the list is complete. Distribution maps and occupancy models are built almost exclusively from the second kind, for a simple reason: a species missing from a certified complete checklist is counted as evidence that it was absent, whereas a species missing from an incidental record is counted as evidence of nothing at all.

So two reports with the same content — no ravens seen — receive opposite evidential weight purely on account of how they were produced. And the practice goes further: it treats the detection of a hundred irrelevant species, and the searched-over emptiness of the rest of the list, as bearing on a claim about one species. There is a well-known paradox in this neighbourhood, on which a white shoe confirms that all ravens are black.

Questions, in order:

1. What account of confirmation does the checklist protocol presuppose? In particular, does it presuppose that confirmation is a relation between an evidence statement and a hypothesis, or something that irreducibly involves the sampling procedure and background information about detectability?
2. Which rival accounts does it rule out, and which does it merely happen to fit well? Distinguish sharply what the practice strictly requires from what would license it just as easily — I expect it is underdetermined between at least two quite different pictures, and I want to know which parts of the protocol force the choice and which do not.
3. Does an ecologist who models occupancy from complete checklists thereby accept the conclusion about the white shoe, or does the practice contain the resources to resist it?
4. Is the practice internally consistent, given that the same databases cheerfully use incidental records for other purposes — rare-bird alerts, first-arrival phenology?
5. What would have to change, in field protocol and in modelling, if the presupposed account were replaced by one on which only the content of a report, never its provenance, determines what it confirms?

Keep the discussion anchored in the details of the practice as described. I want no bibliography and no roll call of who held which view — just the reconstruction, and your judgement about where it is forced and where it is optional.
