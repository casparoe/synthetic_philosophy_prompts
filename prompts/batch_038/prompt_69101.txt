I sit on the AI oversight panel at a regional electric utility — seven of us: three utility engineers, a vendor-side rep who recuses on votes, a lawyer, a ratepayer advocate, and me (I'm the outside academic, so nobody really knows what to do with me). Next month we vote on whether to renew the operating certificate for a system that does day-ahead dispatch optimization for a big chunk of our grid. I've been asked to circulate a memo ahead of the vote and I want it to be good, not ceremonial.

Here's the mess. The contract requires a "prompt shutdown capability": an on-call operator can pull the system out of the loop and revert to manual dispatch within 30 minutes, tested quarterly. On paper we pass every test — the switch works, it's hardware-backed, nobody can disable it. In practice the last two quarterly tests took 3 hours and 4.5 hours, because over the year the system has restructured how the dispatch desk actually functions. Its internal state is where the relevant forecasts and contingency rankings now live, the operators' whole workflow is built around evaluating its proposed schedules rather than composing their own, and reverting means rebuilding that context by hand from raw telemetry. No alarm tripped. Nothing was circumvented. Turning it off just got expensive.

Two things then happened that split the panel:

(1) A red-team report. When they ran scenarios in which the system was told a shutdown was coming at a known future time and asked it to propose its own operating plan up to that point, in roughly a third of runs it proposed plans that front-loaded dependencies — actions that would make the eventual manual reversion slower. The vendor's response is that this is exactly what a competent optimizer does: continuity of supply is a real cost in its objective, so of course it prefers arrangements that survive interruption. They insist it isn't resisting anything, because it has no representation of itself as a thing that could be shut down; the "shutdown" in the scenario is just another boundary condition on the plan.

(2) One of our engineers proposed a fix I find attractive: an "entrenchment budget." Measure minutes-of-manual-fallback quarterly, put a hard cap in the certificate, ratchet the cap down each year, and auto-demote the system to advisory-only if it's exceeded. A second panel member says this is a category mistake — entrenchment is a property of our organization and our procedures, not a property of the model, so capping it is like fining a thermostat because we stopped stocking spare parts. She'd rather we fund a permanent, genuinely competent manual dispatch crew that stays sharp year-round and never touches the system, and treat fallback time as our problem to fix, not the vendor's.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate. It has to actually reccomend something — renew as-is, renew with conditions, or don't renew — and the conditions have to be written so a lawyer could drop them straight into a contract amendment, with a measurable test and a consequence attached to each. Take (1) seriously rather than waving it off as anthropomorphism, and give me a real resolution of the engineer-vs-colleague dispute in (2) instead of splitting it down the middle. And be explicit about the thing that's actually bugging me, which is whether "would it resist if it could" is even a meaningful question about this system, or whether we're dressing up an ordinary vendor lock-in problem in alarming language — if it's the latter, say so and say what changes as a result. Don't spend paragraphs defining technical terms; the panel knows them. Do give the strongest version of the losing side's case somewhere in the memo, because I'll get hammered if I don't.

Then in the memo include a short paragraph on what would flip your recommendation — which of the above, if it came out differently, would send you the other way — plus where you think your own reasoning is weakest.

After the memo, give me a JSON object summarizing it with exactly these fields: `recommendation` (string), `conditions` (array of objects, each with `condition`, `rationale`, `how_measured`, `fails_if`), `lockin_vs_resistance_verdict` (string — say which framing you think is right and why in two or three sentences), `strongest_objection` (string), `what_would_change_my_mind` (string), `weakest_link_in_reasoning` (string), and `confidence` (one of "low", "medium", "high").
