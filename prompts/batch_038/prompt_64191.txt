I'm refereeing two papers that both claim to have "formalized" Jain syādvāda — the qualified-predication doctrine behind anekāntavāda — and I don't want to end up just picking whichever one's notation I find prettier. Before I write anything up I want the spec: what would an adequate formal reconstruction of the seven modes actually have to deliver, and which of the things people demand of it are really just the prejudices of the many-valued-logic crowd?

Quick recap of the material as I take it, correct me if I've got it wrong: prefixing a claim with syāt ("in some way") generates seven predications out of three ingredients — is, is not, inexpressible — taken in non-empty combinations: is; is not; is and is not; inexpressible; is and inexpressible; is not and inexpressible; is, is not, and inexpressible. The "is and is not" mode is meant to be asserted successively (krama), whereas the inexpressible mode covers the case where the two have to be said at once (yugapat), which is precisely why they can't be said. And naya, standpoints, are the partial viewpoints that license the qualifications.

So give me the desiderata. For each one, the case or consideration behind it, not just the slogan. Among the things I want covered: what has to be true of the semantics for the number seven to be explained rather than being an accident of enumeration; what it has to do with syāt so the prefix isn't a universal hedge that leaves every claim compatible with its own negation (the standard self-refutation objection — tho note it bites both ways, since a theory that escapes it by making syāt do no real work has just surrendered); what it owes to compositionality, given that actual Jain arguments nest qualified claims inside qualified claims; whether it must treat anekānta as a claim about how things are (objects really having incompatible aspects) or about what an asserter is licensed to say, and what each choice costs; how standpoints are to be modeled and what stops the parameter list from being ad hoc (tho I suspect that's where the real fight is); and what it has to reproduce about disputation, where Jains argue for definite conclusions and treat their opponents as flat-out wrong.

Mark each requirement non-negotiable or a preference belonging to a particular camp — formal, commentarial, comparative-religion. Then say which single requirement knocks out the most candidates, and, the part I most want, whether some subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable; if it is, the list is itself the result and no formalization is going to pass. Two lines at most each on how three standard moves fare against it: many-valued or fuzzy truth-value mappings, dialetheism, and contextualism about the standpoint parameter.

Under 650 words, and put the bottom line — which requirement does the most excluding, and whether the set is consistent — in your first three sentences, then the list. No bibliography, I'm not looking for reading.

One more thing: if you think the request itself is confused — say, because demanding an adequate formalization of a doctrine that denies the adequacy of formal expression is a performative contradiction, or because the two papers aren't even answering the same question so a shared yardstick is a category mistake — say that plainly rather than humoring me with a tidy list. I'd rather be told the task can't be done well than get a polished non-answer.
