I chair the nine-person search committee that will recommend the next executive director of a regional food bank. The members are four board trustees, three staff (the operations manager, the development director, and the warehouse lead), one volunteer coordinator, and me. I have run three previous searches for this organization; the trustees have run none, and between them the three staff members worked directly under both of the last two executive directors.

We narrowed eleven applicants to two finalists, so the recommendation is a plain choice between exactly two people. Our procedure was inherited from my predecessor and has not been altered in a decade: we meet, the longest-serving trustee states her preference and explains it, we go once around the table with each member responding to what has already been said, and we then vote by show of hands.

At that meeting the vote came back 9–0, and everyone left with the sense that we had gotten it obviously right. But in each of the two previous searches this same committee also recommended its candidate unanimously, and both of those directors resigned within fourteen months, one of them in the middle of an audit of the development budget. Afterwards I asked the volunteer coordinator, privately, why she had voted as she did; she said she had no real idea what the job consisted of day to day and had simply gone with the room. Two of the three staff members told me separately that they had serious reservations about the finalist we chose and had meant to raise them, but that by the time the round reached them the reservations no longer seemed worth stating.

Since then a reference we had commissioned arrived, from a former supervisor of the candidate we selected, and it is unflattering in a way the rest of the file was not. So we meet once more on Tuesday, and the board expects to ratify on Thursday.

One colleague holds that the arithmetic settles the question: nine people are likelier to be right than one, an overwhelming vote is precisely what a sound decision looks like, and my unease is nothing more than reluctance to live with a result I did not shape. A second member proposes that on Tuesday we each write our choice on a slip of paper, folded and collected before anybody speaks, with all discussion reserved for after the tally. A third thinks that is a mistake — that writing privately first freezes people's initial impressions and forfeits the only thing a committee is for, which is that hearing one another's reasons makes each person's judgment better than it would have been alone.

I have not been able to say what disturbs me, since a unanimous vote looks like the strongest signal a group can produce. Please address the following: Is my colleague's reasoning correct, and what does our unanimity actually tell us about whether we chose well? Which of the two procedural changes should I adopt, if either, and is there some third arrangement that would serve better? And what should I be alert to during Tuesday's meeting itself, given that a recommendation already exists and most of the room will experience reopening it as a failure of the process rather than as part of it?
