I am preparing a session on the authority of advance directives — the question of why the wishes of a competent earlier self should govern a later self who no longer has them, and cannot form them — and I want to test the standard philosophical account against the case that created the American doctrine: *In re Quinlan* (Supreme Court of New Jersey, decided March 31, 1976). Here are the facts as I have them; correct me if any of this is off.

Karen Ann Quinlan was twenty-one when, in April 1975, she suffered two prolonged periods of interrupted breathing after a night of drinking and sedatives, and survived into a persistent vegetative state, maintained on a ventilator. Her father petitioned to be appointed guardian with express authority to order the discontinuance of "all extraordinary medical procedures." The trial court refused. The state supreme court reversed: it held that a decision to allow a noncognitive, vegetative existence to terminate by natural forces was a valuable incident of Karen's own right of privacy, assertable on her behalf by her guardian — while simultaneously treating the evidence of what Karen had actually said (remarks to family and friends about not wanting to be kept alive by machines in a hopeless condition) as remote and impersonal, of little probative weight. The court made withdrawal conditional on the attending physicians' judgment that there was no reasonable possibility of her ever emerging from the coma to a cognitive, sapient state, together with the concurrence of a hospital ethics committee, the guardian and the family, and on those terms granted immunity from civil and criminal liability. The ventilator was withdrawn in 1976; Quinlan breathed unaided, was sustained by tube feeding, and died of pneumonia in June 1985 without ever regaining consciousness.

I want a case analysis that runs in both directions, and I want you to say plainly where the framework has nothing to offer rather than making it speak.

First, the mapping, done strictly. Who is the earlier self, and what is the content of her directive, given that the utterances in evidence were hypothetical, general, made about other people's situations, and never addressed to anyone as instructions? What is the later self — bearing in mind that the paradigm case in the literature is the contented dementia patient, not the PVS patient, and that the two differ in whether there is anyone home to be harmed? What exactly is the earlier self taken to authorize, and on what evidence?

Second, run the two standard accounts and see what survives. On Dworkin's side: critical versus experiential interests, and the integrity of a life taken as a whole. On the objecting side: the claim that the later self is a subject with a welfare of her own and that executing the directive harms her. Say what each makes of Quinlan, and in particular what each makes of the nine subsequent years of artificial nutrition — a period in which the family invoked the earlier self to remove the ventilator but not to withdraw feeding. Does either account have resources for that asymmetry, or is it simply invisible to them?

Third, and this is what I most want examined: my hypothesis is that the earlier self did no work in the decision at all. The court discounted her actual statements, then attributed to her a choice reconstructed from what a reasonable person in her condition would elect, and fastened the outcome to a medical prognosis condition and an institutional concurrence requirement, neither of which follows from autonomy in any way. On that reading the case is not an exercise of the earlier self's authority but best-interests reasoning wearing autonomy's clothes, with her prior remarks supplying legitimacy rather than content. Test this against what the court actually did. Then generalize it into a diagnostic: what distinguishes "the earlier self decided this" from "the earlier self was cited"? Specify what would have to differ about the evidence, the reasoning, or the counterfactuals — would the court have reached the same result on the same facts with no prior statements in the record at all? — for an appeal to the earlier self to be load-bearing.

Finally, tell me what the episode shows about the theory. Is it a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never contemplated: a doctrine of advance directives founded on a decision that refused to treat the patient's own words as probative? And is the institutional arc that followed — culminating in the clear-and-convincing-evidence standard the Supreme Court imposed in *Cruzan* in 1990 — better read as the fulfillment of Quinlan's principle or as a tacit admission that the principle could not carry the weight put on it?
