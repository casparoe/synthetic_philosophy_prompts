So I run a tiny philosophy-of-physics podcast — think 400 downloads an episode, one guy in a basement — and next weeks guest is a philosopher who defends the epistemic view of the wavefunction, but with a twist I want to be able to actually press on rather than let slide.

Her position, as I understand it from the paper she sent me: ψ isn't part of the furniture of the world, it's something like a summary of what an agent is entitled to expect — but she refuses to be called a QBist, because she insists there really is a mind-independent physical state of affairs that ψ is *about*. Her move is to deny that this real state is a state of a system *at a time*. She wants the underlying state to be spread across a whole history, fixed jointly by a past and a future boundary condition. Then, she says, two different wavefunctions can correctly describe one and the same underlying reality without any contradiction, and the Pusey–Barrett–Rudolph theorem simply doesn't get a grip, because it assumes you can meaningfully talk about the ontic state in the interval between a preparation and a measurement. She also thinks this buys her retrocausality without superdeterminism, which I'm pretty skeptical about.

Write me eight to ten questions for her, each with the follow-up I should fire off based on the answer she's most likely to give — give me that likely answer in a clause or two so I can see why the follow-up is the right follow-up, but dont write her side of the conversation. I want questions that are hard for her, not prompts where she gets to restate the view. At minimum I need one on each of these:

- whether "the real state isn't a state at a time" is a physical claim with content or just a way of ducking the theorem, and if it's real, what exactly the state of an electron is supposed to be between two measurements, in a way she could write down
- preparation independence, since that's the assumption PBR actually needs — I want to ask whether she's rejecting it, and if she is, whether she's paying for the theorem's conclusion with a global correlation constraint that looks a lot like the very thing the theorem was supposed to rule out
- making her say out loud whether her view yields any prediction that differs from textbook QM, and if the answer is no, why we should prefer her picture over plain instrumentalism — what work is the hidden history doing
- retrocausality versus superdeterminism: if the choice of measurement setting later influences the state earlier, what is still up to the agent, and doesn't her answer collapse into "it was always going to be correlated this way"
- why Bell's theorem doesn't already settle the matter against her — isn't the retrocausal move just the local-causality escape with extra steps, and what does she say to someone who thinks she's given up exactly what she claims to be keeping
- the objection from her own flank: if ψ is epistemic and the ontology is a whole history that nobody can ever point to or measure, isn't she doing what she accuses the QBists of doing, only with more metaphysics

Skip anything along the lines of "what first drew you to quantum foundations" — I've got fifteen minutes and she'll have a talk to give the week after.
