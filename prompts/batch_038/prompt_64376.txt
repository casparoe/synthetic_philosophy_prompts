In July 2013 an amendment to China's Law on the Protection of the Rights and Interests of the Elderly took effect, obliging adult children to visit or stay in regular contact with parents over sixty and to attend to what the text calls their "spiritual needs." The statute fixes no minimum frequency and carries no penalty of its own; a drafter described it as chiefly educational, though it also supplies a basis for civil action, and courts had already been resolving such actions with informal visitation arrangements. Public reaction was mostly derision: commentators asked who would tally the visits, while others noted that migrant workers and only children, hundreds of kilometers from home and without paid leave, could not afford the trips the law presupposes.

Analyze this episode through Confucian role ethics — the position Ames and Rosemont developed, on which there are no atomic autonomous individuals who subsequently acquire roles, but persons constituted by the roles they live, and on which order is maintained by ritual and cultivation rather than by edicts and punishment. Run the analysis in both directions. First, what does role ethics make of a statute commanding filial attention: does legislating the performance of a role preserve the role, hollow it out by redirecting the performance from the parent to the state, or is the framing of parent-as-rights-holder and child-as-duty-bearer itself an importation of the individualist ontology the view rejects? Contrast this with a duty dischargeable in money and therefore measurable and coercible, such as the parental-maintenance obligations that tribunals in Singapore and elsewhere adjudicate, and say whether that difference is decisive here. Second, what does the episode expose in the theory — its apparent silence about the demographic and material conditions (labor migration, family planning, a shrinking cohort of children per parent) that made the roles hard to perform in the first place, and its lack of any account of which of state, market, or family should have been the one to decide. Take a definite position rather than tabulating the options: on the best role-ethical analysis, is the law a corruption of filial practice, a defensible stopgap, or a case the theory has no resources to evaluate — and where role ethics falls silent, say so plainly instead of pressing it into service.
