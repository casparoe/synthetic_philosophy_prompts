I sat in on the second half of an argument at a reading group last night (I was just visiting) and I still can't tell who was right, so I want a move-by-move call on it. Two people, I'll call them N and R. Topic was middle knowledge — the claim that God knows, prior to deciding anything, what every possible free creature would freely do in any complete set of circumstances they might be dropped into, and then uses that to choose which world to create. N was defending it, R was attacking. As best I can reconstruct from my notes:

1. N: the relevant counterfactuals — "if this possible person had been placed in that exact situation, they'd have freely chosen X" — are true, and they're true before God wills anything. God doesn't make them true, he just knows them. That's the entire point of slotting this knowledge in between knowledge of what's necessary and knowledge of what God actually decrees.

2. R: then they're brute. The person doesn't exist yet, the situation isn't obtaining, so there is nothing whatsoever in virtue of which one answer is right and the other wrong. You've got truths floating free with nothing holding them up. That's the grounding complaint, and I don't think you can answer it, only shrug at it.

3. N: lots of truths aren't grounded in any thing — "there are no unicorns" isn't made true by some unicorn-shaped fact in the world. And besides, the counterfactual is true *because* the creature would freely choose that way; the free choosing does the explaining and the truth just records it. So there's no threat to freedom in any of this.

4. R: that is precisely the threat. You just said the truth is settled before God creates anything and that God can't alter it. So God creates the person in the circumstance, the counterfactual was already true, and the outcome follows. Call that determination if you like. And if you insist the creature could have done otherwise, then the counterfactual's truth has no explanation at all, you're back at brute — and you've turned God's creative choice into a bet on facts he had no say in.

5. N: could-have-done-otherwise is totally fine, because these counterfactuals are contingent. Had the creature chosen differently, a different counterfactual would have been true and God would have known that one instead. There's no necessity anywhere in the story.

6. R: contingent-but-unexplained is worse than necessary, not better. And there's a further cost you're skipping: which worlds God can bring about now depends on those facts. He can't just actualize whatever world he wants, only ones compatible with what all the creatures would freely do. So you've purchased providence by handing over control, and control is the thing you were trying to protect.

N's parting line was roughly that at least they have an account of how God can know the future without causing it, while R has nothing but "it's a mystery." R said mystery beats incoherence.
