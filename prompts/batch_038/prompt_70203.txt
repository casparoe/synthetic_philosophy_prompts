Someone in my animal-ethics reading group ran this argument and I cant find the hole in it, but I'm sure there is one, because I'm not accepting where it lands.

(1) If you have reason to prevent a harm to an animal, you have reason to prevent more harms of that kind, other things equal.
(2) Most wild animals that get born die within days or weeks of it -- starvation, predation, parasites, exposure -- and those lives are dominated by suffering.
(3) So most of the suffering in nature comes from the sheer number of animals that get produced, not from anything humans are doing.
(4) Making things better for the ones already alive doesn't fix it: any welfare gain that improves survival just raises the population until the amount of suffering per generation is back where it started.
(5) So the only reliable way to cut total wild suffering is to cut the number of wild animals.
(6) Zero animals, zero suffering.
(7) Therefore if you think wild animal suffering matters, you should want every wild animal extinct -- or failing that permanently sterile -- and you should work toward it.
(8) But extinction also destroys whatever is good in wild animal lives, and a world with none of them isn't better than a world with thriving ones, so (7) has to be wrong.
(9) Which means (1) is the culprit: harms just don't aggregate across individuals the way (1) assumes.

He thinks (9) is the payoff, that his argument demolishes aggregation. I'd a lot rather keep (1) than give it up, and I also don't want to sign on to (7), but I can't say where the chain actually snaps -- there seem to be too many candidates and I don't trust that I'd pick the right one. So: which step do you reject? And then tell me what the rejection costs, since (4) and (6) both sound like things I'd normally agree with.
