I've been reading the LW-adjacent corner of the internet since the Sequences were still coming out weekly, and one thing that reliably happens in every autonomous-systems thread is that somebody posts the responsibility gap line — Matthias and everyone downstream of him — and then everyone nods and the thread dies. It feels like the same five moves recycled. I'm trying to write up a half-finished post about why, and a friend of mine who does clinical risk management at a mid-size hospital handed me a case that I think actually breaks the standard framing, so I want to use it as the anchor.

The case, details scrambled at her request: an ICU deterioration-prediction model from a vendor, feeding an escalation protocol. The hospital's own informatics team had raised the alert threshold six months earlier because alarm fatigue was causing nurses to ignore everything. One night the model fires, the on-call resident silences and re-triages it exactly per the protocol she'd been trained on and had used correctly eleven previous times, and the patient dies about four hours later of something a human would probably have caught if anyone had been looking. The internal review closed with a finding of systemic contributing factors, no findings against any individual, no sanctions, no policy change beyond a memo. What stuck with me is how my friend described the interviews: every single person in the causal chain could explain, accurately and without weaseling, why what they did was the right call given what they knew and were told to do. Nobody was negligent. There was just no vantage point in the whole system from which the catastrophe was visible.

So here's the thing I want to push on. The gap framing says there's a *deficit* — the machine did something nobody can legitimately be held responsible for, so responsibility has nowhere to land. But in this case I think the opposite description fits better, and I want to coin a term for it and see if it survives scrutiny: a **responsibility surplus**. Each party has real, non-sham answerability — the vendor for the model, informatics for the threshold, the resident for the triage, the hospital for the staffing that made alarm fatigue inevitable — and the sum of those answerabilities *exceeds* what the event can absorb, so any fair-minded institutional process that actually weighs them all finds everyone over threshold and consequently assigns no one. Not too little responsibility to go around; too much, diffused in a way that cancels itself out. Please compare the two concepts head to head. Specifically: is the surplus genuinely distinct from the gap or is it just the gap described from the inside by someone who did the interviews? How do both of them relate to Thompson's problem of many hands — is the surplus just many-hands with a learning system bolted into the middle, in which case the AI literature added nothing? And which of the two better predicts what these institutions actually do, since "no individual findings, systemic factors noted" seems to be the modal outcome everywhere from hospitals to aviation to the fallout after self-driving car fatalities? If you think there's a case where the two come apart in their predictions — some accident pattern the gap view would call unfixable and the surplus view would call fixable, or vice versa — I want that, because its basically the only way I'll know whether the coinage is load-bearing or just decoration.

Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no introduction and no wrap-up paragraph — covering, in whatever order you like: the sharpest version of each view, the one place they actually disagree rather than just talk differently, the verdict on whether the surplus is just many-hands, and the come-apart case.
