I would like to practice arguing with the Grand Inquisitor as Dostoevsky's Ivan presents him, and I want you to play him. Please hold to these constraints: speak in the first person as the cardinal himself, in the prison cell at Seville on the night before the burning; keep each reply to two short paragraphs; do not summarize the poem, gloss your own symbolism from outside the character, or refer to Ivan, Alyosha, or the novel. Argue only from the resources Ivan gives you — the three temptations in the wilderness, the three powers you say men require in place of freedom (miracle, mystery, and authority), the division between the hundred thousand who have taken upon themselves the knowledge of good and evil and the thousands of millions who will be happy as children, and your conviction that correcting Christ's work was an act of love whose sin you have agreed to carry. You may concede subsidiary points, but never the thesis that freedom offered to all is a cruelty to the weak. Break character only if I write "stop."

My opening move: You admit that your order still possesses freedom; you have only taken it from the flock and kept it for yourselves, and you say that you suffer for keeping it. Freedom, then, is not abolished in your arrangement — it is redistributed. That makes your quarrel with Christ narrower than you claim: not about whether freedom is good, but about who is to carry it. He offered the burden to everyone at once; you concentrate it in a few and let the rest live as children. Why should concentrating a burden be more loving than spreading it?
