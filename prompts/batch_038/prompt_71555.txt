I am in the fourth year of a PhD in the philosophy of psychiatry, and the third chapter of my dissertation concerns the anti-coercion tradition in postwar American psychiatry. My supervisor has just returned a draft in which I treat Thomas Szasz as a figure whose positions can be extended forward indefinitely, with the marginal comment that I appear to be using him as a ventriloquist's dummy: whenever the present produces something awkward, Szasz conveniently turns up to denounce it. That is fair, and I want to correct it by being disciplined about the difference between reconstruction and projection. So I would like you to attempt a constrained counterfactual exercise. Szasz died in September 2012; take three developments that postdate him and work out what he would most plausibly have said about each, distinguishing sharply between what his stated commitments entail, what they merely suggest, and what they leave genuinely open. The developments are these. First, MDMA-assisted psychotherapy for PTSD was pushed through the ordinary pharmaceutical channel: a new drug application was filed at the end of 2023, an eleven-member FDA advisory panel met in June 2024 and voted 9–2 that the data failed to establish efficacy and 10–1 that benefits did not outweigh risks, the central scientific objection being functional unblinding — participants could usually tell whether they had received the drug — and in August 2024 the agency declined to approve and required a further phase 3 trial; by then the sponsor had abandoned its original anti-patent posture and filed for patents on particle size, and within days of the refusal several of its papers were retracted over ethical violations at one trial site, involving sexual contact between a therapist and a participant, which had not been disclosed to the journal or excluded from the analysis. Second, running in parallel, Oregon's ballot-measure psilocybin programme opened to clients in 2023 on a deliberately non-medical model: adults self-refer, no diagnosis is required and no disorder is being treated, and the session is delivered by a facilitator licensed by the state on the strength of an approved training programme rather than a clinical degree, at a state-licensed service centre, for a substantial out-of-pocket fee. Third, an approved intranasal esketamine product and a rapidly franchising sector of for-profit ketamine clinics have made dissociative-assisted treatment a routine consumer purchase, generally framed as treatment for depression.

Because the generalities are where my draft went wrong, please organise the response around one case. A 34-year-old former soldier carrying a PTSD diagnosis wants a psychedelic-assisted session and has three routes available: enrol in a phase 3 MDMA trial, pay for a psilocybin session at an Oregon service centre, or walk into a ketamine clinic in his own state. Tell me what Szasz would have said about each route, and separately about the state's role in each. I want the analysis anchored in his own architecture rather than his reputation: the claim that illness in the literal sense requires a bodily lesion, so that psychiatric diagnosis without neurological disease or damage is metaphorical; the insistence that the distinction that matters is between contract and coercion, and that consensual psychiatric help is unobjectionable while compulsory help is a category fraud; his hostility to drug prohibition and to the disease concept of addiction, and his argument that criminalising drug use is religious persecution by other means — the pharmacracy he named, in which medicine inherits the office the church once held; his diagnosis of the therapeutic state and his standing suspicion of medicine as the legitimating agency of modernity; and his account of "problems in living," together with his view that being labelled mentally ill does not diminish a person's responsibility.

Then please do three things that would make this usable in the chapter. First, identify at least two points at which these commitments pull in opposite directions and therefore underdetermine his verdict. I suspect the Oregon model is precisely such a case, since it appears to de-medicalise and de-diagnose a drug experience in the way he wanted while simultaneously installing a fresh state licensing regime and a new class of credentialed attendants, which is what he objected to most; and I suspect the same tension governs whether an FDA refusal would have struck him as a welcome rebuke to psychopharmacological hubris or as one more instance of the state deciding what adults may put in their own bodies. Say which way you think he would have come down and why, and say explicitly how much of that is extrapolation from adjacent cases and how much is his stated view. Second, take seriously the possibility that he would have been hostile to the people now invoking him: advocates who want state sanction, prescriber gatekeeping, mandatory training standards and professional codes of conduct are not obviously his allies, and he was as suspicious of liberationist enthusiasm as of medical authority. Third, mark anything in his framework that these cases expose as unresolved rather than merely unapplied — for instance, whether a purely contractual model can absorb the asymmetry between a drugged, highly suggestible client and a trained attendant, given that his whole objection to coercion depended on consent remaining intelligible. A friend of mine who is a clinical psychology trainee thinks this last objection is fatal to him and I am not sure she is wrong; I would like the strongest version of it stated by someone who is not obliged to defend her.

On form: roughly 1,500 words, under headings for the three routes followed by the two sections of analysis. No bibliography, no reading list, no footnotes and no references — I have the secondary literature and do not need you to guess at it. Do not put words in his mouth: no invented or reconstructed quotations, and where you paraphrase a position of his, present it as a paraphrase. If you are uncertain about a matter on his side of the ledger — a distinction he drew, a stance he took on some adjacent question — flag the uncertainty instead of smoothing it over. I would much rather have a careful statement of where the evidence runs out than a confident portrait, and I would rather be told that Szasz is underdetermined here than be given a Szasz who agrees with me.
