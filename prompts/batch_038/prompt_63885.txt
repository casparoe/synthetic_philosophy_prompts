Our philosophy-of-time reading group ends each term with a session where everyone has to bring something that isn't an article — usually someone reads out a bad haiku, once a guy brought a cake shaped like a light cone — and this term's last session is Gödel's rotating universe. I've been voluntold. I'd like a sestina, six stanzas of six plus the three-line envoi, 39 lines total, that I can read aloud in a pub back room in about three minutes without dying of embarrassment. Half the room are relativists and they will absolutely wince at the wrong thing, so I want a real poem rather than a versified abstract.

Here's the content, so you're not guessing what I mean by the Gödel week. The solution he dropped into the volume for Einstein's seventieth birthday in 1949 is a universe of pressureless dust rotating rigidly, held in place by a negative cosmological constant, singularity-free and homogeneous — every point as good as any other, so there's no centre you could point at and no edge to run away to. Through every event in it there passes a closed timelike curve, though in Gödel's own coordinates the curves only close once you're past a certain critical radius, so you have to travel outward before you can come home. And the inference he drew wasn't the popular one: not "simultaneity is relative so there's no universal now," but that a world where you can loop around and stand in your own past admits no consistent division into past, present and future at all, and therefore the lapse of time — the passing, not the dimension, not the metric — can't be part of physical reality, only part of how we happen to be built to experience it. He knew perfectly well his universe isn't ours; nothing observed rotates like that. He thought that didn't matter, that the field equations permitting such a world was enough to settle it.

The form should be doing that work rather than decorating it. I like that a sestina's six end-words get rotated by a fixed permutation they have no say in — rigid rotation, exactly — and that the seventh station is where the original order would come back around, except instead of a full stanza you get the envoi with all six words crammed into three lines. That's the closed curve: you arrive back where you started without the poem ever having repeated itself. So split the six end-words roughly between the machinery (something like dust, radius, axis, curve — your pick) and the inner life (something like now, or waiting, or whatever earns its place), and let the rotation slowly make it impossible to say which set was which. No punning on the end-words to fake variety, please: same word, same sense, new company. Tonally, our resident relativist thinks Gödel's argument is simply a bad argument and also the most beautiful object in the entire debate, and I want the poem holding both at once — in love with the solution, leaving the inference hanging in the envoi instead of announcing either that time is an illusion or that Gödel overreached. Nothing sentimental, no "and yet love endures," definately no ticking clocks, and please don't make the dust a metaphor for somebody's grandmother.
