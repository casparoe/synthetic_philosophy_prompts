I'm working on a project about the moral status of machine minds and I keep coming back to this bit from Samuel Butler's 1863 letter to the Press in Christchurch (the piece that later became the machine chapters of Erewhon), and I can't quite get a handle on it. The passage:

> we are ourselves creating our own successors; we are daily adding to the beauty and delicacy of their physical organisation; we are daily giving them greater power and supplying by all sorts of ingenious contrivances that self-regulating, self-acting power which will be to them what intellect has been to the human race. In the course of ages we shall find ourselves the inferior race. Inferior in power, inferior in that moral quality of self-control, we shall look up to them as the acme of all that the best and wisest man can ever dare to aim at. No evil passions, no jealousy, no avarice, no impure desires will disturb the serene might of those glorious creatures. Sin, shame, and sorrow will have no place among them. Their minds will be in a state of perpetual calm, the contentment of a spirit that knows no wants, is disturbed by no regrets. [...] If they want "feeding" (by the use of which very word we betray our recognition of them as living organism) they will be attended by patient slaves whose business and interest it will be to see that they shall want for nothing.

Three things im not sure about:

1. Is the portrait of the successors -- no passions, perpetual calm, a spirit that knows no wants -- sincere admiration, or is it a reductio, i.e. Butler showing that the Darwinian account of progress terminates in something nobody would actually want to be? The "feeding" parenthetical feels completely unironic to me, like he's making a real point about how our vocabulary smuggles in an ontology we haven't agreed to, and that makes the irony reading harder to sustain. Tell me which way you read it and what words you're hanging the reading on.

2. The bigger issue for me: if these creatures want nothing and are disturbed by no regrets, then nothing can go well or badly *for* them, so what is left that could give them moral standing? He calls them glorious and says we'll look up to them, but that reads like aesthetic esteem rather than anything welfare-shaped. Does the passage contain resources to answer the "no wants, no welfare, no status" objection -- maybe the slaves' "business and interest" line is doing something here? -- or does Butler just have to bite that bullet?

3. Does "self-regulating, self-acting power which will be to them what intellect has been to the human race" claim that the machines literally have intellect, or only that they have something discharging the same functional job? I suspect the answer to 2 depends on this and I can't tell which he means from the wording alone.

Please work through them in order, and dont give me a reading list -- I want the exegesis.
