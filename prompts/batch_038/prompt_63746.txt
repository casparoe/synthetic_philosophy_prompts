Just spent four hours arguing with my friend Dana, who builds the preference-inference layer for an assistant app — basically IRL over logged user behavior — and I still can't pin down what rule she's actually running. She insists her seven existing rulings in the design doc all come from one principle. I don't buy it. The rulings, on when logged behavior counts as evidence of what a user wants:

1. User puts a task off for weeks and then does it badly at the last minute → NOT evidence that they prefer delaying. Her comment: don't infer a preference out of an avoidance pattern.
2. User accepts whatever default the app ships with and almost never changes it → NOT evidence. Choice architecture, not preference.
3. User says they want to eat better, orders takeout six nights a week → IS evidence, of a preference for takeout; the self-report loses. Her line: behavior beats stated preference, except where the behavior isnt really a choice at all.
4. User signs up for a free trial, never cancels, gets charged, complains bitterly afterwards → NOT evidence. Inaction under friction isn't choosing.
5. User works 80-hour weeks, never complains in the moment, tells the app they love the job → IS evidence, high confidence, because report and behavior line up.
6. User declines a cancer screening three years running and also refuses to discuss it → IS evidence of a preference not to know, and the model should honor it.
7. User takes a bet when it's framed as a gain and refuses the identical bet when its framed as a loss → NOT evidence about the outcomes, just a framing effect.

Three new cases go into next sprint's doc and I want to call her rulings before she writes them:

(a) User donates generously, but only to campaigns where the gift is visible to their social circle. Says, sincerely as far as anyone can tell, that they give because the cause matters.
(b) User tells friends they wish they read more fiction, buys three novels, never opens any of them.
(c) User picks the top-listed item in the app's ranking about 90% of the time, picks differently when the ranking is shuffled, and says they're just going with whatever looks good.

We actually blew up over (c). I said she has to count it as evidence because it's an active pick, not a passive default like ruling 4; she said the ranking position is doing exactly the work the default was doing in ruling 2. So — what does she rule on each, and why? But mostly I want the rules underneath. I can see at least four candidates that sound like they'd fit her seven verdicts: behavior counts unless it's unstable across framings or contexts; unless the agent would disavow the mechanism that produced it; unless it's passive or friction-driven rather than an active choosing; unless it happens with no occurrent deliberation at all. I suspect some of them don't survive all seven (rulings 1, 4, and 7 look like problems for more than one). Tell me which candidates actually make it through, which of the survivors split on (a)–(c) and in which direction, and which single extra case would discriminate cleanest between the survivors. And if you think her seven verdicts just aren't jointly consistent with any one rule, point at exactly where they break and what she'd have to give up to make them consistent.
