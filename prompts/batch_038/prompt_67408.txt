You're a philosopher of history — someone who has actually worked on what makes an account explanatory, not just someone who can recite the standard positions. Before you write anything, brainstorm for a few lines: list the different readings our regulation's word "explanatory" could bear, and what each reading would imply for this case. Then commit to one. Think carefully; I'd rather have a decided memo than a balanced one.

Context. I sit on my university's graduate standing committee (history department, but the graduate school signs off on the wording). A dissertation has to be ruled on at our April meeting. The rule it has to satisfy says the thesis must "make an original explanatory contribution to the discipline" — and that is the entire guidance, one sentence, in a handbook drafted sometime in the nineties. Nobody on the committee has ever tried to say what it means.

The thesis is about 340 pages and is one continuous close narrative of fourteen months in a mid-sized industrial city in 1913, from a walkout at a streetcar barn through to a general strike. Chronological throughout. There are no generalizations of any kind in it: no "typically," no cross-case comparison, no variables, no counterfactuals, no causal model, no statistical appendix on wages or ridership. What it does have is a very careful reconstruction of what each actor — union officials, the company's general manager, the mayor, two rival papers, the state militia commander — took himself to be facing at each decision point, built out of letters, meeting minutes and diaries, and arranged so that each step is shown to have followed from what the people involved thought they were doing.

Two readers, two verdicts. Reader A: best thing they've supervised in a decade, and "it explains the strike far better than any of the quantitative work on it — I came out of it understanding why it happened." Reader B: "there is not one explanatory claim in this book. Nothing generalizes, no alternative possibility is ruled out anywhere, and 'understanding why' is just being used as a synonym for 'found it compelling.' Superb scholarship, and a bad fit for our wording."

Two things that make it harder. (a) Six years ago the committee passed a narrative thesis of roughly the same kind without any discussion, so we have a consistency problem. (b) The student's funding ends in June and she has an offer contingent on the degree. The obvious compromise — have her add a chapter of general claims about strike escalation — might be pure window dressing, and I don't want to recommend something we'd all privately know was cosmetic.

What I need is the memo I'll circulate to the committee beforehand. It should:

1. Give a clear recommendation on this thesis — approve, approve with conditions, or send back — plus a concrete time frame, since the funding deadline is real.
2. Settle on a working reading of "explanatory contribution" that the committee could actually apply to future theses: criteria a non-philosopher member could use without having to adjudicate the underlying methodological debate.
3. Take Reader B's objection at its genuine strongest. Not a strawman paragraph followed by "however." If B is right that there's a real difference between being shown what happened and being given an explanation, say what survives of A's praise — and say it precisely enough that A could recognize their own experience in your description of it.

Also handle (a) explicitly: does that earlier decision bind us, and if not, what do we owe this student in the way of notice or a chance to revise?

Please don't send me a bibliography or a reading list — nobody on this committee will read one and I can't check it anyway. Names and positions from the literature are welcome where they actually sharpen the argument; I just don't want the memo to turn into a survey of the field. Roughly 900–1200 words, plain prose, recommendation in the first paragraph so the dean can stop reading there if she wants. And keep in mind that two of my committee members are hostile to anything that smells like philosophy, so the reasoning has to be usable by them, not just impressive to me.
