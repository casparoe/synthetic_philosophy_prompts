I would like to subject an entirely ordinary piece of asset-management practice to philosophical examination. My interest is not in whether it is sound investment advice but in the theory of knowledge it cannot do without.

Consider a large manager that sells, from the same product shelf and frequently to the same clients, both passively managed index funds and actively managed funds. The public rationale for the index products is negative in form: over long horizons, the great majority of active managers fail to outperform the benchmarks they are measured against once fees are deducted, so the sensible investor should decline to pay for anyone's judgment about particular securities, hold the whole market cheaply, and let the index do the work. The rationale for the active products is the reverse: the firm's research identifies mispricings that the market has not yet absorbed, and clients should pay for that judgment. The firm asserts both, at once, and does not appear to find this embarrassing.

One mechanical detail matters more than it may seem. When an index fund takes in flows or must rebalance, it is not trading on a view; it is trading in order to match the benchmark, and it executes much of that business at the closing prices of the benchmark's constituents precisely so that its returns track the index rather than diverge from it. Whoever is on the other side of that transaction very often does have a view. The index fund is therefore, by construction and continuously, the uninformed party in trades with better-informed counterparties, at prices set by an auction it does nothing to shape, and it treats those prices as good enough to transact at. Nothing prevents the informed counterparty from being the same firm's active desk.

The background results are familiar. Aumann's agreement theorem and the no-trade results built on it say that traders who share a common prior, and for whom it is common knowledge that all are rational and update by conditionalization, cannot agree to disagree and cannot both find purely speculative trade acceptable; trade on that picture must ultimately be about risk-sharing or liquidity. The Grossman–Stiglitz argument runs the other way: prices cannot be perfectly informative, because gathering information must be paid for, so there must remain enough uninformed trading and enough noise for information-gathering to be worth doing.

Please address the following.

1. What view of knowledge, and of whose knowledge, does the index fund presuppose in order to be a rational thing both to sell and to buy? Be specific about the commitments concerning the informativeness of closing prices, the existence and identity of better-informed counterparties, and the magnitude of what the fund expects to lose to them over time — that expected loss looks suspiciously like a toll the indexer pays for information it declines to gather.
2. Which antecedent of the no-trade and agreement results does the practice suppose to fail in real markets: common priors, common knowledge of rationality, or common knowledge of who knows what? Does it need any of them to fail, or can indexing be coherent on a common-prior picture?
3. Is the two-sided product line coherent under a single epistemology, or does it require the firm to hold incompatible views of the same prices? At minimum, distinguish an inconsistency about markets from an inconsistency about clients, and say whether any reading rescues the firm from both.
4. Separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely sits comfortably with. I suspect that a good deal of what looks presupposed is only one of several theories that would license the practice; identify the rivals, and say where the practice is philosophically underdetermined by its own content.
5. In social-epistemological terms, what attitude is indexing? It is not first-hand inquiry. Is it closer to deferring to an expert, to aggregating a crowd's judgments, to trusting a measuring instrument whose calibration one has not checked, or to accepting testimony whose content one could not survey even in principle? Does the classification bear on whether the practice is defensible?
6. Finally, suppose we substituted the strong common-prior picture, on which speculative trade is impossible and every transaction is at bottom a hedge or a liquidity need. What would have to change — in fund design, in execution practice, in what the manager may tell clients — for index funds to remain the thing they are?
