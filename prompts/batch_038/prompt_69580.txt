I am convening a second-year metaethics seminar and, for the week on the Euthyphro, I have paired Plato's dialogue with Kazuo Ishiguro's The Remains of the Day. The pairing is not arbitrary, though I have not yet been able to defend it to my satisfaction, and defending it is what I want from you. My claim is that Lord Darlington occupies the structural position the gods occupy in the dialogue, and that Stevens's professional ideal of dignity is a secular version of the first horn: on Stevens's own account, what makes his conduct right is that it was willed by his employer, so that Darlington's judgment functions for him not as evidence about what he ought to do but as the thing that makes it what he ought to do. The novel then appears to punish him for holding that view. He carries out the instruction to dismiss two housemaids because they are Jewish, over his own private unease and against Miss Kenton's protests; he stays at his post through the international gathering on the night his father dies; and on the seafront bench at the end he concedes to a stranger that Darlington at least made his own mistakes, whereas he trusted so completely that he cannot even claim that much for himself.

I want the analogy stress-tested rather than celebrated, in three specific directions. First, the standard objections to divine command theory — arbitrariness on the first horn, the redundancy of the commander on the second — get their force partly from the assumption that the commanding being is omniscient and perfectly good, and Darlington is neither. Does that gap weaken the analogy or sharpen it? More importantly: does the novel's implied lesson commit Ishiguro to the second horn, or only to the far more modest claim that a fallible human authority is an unreliable guide to reasons that exist independently of him? A defender of Robert Adams's modified theory would insist that these are different claims, and that the charge of collapsing a grounding thesis into a deference thesis is precisely the sleight Socrates' question performs; is there any room in the novel for what I have been privately calling a modified Stevens, a butler who takes his employer's wishes as constitutive of his duties exactly because he has independent grounds for regarding the man as benevolent rather than merely powerful? Second, consider the inversion with Euthyphro himself, who is on his way to answer a homicide charge for prosecuting his own father over the death of a bound laborer, and whose disease is not abdication but presumption — a total confidence that he can read off what the divine requires. If that is the right contrast, the dialogue and the novel diagnose opposite vices, and the interesting question becomes whether Ishiguro offers anything between Euthyphro's presumption and Stevens's surrender, and whether Miss Kenton, or the stranger on the bench, is supposed to supply it. Third, I want you to tell me where the lens distorts. Stevens is unreliable in a way no interlocutor in a Platonic dialogue is: his protestations that Darlington was never antisemitic and that the dismissals were trifling are rationalizations, and the reader is positioned as his judge. So the first-horn view may be a reconstruction imposed by a critic rather than anything the text licenses, since Ishiguro gives us access only to Stevens's self-presentation, not to his metaethics. There is also the worry that what the novel is actually about is role morality and the institutional division of moral labor — the conditions under which an office may legitimately displace first-person judgment, and the absence of any exit clause in Stevens's version of it — which is a normative question the Euthyphro framing simply does not ask.

Please write this as criticism, not as a survey, and take a definite position: tell me which of these readings you think the novel genuinely supports, whether the Euthyphro is doing real work or supplying vocabulary, and whether I should keep the pairing, keep it with heavy qualifications, or drop it. Where the analogy breaks, say so and say why rather than smoothing it over; I would rather abandon the pairing than teach a forced one. Concretely, I would also like to know which two or three episodes you would put in front of twelve students and which you would warn them against over-reading — and, since none of them will have read the text closely, please paraphrase rather than quote when you invoke scenes. Note anywhere you think you are leaning on a contested interpretation of Ishiguro, and flag any point at which you suspect I have mischaracterized the philosophical debate in order to make the pairing work.
