For a graduate seminar I am co-teaching on the philosophy of social science, a colleague constructed the following argument, whose conclusion is that disputes over whether the world is becoming less violent are not substantive. I have gone over it repeatedly without locating the error, and I am nonetheless unwilling to accept the conclusion. I would like a diagnosis rather than a survey.

(1) If a claim about the world is true, some fact makes it true, and that fact is fixed by which events occur.

(2) Take any two disputants about whether violence is declining — call them the optimist and the pessimist. They disagree about no event. Every death, injury, imprisonment, and act of destruction that has occurred is common ground: the optimist accepts the pessimist's ledger and the pessimist accepts the optimist's.

(3) Their disagreement therefore concerns only how the ledger is processed: which entries count as violence at all; whether tallies are divided by population; whether one compares absolute numbers or rates; how episodes are grouped into wars, and whether a war is dated to its first shots or to a declaration; and how deaths are weighted — by count, by severity, or by years of life lost.

(4) Call any scheme of counting, grouping, and weighting a measure. For every measure M on which the optimist's verdict comes out true, there is a measure M* on which the pessimist's verdict comes out true and which is equally faithful to the ledger. A falling per-capita rate is compatible with a rising absolute total, once population growth is held in view; and a single atrocity can be made to outweigh a century of ordinary peace by weighting for severity.

(5) Nothing in the world — no further event, and no fact about the events already agreed upon — selects M over M*.

(6) Therefore there is no fact of the matter about whether the world is becoming less violent. The dispute is merely verbal: the parties differ only in the conventions they have adopted for describing a ledger on which they are in full agreement.

Two clarifications I would offer on the argument's behalf. By "equally faithful to the ledger" in (4) I mean only that M* ascribes no different properties to any event than M does; it counts, groups, and weights the same events differently, but it misdescribes none of them. By "merely verbal" in (6) I mean the technical sense: the truth of each party's claim is settled entirely by that party's conventions together with the shared ledger.

Two things about my own position. First, I am attached to (1) and (2), and I would rather abandon the conclusion than either of them. Second, I suspect that whichever premise must go, giving it up will leave me owing an account of why the ordinary questions we actually ask are answerable at all — whether a particular country's homicide trend is real, whether a given famine was violent, whether one decade was more peaceful than the one before it. I do not want to buy my way out of (6) by making all of those questions unintelligible.

So, please: identify the step or steps you reject. Explain what makes each of them look acceptable, since I think the argument's persuasiveness depends precisely on their looking that way, and a diagnosis that leaves their appeal unexplained has not finished the job. Then state what is forfeited by rejecting them, including what I would owe in return about the ordinary questions above. Where the diagnosis turns on a distinction — between a measure and a verdict, for instance, or between the world's failing to select one measure and the world's failing to settle a claim, or between agreeing on a ledger and agreeing on what the ledger contains — draw the distinction explicitly instead of gesturing at it.

One constraint on presentation. The seminar includes several students trained in epidemiology and demography, so wherever the diagnosis depends on a technical point about rates, aggregation, base populations, or the periodization of events, state it in terms those students would recognize as belonging to their own discipline, and say whether the philosophical difficulty is one they could encounter in their own work or only in a question they would regard as malformed.

Finally, tell me whether any residue of the conclusion survives: is there a thesis weaker than (6) that a defender of this argument is entitled to keep, and what exactly would it commit me to?
