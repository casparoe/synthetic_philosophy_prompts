Having retired four years ago after a career in structural enzymology, I have taken up the philosophy of biology in earnest and have been invited to address a local philosophy society on whether DNA is literally a code. Before I commit my position to slides, I should like an audit of its internal coherence. This is the package I intend to defend.

(1) The assignments of the standard genetic code are best explained by direct physicochemical affinity between an amino acid and the codons or anticodons that specify it. The code is at bottom a fact about molecular recognition, not a contingency that happened to be frozen in place.

(2) DNA is nevertheless a code in the strict sense, not merely a serviceable figure of speech: its elements stand for something other than themselves, and it is legitimate to speak of what a genome means.

(3) What licenses (2) is the possibility of error. Frameshifts, nonsense substitutions and mistranslations are genuine mistakes of reading, and a system in which nothing can be misread is a causal chain rather than a code.

(4) I am nonetheless unwilling to grant biology any normativity beyond fitness. A mutation counts as a mistake only insofar as it reduces reproductive success, which is a statistical property of populations and not a rule that molecules can be said to violate.

(5) I accept the causal-parity objection in full: the ribosome and the tRNA pool, the spliceosome, chromatin state, maternal cytoplasm and the physical environment each contribute what the genome does not, and no developmental outcome is produced by DNA alone. Even so, I hold that the genome is the only cellular component that carries a specification rather than merely participating in one, and that this asymmetry is precisely what makes the informational idiom appropriate.

(6) I also maintain both that the code is universal across living things and that mitochondria, ciliates and certain bacteria employ variant codes with reassigned codons.

Please tell me which of these genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to. Where a tension is real, rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, state what would have to be surrendered or weakened to remove each, and say what is lost in the bargain. Where a tension is only apparent, explain why it dissolves. Then identify the single revision that would do the most work toward making the whole package defensible. I should add that I have no antecedent preference among these items; I would rather be told which of them I must give up than be assured that all of them can be kept.
