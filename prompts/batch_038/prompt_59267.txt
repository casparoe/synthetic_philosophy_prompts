You're going to be my philosopher here, not my friend — somebody who works on agency and groups for a living. Think it through properly before you answer; I don't need to see the working, just where you land.

I write novels. The third one came out in the spring, and it started about two and a half years ago with my friend Priya, who was renting the other room in my flat at the time. Every Tuesday night we sat at the kitchen table with a bottle of something and talked the book out loud. The whole double-timeline structure was hers — I'd been going round in circles with a straight chronology and she drew the thing on the back of an envelope and it just clicked. We kept a shared notebook, we called it the bible, her handwriting on the left-hand pages and mine on the right. She read every draft and marked them up in red pen, brutal about it. I wrote every actual sentence of the book. That was never in question between us.

About ten months in she got a job in Lisbon and left. She sent two emails with notes early on and then it kind of went quiet. No fight, nothing, we just stopped being people who did Tuesday.

The book came out with my name on it and I thanked her properly in the acknowledgments. Last month she was on a festival panel about writing alongside other people and said I'd published our book with only my name on it, and now her people are asking my publisher for a co-author credit on the paperback and half the advance. I read that and went cold, and then felt guilty about going cold, which is worse.

Here's what I can't work out. Part of me thinks she's right that we did it — for a year that book was ours in the way we talked about it, and I notice that after she left I started saying "my book" in my head without ever deciding to. Another part of me thinks that if she's a co-author then so is the man on the train who told me the story the whole second half is built on, and my old flatmate's mother, who corrected one detail about a coroner's inquest, and my ex, who handed me the last line of chapter nine over dinner and has no idea. Priya's argument is basically: this book would not exist without me, you know that, and you and I made every decision about it together. Which is true. It's definately true.

But is it enough? Was this two people writing a book, or was it one person writing a book with a huge amount of help from somebody who mattered enormously? I want to know what actually decides that — not etiquette, not what my contract says, the underlying thing. And I want to know whether her pointing out that the book wouldn't exist without her settles the question or is missing something.

Give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each. No intro, no summing up at the end, just the five.
