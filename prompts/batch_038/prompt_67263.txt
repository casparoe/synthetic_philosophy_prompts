A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about whether Bernard Williams's internal reasons thesis and Fischer and Ravizza's reasons-responsiveness account of moral responsibility are really one view wearing two hats. His case had three steps: both make normative status depend on what an agent could recognize or respond to from where she actually stands rather than on her occurrent desires; both build imagination, correction of mistaken belief, and a patterned sensitivity to reasons into the relevant capacity; and so Williams's talk of a sound deliberative route from the agent's subjective motivational set is just reasons-responsiveness with the responsibility vocabulary stripped out. My case was that Williams is offering a criterion for when a reason exists at all, whereas Fischer and Ravizza are offering a criterion for when an agent is a fair target of blame, and the latter sits comfortably with there being reasons the agent could never recognize.

Compare the two properly rather than picking a side. Which of his three steps survive scrutiny; at what point do the accounts come apart in a way that makes a difference to their verdicts on the same agent; and does the familiar charge that Williams's deliberative route is permissive enough to smuggle the externalism back in have an exact counterpart in the receptivity condition, or does the difference in subject matter defuse it? Throughout, mark clearly what is settled among people who work on these debates and what remains genuinely contested, and do not present one contested interpretation of Williams as though it were the standard reading.
