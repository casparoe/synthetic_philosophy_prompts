I convene a reading group whose members are split roughly evenly between working evolutionary biologists and philosophers of biology, and our first session on the adaptationism debate keeps collapsing into people discovering that they were using the same words for different commitments. I would therefore like a diagnostic instrument rather than a survey of the literature: a decision tree that carries a participant from one shared starting point to a named position in the debate. The starting point is a trait that appears well suited to something the organism does with it, together with the question of what that trait's presence and form are evidence of.

Please observe the following in constructing it.

First, lay out the logical space before you populate it with names. The opening section should identify the questions that genuinely separate the positions, in an order you can defend, each phrased so that a participant can answer it by asking how they would treat a piece of evidence rather than by recognizing a school of thought. Candidate questions of that kind, which you should use, discard, reorder, or replace as you see fit: does a good fit between form and current function license an inference about origin; what is shown by the absence of a trait in close relatives that perform the same function, or by its presence in relatives that do not; what should one make of a form that is predictable from general geometry or physics across unrelated lineages; when a trait could only have arisen through prior selection on something else, is that a claim about origin or about present utility; what follows when a trait is near optimal under one description of the environment and badly suboptimal under another; does the availability of many alternative adaptive stories for a single trait weaken adaptationism, or merely reflect its fertility as a method; and is the programme's capacity to absorb criticism a virtue or a vice.

Second, the leaves must include at least these: strong adaptationism in the sense its critics attack, on which traits are both ubiquitously adaptive and near optimal; the architectural-byproduct position that treats many salient features as necessary consequences of building the organism the way it is built; the co-option position, on which current function tells you about maintenance but not origin; the physical-necessity or self-organization position in the tradition that explains form by the physics of growing matter rather than by selection; constraint-based positions grounded in development and in the unavailability of heritable variation; phylogenetic inertia; a weighted pluralism that assigns the burden of proof to one side; and the methodological position that the adaptationist programme is a heuristic whose fruitfulness is separable from any claim about how often traits turn out to be adaptations. If some further position deserves a leaf, add it; if you judge that one of the above is not a position at all but collapses into another, say so and show the collapse.

Third, apply the finished tree to three traits, chosen so that they route to different leaves: a feature whose presence follows from chemistry rather than from design, such as the redness of blood; a trait whose distribution across related species is patchy in a way that tracks body size or shape rather than ecology; and a trait for which the evidence of current function is good but the evidence about origin is conjectural. For each, trace the path through your nodes and indicate where two reasonable participants who agree on all the descriptive facts would nonetheless diverge.

Fourth, be candid about the tree's failures. State which pairs of leaves it does not really separate — the byproduct and co-option positions are the obvious suspects, since a trait can be at once a structural consequence and later recruited — and which nodes can only be answered by someone who already suspects where they lead. A node that pre-commits the participant is worse than a missing node; either fix it or label it as loaded.

Fifth, mark the unoccupied cells. Where a combination of answers is coherent but has no defenders, say whether the vacancy is due to incoherence, to unattractiveness, or merely to nobody having explored it. I would particularly like one empty slot defended in detail: my own suspicion is that the conjunction of no trait is anywhere near optimally designed with the adaptationist programme remains the best method available is held far more often in practice than it is defended in print, and I want to know whether that conjunction is stable or quietly self-undermining.

Finally, because a tree of this kind encodes judgment calls about which distinctions are real, close with a short statement of what would change your mind about its structure: which argument, or which kind of empirical finding, would force you to rebranch it, and which of your nodes you consider least stable. I would much rather see the points at which you think the design is contestable than a confident-looking diagram.
