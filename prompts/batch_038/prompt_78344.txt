i'm prepping a short talk for a mixed comp-sci / philosophy-of-mind audience and i want to open with a poem instead of a title slide. the subject is the analogy von Neumann built in The Computer and the Brain — the unfinished set of Yale lectures published after his death in 1958 — the brain as a computing machine, and more importantly the specific joints where his own analogy starts to slip.

here's the conceit, and i want the form to carry it rather than just narrate it:

- part one is the machine, and it has to feel serial and deep: one single sentence with no full stop in it, running down the page in narrow indented lines where each line is syntactically hanging off the previous one, clause stacked on clause. the content should be his picture of the automaton — the all-or-none pulse as a standard unit, precision counted in decimal digits, arithmetical depth in the hundreds of thousands of successive operations, memory kept in a distinct organ with addresses you can point at.
- part two is the nervous system, and it has to feel parallel and shallow: many short independent lines, the same simple shape repeated with small variations, almost no subordination, very few steps between stimulus and response. same pulse, but riding on chemical gradients so the whole thing is mixed digital/analog; precision at a synapse maybe a dozen bits instead of ten decimal digits; depth of a handful of steps; unreliable components turned reliable by sheer redundancy and repetition. and then the thing he could not locate: no separate memory organ, memory statistical and smeared through the entire system instead of sitting in an addressable box.

the turn should be his suspicion that whatever code the central nervous system runs on is not mathematics and not formal logic at all — some primary language we simply don't speak — and by that point the reader should have just watched the two sections fail to translate into each other, structurally, not just be told about it.

other constraints: 30 to 40 lines total, and part two should have visibly more lines than part one but fewer words overall, so the width-versus-depth contrast is something you can see on the page. no rhyme scheme required, but part two's repetitions should be strict enough to be recognizable as a pattern.

accuracy matters a lot to me here: don't turn him into a cartoon who concedes a soul at the end. he was committed to the analogy to the last page — the differences he cared about were differences of structure, precision, and statistics, and the residual mystery was a coding problem, not a ghost in the machine. someone who's actually read the lectures should nod; someone who hasn't should still get a good poem. oh, and try not to use the word "neuron" more than twice, it gets dead fast.
