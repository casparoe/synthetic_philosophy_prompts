I'm a third-year PhD student in political theory working on the concept of natural dependence, and this spring I'm teaching a section of our core history-of-political-thought course that pairs Book I of the *Politics* with the sixteenth-century Spanish controversy over the treatment of the American Indians. My working thesis — the one I want pressure-tested before I commit it to a chapter — is that the Valladolid junta of 1550–51 is the best surviving test case for the doctrine of natural slavery, because it is the rare moment when the doctrine was not merely assumed in the background but argued over formally, before a panel the crown convened, with a suspension of further conquest hanging on the outcome. So that you aren't guessing at the facts: the crown halted expansion and assembled a junta of theologians and jurists to rule on the question; Sepúlveda defended conquest with four arguments drawn from a dialogue on the just causes of war he had written a few years earlier — that the Indians' condition made them unfit to govern themselves and that Spaniards were therefore obliged to rule them as masters; that war was licit to suppress cannibalism and other crimes against nature; that it was licit to stop the sacrifice of innocent victims; and that it served the propagation of the faith — while Las Casas had prepared a Latin rebuttal insisting that Aristotle's categories of barbarian and natural slave simply did not fit peoples with cities, laws, crafts, settled polities and prudent rulers, and that the faith could not be imposed by force. The junta issued nothing that settled the matter, and both sides claimed victory. Two stipulations before you begin, because they come up in every undergraduate paper I mark: the junta was not about whether the Indians had souls, and the New Laws of 1542 had already attempted to wind down the encomienda, with limited success.

What I want first is the mapping, run from theory to case, and I'd like it disciplined rather than impressionistic. Which parts of Sepúlveda's case are actually Aristotelian, and which are not? Once you notice that three of his four arguments concern crimes against innocents and the duty to evangelize, and could be made without Aristotle at all, how much work is the natural-slavery doctrine really doing? Then: does Aristotle's own doctrine license what Sepúlveda needs from it? The natural slave is defined by a cognitive test — someone able to follow a rational directive issued by another but not to originate or exercise one himself — and the entire justification of the relationship is that ruler and ruled share an interest, which in Jowett's translation of the opening chapters reads: "For he who can foresee with his mind is by nature intended to be lord and master, and he who can work with his body is a subject, and by nature a slave; hence master and slave have the same interest." But Aristotle also holds that actual slavery as practiced rests largely on capture in war, that superior strength does not by itself make a man a natural master, and that where there is no natural fit between ruler and ruled the arrangement has nothing in common in it, is held together by force, and is odious to both sides. He confines the doctrine to barbarians and is visibly uneasy even there, citing the poets' line that it is meet for Hellenes to rule barbarians as evidence that the two categories had simply been collapsed into one, and elsewhere remarking that among barbarians there is no natural ruler at all, so that they are "a community of slaves, male and female." Sepúlveda, by contrast, needed several extensions: that natural slavery can attach to a whole people rather than to assessed individuals; that it can be transmitted to children; that it can be inferred from customs and institutions rather than from any examination of capacities; and that it licenses dominion and not merely direction or guardianship. I'd like you to say, one by one, which of those the doctrine can be stretched to cover, which it cannot, and which Aristotle's own concessions actively forbid. You may treat Vitoria's deflationary reading — that Aristotle's natural slave is a person who needs direction, not a person who can be owned, and that this grounds no right of dominion whatever — as a third position in the mapping, but tell me whether it applies the theory or quietly dissolves it.

Then run it back, case to theory, and here I want you to be decisive rather than even-handed, because I can read a survey for myself. There are two readings of what Valladolid shows, and I want you to commit to one and argue for it. Either the doctrine is a substantive empirical thesis about human capacities which the encounter with the Americas simply falsified — Las Casas's ethnography is a refutation, and the junta's indecision reflects a failure of nerve rather than a genuine stalemate — or the doctrine was never the sort of thing a case could confirm or disconfirm, because it supplies no criterion of application that is not already a verdict about who may rule whom, which would explain how the same texts sustained Sepúlveda's argument, Las Casas's rebuttal, and Vitoria's deflation of both, and how the junta could adjourn with each side convinced it had won. Pick one, give your reasons, and then state the strongest evidence for the other reading and why it fails to carry. If you think the honest verdict is a third thing — that the episode discredits Sepúlveda's *use* of Aristotle without touching the doctrine, because the doctrine was built to describe a household relation and was never designed to license a conquest across an ocean — say so plainly and defend it, but don't leave me with a menu to choose from. Finally, flag explicitly the places where the theory goes silent rather than forcing it to speak: I suspect it has nothing to say about whether a natural slave could ever become a citizen, about what follows when the ruler's own injustice destroys the supposed common interest, and about whether a people said to be naturally subject has any standing at all to object to the arrangement. A colleague in the history department tells me the whole junta is overread, that it changed no policy and that its status as a landmark is a later invention; take that objection seriously in passing, but don't let it swallow the analysis.
