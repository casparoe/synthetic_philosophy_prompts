I'm preparing a talk for a reading group that is about half formal epistemologists and half machine-learning engineers, and a disagreement has come up that I want to settle before I present it. One of the engineers, defending a proposal to wire every available data channel into a deployed screening model, invoked Good's theorem: for an agent who conditionalizes on the outcome of an experiment and then picks the utility-maximizing act, free information about the state of the world can never be expected to leave her worse off, provided the information does not alter her available acts and does not enter her utility function directly. He read this as licensing a design default — all else equal, more observation channels are better — and treated any proposal to blind the model to some channel as something that always requires a special justification, such as cost, latency, or privacy. I want to argue that the design default does not follow from the theorem, and that it is not merely unsupported but backwards.

My case, roughly, is that the entire guarantee is purchased by the assumption that the agent chooses optimally after the observation arrives. That assumption makes the old decision rule available as one member of the new and larger menu of rules, so the familiar "she can always ignore it" step does all of the work, and the theorem is essentially a claim that maximizing over a richer set of information-conditioned rules cannot do worse than maximizing over a poorer one. But the engineering question is a different comparison. It holds the response rule fixed — a trained policy, a written clinical protocol, a regulatory scoring formula, anything that cannot be re-tuned between now and the moment of decision — and asks what happens when its inputs are widened. Under that comparison the sign can flip, and it flips routinely.

Here is the example I intend to use, and I would like you to check my arithmetic rather than take it on faith. Two percent of applications in a lending portfolio are fraudulent. Per application, approving a legitimate one is worth +1, approving a fraudulent one is worth −20, and rejecting anything is worth 0. Flags A and B each fire on 50 percent of fraudulent and 40 percent of legitimate applications, and the flags are independent given the type. The deployed rule is "reject if any two flags fire," and at present only A and B are wired in, so in practice it rejects exactly when both fire. Now flag C arrives: free, perfectly reliable about what it measures, and mildly predictive — it fires on 55 percent of fraudulent and 50 percent of legitimate applications. The model is not retrained; C is simply added to the input set and the any-two rule now runs over three flags. By my calculation the expected value per application falls from about +0.52 to about +0.40, while an agent free to re-optimize its rule over all three flags is not hurt by C at all. Same observation, opposite sign, and the difference is entirely about who gets to choose the rule and when.

So the thesis I want argued is this: Good's theorem is a theorem about the value of information for agents who remain free to select their decision rule after the evidence arrives, and it is not weak but silent with respect to agents whose rule is fixed in advance — which is the normal condition of anything actually deployed, and arguably the normal condition of any deliberator at the moment of decision. Consequently the default presumption should be reversed: for such an agent, adding an input channel is not a free enrichment of her evidence but a change to her decision procedure, and it ought to require the same justification as any other change to that procedure.

Please argue this position as strongly as you can, and take a definite stand. I do not want a balanced survey that lays out the readings and leaves me to choose; I want a committed case with reasons, and if you think the thesis is wrong, say so plainly in the closing section and tell me what you would assert in its place instead of hedging. Where you think the argument overreaches — for instance, if you believe the fixed-rule case is a trivial assumption-violation rather than a substantive point, or that retraining dissolves it — say so directly rather than burying it.

Please organize the response under these headings, in this order: "What the theorem compares" (about 200 words, pinning down exactly which assumption carries the guarantee and why the ignore-the-information step is doing the work); "The argument" (about 600 words, including a verification of my numerical example, corrected or replaced if I have botched it); "Objections that land" (about 400 words, giving the three strongest objections you can find and stating which of them you think actually succeeds); "Qualifications" (about 150 words, on how the thesis must be narrowed to survive); and "Bottom line" (about 75 words, ending in a single sentence stating whether you accept the thesis as I have stated it).
