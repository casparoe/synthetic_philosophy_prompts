I'm working on moral worth and want to know whether my case-by-case intuitions can be captured by a single principle. My working proposal: an act has moral worth just in case it is right, the agent is motivated by at least one of the features that make it right, and the fit between that motive and those features is neither lucky nor coincidental — it would have held across a reasonable range of nearby variations. Here are the verdicts I am trying to fit.

(1) A restaurateur refuses to serve anything he would not eat himself, purely out of fastidiousness; he has never reflected on his customers' health. Worthy.

(2) A wealthy man gives a large sum to a charity whose effectiveness he has studied in detail, solely to outdo a rival he despises; absent the rival he would have given nothing. Not worthy.

(3) A clerk wants above all to do the right thing and believes that means following her office manual; her copy is corrupted, but by luck the corrupted line tells her to do what is in fact right in this instance. Not worthy.

(4) A man pulls a child from a burning car for the exhilaration of it; had the child merely been lost in a shopping mall, he would have walked past. Not worthy.

(5) A woman sits up all night with a distressed friend out of plain affection, with no moral reflection at all; she would have done so under many variations of the circumstances. Worthy.

(6) A man has adopted the standing policy of doing the right thing because he finds the shame of being caught doing wrong unbearable; he identifies the right-making features accurately and tracks them reliably, so that across nearby variations he does the right thing for the right reasons. Not worthy — and this is the case my principle seems unable to rule out, since everything it asks for appears to be present.

I know the standard dialectic here — the fetishism worry about acting from a desire to do what is right, the defense of the unprincipled but reliably decent agent, and the attempts to cash the requirement in counterfactual terms — but I want the principle itself, not a survey of the literature. So: state the principle, or the small set of candidates, that you think fits best, clause by clause, identifying which of my cases motivates each clause; say what your account yields for a case I have not listed, namely someone who has spent years training himself to desire what he judges good and now acts from those trained desires without further reflection; and tell me plainly which of my six verdicts, if any, your account cannot accommodate and which of them you would advise me to abandon.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the principle you would endorse, or the verdict that has to go — and then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restatement of the question.
