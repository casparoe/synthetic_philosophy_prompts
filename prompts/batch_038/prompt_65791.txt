I sit on the research ethics panel of a mid-size lab that trains agents by reinforcement learning in simulated environments, and tonight's meeting is going to be uncomfortable. A colleague's group has proposed a fourteen-month training campaign with a persistent agent — one whose policy and episodic memory carry across episodes rather than being reset, because the scientific question concerns long-horizon credit assignment. The task is deliberately punishing: for most of each episode the agent accrues a small negative reward and only occasionally reaches a positive terminal bonus, so the designers expect the return distribution to be dominated by negative values for the first several months. Attached to the proposal is what they call a welfare annex, and that is what the panel must actually rule on. The annex would instrument the runs to log the distribution of temporal-difference errors, flag any stretch in which those errors remain negative for more than a threshold number of consecutive updates, and, when a flag fires, require a human to choose among shortening the episode, adding shaping rewards, or terminating the run. Their stated rationale is that sustained negative prediction error is the closest thing we can measure to distress in a system like this, and that if any broadly functionalist account of valence turns out to be right, we will have spent a year running something dreadful while reassuring ourselves it was only arithmetic.

I spent three hours last night arguing about this with a friend who leads an RL team at another lab, and neither of us won. Her charge is that the annex is not merely unproven but conceptually confused, because reward is an optimization target rather than a feeling, and its sign is fixed only relative to a scale the group is free to move at will: in a discounted continuing task, adding a constant to every reward leaves the optimal policy untouched, so a monitor keyed to how negative the returns are can be silenced by a shift of coordinates that changes nothing the agent does. If a quantity can be made to vanish by a transformation that leaves behaviour identical, she says, then a compliance regime built on it is not caution but superstition — and adopting it would entrench the anthropomorphic idiom we already struggle to police, since the same group writes about the agent being punished, preferring, avoiding, and wanting. My reply, which she called evasive, was that her objection misses the quantity the annex actually uses: a uniform shift in reward leaves the TD error stream exactly as it was once the value function re-equilibrates, and so does the standard potential-based shaping transformation, which adds γΦ(s′) − Φ(s) for an arbitrary function of state and provably preserves the policy. I added that affective neuroscience has long separated the hedonic impact of a stimulus from its motivational pull, so that liking and wanting come apart, and that prediction error is a candidate for neither until someone says which — but also that invariance under rescaling is a fact about a metric, not obviously a fact about the system's relation to what the metric tracks. She thought I was smuggling phenomenology in through a footnote.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the panel beforehand. It should land on a clear recommendation — approve the annex as submitted, approve it with named amendments, or reject it and say what we should do in its place — and it should give the other members criteria they can apply to future persistent-agent proposals rather than a verdict on this one alone. I want it to engage my friend's invariance objection head-on and either defeat it or concede it, to state plainly whether the TD-error response I gave her actually rescues the monitor or relocates the problem, and to say what our routine description of negative reward terms as punishment already commits us to, if anything. One panel member holds that terminating a run cannot be justified by welfare considerations at all, whatever else the annex gets right; treat that as the strongest consideration on the losing side of whichever way you come out, and address it honestly rather than noting it in passing. Keep it to one page, no more than 700 words, with the bottom line stated inside the first three sentences, and no citations, bibliography, or reading list — I want your reasoning, not references I cannot check.
