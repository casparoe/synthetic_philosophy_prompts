I'm on a small working group in my school district rewriting the 8th-grade unit on contact and colonization, and we've hit a wall over a couple of paragraphs. Someone brought in a scan of an old English translation of Las Casas's Short Account of the Destruction of the Indies — the scan's bad, no title page, and the spelling is all over the place ("sumissively", "Furvor", "praedominant"), so I think its a 17th-century printing rather than a modern translation. It's the opening of the chapter on Hispaniola. Here's the bit we keep going round in circles on:

Now this infinite multitude of Men are by the Creation of God innocently simple, altogether void of and averse to all manner of Craft, Subtlety and Malice, and most Obedient and Loyal Subjects to their Native Sovereigns; and behave themselves very patiently, sumissively and quietly towards the Spaniards, to whom they are subservient and subject; so that finally they live without the least thirst after revenge, laying aside all litigiousness, Commotion and hatred.

This is a most tender and effeminate people, and so imbecile and unequal-balanced temper, that they are altogether incapable of hard labour, and in few years, by one Distemper or other soon expire, so that the very issue of Lords and Princes, who among us live with great affluence, and fard deliciously, are not more effeminate and tender than the Children of their Husbandmen or Labourers: This Nation is very Necessitous and Indigent, Masters of very slender Possessions, and consequently, neither Haughty, nor Ambitious. […] The Men are pregnant and docible. The natives tractable, and capable of Morality or Goodness, very apt to receive the instill'd principles of Catholick Religion; nor are they averse to Civility and good Manners, being not so much discompos'd by variety of Obstructions, as the rest of Mankind; insomuch, that having suckt in (if I may so express my self) the the very first Rudiments of the Christian Faith, they are so transported with Zeal and Furvor in the exercise of Ecclesiastical Sacraments, and Divine Service, that the very Religioso's themselves, stand in need of the greatest and most signal patience to undergo such extream Transports. And to conclude, I my self have heard the Spaniards themselves (who dare not assume the Confidence to deny the good Nature praedominant in them) declare, that there was nothing wanting in them for the acquisition of Eternal Beatitude, but the sole Knowledge and Understanding of the Deity.

Then a few lines later: The Spaniards first assaulted the innocent Sheep, so qualified by the Almighty, as is premention'd, like most cruel Tygers, Wolves and Lions hunger-starv'd.

The dispute. One colleague wants to hang the unit on the claim that Las Casas kills the "man of his time" defence for this whole period: a Spanish friar, writing while the conquest was still running, said in print that it was monstrous — so the conquistadors weren't just tracking the norms of their era, they were violating them, and we shouldn't let students park the question at "everyone thought that way back then." The other colleague thinks that's using him backwards: the same pages call these people effeminate, imbecile, incapable of hard labour, and liken them to sheep, and the reason offered for sparing them is mostly that they'd make perfect obedient Christians and loyal subjects. On his reading, Las Casas is evidence the period's norms did *not* include anything like equal standing, and trotting him out as the era's conscience is a cheat.

Can you help me get clear on what the passage is actually doing? Specifically:

- a plain-language gloss of the first paragraph and a half. I mostly get the shape of it, but I'm shaky on period senses of the words: what would "imbecile and unequal-balanced temper," "effeminate," and especially "The Men are pregnant and docible" have conveyed to a reader then, and is any of it harsher or softer than it sounds to us?
- that last sentence — nothing wanting in them for Eternal Beatitude but knowledge of the Deity. Is that a claim about their standing as human beings, or a claim about how well they'll take instruction? Can it be both at once without contradiction?
- the sheep image. Is that just stock pastoral/preaching imagery of the period, or is it doing real demeaning work here given it sits next to "incapable of hard labour"?
- the bigger one: is there a clean way to distinguish "someone at the time objected" from "the standards of the time forbade it"? I suspect both my colleagues are sliding between those two and I don't know how to say so without sounding like I'm splitting hairs in front of teachers who have to actually plan Tuesday's lesson.

Just tell me how much weight that sort of thing ought to carry for the argument above, and, separately, how much of it the paragraph in front of us can actually settle on its own without importing the rest of his career.

Bottom line I'm after: which of my two colleagues is closer, or is the question malformed and should we be teaching the passage as evidence of something else entirely? And flag anywhere you think I'm over-reading — i.e. reacting to archaic translation choices rather than to what he's committed to.
