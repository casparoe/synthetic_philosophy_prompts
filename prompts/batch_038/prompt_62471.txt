I'm putting together a set of thought experiments for a postgrad seminar on political obligation, and I've got a gap in the set I can't fill myself. Nearly every case in the literature pushes one way — the state claims authority, the conscientious person resists, and the interesting question is whether the duty to obey survives. I want one that runs the other way.

So: build me a case where a philosophical anarchist — someone who denies there's any general duty to obey the law, in the Simmons mould — ends up more firmly against a particular act of civil disobedience than a theorist of political obligation does. The obligation theorist, on their own principles, should come out saying the duty to obey barely bites in this case, or doesn't arise at all. And the anarchist should say: don't do it, and mean it — while insisting that nothing whatever to do with the state's authority features in their reasons.

Constraints, and they're all load-bearing:

- One paragraph for the scenario, then the analysis separately.
- A realistically sized polity with a genuine scheme of social cooperation producing benefits that can't be withheld from people who don't contribute — pick something concrete, flood defences or a vaccination programme or whatever you like, so long as it isn't a whole modern state.
- The person acting has clean hands: no promise, no participation, no acceptance of benefits of the sort that's supposed to generate fair-play obligations. They're not a free rider on anything.
- The disobedience itself has to be one we'd ordinarily cheer: public, non-violent, sincere, aimed at a real wrong the polity is doing, and the ordinary channels are genuinely closed to them. No straw man.
- The weight against it must come from ordinary face-to-face morality — harm to identifiable people, duties owed to specific named human beings, predictable knock-on effects — and it has to be clear that none of it derives from the state's say-so.

What I want after the scenario is the run-through: each theory's verdict and the mechanism producing it, not just a label. Then say which single feature of the case does the work, and test that by deleting it and telling me where the verdicts land once it's gone. Last bit: have a defender of fair play attack the case — argue that the benefits really were accepted after all, so the obligation does bite and the asymmetry I asked for dissolves — and give the anarchist's best reply.

One warning. It's very easy to cheat here by quietly stipulating a special obligation, or by making the anarchist's reasons secretely authority-shaped, and then claiming you've generated the verdicts. Check your own case against that before you hand it over, and tell me honestly if you think it can't be done without one of those moves.
