I serve on the board of a regional water district whose groundwater withdrawals are unmetered and unenforced. There is no legal mechanism by which we can sanction an over-pumper, and there is not likely ever to be one: the state legislature has refused for two decades to grant the district any enforcement power at all. What we did have, for eleven years, was a voluntary annual self-report of withdrawals, published by parcel, in a ledger anyone could read for the cost of a photocopy. Reporting never exceeded roughly sixty percent of parcels, the figures were unverifiable, and by any ordinary standard the ledger was a joke. Yet over that period total reported withdrawals in the sub-basin fell by something like a third — and the fall was concentrated among parcels whose neighbors had begun reporting, not among parcels that had been singled out, complained about, or shamed in any organized fashion. There was no sanction. There was no community of the kind the self-governance literature assumes, either: the participants are absentee agricultural investors and exurban lot owners who do not know one another and never meet. And there was no change in the property regime; the aquifer remained exactly as open at the end as at the beginning.

Out of that experience I want to name a mechanism that seems to me to be missing from the standard taxonomy, and I want you to argue for it. Call the mechanism disclosure without sanction: the practice of making each participant's contribution to, or withdrawal from, a common pool legible to the other participants, where the sole channel through which that legibility operates is each participant's belief about how much the others are doing. The mechanism does not work by raising the cost of defection, because there is no cost to raise. It works by converting a coordination problem under uncertainty into a coordination problem under something closer to common knowledge. A conditional cooperator who believes that most others are taking the maximum will himself take the maximum as a defensive measure; a conditional cooperator who can see that most others are restraining themselves will restrain himself. Publishing the ledger changes beliefs, and beliefs — not payoffs — were the binding constraint.

My claim is that this is a fourth mechanism, distinct in kind from the three that discussions of common-pool resources typically treat as jointly exhaustive: privatization, which changes the property regime so that exclusion becomes possible; Leviathan, which imports an external enforcer with sanctioning power; and self-governance, in which a community monitors, sanctions, and allocates through institutions of its own making. Disclosure without sanction appears to require none of the three. It requires only that a public record exist and that participants read it and update on it.

Please argue for that claim as rigorously as you can. I want the argument itself rather than a survey of positions. In particular:

1. State precisely what the mechanism presupposes about preferences. Something like conditional cooperation is the obvious candidate, but say what must be true of that motive — is it reciprocity, assurance, conformity, or reputational anxiety — and determine whether the mechanism still functions if the operative motive is reputation rather than fairness, given that in my case nobody's reputation was actually at stake. State equally precisely what it presupposes about the information structure of the pool: what must be legible, to whom, at what granularity, with what delay.

2. Show why the mechanism is not reducible to any of the three standard ones. The reduction I most want defeated is the suggestion that a public ledger is merely informal sanctioning under another name — that it can only work because readers impose diffuse social costs on the shirker, costs that add up to a sanction after all. Explain what would distinguish a genuine case of disclosure without sanction from a disguised case of informal sanction, and whether that distinction can be drawn observationally or only in principle. If it cannot be drawn observationally, say what is left of my claim.

3. Address the strongest objection available, which is that the ledger is itself a public good. Someone must compile it, someone must publish it, and in a well-designed version someone must audit it; that work is costly and its product is non-excludable; so the mechanism faces a second-order free-rider problem that it does nothing to solve. On this line of thought the mechanism does not escape the tragedy but relocates it one level up, onto the smaller group that might produce the record. Either show that the second-order problem is genuinely easier — smaller group, different motives, some selective benefit accruing to the compiler — or concede that it is not and explain what survives.

4. Give the boundary conditions. Identify the classes of common-pool problem in which disclosure without sanction should be expected to fail outright, and say which features of the resource do the explanatory work: subtractability, whether the act of withdrawal is itself observable, whether contributions are lumpy or continuous, whether membership is stable, whether the pool is small enough for a participant to locate himself in the distribution.

Do not attempt to supply references, and do not hedge by noting that the empirical literature is mixed; argue from the structure of the problem. When you are finished, grade your own argument. On the final line, and on that line alone, write Verdict: followed by exactly one of SOUND, QUALIFIED, or UNSOUND, where QUALIFIED means that the claim survives only on some narrowed formulation that you should already have stated in the body of your answer.
