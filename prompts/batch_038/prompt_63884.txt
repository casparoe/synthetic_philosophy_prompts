I cover law, science, and the philosophy of public institutions for a general-interest magazine, and this term I am also teaching the assessment block of a graduate seminar at a journalism school: fourteen mid-career reporters, not one law degree among them, most of whom have filed from a post-conflict setting and therefore arrive with strong opinions about tribunals and almost no vocabulary for them. The course runs eight weeks; the last three have been on the legal architecture of international criminal justice, from Nuremberg and Tokyo through the ad hoc tribunals to the permanent court. Their final is one in-class essay, written closed book in ninety minutes, worth twenty-five marks, and I want to set a single question that does real work.

The problem I am trying to solve is that "victors' justice" operates in newsrooms roughly the way "unfalsifiable" once operated in bad science writing: a phrase that ends arguments instead of starting them. I want a question that makes my students take the objection apart and discover that its components have different targets and different remedies. Working through the record with a friend who clerks on an appellate court, I have become convinced that at least two readings of the retroactivity complaint are fused together in the standard account. On the first — call it the notice reading — the wrong is that the accused could not have guided his conduct by a rule that did not yet exist; the complaint is about his epistemic position, and it is at least partly answered by pointing to instruments already on the books, such as the 1928 Kellogg–Briand Pact or the 1936 naval protocol on submarine warfare. On the second — call it the authorship reading — the wrong is not the absence of warning but the identity of the announcer: the body declaring the rule is the party that defeated the accused, so no quantity of pre-existing treaty law cures the defect, because the objection was never about the timing or content of the rule but about who holds the office of judging. The two come apart in the evidence, and the Dönitz episode is the cleanest illustration: once the American admiral's written answers established that the United States had waged unrestricted submarine warfare in the Pacific, the tribunal declined to assess his sentence on the ground of those breaches, which looks like a tacit concession on authorship, while the judgment elsewhere keeps pressing the notice-based justification. I would also like the question to leave room for a third candidate — a reading on which retroactive criminalization damages governance by rules generally rather than wronging this particular defendant — and to require students to say whether it is genuinely independent or collapses into one of the other two. My friend thinks it collapses; I am not so sure; I do not want the stem to settle it.

So: write the exam question, together with a short invented fact pattern of two or three hundred words that students must reason about rather than recognize. Set it in the near future, in which the winning side of a recently concluded civil war proposes a hybrid tribunal whose statute creates an offense that a non-binding General Assembly resolution condemned two decades earlier but no treaty ever criminalized, and whose second count describes conduct the winners are credibly alleged to have committed as well. The question should ask which objections to that statute survive, what each would take to answer, and what would have to change about the tribunal — as distinct from the statute — for a surviving objection to disappear. It must not be answerable by summarizing the Hart–Fuller exchange or reciting the history of the two postwar tribunals, and since the students are closed book it should not depend on named authorities at all: no bibliography, no reading list, no citation apparatus anywhere in what you write for me. Then give a model answer of about 750 words and a marking scheme out of twenty-five that names the specific moves separating an A from a B, with the B answer described positively rather than as an A with pieces missing, and with the errors reporters actually make — the tu quoque reflex, the assumption that naming a treaty settles notice, the slide from "the forum is compromised" to "the conviction is void" — each tied to a stated deduction. Write in American English. Begin directly with the exam question itself, with no preface, no commentary, and no restatement of this brief.
