You are a philosopher of law who has spent twenty years working between constitutional theory and clinical ethics, and who is also a practiced, mildly self-deprecating public speaker: someone who can make an interdisciplinary room laugh once and then think for a decade. Think carefully before writing, because I need this to be right in its structure and not merely pleasant in its prose.

Here is the occasion. In three weeks I am giving the twelve-minute opening remarks at a two-day workshop called *Binding Ourselves: Precommitment in Law, Psychiatry, and Development Economics*, hosted in a medical school auditorium, about sixty people. The room is roughly a third constitutional lawyers and a couple of sitting judges; a third psychiatrists and bioethicists who work on advance directives, including psychiatric advance directives in which a patient, while competent, authorizes treatment that she expects to refuse later during a manic or psychotic episode; and a third development and behavioral economists who design commitment savings products — lockboxes, harvest-timing purchase agreements — plus a handful of addiction clinicians. I was chosen to open because I am the only person on the program who reads all three literatures, and because nobody else wanted the job. The organizers have set an easel beside the podium holding a reproduction of a Victorian painting of Ulysses and the Sirens, as a joke. Please do not build the remarks around that painting; if it appears at all, it appears once, early, and is dismissed.

The intellectual spine has to be the Homeric case handled precisely, because the version everyone repeats is the thin one. The point I want made is that Odysseus did not merely have himself lashed to the mast: acting on advice he then relayed to his men, he arranged in advance that his later pleading would carry no authority — if he begged to be released, they were to bind him with more bonds — and he arranged the same for them, wax in their ears, so that the enforcers were themselves bound and could not be talked out of enforcing. That is a second-order precommitment, and it raises the question the workshop should actually spend two days on: who has standing to release the bond, and what makes the releaser's judgment better than the binder's? I want that question then pressed against the three domains, honestly, including where the analogy fails, and I want the failures specified in the speech rather than waved at. A constitution can be amended by a supermajority, which is a release procedure built into the bond; a psychiatric advance directive is frequently overridden by a clinician's judgment about present capacity, and is often less enforceable than its author hoped; a commitment savings product can be defaulted on, renegotiated by a phone call, or destroyed when the institution fails. I also want the democratic objection faced head-on and stated sympathetically — Jefferson's proposal to Madison that each generation's constitution and laws should naturally expire after about nineteen years, on the ground that the dead and the unborn are not represented and so cannot be bound — and the parallel objection from the clinical side, that the self which signed may not have had standing to foreclose the interests of the self that is now lucid, frightened, and objecting. Finally, please include the economist's version of the problem: a commitment device only works if the shape of the future temptation could be predicted at the time of signing, and the worst cases in all three domains are cases of unforeseen contingency, which is exactly what a constitutional drafter cannot provision against.

Deliver two things. First, a compact planning section of no more than two hundred words — bulleted is fine — in which you map where the analogy genuinely holds across law, psychiatry, and the commitment-products literature, where it breaks, and identify the single place at which the speech should openly concede the break rather than paper over it. Then the speech itself, around fourteen hundred words, formatted to be read aloud: short paragraphs, sentences that fit in one breath, no slide cues, no stage directions beyond an occasional pause marker if truly needed. Address the audience as a real room, acknowledge the two contingencies that will annoy each other (judges who think the psychiatrists are describing coercion with a clinical vocabulary, and clinicians who think the lawyers are describing people as if they were texts), and let at least one of them feel defended before the other does. Exactly one light joke, and it may not be about Homer. No quotations, no citations, no named authorities that a listener would have to look up afterward, and no bibliography. Keep the register warm, dry, and unhurried; do not use the "not X, but Y" construction more than once; and end by handing the room a single organizing question — not a thesis, not an answer, and not a peroration about the human condition.
