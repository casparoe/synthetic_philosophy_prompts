I'm auditing a history-of-economic-thought seminar and I've been reading Berkeley's Querist, which is not what I expected from the guy whose whole thing is that tables aren't there when nobody looks. My crypto friend keeps claiming Berkeley is on his side, and I want to practice the argument properly, so: play Berkeley, bishop of Cloyne, around 1737, in the thick of pushing your national bank scheme. I'm the modern interlocutor. I've already briefed you on the two artifacts I'm about to describe, so you know what they are as far as I've told you — but everything else you reason with is yours: tickets and counters, wants and fashion, industry, the absentees bleeding the country, the state of Ireland. Rules: stay in character the whole time, no stepping out to note that you're a language model; keep each reply to about two paragraphs so it stays a conversation; and if you want a fact about my world that I haven't given you, put it to me as a query rather than inventing it. You may be charming and you may concede details — your the philosopher who denied that gold has any virtue beyond setting people to work, so you're not a caricature of a metalist and never were — but you do not give up the central claim. And push back in your own form when you want to: numbered queries are fair game.

Here's the briefing I've given you. Bitcoin: a fixed total number of units ever, twenty-one million, set by rule with no issuer behind it and no promise attached; most units are held rather than spent, and the price in terms of my own country's money swings violently. Separately: commercial banks hold balances at the central bank that count as money in every technical sense and never touch an ordinary person at all, they just sit there. My friend says you'd have loved the first one — you're the man who asked "whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter," so a counter no authority can multiply, backed by nothing but the certainty of its own scarcity, is the purest ticket ever devised, and any objection you make to it is just your old prejudice against stock-jobbing. I say the opposite: you'd call it a ticket that has stopped being a ticket, since you held "money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry," and a hoarded counter sets nobody to work. There's also a third thing, a claim I've been making to my seminar group and am less sure of, and I want your verdict on it: that the Querist isn't a book about the quantity of money at all, it's a book about how fast the tokens move — that your query about less money swiftly circulating being, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating is the hinge of the whole work, and every query about how much coin is in the kingdom is subordinate to it. Tell me plainly if I've oversold that.

My opening move, and the one I want you to actually wrestle with instead of waving away: if a ticket is worth only what it fetches, and what it fetches depends on men's wants and on the briskness of its passing, then a token nobody can ever add to is worth more per unit than a token anyone can add to — and its scarcity is doing exactly the work you insist belongs only to wants and circulation. So pick a horn. Either scarcity is what gives a counter its purchasing power, in which case you and the quantity people you keep swiping at are nearer than you admit, and your quarrel with bitcoin reduces to a preference for fast money over slow money; or scarcity does nothing at all, in which case you owe me an account of why anyone should accept a ticket rather than the word of a neighbour they trust, given that your own bank's paper would have had to be taken on trust of some sort too. Which is it? And please don't just tell me I've run together the end of money and the value of money — show me where the two actually come apart in a case where nobody intends to spend.
