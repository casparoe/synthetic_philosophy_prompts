I don't have any philosophy training, I run a small HVAC outfit, but for about six years I've volunteered as a courtroom observer for a local group, so I've sat through a lot of trials and read a lot about the ones that went sideways. There's a question I keep circling and I think philosophers have names for the two sides, I just don't know them well, so bear with me.

The case I can't shake is the Ronald Cotton / Jennifer Thompson one in North Carolina. Here's my version — if I've got a detail wrong just go with mine, I'm giving you the facts so you don't have to guess. July 1984, a man breaks into Thompson's apartment in Burlington and rapes her. She decides in the moment that if she gets out alive she's going to be able to pick him out, so she studies his face, she memorizes what she can. Days later she picks Cotton out of a photo array, then out of a live lineup, then identifies him in court and tells the jury she's absolutely certain. The state's case basically rests on her. He says he's innocent the whole time. He goes through two trials, one involving a second woman attacked the same night by the same man, and ends up with life plus 54 years. In prison there's another inmate, Bobby Poole, who apparently looks similar enough to Cotton that people mix them up, and who's boasting about the attacks. Poole gets put in front of Thompson at a hearing after the trial and she says she has never seen him before in her life. March 1995, the DNA from the rape kits comes back, Cotton is excluded, it matches Poole, Cotton walks out after about ten and a half years. Thompson and Cotton later become friends and now travel around together pushing for changes in how lineups get run.

What bothers me is that everyone did what they were supposed to. She wasn't lying. The jury wasn't stupid. By the end she was more sure than at the beginning, and she had no way to notice that.

So here's what I want. I've read a bit of Hume on testimony and something about Reid pushing back, and I gather the modern versions get called reductionism and anti-reductionism — roughly, one side says you need positive reasons, cashed out in your own perception and induction, before you're entitled to believe someone, and the other says testimony is a basic source so you're entitled by default and the burden is on whoever has a reason to doubt. Run this case through both of them, properly, and then tell me which one the case actually favors, or whether it favors neither and instead shows a hole both of them have.

Some specific things I want you to be disciplined about:

Who is the testifier and who is the hearer at each of the three moments — the photo array, the courtroom identification, the Poole hearing? Note that Thompson is a witness to the jury but she's also a hearer herself, of the officer who ran the lineup and reassured her afterwards that she'd done fine. What do the two views do with that second role?

Her positive reasons. She was close, she was paying attention on purpose, she had time. Does the reductionist get to count those as reasons? They're her own report about the conditions of her memory, so the reason and the thing it supports come out of the same person and the same faculty. I can't see how reductionism escapes that without making almost all testimony unusable. Does anti-reductionism inherit the same problem, or is it genuinely cleaner here?

The officer's reassurance. I have an idea I call the ratchet and I want you to take it seriously rather than be polite about it: in a case like this, confidence only ever goes up. Every retelling firms it up, every person who believes you firms it up, the trial prep firms it up, and nobody in the building has the job of making the witness less sure. Which view can even describe a ratchet? Anti-reductionism leans on defeaters — but the defeater (that an officer had patted her on the back right after the pick) was never in front of the jury, so relative to the evidence the jury had, there was nothing to defeat anything. Was the verdict justified? Be explicit about whether justification gets scored on what the hearer had or on what was true in the world, and whether the two views score it differently.

And don't let reductionism off easy just because the outcome was bad. If a careful reductionist juror in 1985 had actually run the checks — lighting, distance, attention, motive to lie, consistency — I think they land on accept, same as everybody else. If that's right then both views tell you to convict, and the case is not a win for either. Tell me whether that's right or whether one of them has a check that actually fires here.

Where does the reductionist get the base rate for how often eyewitness identifications are wrong? If the only route to that number is more testimony, about studies, does the view go in a circle, and does the anti-reductionist have an advantage there or the same problem?

Then the design question. Is a courtroom a reductionist machine or an anti-reductionist one? Sort the parts — the oath, cross-examination, corroboration rules, reasonable doubt, the fact that the witness gets prepped by one side — into which view each one serves. Give me two changes each view would recommend on its own logic. And say whether the reforms people actually pushed for after cases like this (having someone who doesn't know who the suspect is run the lineup, showing the photos one at a time instead of all together, making the witness state how confident they are out loud at the moment of the pick and writing it down right then) follow from one of the two views, both, or neither.

Finally, tell me where each view just goes quiet. My suspicion is that both of them picture testimony as somebody handing over a belief they already had, and neither has a slot for a belief that gets manufactured by the process of asking for it. If that's right, is this episode a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theories were never built to handle? And if there's a version of the debate — I've seen "local" and "global" somewhere — where my question gets a different answer, flag it briefly instead of pretending there's one single position on each side.

No bibliography, no reading list, no page numbers — I can't look anything up and I'd rather have your reasoning than invented citations. Plain names for the positions are fine.

End the whole thing with a line that's exactly Verdict: followed by one word, either reductionism, antireductionism, or neither.
