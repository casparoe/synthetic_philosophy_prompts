So I got voluntold to write the conceptual section of our org's methods guide — the part that's supposed to tell survey teams when they're allowed to stratify by birth cohort and when they're just doing astrology for journalists. This came out of an arguement at lunch: one colleague thinks "Boomer" and "Gen Z" are as real as class categories, another thinks they're marketing noise with a fake birth-year range stapled on, and honestly I don't know which of them I'm closer to. The standard I need is practical: an account good enough that a research team could defend using generational variables in a report, and good enough that a critic could point at the specific step that was illegitimate. Before I read another take on it, I want the spec.

Write the desiderata — what any adequate account of what generations are has to deliver. For each one, give the case or the consideration behind it, not just the label. Things I'd expect to see, feel free to add others or tell me I'm wrong to want them: the boundary problem (why does the Millennial/Gen Z line fall where the research firms put it, and what would make any cut non-arbitrary — is sharpness even the right thing to want here?); the heterogeneity problem (someone born in 1998 in Seoul, in Lagos, and in rural Ohio share a label and almost nothing else); the within-cohort stratification problem (class, race, and region seem to do more explanatory work than birth year, so what's actually left over for the generation?); the history problem (were there generations before anyone had the concept — people born in the 1650s, say — or does the kind come into being along with the label?); the reflexivity problem (once you're told you're Gen Z you can perform it, which looks like it makes the classification partly self-fulfilling, and I don't know if that's a bug or the whole point); and the measurement problem, which is the one I understand least — in age-period-cohort models cohort is just period minus age, so the three linear trends are perfectly collinear and you can't seperate them without extra assumptions. Tell me what an ontology of generations is supposed to say about that, because if the statistics can't identify a cohort effect, what is the metaphysician adding? Also: does the account owe an explanation of why these categories are so sticky in institutions even if they track little, and does it have to constrain inference — i.e. block the slide from "Gen Z reports more anxiety" to "this 22-year-old job applicant is anxious"? And it should say what a generation is meant to be, a set, a distribution, a causal cluster, a narrative, a self-sorting kind, since that choice looks like it's doing a lot of quiet work.

Then mark which of these are non-negotiable and which are really one camp's prejudices smuggled in as criteria (my suspicion is that "must have determinate boundaries" is a prejudice, but argue with me). Flag any pair or trio you think can't be jointly satisfied — if the list turns out to be unsatisfiable, that's a result and I'd much rather know before writing, because it would mean my second colleague is right and I should say so. Say which single requirement knocks out the most candidates. And then briefly, a couple of sentences each at most: how do the main contenders do against the list — eliminativism, the formative-exposure view where shared historical timing produces durable dispositions, the view that they're real because people enact them and sort themselves into them, and the deflationary line that a cohort is just a birth-year bin, no more mysterious than a height bin. No bibliography and please don't invent citations, I'll handle the references myself.
