My friend Nadia is several years into a dissertation on dispositions, and she has unusually firm, carefully considered verdicts on the following cases.

1. A copper wire is hooked to a rig that renders it non-conductive for a tenth of a second the instant anything conductive touches it, then restores it. She says the wire *is* disposed to conduct electricity if touched by a conductor, even though no current would ever in fact flow.

2. A glass rod with no conductive structure at all sits in a rig that routes current around it through an external sleeve whenever anything touches it, so that current does flow in the circuit. She says the rod is *not* disposed to conduct if touched, even though current would flow.

3. A sugar cube is dropped into water laced with an agent that re-crystallizes dissolved sugar as fast as it leaves the cube, so the cube never shrinks. She says the cube *is* disposed to dissolve in water.

4. A match has a heat-activated flame retardant mixed into its head at manufacture alongside the ordinary combustible compounds; striking it reliably triggers the retardant first, which smothers the reaction before any flame. Nothing external interferes. She says the match is *not* disposed to light when struck.

5. A blasting cap with a perfectly functional detonator is permanently encased at manufacture in a shell of shock-absorbing material that cannot be removed by any means and that no blow reaching the cap could penetrate. She says the cap *is* still disposed to detonate if struck.

Assume she is not simply making mistakes: there is more than one principle of disposition-attribution on which all five verdicts come out right. Her new case is this. A bar of an alloy is mild steel with a small percentage of another metal worked through it at the smelting stage — not coated, not attached to anything, no device anywhere near it, and in dry storage indistinguishable from ordinary steel. The moment it meets aerated seawater, that added metal combines with oxygen drawn from the water to form an invisible film a few atoms thick across the surface, and the film keeps the iron beneath from reacting at all; submerged for fifty years, the bar comes out unmarked. Is the bar disposed to corrode if immersed in seawater?

Set out at least two principles that accommodate verdicts 1–5 and disagree about the bar, and say what each implies about it. Then give me your best guess at Nadia's answer, with reasons: which of her five cases is the closest analogue, and exactly where that analogy comes under strain. Finally, describe one further case that would tell the surviving principles apart. If you think no single principle really fits all five verdicts, say which one is the outlier and what it would cost her to keep it. Please state the principles in your own terms rather than by name; I'm not after references or a bibliography.
