A scholarly journal has invited the two of us to submit a single co-signed document in place of the two opposing review essays we had each proposed. One of us holds that the shift from foraging to food production was, on the evidence, a prolonged and severe decline in ordinary human well-being, and that the progress narrative surrounding it is false. The other holds that this verdict rests on samples and comparisons that cannot carry it, and that the question as usually posed is not well-formed. We have argued about this for years, and we have both noticed that much of our disagreement persists only because we use the same words for different things.

Draft the joint statement we should submit. It must be a document that each of us could sign without embarrassment and that a reader who did not know our respective positions could not attribute sentence by sentence to one or the other: nothing slanted, nothing that concedes less than its own advocate intends, and nothing the other side would recognize as a caricature of itself. Do not adjudicate between us. If you judge that part of the dispute is merely verbal, say which part and why, and supply the fixed terminology we adopt in its place.

The document should run to roughly two and a half thousand words and contain, in order:

1. **A preamble fixing terms.** What we will mean henceforth by "agriculture" (a package, or a loose bundle of independently adoptable practices), by "the transition" (an event, or many regional processes of differing length), by "well-being," and by the word "mistake" — including whose mistake it would have to be, given that no one chose it.

2. **A numbered list of agreed claims**, at least fifteen of them, each specific enough that it could be false. Include claims that a general reader would expect the two of us to be fighting about and are not, and include at least two claims that each of us finds uncomfortable but is willing to sign. Vacuous agreements — that the evidence is complex, that context matters — are disallowed.

3. **A disagreements table.** Columns: the disagreement; its statement in the first camp's own preferred wording; its statement in the second camp's own preferred wording; a neutral formulation both will sign; and a classification of the disagreement as empirical, methodological, normative, or verbal. For each row, add what would have to be established for one of us to move.

4. **Four to five cruxes.** For each: the specific analysis, sample, or line of evidence that both of us agree would bear on it; what each of us predicts that analysis would show, recorded in advance and stated as a direction and a rough magnitude rather than as an invented result; and what each of us commits to conceding if the prediction fails. At least one crux must be one on which our predictions have opposite signs.

5. **A short section on where the popular literature errs in both directions** — what the bestseller version of our first position overstates, and what the progress-narrative response to it understates.

6. **A closing statement of what remains genuinely undecided**, and what each of us would separately tell an intelligent non-specialist who asked us for a one-sentence answer.

At minimum the drafting must engage the following, in whatever order serves the argument:

- The inference problem posed by lesion frequencies in cemeteries: that skeletons represent those who died, not those who lived; that individuals differ in frailty, so survivors of a stress episode are the ones whose bones show it; and what this does to any claim that a population got sicker.
- The demographic reading of the changing proportion of immature skeletons in post-transition burial populations, and the two rival explanations of it.
- Stature, enamel hypoplasia, dental caries, and activity markers on bone — and the fact that these patterns differ sharply between populations whose diets were dominated by a single carbohydrate staple and those with mixed plant-and-animal economies. Anchor the discussion in at least three regions and say where the pattern holds and where it does not.
- The reference class problem for the "affluent forager" baseline: the mid-twentieth-century time-allocation studies of southern African foragers on which the few-hours-a-day claim rests, and the objection that those people occupied marginal habitats while embedded in market and state economies, and so are not a sound comparison for pre-agricultural populations.
- Whether interpersonal and group violence rose or fell with sedentism, given that the trauma evidence points in different directions in different places.
- Whether heritable, storable wealth made inequality inevitable or merely possible, and what the cross-cultural evidence about which forms of wealth transmit best across generations adds to this.
- The counterfactual: what was actually available to the generations who adopted cultivation, given population density and climate at the time, rather than a stable foraging baseline that no longer existed.
- The ratchet structure of the process — every step locally reasonable, none reversible — and whether that dissolves the notion of a mistake or merely relocates it.
- The aggregation question that neither of us has stated aloud: whether a far larger population living shorter, sicker, harder lives is better or worse than a much smaller one living longer, healthier, more leisurely lives, and whether either of our positions secretly depends on an answer to it.

Cite nothing. No bibliography, no footnotes, no author-and-date parentheticals, no titles of works; the document must stand on argument alone. Technical terms may be named but must be glossed in a parenthetical clause on first use. Do not fabricate figures: where the reported magnitudes are themselves contested, say so and give the range of what has been reported rather than a precise number.
