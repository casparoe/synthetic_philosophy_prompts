I'm assembling a small anthology of poems about problems in logic, and I want an entry on the asymmetry between indicative and counterfactual conditionals. Write a sonnet with a genuine volta between octave and sestet, and have the sestet reuse the octave's two rhyme sounds in a new order, so that the sameness of the material and the difference in what is being done with it are visible on the page before the reader has worked out why. The octave should carry the Oswald case in the indicative — that if he didn't shoot Kennedy, someone else did — which anyone who believes Kennedy was shot by somebody accepts; the sestet should carry the same content in the subjunctive — that if he hadn't, someone else would have — which almost no one accepts. The turn ought to land on the fact that the two differ only in mood, and that asserting the indicative isn't describing something a truth-functional account would make true in every world where the shooting never happened, but is closer to reporting what one is prepared to say on supposing the antecedent. Get the pressure right: the acceptability of the indicative tracks something like the probability of the consequent given the antecedent, whereas the counterfactual answers to how the nearest antecedent-world turns out. No naming of theories, authors, or possible-worlds machinery inside the poem, and no resolution — the couplet must not deliver a verdict on whether indicatives have truth conditions at all. A reader who knows the literature should be able to say which account the poem is written from and which line would come out false on the main rival. After the poem, give me four or five sentences saying which account of the indicative you committed to, where the verse takes liberties with it, and whether the couplet survives the rival reading; take a definite position there rather than laying the options out for me.
