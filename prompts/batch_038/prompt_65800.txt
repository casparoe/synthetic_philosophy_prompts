For twenty-six years I earned my living walking into damaged buildings within hours of an earthquake and deciding whether people could go back inside. I have no academic training in philosophy or statistics; I came to Keynes's Treatise on Probability as a retiree and have worked through it slowly, twice. What I think I found there is a distinction my profession quietly discarded. Keynes separates the probability that an argument confers on a conclusion from the weight of the evidence standing behind it: new evidence can leave the probability exactly where it was while giving the conclusion a firmer footing. He also concedes, so far as I can make out, that only in a restricted class of cases can we say that one argument carries more weight than another.

The claim I intend to defend in a twenty-five-minute talk is that the push in post-disaster safety assessment toward a single numeric vulnerability score is a loss rather than a refinement, because two assessments reporting the same figure can differ enormously in weight — and the calls that held up over my career were the ones where we kept asking how we knew what we knew rather than what the number was. I want to go past complaint. My own working view is that weight splits into three things Keynes runs together: how many genuinely independent lines of evidence support the figure; how closely the structure in front of me resembles the stock the figure was derived from; and how much of what would have settled the matter was simply not observable that afternoon. My hunch is that the supposed unmeasurability of weight survives mainly because those three are conflated, and that at least two of them can be pinned down.

The room will be mostly practicing structural engineers, two academics who build fragility curves and Bayesian networks for a living, and one official from the state building-code office. None of them will have read Keynes.

Please prepare me for the question period. Begin with a verdict in two sentences on whether this thesis survives contact with that audience or collapses into apparatus they already have. Then give me no more than eight objections, ordered by how much damage each would do, phrased the way a probabilist or a code official would actually phrase it, each with a reply I could deliver standing up in about ninety seconds and one sentence naming the softest point in that reply. Where a technical term is doing the work in an objection, gloss it in a phrase, since some of this vocabulary will be new to me. Press hardest on the objection a colleague has already put to me: that what I call weight is what risk analysts mean by epistemic as against aleatory uncertainty, or what the formal work on imprecise probabilities already handles, so that I am offering a redescription with no new consequences. Tell me plainly which of my three components that audience would grant is measurable and which they would dismiss as a mood, what I should concede in my opening minutes so that the concessions cost least, and the one question I will be hoping nobody asks.

I do not want references or a reading list; I will track anything down myself. Keep the whole thing under nine hundred words.
