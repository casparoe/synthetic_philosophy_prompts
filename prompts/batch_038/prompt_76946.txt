I run a 90-minute upper-level philosophy of mind seminar with 16 students who can recite "the brain minimizes prediction error" but have not felt what that commits them to. Design an activity, using nothing but paper, pencils, and perhaps a deck of cards, in which the students make a run of noisy perceptual discriminations under an explicit scoring rule and discover by playing that a scheme which rewards accuracy while permitting abstention drives the rational player to sit out nearly every trial — the same shape as the objection that a system devoted purely to minimizing prediction error would do best to switch off its senses. Then a second phase in which abstaining costs something and taking another look also costs something, so they must trade sampling against guessing, plus ideally a round where a prior they were told is only usually right pulls on their judgments too hard. Give me the rules, a cheap way to manufacture the noise, a payoff scheme that needs no real money, a minute-by-minute timeline, and a debrief that maps specific moments onto precision weighting, epistemic versus pragmatic value, and the dark-room objection. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — above all, where students are liable to conclude the game is simply broken rather than feel the point — and be candid about which feature of the framework no classroom game can reproduce and I will have to supply in words. Throughout, mark what is settled against what remains genuinely contested among people who work on the free energy principle, and do not hand me a contested reading as if it were the consensus.
