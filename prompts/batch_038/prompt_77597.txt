Last spring our head of government stood in the legislature and apologized, on behalf of the country, for a policy of removing children from their families several decades earlier — a policy that everyone now agrees was monstrous, though it was perfectly legal at the time and was carried out by civil servants who thought they were doing good. I was watching with a friend, and I said something aloud that I have not been able to put down since: that I could not see how his sentence could even mean what it appeared to mean. My reasoning goes like this, and I would like you to check it rather than comfort me. An apology is offered by a person for a wrong that person committed; that is what distinguishes it from an expression of sympathy. The people who designed and executed that policy are dead. The man who spoke was not born when it happened, and neither were most of the citizens in whose name he spoke. So the "we" in his apology seems to have no referent — or, if it does, it picks out a group that did nothing. If I apologized to you for a cruel remark my late grandfather made to yours, you would reasonably tell me there is nothing for me to apologize for. I have been working very slowly through part of J. L. Austin's book on performative utterances, and I got as far as the idea that some utterances do things rather than describe them, and that they misfire when the procedure is invoked by someone not in a position to invoke it. So a state apology for a historical wrong looks to me like precisely that: the right words, in the right solemn register, spoken by someone with no standing to speak them, and therefore nothing actually accomplished. I would simply conclude the whole practice is theatre, except for one fact I cannot reconcile with that verdict, which is that the survivors and their descendants I have heard interviewed very often say the apology mattered to them more than anything else that was offered. That makes me suspect it is my model that is broken somewhere, not theirs. But I have not been able to locate the break.

So my question is narrow: which step is wrong? I am not asking for a defense of state apologies in general, and I would rather you not hedge. Tell me the specific point at which my reasoning derails — whether it is my account of what an apology is, my account of what a state is, or my analogy to a misfired performative — and if the answer is that an apology made by an institution that outlives every one of its members is a different kind of act from a personal apology, then tell me what that different kind of act commits the apologizer to. I suspect that is where the real difficulty sits, because it seems to run into a second problem I keep circling: if the living citizens did none of it, what exactly are they answerable for, and is an apology that costs the apologizer nothing but words an apology at all? For context, I am a retired civil engineer with no philosophy training, and I first got interested in this because the local history society I belong to spent two meetings arguing about whether our town should make its own acknowledgment of the same policy; I ended up on both sides and would like to stop. Please open with one sentence naming the false assumption you think I am making, then explain, and keep the entire answer under 350 words. Plain language is welcome — but do not soften the argument for my sake.
