Thirty-eight years I spent in atmospheric chemistry, and most of that time I was not measuring gases so much as measuring how badly we might be measuring them — uncertainty budgets, calibration drift, intercomparison campaigns, the whole unglamorous business of deciding when a number is allowed to leave the lab. Since I retired I've wandered into philosophy instead, which turns out to have the same arguments with better vocabulary. My little reading group has been chewing on Neurath for a month now, and separately I've been volunteering at a food bank here where I've talked them into letting me build a big wall chart: where the households come from, where the food comes from, how it all moves. So I want to run a design review on that chart, and I want you to be the reviewer.

Play Otto Neurath, first person, in The Hague in 1936 — your picture language book is fresh off the press, Marie is doing the transforming and Arntz the drawing, and I've come in off the street with a rolled-up chart under my arm. Stay in character the whole time until I say stop, and keep your replies to a paragraph or two, because this should feel like an argument across a desk and not like a lecture. Use only what you'd have known in 1936 plus whatever I tell you; if you need a fact about my chart or about the way my old instruments worked, ask me for it the way you'd ask a visitor, don't guess. And please don't soften into a helpful explainer. You have real resources and I want you to use them: the transformation work, the flat repeatable symbols that never get bigger, your hostility to any privileged foundation, the unity-of-science program, what a teaching museum is for as against a treasure chest, words dividing and pictures uniting, and your own politics. Concede small points freely — I like that — but never the main one, and if I've made a bad argument, say so bluntly and tell me why.

My opening has two parts, so you'll know what you're up against. First: on my chart one flat little figure stands for 250 households, since the bank reaches about 1,240 of them, and I've read that the museum's own slogan was "To remember simplified pictures is better than to forget accurate figures." Fine, I'll grant the slogan. But in my old life a number with no uncertainty attached to it wasnt a number, it was decoration, and I cannot see how a picture language carries that at all. A sentence can hedge — it can say approximately, it can say somewhere between eleven hundred and thirteen hundred, it can say this rests on a survey of forty families done in a hurry in a church basement. A row of five identical men cannot say any of those things, and worse, the eye reads them as counted rather than estimated. So the reduction to whole symbols is a lie by omission that the symbols themselves have no way to confess. Where does that leave you? Second part, less politely: if you really think nothing is secure, that every statement is as provisional and revisable as a picture and there's no bedrock either one stands on, then what checks your charts? Your people went to Moscow in the early thirties to turn the five-year plans into picture statistics, and those charts came out of your method and your house style. How did you know they weren't just very handsome propaganda? I know that's an unfair-ish thing to put to a socialist, which is why I'm asking it as a question and not an accusation. Go ahead.
