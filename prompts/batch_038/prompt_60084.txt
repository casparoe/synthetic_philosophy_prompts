I'm about 70% through the draft of my fourth novel and I've backed myself into a corner that I think a philosopher could get me out of, or at least measure for me. One of my two leads, Vera Kestrel, is a retired game theorist who's fallen out of academia and makes her living off a small paid newsletter called LAST ROUND. Subscribers send her a description of a situation they're actually standing in — a supplier standoff, a land deal, a custody fight with money in it — and she tells them what the rational play is and why. Each of these is a set piece at the end of a chapter, and I've written and locked eight of them, locked for real, because in chapter 26 another character reads one back at her out loud, nearly word for word, and my editor already has those pages. Here they are, in my paraphrase of her voice:

- Two strangers, one round of the usual confess-or-stay-quiet setup, each certain the other cares only about their own payoff. Verdict: confess. She tells the subscriber that cooperating there isn't decency, it's arithmetic he declined to do.
- The same game played exactly 100 times, both told the exact count in advance, both certain the other is a hard calculator. Verdict: confess every round, first one included. She says the number of rounds is a red herring.
- The same game with no announced end, just a stated nine-in-ten chance of another round after any given round. Verdict: a working cooperative arrangement is rational and can hold indefinitely. Her printed reason is that there's no last round to argue backwards from.
- The take-or-pass game (my consultant calls it the centipede, I call it the take-or-pass game in the book), two nodes only. Verdict: take, immediately.
- Same game, six nodes, the players anonymous and never doing business again. Verdict: take, immediately — and she adds that the case does not get weaker as the game gets longer, and that subscribers who feel it does are reporting what they'd like to do rather than what they'd be entitled to do.
- A near-monopolist facing twenty would-be competitors, one market at a time, all twenty visible in advance. Verdict: making a bloody example of the first entrant is not rational, even though a reputation for it would be worth a fortune. She gives two reasons and the readers inside the book have been fighting about which one she means: the threat is empty because you'll flinch when the day comes, and anyway the entrants aren't fools.
- A subscriber who, before a race-to-the-intersection game, announced publicly that he was keeping his foot down. Verdict: worse than useless. Noise that a calculator ignores and only a fool believes.
- A subscriber who, before the same game, unbolted his steering wheel and threw it out the window in full view of the other driver. Verdict: rational, and the other driver should now fold.

That last pair is what my beta readers keep tripping over, since it looks flatly at odds with the monopolist ruling, and I don't think it is, but I can't say why in one sentence, which is a bad sign for me.

Chapter 21 is the one I need help with. A subscriber writes that an agency has matched her into a six-node take-or-pass game — standard shape, the pot climbs as you go, taking ends it, passing lets it grow. In her numbers: taking at her first node nets her 1 and her opponent nothing; if she passes and he takes at his first node she gets nothing at all; if nobody ever takes, she ends with 8. The agency has told her, and told him, that roughly one client in five is what they call a softie, someone who cant stand ending a game early and will keep passing no matter what — but the agency won't say which clients are which. She is not a softie, and her opponent has been told as much, so there's no mystery about her. Once a softie passes at his first node she'll know exactly what she's got. What does Vera print?

Give me the analysis a smart reader who knows this material cold would give me, minus the sourcing — I have an academic consultant on retainer for footnotes and I specifically don't want a reading list or paper titles, just the reasoning. Concretely: lay out the candidate rules Vera could be running that fit all eight locked verdicts at once, steering wheel and monopolist included, and tell me how many genuinely distinct rules there are once you've done the work; say what each of them predicts she tells the chapter-21 subscriber, and whether they actually diverge or all land in the same place on this one; then commit to a best guess at her answer and, more important for me, at the reason she'd print, because the character in chapter 26 quotes her in order to accuse her of one specific kind of inconsistency and I need to know which accusation the scene can bear. Flag anything in my description of the letter that's doing hidden work — I have a creeping feeling the phrase "cant stand ending a game early" is load-bearing in a way I never intended. And invent me one more subscriber letter, concrete enough that I could drop it into chapter 9 or 12, whose verdict would settle which rule she's on, so the chapter-21 answer reads as earned instead of convenient. American English, please. And don't be diplomatic about it: if these eight verdicts are simply inconsistent and I need to go rewrite chapter 26, I'd much rather hear that now than in copyedit.
