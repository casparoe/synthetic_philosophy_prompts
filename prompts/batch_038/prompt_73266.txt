Three months ago I moved from Chicago to Berlin for a two-year secondment, and before I left I sat down and wrote out an argument to settle something my partner and I had been circling since the offer letter arrived: whether a newcomer owes it to the locals to follow conventions he has not endorsed and in some cases actively dislikes. I wanted a principle I could apply mechanically at the crossing with no traffic at two in the morning, at the card terminal with the tip line, at a counter where everyone else is visibly queuing according to some rule I have not yet inferred, and at the office collection for a colleague I have met twice. The argument was supposed to end in a modest conclusion about how much conformity a visitor owes. It ends somewhere else, and I cannot find the crack.

(1) If I accept a benefit that others produce by restraining themselves, then fairness requires me to accept the same restraint when I am in a position to take that benefit without paying for it.

(2) Orderly service at a counter, a predictable crossing, and a meal brought to my table are benefits produced by exactly that kind of restraint: each person in the line, at the curb, and behind the register is forgoing an advantage they could take, and the scheme asks the same forbearance of me in return.

(3) So fairness requires me to queue as locals queue, to wait at the signal even when the street is empty, and to supplement a service worker's wage in the local manner and the local amount — the euro or two rounded up rather than a fifth of the bill — even where I think the arrangement the supplement props up is unjust.

(4) Nothing in the move from (1) to (3) turns on which practices these are. The same reasoning reaches every scheme in which others' forbearance produces benefits I accept: buying the ring whose price depends on everyone going on buying rings, standing when the judge enters, buying the round after I have drunk the rounds bought for me, paying into the collection for a departing colleague, sending cash to a wedding I attended only because I was asked.

(5) Fairness requires none of those. I can enjoy an orderly courtroom and a flourishing jewelry trade without the slightest unfairness in staying seated and staying unmarried.

(6) So (1) is false, or at least stands in need of a restriction I have not supplied.

(7) The obvious restriction — that the practice must solve a coordination problem or prevent harm — does no work here. Ring-buying, round-buying, and standing for judges coordinate expectations and prevent real friction and hurt feelings; the queue and the crossing signal do the same, only more visibly. The restriction fails to sort my keepers from my escapers.

(8) Nor does restricting (1) to practices whose rules I could reasonably have agreed to. Neither set is of my choosing: I no more chose the queue than the ring.

(9) So the fairness of participating in a practice never by itself obliges me to conform to it. What I owe the locals is only what independent reasons already require: do not shove, do not cause collisions, do not defraud, do not insult deliberately. Convention as such obliges a newcomer to nothing.

(10) But promising looks like a practice of precisely that kind. Apart from a convention that turns certain sounds into the undertaking of an obligation, there is nothing morally loaded about my saying that I will meet you at the station at six; the wrong of breaking the promise is generated by the practice rather than by any harm that exists independently of it.

(11) So if (9) holds, promise-breaking is not wrong in the practice-generated way everyone supposes it is.

(12) That I cannot accept. My lease, my lab's data-sharing agreement, and the reason my neighbors let me collect their mail all rest on the falsity of (11).

Every step looked fine to me when I wrote it, and it has looked fine on a dozen rereadings since. I would rather abandon almost anything in the list than abandon (1) or (12): I am attached to the thought that taking a benefit while refusing its cost is a distinct kind of wrong, and I cannot run my life on the idea that my word generates nothing.

Tell me where it breaks. I want a diagnosis rather than a survey: pick the step you would reject and defend the pick, explain what made that step look innocuous when I wrote it, and then price the alternatives — what I am left holding if I instead give up (1), or (5), or (10), since I assume each of those has a defensible version and I want to know what each costs. If you think there are two independent cracks, say which one is load-bearing. And end by telling me, without hedging, what I should do at the empty crossing at two in the morning and what I should enter on the tip line, since (9) and (12) appear to give different answers.
