Last night I had a three-hour argument with a friend of mine — she consults on organizational decision-making and wrote her undergraduate thesis on escalation of commitment — and she holds the orthodox position in its most uncompromising form: that once a cost is genuinely sunk it can never figure in a rational decision, and that every supposed counterexample dissolves on inspection into some *other* thing that has changed. She treats the textbook characterization, the one from the Arkes and Blumer paper that made the term standard — a greater tendency to persist with an endeavor once money, effort, or time has been sunk in it — as picking out a bias by definition rather than a sometimes-correct habit. I thought I had counterexamples, and by the end of the evening she had defeated three of them. The season-ticket case fails because the ticket-holder is really deciding how to spend an evening, not how to recover money. The reputation case fails because what does the work there is what other people will infer, not the expenditure itself. The "this project is now part of who I am" case fails because the investment changed the payoffs: finishing is worth more to me now than it was before, so I am responding to a new value rather than honoring a dead one. What she has not defeated, and what I want your help constructing, is a case in which none of those escape routes is available.

Here is the specification. Build me a thought experiment in which honoring a sunk cost is not merely excusable but required of a fully rational agent, on an evidential mechanism rather than a reputational or identity-based one. The setting should be entirely ordinary and contemporary — a small business, a piece of land, a house, a research project, a wine cellar — with no exotic physics, no perfect predictors, and payoffs of a size a normal person might face; one paragraph for the scenario and one paragraph for a matched control. The mechanism I have in mind is this: at an earlier time the agent invested heavily on the basis of private information she once had and can no longer reconstruct — the documents are gone, or the person who told her is unreachable, or she simply cannot retrieve her own reasoning — so that the bare fact of her past expenditure is now the only surviving trace of that information, and therefore itself evidence about the state of the world. The constraints she will insist on, and which you must respect: no one ever learns of her decision at the later time, so there is no reputation, no signaling, and no effect on her future credibility; the expenditure is unrecoverable, has no resale value, and has produced no skill, taste, attachment, or option that would raise what finishing is worth to her; and the objective payoffs of the remaining choices, conditional on each state of the world, are identical for her and for a matched counterpart who inherited the same project without ever having invested in it. Give me the numbers — priors, likelihoods, utilities, small integers are fine — and show the expected-value comparison at the decision point for both agents, so that the counterpart should abandon, the investor should continue, and both are reasoning impeccably: no weakness of will, no motivated reasoning, no self-deception, and no inference she could not state aloud and defend.

Then run the checks. Tell me which feature of the case is doing the work, and give me two ablations: what happens if she had kept a written record and could re-read the original evidence, and what happens if the investment had been made for a reason that carries no information at all — under a misapprehension about a deadline, say, or by a rule her accountant applied mechanically. Say which ablation destroys the case and which merely weakens it. Next, give me her best reply, which I expect will be that I have not found a rational sunk cost but a piece of evidence that happens to be stored in my own history: the expenditure matters only insofar as it is informative, and informativeness is a payoff-relevant feature like any other, so the strict irrelevance of *costs as costs* is untouched. Tell me whether that reply is substantive or merely a redescription, and what it implies about how the fallacy should be defined — in terms of treating a cost as a reason in itself, versus treating a cost as evidence when in fact it carries none. Finally, say in two or three sentences whether any non-evidential route to honoring sunk costs could survive her constraints, or whether the evidential route is the only one left standing. I do not want references or a reading list and please do not invent any; what I need is something precise enough to forward to her this week, and a case that generates its verdict from the numbers rather than announcing it — a scenario that simply stipulates the investor is rational will not convince her, and it should not convince me either.
