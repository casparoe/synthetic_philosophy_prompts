I want to defend a rather radical form of disjunctivism, and I want to know what would defeat it. The view: in the good case, a perceptual experience is a relation to the mind-independent object itself, so its character is partly constituted by that object; in the hallucination case there is no experience at all — not an episode with a phenomenal character that fails to match the world, but only a bundle of dispositions (to reach, to assent, to report) that explains why the subject cannot tell the difference and why no introspective mark separates the two cases. Indistinguishability, on this account, is a fact about what the subject can and cannot discriminate, not evidence of a shared ingredient.

First, tell me what would have to be true for this to be wrong: what the psychology and neuroscience of hallucination and mental imagery would have to show, what a notion of experience must be able to do if it is to earn its place in explanations of behavior and of perceptual knowledge, and which cases — drug-induced, pathological, dreamlike, or merely ordinary — the view cannot describe. Second, say what the position predicts that a sense-datum or common-factor rival does not, and whether those predictions could come out badly. Then rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, and be explicit about which ones disjunctivists themselves tend to concede rather than wave away. Finally, give me a straight answer on whether turning the bad case into an absence has insulated the view to the point where nothing could count against it, and whether that insulation is a flaw or a legitimate strength for a thesis of this kind.

Please write in continuous prose, as you would in a letter — no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables. Tell me how confident you are in your main claims and single out the point you are least sure of.
