I run a monthly reading group for graduate students in a joint economics–philosophy program, and this term's topic is what utility claims are actually claiming. The group keeps talking past itself: half the room treats "she chose it" as settling what she prefers, half treats it as settling what is good for her, and nobody notices that these are two different arguments. I want a self-diagnostic instrument — a sorting quiz with a scoring key — that readers can take before the first session, which locates them in the space of views about the meaning of utility rather than asking them to self-identify.

The audience has had one semester of intermediate micro and some philosophy; they know what an indifference curve is and have met expected utility theory, but they will not reliably recognize the names of positions. The instrument must therefore sort them by what they say about cases. Nine questions, each a concrete scenario with two or three answer options. No option may name a school, a philosopher, or an ism, and no option may turn on a term of art (ordinal, cardinal, welfarist, betterness, revealed, all-things-considered): every option should be something a reader can pick out of their own reaction to the case. The five questions below must appear, in substance; you may tighten the wording and re-order, but keep what makes them discriminating. The other four are yours to write, and they must between them probe (i) whether utilities can be compared across people and whether they are more than rankings, (ii) what an indifference between two options shows — equal utility, or merely nothing observable that breaks the tie, (iii) a case in which satisfying an agent's preferences seems to make their life go worse by some measure of how their days actually feel, and (iv) whether the mental-state vocabulary of wanting and preferring survives at all, or is just a loose description of behavior plus circumstances.

The five fixed questions, and my own answers to them, which you will need at the end:

1. A machinist has been passed over for the supervisor post three times and has stopped applying. She says she never wanted it. Her choices and her words agree. Does this pattern tell you what would make her life go better? (a) Yes — choice is the only evidence not already contaminated by theory. (b) It tells you what she wants, but her wants were shaped by the options she was refused, so it tells you nothing about her good. (c) It tells you nothing yet — she may be choosing against her own settled evaluation. **My answer: (b).**

2. One slice of cake is left. Guest A passes on it, though he would have enjoyed it. Guest B also passes, but he decided in advance that he would never take the last slice, because he wants to be the sort of person who doesn't. What do the two refusals show? (a) The same thing: in each case a ranking over what was available, expressed in action. (b) Different things — B's refusal is governed by a view about which person to be, so his action is poor evidence about how he ranks the slices. (c) Neither shows much; the interesting ranking is over states of the world, not over acts. **My answer: (b).**

3. A friend is wretched in work she loves and believes in. A pill would remove the wretchedness and leave her projects, her convictions, and her behavior untouched. Would she be better off on the pill? (a) Yes — misery is bad for her, and the work would still be there. (b) No — if what she wants and what she does are unchanged, nothing that matters to her has changed. (c) Only she can settle it: ask which of the two lives she would take if both were offered. **My answer: (b).**

4. A café sells espresso and tea. A regular takes espresso every morning for a year. The café adds an elaborate pour-over to the board, and the regular switches to tea and stays with tea. How should this be described? (a) The new item changed what ordering the espresso meant, so what he ranks is not the drink alone but the whole situation of choosing it. (b) He simply has inconsistent preferences, and the honest move is to record the inconsistency. (c) Nothing here is a preference change; the word was only ever a way of compressing his choices, and the compression changed. **My answer: (a).**

5. A patient elects an operation she has been told she will afterwards wish she had declined. Whose view decides? (a) Hers now — the agent at the time of choice is the one whose evaluation counts. (b) Hers later — the person who will live with the outcome is the one it happens to. (c) Neither — what should decide is which course leaves her better off overall, summed across both of her. **My answer: (a).**

Structure the deliverable in three parts. First, lay out the logical space before you name anything: identify the questions that actually divide the views — whether choice is evidence of a separate mental state or the whole of what there is; whether that mental state, when it exists, is a ranking of options, an evaluation of outcomes, or a judgement about how to live; whether it is distorted or rather constituted by the conditions under which it forms; whether it is interpersonally comparable and cardinal; whether it tracks the agent's good, and if so why — and show which combinations of answers those questions permit. Build the tree from that, not from a roster of positions, and then locate the actual views in it.

Second, give the quiz itself: nine cases with options, then a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves. I want at most eight leaves, and they must cover at least: a view on which choice is all there is and utility talk is a behavioral summary; a view on which preference is a genuine all-in mental evaluation that choice only imperfectly reveals; a view on which choice systematically fails to track preference because agents bind themselves, take up commitments, or act on principles they have not ranked against the alternatives; a view on which preferences formed under deprivation are not evidence of well-being; a view that separates what people choose from how their lives feel to them and sides with the latter; a view on which utility statements are normative claims about what is better rather than psychological reports; a view that takes utilities to be cardinal and comparable across persons; and a deflationary view on which the whole vocabulary should be replaced by talk of behavior and circumstances. If you judge that any of these collapses into another, say so and merge them rather than padding the count. Each leaf gets its descriptive name and one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing its defender will least like being reminded of.

Third, be explicit about the instrument's limits. Flag every pair of leaves the nine questions cannot really separate, and say which answers would be needed to tell them apart. Then list the answer-combinations the logical space permits that no leaf occupies, and for each, diagnose briefly whether the cell is empty because the view would be incoherent, because it is available but unattractive, or because nobody has simply articulated it yet.

No bibliography, no citations, no suggested further reading — I am working offline and want the instrument, not references. Where you are tempted to attach a philosopher's name to a leaf, do so only if you are confident the view is genuinely theirs; a descriptive label is always acceptable and often better.

Finally, run me through your own instrument using the five answers I gave above (treating my four invented-question slots as unanswered, and telling me whether that leaves anything undetermined). End your response with a single last line of exactly this form, containing the leaf I sort to and nothing else: \answer{<leaf name>} — or, if my pattern genuinely straddles two leaves, \answer{<leaf name>/<leaf name>}.
