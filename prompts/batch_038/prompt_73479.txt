My department runs a little internal seed grant — one course buyout plus about $5k for an RA — and the proposal is capped at two pages, but the committee is deliberately mixed (three philosophy people, two clinical psych, one from medical humanities), so it has to land with readers who don't share my jargon. My half-formed idea, which I've been chewing on with a friend who's doing her psychiatry rotation: everybody invokes Hacking's looping effects as if it were one feedback process, but I'm pretty sure there are at least two channels in there that behave very differently and get lumped together. One is descriptive — people learn a label, the label changes what gets noticed, reported, and even experienced, so the kind chases its own classification (the rise-and-fall of multiple personality diagnoses being the stock example). The other is organizational-political — the label becomes something people build an identity and a movement around, and then they turn around and contest and revise the classification itself (autism and ADHD are the obvious cases). My hunch is that these two push in opposite directions on the question of whether a psychiatric classification can be true of anything: descriptive looping makes the target move, whereas political looping actually pins it down enough that revision has a determinate object to fail or succeed at. And there's a second wrinkle I want to build in, which is that looping is nearly always described as a causal loop, but if you take an interventionist account of causation seriously the loop is non-modular — an intervention on the classification simultaneously changes the classified population and the conditions under which the classifier gathers evidence, so you can't hold the rest of the system fixed the way a clean intervention requires. I don't know yet whether that's a fatal problem for the whole framework or a technicality, and I'd rather the proposal say which.
