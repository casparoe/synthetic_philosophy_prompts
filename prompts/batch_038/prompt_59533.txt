I am a second-year graduate student in philosophy, currently enrolled in a seminar on the foundations of welfare economics and social choice, and I also sit — as the designated student member — on my city's Wellbeing Budget Advisory Panel. The panel is a seven-member body charged with producing a single, complete ranking of roughly forty program proposals so that the council can allocate next year's discretionary funds; our charter requires that ranking by the end of the month, so simply declining to aggregate is not an available move. At Thursday's meeting we are voting between two scoring rules, and the chair (a former county public health official) has asked me to circulate a written opinion by Wednesday noon that the panel can actually work from. I would like you to draft that memo for me.

The two rules on the table are as follows. Rule A, which I will call the sum rule, takes the panel's survey instrument — a 0-to-10 life-satisfaction item administered to a representative sample — converts each program's projected effects into a score for each affected resident, and sums those scores across the population with equal weights. Rule B, the prioritarian rule, applies a strictly concave transformation to each resident's score before summing, so that a given improvement counts for more when it accrues to someone in the bottom quintile of the baseline distribution. Our panel's economist argues that Rule B is not merely unattractive but irrational, on the grounds of Harsanyi's aggregation theorem: if both the individuals' and the panel's preferences over the relevant prospects are expected-utility rational, if the panel's ranking respects unanimous indifference, and if individuals are treated symmetrically, then the panel's ranking is necessarily representable as a linear sum of individual utilities with equal weights — so concave reweighting either violates the Pareto condition or smuggles in arbitrary weights. Two members push back. One, a legal aid attorney, says the theorem's conclusion only looks like a claim about wellbeing because it is stated in terms of von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities, which are unique only up to a positive affine transformation for each person separately; she thinks the sum is therefore well-defined only if one additionally assumes that the scales are cardinally comparable across people and that decision-theoretic utility tracks wellbeing, and that those are exactly the assumptions in dispute rather than deliverances of the theorem. The other, a community organizer, says Rule A is committed to being indifferent between, for instance, assigning a scarce benefit determinately to one household and assigning it by fair lottery among otherwise identically situated households, and that this indifference is the real content of the economist's "rationality." Meanwhile I have my own worry from the seminar: our instrument is at best ordinal, it is top-coded at 10, and respondents in long-disadvantaged neighborhoods may have adaptive expectations that depress their scores, so the expected-utility axioms may fail at the level of the data even if the theorem is sound.

Please take on the role of an expert in social choice theory and its philosophical foundations, and think carefully rather than reaching for the standard talking points on either side. Before drafting, begin with a short brainstorming section (a few paragraphs, clearly separated from the memo itself) in which you work out what actually turns on which premise: identify the theorem's assumptions one by one, state for each whether the panel's data and decision context plausibly satisfy it, and note which assumption, if dropped, would license Rule B and at what cost. Then write the memo: 900 to 1,200 words, formal internal-memo format with To/From/Date/Re lines and section headings, recommendation stated in the first paragraph, followed by the reasoning, an honest and steelmanned treatment of the strongest consideration on the losing side, and a closing implementation section specifying conditions and triggers (for example, what we should do if a cardinal instrument becomes available, or whether a hybrid — a sum subject to distributional constraints — is coherent rather than a fudge). State plainly which option you recommend and which premise you think the panel should reject, and say whether you regard that rejection as a theoretical cost or as the correct diagnosis of what the theorem was ever able to show. Do not hedge by splitting the difference, do not pad the memo with background on utilitarianism generally, and do not include any footnotes, citations, named papers, or reading lists — the panel members will not chase references, and I need the argument to stand on its own. Be technically precise where precision matters, but write for intelligent non-specialists, and do not invent numbers, program names, or facts about my city beyond what I have given you.
