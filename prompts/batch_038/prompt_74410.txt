I am three-quarters through my fourth novel, and the whole of its second half rests on a counterfactual: the demagogue around whom my book's movement has crystallised dies of a stroke in the late autumn of 1932, a few weeks before the appointment that would have carried him into the chancellery, and the remaining pages track whether the regime my readers half-expect arrives all the same, arrives late, arrives in a duller and more administrative form, or fails to arrive at all. He is a composite, thinly enough disguised that no reviewer will need me to say so, and the machinery around him is drawn from the real thing — the collapse of parliamentary legitimacy, the army, the landed and industrial elites who believed they could use him and discard him, the party's own regional bureaucracy. My sister, who teaches twentieth-century social history and has read the manuscript in stages out of familial loyalty rather than enthusiasm, has resisted the book from the outset. Her resistance has now condensed into a single sentence that she writes in the margin at every point where my characters' decisions appear to move events: history would have found someone else. In an accompanying letter she elaborates that what I treat as the hinge — one particular man's talents, resentments, and timing — is a symptom of forces that would have produced an equivalent outcome regardless, and that individuals occupying such positions are interchangeable in the way a particular general is interchangeable: had he not risen, another would have filled the role. She does not think she is accusing me of bad history. She thinks she is accusing me of bad metaphysics, and she has told me plainly that unless I can say what I mean by the man mattered, my novel is a fairy tale with unusually good research. An early reader of the same draft, by contrast, wrote me a warm note congratulating me on dramatising the truth that great men make history, which is roughly the proposition my book has been read as advancing by everyone except my sister.

Before I undertake the rewrite, I want those two sentences taken apart, and I want them taken apart in a way that respects how little they actually say. Please set out, as a menu rather than as a single favoured gloss, the distinct precise claims that history would have found someone else could be making, and alongside them the distinct claims that great men make history could be making. For each reading I would like a formulation explicit enough to be assessed — whose properties are being compared to whose, what is held fixed and what is allowed to vary, at what level of description the outcome is specified (the regime, its ideology, its timing, its particular atrocities, the mere fact of authoritarian collapse), whether the claim concerns a single case or a general pattern across societies, and whether it is stated as a matter of certainty, of probability, or of our capacity to know. For each, say whether it is a platitude no historian would dispute, a substantive and defensible thesis, a thesis that is simply false, or a pseudo-claim that cannot be assessed at all, and indicate what sort of evidence would bear on it. I would also like you to say which readings of her sentence are even in contradiction with which readings of mine; my own suspicion, which I cannot yet articulate, is that several pairings leave both of us speaking truly about different things, and that the argument she believes she is having with my book may be one she is not in fact having.

Finally, tell me which reading she most plausibly intends, given the work the sentence is doing in her marginalia — it is being used to dissolve the significance of particular scenes, not merely to register an historiographical preference — and which readings, if true, would genuinely license her verdict that the novel's architecture is metaphysically confused, as distinct from merely unfashionable. If some readings would leave my book intact while conceding everything she says, I want to know which, since I can rewrite an ending but cannot rewrite it well until I know what I am committed to. We are both appearing on a panel about counterfactual fiction at a festival in the autumn, which is the deadline my agent keeps mentioning and I keep ignoring; my interest is in the distinctions themselves, so please do not send me a reading list or a survey of who has argued what — I want the claims, their precise content, and your verdicts on them.
