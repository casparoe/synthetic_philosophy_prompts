I have spent the past year assembling an absolutist account of lying that I want to be Augustinian rather than Kantian: the prohibition should rest on what a sign is for, not on any test of universalizability. Before I write it up, I would like the package pressure-tested. Here is what I hold.

1. Lying is never permissible. On my definition, following *De mendacio*, a lie is a false signification produced with the will to deceive — putting into words what one does not hold in one's heart — and no circumstance makes it permissible. This includes the case I take to be decisive: a searcher demanding to know where a fugitive is hiding. My verdict there is that the person asked must refuse to answer, or answer in a way that is true, but must not assert what they believe to be false, whatever the consequences of refusing.

2. The wrong of lying is essentially a wrong to the person lied to. It withholds something owed to them and commandeers their practical reasoning for the speaker's ends; the liar makes a tool of the hearer. This, and not any impersonal disorder in the faculty of speech, is what gives the prohibition its moral force on my account.

3. Concealing the truth is not the same as denying it, and concealment can be permissible or even praiseworthy. Silence, refusal to answer, and literally true statements deliberately shaped so that the hearer will draw a false conclusion from them are not lies, and I see no ground for condemning them when they are the only way to save someone.

4. The same holds for non-verbal staging. A household that lays an extra place at the table or hangs a coat by the door in order to suggest to a searcher that the house holds more people than it does has done nothing wrong, and may have acted well.

5. Culpability admits of degrees even where permissibility does not. I accept the graded taxonomy of lies on which the mildest kinds remain sins, and I accept Augustine's treatment of the Hebrew midwives and of Rahab in *Contra mendacium*, on which what God rewarded was their mercy toward his people rather than their falsehood.

6. Methodologically, I want the wrongness of lying to be non-conventional. The verdict should not turn on what the norms of a given conversational community happen to be, nor on fine-grained facts about what this particular hearer already suspects, already knows, or is disposed to infer. A competent speaker should be able to determine whether an act is a lie without first doing social epistemology.

Audit this set. Which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another, and which only appear to? Please rank the real tensions by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list, and for each say precisely what would have to be abandoned or reinterpreted, and what the resulting view would then be unable to account for. I would also like to know where you think the apparent tensions lie, since I expect that some pairs I would have flagged are in fact compatible, and I do not want to revise those.

Do not hand me a menu. If only one of these six could be given up in order to make the rest cohere, say which one you would sacrifice, argue for that choice on the merits, and then state as clearly as you can what the resulting position costs me — including whether it still deserves to be called Augustinian.
