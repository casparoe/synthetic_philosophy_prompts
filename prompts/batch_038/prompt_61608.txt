For two years now, a reading group I belong to has been stuck on a question that ought to be settleable by arithmetic. The question: does the apparent fine-tuning of our universe's constants constitute evidence for a multiverse — the hypothesis that a great many universes exist, produced by some process that assigns them varying constants — rather than merely for the one we inhabit?

The two opposed members are both technically competent, and their positions run as follows. The first holds that inferring a multiverse from fine-tuning inverts the gambler's fallacy. Learning that a lottery with astronomical odds had a winner does not support the hypothesis that many tickets were sold, because the datum you actually have concerns the particular ticket that won; likewise, your datum concerns the particular universe you are in, and its being life-permitting is exactly as unsurprising on the single-universe hypothesis. The second holds that this objection neglects observation selection effects: you could not have found yourself in a universe that did not permit life, so the evidence genuinely available to you is not "this universe permits life" but something indexical and weaker — "at least one universe I could have observed permits life" — and conditionalized on that, fine-tuning does raise the probability of the multiverse.

What makes the stalemate peculiar is this. Each has written out an explicit model, and when they stipulate a common evidence proposition they recover the same likelihood ratios and largely agree about the direction of the update. The disagreement returns, undiminished, one step upstream: at the question of which proposition is the evidence. Neither thinks the other is making a computational error; each thinks the other is smuggling in an assumption about the epistemic situation of the observer.

My hypothesis, offered as something to be tested rather than a conclusion: the dispute is consequently not about probability theory and not about cosmology, but about the epistemology of self-locating belief — about what constitutes an agent's total evidence before and after she learns where she sits in the space of possibilities — and the two are applying "confirm" to different agent-relative propositions under a single heading.

Is that diagnosis right, or is something else doing the work? Please weigh at least these alternatives: that this is a merely verbal dispute about "evidence"; that it is a substantive but hidden technical or empirical question — for instance, whether the universe-generating process yields independent trials with a well-defined measure over the constants, without which neither side's likelihoods are defined at all; that it is a dispute about priors over observer-relative possibilities that neither side can argue for beyond bedrock intuition; or that the original question is ill-posed until an agent and an epistemic situation are supplied, in which case the impasse is not a failure of the group but a correct response to a malformed question.

What I want is a diagnosis defended by reference to what each side actually argues, your judgment about which candidate best explains a two-year deadlock, and an account of what would count as progress — a distinction, a formal result about observation selection, a change in the question asked, an empirical or mathematical finding — or, if you think no progress should be expected, an argument for that rather than a shrug. I am not after references or a survey of positions; the reasoning is the deliverable.
