I serve on the programming committee of the only civic theatre in a market town of some forty thousand people. Ten years ago the committee adopted an unusual standing rule: every proposal is to be tested against the case made in Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert on the theatre, and the grounds of each decision are to be minuted in Rousseauian terms. The minutes record the committee's own reasoning rather than quotations from the letter, and no member of the committee is a scholar of it. Our record to date runs as follows.

1. A touring production of Molière's *Le Misanthrope*, professional, ticketed, three nights in the town hall. Refused. Minute: the one character who says plainly what he thinks is written as an insufferable bore, and the audience leaves in sympathy with the polished evasions of those who mock him; a play that renders honesty ridiculous is not excused by the honest man also being tiresome.

2. A classical five-act tragedy in verse, professional and ticketed, whose entire action consists in a ruler's passion for a captive princess and his eventual decision to send her away at the cost of his own happiness. Refused, narrowly. Minute: an evening given wholly to a passion that has already made a public man forget his duty leaves the house tenderer than it arrived and no better able to act, and nothing in the play costs anybody anything.

3. A West End musical on a two-night stop, top-price tickets, no local performers and no local involvement of any kind, whose hero is a likeable rogue who swindles everyone on stage and is last seen making off with the money. Refused unanimously. Minute: vice is here rendered so agreeable and so successful that no punishment ever arrives, and the town pays for the pleasure of admiring it.

4. An offer of a legacy sufficient to build a four-hundred-seat playhouse and endow a resident professional company. Declined. Minute: a town of our size does not want a playhouse; a great city whose morals are already gone may reasonably decorate its corruption with a theatre, whereas we should pay for ours in idle evenings, in money diverted from work and family, and in the loss of the amusements we can make for ourselves. The legacy was accepted instead for a bandstand and for the floodlighting of the market square.

5. The midsummer gathering in the market square: no stage, no enclosure, no tickets, no performers, a pole planted in the centre, two brass bands, long tables, the whole town dancing. Minuted as the committee's model of a public occasion, on the ground that there the people are their own spectacle and nobody is cut off from anybody else by a row of seats or by a payment.

6. A play written and performed by fourteen-year-olds from the town's secondary school about the dispute over the closing of the factory, played twice to their own families, free. Approved without discussion. Minute: they are not watching themselves represented by strangers; they are speaking, and their audience is composed of people who know them.

7. An improving modern drama staged by the town's amateur dramatic society in the town hall at fifty pence, in which a corrupt building contractor is exposed and a generous doctor rewarded; the minute records that the house wept at the close. Approved with enthusiasm, on the ground that virtue was made lovable and vice hateful and that the money stayed in the town.

The case now before us is this. A company has offered a free promenade production in the market square over one weekend, funded entirely by a regional arts grant, so that nothing is charged to the audience and nothing is drawn from local pockets. The piece is built from verbatim transcripts of our own council minutes and of interviews with former workers at the closed factory. It is performed by eight paid professional actors alongside some thirty unpaid local volunteers. There is no stage, no seating and no enclosure; the audience walks among the action, is twice asked to vote on what should happen next, and is once asked to speak a line in chorus. In the piece as written, the character who comes off worst is a long-serving and widely respected local magistrate, played throughout as a pompous fraud; and the character the audience is plainly meant to warm to is a sharp-tongued machinist who mocks everyone, including the two honestly drawn characters, and who ends by shrugging and taking a better-paid job with the developer who closed the factory.

What I should like from you:

(a) the verdict the committee ought to reach if it is to be consistent with its own record, with reasons;

(b) the candidate rules that accommodate all seven precedents equally well, stated precisely enough to be applied to a new proposal, together with what each of them implies about the case now before us;

(c) which single precedent is the closest analogue, and on which axis it is closest, given that here the structure of the occasion, the content of the piece, and the question of money do not point the same way;

(d) an honest account of the principle the committee would be adopting with each option available to it — refusal, unqualified approval, and approval subject to conditions such as cutting the ridicule of the magistrate or giving the honestly drawn characters the last word — including what approval-on-conditions would commit us to about other people's scripts in future;

(e) the further case that would tell the competing rules apart, and, if it is a different case, the one that would settle whether the structural limb of our record (no enclosure, no tickets, the people as their own spectacle) or the professional limb (paid impersonators who sell themselves by the evening) is doing the real work in the approvals and refusals we have already given;

(f) where the reasons minuted at items 1 to 7 depart from arguments actually available in Rousseau's letter, and whether any of those departures should be corrected before we decide this case. Item 7 is the one I have in mind, since the committee there appears to have treated the weeping of the audience as a point in the play's favour.

Please write in British English. I am not asking for citations, a bibliography or a reading list, and no quotations from Rousseau are required; work from the record set out above and from your understanding of the argument of the letter.
