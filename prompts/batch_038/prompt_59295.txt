Context so you can calibrate: I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size dev-tools company, zero formal philosophy background (a few blog posts and one very slow partial read of something about utilitarianism, that's it). Over the last year I've built an internal scoring thing I half-jokingly call welfare.py, and last week I wrote down what I actually believe so a colleague who's doing a part-time philosophy MA could attack it. He read it, said "several of these can't all be true," and then wouldn't say which. So I'm asking you instead.

Please take the role of a philosopher who works on social choice and welfare economics and who is used to explaining things to engineers without being condescending about it. Think it through carefully before you answer: first do the pairwise work out loud in a short plain-English section (a couple of sentences per pair you actually flag, brainstorm freely there, including pairs you end up deciding are fine), and only then give me the JSON.

Here's the list.

1. Nothing counts morally except how much it improves or worsens individual people's own situations. There's no seperate "company good" or "social good" floating above the users that could outweigh them.

2. Harsanyi's aggregation theorem is correct and I accept its conclusion: the right social ranking is a weighted sum of individual utilities. I think of welfare.py as a literal implementation of that. I wouldn't call myself a utilitarian, I just think the math forces the adding whether you like it or not.

3. Every user gets the same weight in that sum. Nobody's utility gets a bigger coefficient for being louder, richer, or having more seats. I'd be genuinely annoyed if someone showed me my sum secretly favours big accounts.

4. That said, I do apply a multiplier to accounts with deep, complicated workflows, on top of whatever utility gain we measure for them. Two accounts that both show a +50 gain shouldn't count the same if one of them is running a much more sophisticated setup. That's not status, it's that there's more there to affect.

5. Comparing utils between different people is meaningless. There's no fact of the matter about whether my 3 is your 3. I've said this out loud in meetings, several times.

6. But welfare.py fixes a common zero and a common unit across all ~4,000 accounts: zero is an account that does nothing all week, and one util is the measured lift from a 1% increase in weekly active usage in our standard A/B test, same conversion constant applied to everybody. So when I sum them I'm not doing anything dodgy, I have an actual common scale. I've always treated 5 as a philosophical worry that 6 just discharges.

7. I don't buy the linearity-in-probabilities assumption. A one-in-a-million chance of catastrophic data loss shouldn't be exactly one millionth as bad as the loss happening for certain; tiny probabilities should be discounted harder than expected-value math says. I've coded it that way for the outage-risk part of the score, and I'm confident that's the right call (Allais-type intuitions, from what little I've read).

8. At equal totals I always take the flatter distribution. Concretely: last quarter we had option A, modelled total +8.2k utils but one mid-size account down about 400, and option B, total +7.9k with nobody under the floor. We shipped B. I'd ship B again tomorrow and I'd defend that as the correct decision, not as a political compromise or a fudge.

9. I don't buy the veil-of-ignorance / "imagine you could be anyone, with their preferences" version of Harsanyi's argument; the psychology behind it looks invented to me. I've been assuming that discredits the theorem in 2 as well, but I still accept 2, and I can't tell whether I'm allowed to.

10. Method rule I hold onto: if a principle requires a fact I can't measure, I treat it as not real. This is a big part of why I like this whole approach.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look like they do? I honestly don't know where the problem is, and I'm not going to tell you which one I'd give up, because I don't know — assume I'm equally attached to all ten.
