so I run a little reading group — six of us, mostly people who did philosophy in grad school and never quite quit — and next month we're finally doing Pynchon's *The Crying of Lot 49*. Half of us read it in college, half have been putting it off for years. My problem is that I've spent the last decade on imprecise probabilities in formal epistemology and now I literally can't read the book as anything else than a case study in credal sets. Quick anchor for the version of the story I want read against, since nobody's re-reading first: Oedipa Maas is named executrix of Pierce Inverarity's estate, starts seeing a muted post horn symbol everywhere, hears the name Trystero/Tristero in a performance of Wharfinger's *The Courier's Tragedy* and from Mike Fallopian (the right-wing guy writing a history of them), finds the horn watermarked into Inverarity's private stamp collection, digs up an older edition of the play that shows Driblette put the Tristero line in deliberately, tries the Nefastis machine — the Maxwell's demon box — and gets nowhere with it, watches Dr. Hilarius and Mucho both come apart in different ways, learns Driblette has killed himself, gets told by some stranger in a bar that the symbol just stands for a support group for the brokenhearted, and ends up at the estate auction waiting for lot 49, the stamps, to be cried, hoping whoever bids on them is one of them.

What I want from you: take her belief state seriously as an imprecise one — a set of probability functions rather than a point — and tell me whether the novel is actually dramatizing dilation. I mean the Seidenfeld result where learning E widens your interval on X instead of narrowing it, because X and E fail to be independent across every member of the credal set. My read is that its the deep joke of the book: every new "confirmation" (another post horn, the watermarks, the deliberate line in the play) is evidence that makes her *less* determinate about whether Trystero is real, not more, because it raises the salience of hypotheses she hadn't had in her space before. In my own notes I've been calling the W.A.S.T.E. network an ambiguity pump — a channel that delivers information which increases the spread. Push on that hard. Can you actually sketch the formal structure, even informally with an unknown-bias-coin style example, where post horn sightings genuinely dilate? Or is Oedipa just a bad Bayesian who should have had sharp-but-humble credences all along, which is what my friend Dev insists — Dev also says she violates the reflection principle because she can foresee that she'll be no better off when lot 49 comes up than she is now, and that Elga's arguments against mushy credences and diachronic betting show she'd rationally refuse a sequence of bets she should accept. I'm not sure Dev has the book right and I'm not at all sure I have the theory right.

The other thing I want is the ending, which I think is where the imprecise-Bayes framing actually earns its keep. She's in a state where the interval on the question that matters is as wide as it can get and she still has to act — she has to sit in that room and decide whether to bid. What does an ambiguity-averse rule like Γ-maximin tell her to do there, versus Γ-maximax or an epsilon-contamination rule, and does the spread between those recommendations change how we should read the last page — i.e. is the suspension of the ending a *decision-theoretic* state (no admissible action) rather than a mystical one? And then be honest with me about where I'm forcing it: where the lens illuminates the book, and where Pynchon is doing something the theory has no vocabulary for at all. Two candidates: the possibility that the whole thing is an elaborate joke Inverarity planted for her, which isn't a hypothesis anyone would put in the algebra; and the brokenhearted-sailor explanation, which retroactively shows her space was mis-specified from the start rather than merely wide. Finish by telling me what the novel contributes to the debate over whether mushy credences are rationally permissible that a pile of coin-flip thought experiments doesn't. No reading list please, we're not that kind of group, and don't quote the book — nobody is going to check page numbers and I'd rather you paraphrase than invent.
