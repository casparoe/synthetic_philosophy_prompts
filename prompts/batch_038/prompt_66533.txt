Here's the answer, transcribed out of the blue book (handwriting was rough, so a word or two is my guess):

"Schellenberg's argument goes something like this.

1. If God exists, God is a personal being who perfectly loves each human person.
2. If God perfectly loves a person, God seeks a personal relationship with that person.
3. A personal relationship with someone requires that they believe that person exists and is open to a relationship with them.
4. So if God exists, every person who is capable of a relationship with God and is not culpably resisting it believes that God exists.
5. But there are people who are capable of such a relationship, are not resisting God, and still don't believe. (My roommate was raised in a church, prayed about it for years, tried hard to believe, and just couldn't. Nobody could call that resistance.)
6. Therefore God does not exist.

The argument is valid, so what's at issue is which premise is weakest. I'd say 2. 'Perfectly loving' is a theological term of art and the argument seems to just read it as 'wants to be close to.' But love doesn't always require disclosure. A parent sometimes withholds something from a child for the child's own sake, and we don't call that unloving; friends keep things from each other for good reasons. So 2 needs a lot more support than it gets here.

The free-will defense can be adapted pretty directly. The idea is that God creates an epistemic distance: if God's existence were overwhelmingly evident we couldn't freely respond to God, and a coerced relationship wouldn't be worth having, so God stays hidden in order to preserve significant freedom. This is the same shape as the free-will defense against the problem of evil — in both cases God permits a bad state of affairs because preventing it would cost more in freedom than the bad state costs. So the response is at least available to the theist, and hiddenness is not the knockdown argument it's usually presented as.

It doesn't cover everything though. The people in premise 5 aren't trying to resist God, they're trying to believe. Preserving room for the resister's freedom doesn't explain why the sincere seeker is left in the dark, because nothing the seeker does with their freedom calls for that. And the theist can't just insist that the seeker is secretly resisting, since then premise 5 becomes unfalsifiable — any sincere non-believer gets reclassified as a resister and the debate stalls. So the adaptation works for some unbelief and not for the relevant kind. Since the argument only needs a few cases of non-resistant non-belief to go through, the theist hasn't escaped it, and unbelief in general turns out not to be a matter of blame, whatever people say about atheists suppressing the truth. Worth noting the conclusion is limited though: it rules out a perfectly loving personal God, not any kind of divine reality."
