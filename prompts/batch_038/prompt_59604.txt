I chair the interventions review panel of a publicly funded health service. Our remit is to determine whether a requested intervention constitutes treatment, which we must fund, or enhancement, which we may decline. Our determinations are appealable, and the appeal body tests them against our own earlier determinations rather than against any statute or national guideline, so consistency with the record carries most of the argumentative weight. I should be grateful for your assessment of a case coming before us next month.

The record to date, in the order decided:

1. Cochlear implantation for children born deaf: approved.
2. A second, contralateral implant for a child already implanted in one ear, where a single implant would have afforded serviceable hearing: approved, on the ground that binaural hearing is what the typical auditory system does.
3. Recombinant growth hormone for a child with documented pituitary deficiency: approved.
4. Recombinant growth hormone for a child of short stature with normal endocrine tests and no syndrome, predicted adult height in the lowest percentile: refused; the recorded reason was that 'the child is not ill'.
5. Methylphenidate for adults meeting diagnostic criteria for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder: approved.
6. Modafinil for narcolepsy, and for a rostered night nurse with shift-work sleep disorder: approved.
7. Modafinil requested by a trainee accountant to sustain revision: refused, on the ground that the benefit was 'advantage over competitors rather than relief of a deficit'.
8. A beta-blocker for a professional cellist whose performance anxiety had already caused her to withdraw from engagements: approved.
9. Cataract surgery: approved. Refractive laser surgery for a myope fully and comfortably corrected by spectacles: refused. The same surgery for a patient who could tolerate neither spectacles nor contact lenses, and who was therefore functionally uncorrectable: approved.
10. Cosmetic rhinoplasty: refused. Septorhinoplasty for a deviated septum causing obstruction: approved.
11. Testosterone replacement for a man with levels below the laboratory reference range and with symptoms: approved. Testosterone for a man in the lower-normal range seeking muscle mass and energy: refused, on the ground that there was 'no deficiency'.
12. Tendon reconstruction and physiotherapy for a semi-professional cricketer, restoring bowling pace to something near his pre-injury level although the reconstructed joint now exceeds what is typical for his age: approved, on the ground that the aim was return to pre-injury capacity.
13. Deep brain stimulation for treatment-resistant depression: approved.
14. Hand surgery for a watchmaker with early osteoarthritis of the thumb, whose grip remained within the population-typical range for his age but fell below what his trade required: approved.
15. A request for home adaptations for a wheelchair user: refused as outside our remit, though we recorded a recommendation that the local authority fund them.

The new case. A woman of 31, height 149 centimetres, applies for distraction osteogenesis of the femurs and tibiae in order to gain some six centimetres. There is no endocrine pathology, no syndrome, and no measurable deficiency in any physiological parameter: her stature is a statistical outlier with normal physiology throughout. She has passed every component of the national fire service's selection process except the height standard, which is set at 152 centimetres; the service defends that standard as a proxy for reach and load-carrying, and a functional reach test exists but has not been adopted. She has had cognitive behavioural therapy for the distress the situation causes her, with partial benefit. The procedure would cost roughly forty thousand pounds and involve eighteen months of rehabilitation, including two periods of restricted weight-bearing. She states plainly that she would withdraw the request if the height standard were removed. My own inclination is not worth reporting, since I want an assessment of what the record supports rather than of what I happen to prefer.

Please provide the following.

(a) Set out the candidate principles that would account for the whole record, not merely for a subset of it. I expect at least four, and I expect them stated in your own words; where a principle has a standard name in the literature you may use it, but define it as you use it. Do not give me a reading list, and do not cite anything: work from the record.

(b) For each candidate principle, state the verdict it yields in the new case, and say how much strain it is under elsewhere in the record. In particular, explain how a single principle is to accommodate precedent 4 alongside precedent 11, where being below a normal range was decisive in opposite directions, and precedent 7 alongside precedent 8, where a competitive advantage was refused in one instance and funded in another. Say whether the new case forces us to resolve either tension, or whether it can be decided without doing so.

(c) Identify the closest analogues in the record, and say which feature makes each one close: the diagnostic picture, the mechanism of the intervention, the purpose the benefit serves, or the reason we actually gave. Where the analogues point in different directions, say which feature you take to be the salient one and why.

(d) Recommend a determination — fund, refuse, or fund subject to conditions — and justify it. Address explicitly whether the availability of an unadopted reach test, and the precedent at 15, bear on our remit or only on the merits.

(e) State, without hedging, what principle we would be adopting by deciding either way, and name two or three further applications that would then be committed to. I want the cost of each choice, not only its benefits.

(f) Name the single hypothetical application that would best discriminate between the two leading candidate principles you have identified, and say what each predicts about it.

(g) Close with a draft determination of roughly 250 words, in a register suitable for sending to the applicant, which states the outcome, the principle relied upon, and how it follows from our own earlier determinations.
