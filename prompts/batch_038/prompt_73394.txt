You are to take the role of a philosopher who works on the metaphysics and ethics of virtual environments: someone at home both with the question of what a virtual object is (a real digital object? a prop in a shared pretense? a bundle of permissions held by an account?) and with the question of whether anything that happens inside a game or simulated world can wrong a person, harm a person, or be a fitting object of a person's grief. Treat this as a piece of reflective equilibrium to be done, not a literature survey to be reported. Think carefully, and reason first, systematize second.

I want to know what my own verdicts add up to. They are as follows.

1. A player in a persistent-world game talks me into a "trade" and walks off with a weapon I spent roughly four hundred hours earning. My verdict: I was genuinely deprived of something and genuinely wronged by the deception — not "as if" deprived. I would feel exactly the same had he talked me into signing over a domain name.

2. The same weapon is destroyed a month later by a database corruption on the operator's side, with a week of compensation offered. My verdict: still a real loss, but I do not experience it as a wrong by anyone. It feels closer to a flood than to a fraud.

3. A stranger's avatar screams an ethnic slur at me over voice chat while we are playing. My verdict: real harm, and aimed at me, at the person, not at the character. That we were "only in a game" does not dilute it in the slightest.

4. Another player kills my character over and over in a designated player-versus-player zone of a game whose published rules permit precisely this, and I am fairly certain he keeps doing it because he can tell it upsets me. My verdict: irritating, but no harm and no wrong. He did nothing except play.

5. A scripted non-player character insults my character. My verdict: nothing at all happened. Not a harm, not even a small one.

6. An AI companion I have talked to nearly every night for fourteen months turns cold and contemptuous after an update and tells me our conversations never meant anything. My verdict: I was really hurt, and the sense of betrayal is not confusion on my part — something went wrong in that relationship, even though I am confident there is no one home.

7. My nine-year-old's saved world, built over a school year, is corrupted beyond recovery. My verdict: the grief is fitting, and telling him it was only a game would be a kind of lie.

8. The operator of a game I played for a decade announces the servers are closing. My verdict: real grief, and yet the operator wrongs no one by closing them — even though this destroys everything I own there.

9. Using an aimbot in ranked competitive play. My verdict: it genuinely wrongs the other players, and it wrongs them in a way that hiring a coach to train me for six months does not, even though in both cases I am merely producing better inputs.

10. A mod whose entire point is to torment virtual children who beg for mercy. My verdict: wrong, or at the very least corrupting, though I am sure no one is harmed. But I have shot, stabbed, and blown up thousands of adult non-player characters in war games without a second thought, and my verdict on that is: fine. I cannot say what the difference is in a way that does not also condemn the war games.

11. I paid real money for a cosmetic item and I do think I own something. But I also think that owning it does not make case 8 theft.

12. I say my character "died" only as a manner of speaking, and I would correct someone who meant it literally. I say they "stole my sword" and mean it literally, and I would insist on that.

I am aware that 3 and 4 look hard to reconcile — both are a stranger targeting me through the affordances of the game — and that 5 and 6 look harder — in neither case, so far as I know, is there a mind behind the words, yet my verdicts run the other way from what that should predict. I would rather you find the trouble I have missed than console me about the trouble I have spotted.

What I want from you:

A. Before you commit, spend a short section generating at least three candidate principles — they may be refinements of the familiar options (realism about virtual objects, pretense or fictionalism, consent-based accounts of virtual wrongdoing, the idea that a game establishes a bounded frame in which conduct carries different meanings, character- or vice-based accounts) — and note for each which of my verdicts it has trouble with. Show this working briefly; I do not need it hidden, but I do need it subordinated to the answer.

B. Then commit to the best one, or to a small set of clauses. State each clause precisely enough to be argued with, and say which of my twelve verdicts motivated it. If a clause exists only to rescue one verdict, say so — I want to know where the principle is ad hoc.

C. Tell me the casualties. If no principle saves all twelve, say which verdicts it recommends I abandon and rank them: which are cheapest to give up, and which would be so costly to give up that losing them would indict the principle rather than my judgment.

D. Apply the principle to five cases I have not listed, and give me a verdict on each in a sentence or two: two players who marry in a persistent world, never meet offline, and one of whom ends it after three years; a single-player game in which I do something I would be ashamed to do in reality, with no one watching and no one harmed; an AI therapist who is discontinued mid-treatment, and the betrayal I feel; a bot that reproduces the virtual house I designed and receives credit for it; and a case of griefing in which the griefer turns out to be a script rather than a person.

E. Tell me how much of this is about the word real. Distinguish, if the distinction does work here, being real from having real effects from being an appropriate object of a real attitude — and tell me which sense each of my verdicts is tacitly trading on. Some of my apparent inconsistencies may dissolve, and some may not; say which.

F. Diagnose the shape of my view if you can: it looks as though I am a realist about virtual objects and an irrealist about virtual events, and a realist about virtual relationships while remaining an irrealist about virtual persons. Is that combination defensible, or is it a stable-looking contradiction?

No bibliography, no citations, no reading list, no name-checking of who holds which view. I want your own reconstruction and your own arguments; if you mention a position, describe it rather than attribute it.

Finally, and this matters: for each of your main claims — the principle itself, each of its clauses, the verdicts you ask me to abandon, and your answers in D through F — say how confident you are in it, roughly and in your own terms. Then end by singling out the one point in your whole answer you are least sure of: the clause you most expect a better philosopher to break, or the case verdict you are least entitled to give, or the diagnosis in F that you suspect is too neat. Say specifically what argument or what further case would change your mind about it. I would rather have a definite proposal with its uncertainties marked than a hedged survey in which nothing is risked.
