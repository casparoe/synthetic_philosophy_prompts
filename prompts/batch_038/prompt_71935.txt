I'm prepping a seminar session on Aumann's agreement theorem and I keep running into the same wall. Half the room hears the theorem as "if two rational people just talked long enough and put all their cards on the table, they'd end up agreeing," and then uses it to dismiss every real disagreement as a failure of communication or of rationality. I want to hand them a case where the talking does happen, perfectly and in full, and the disagreement survives anyway. Build me that thought experiment to spec:

Setting: one ordinary workplace where numbers actually get argued over — a credit desk, a market research outfit, a hospital quality committee, an elections desk at a paper, your pick. Two people. No sci-fi, no exotic probability apparatus.

The spec:

- They demonstrably share a common prior. Don't hand-wave this. Show it on the page — same training, same public dataset, same model of the situation — and write it so a reader can actually read off the prior probabilities.
- They are flawless Bayesians. No ambiguity aversion, no multiple priors, no motivated reasoning, no heuristics, no forgetting, no bounded anything.
- All their evidence is public between them. Every datum one of them has, the other has too, or gets told about in full before the first announcement.
- They announce their exact posteriors to each other in atleast three rounds, each conditioning on everything the other has said, and each knowing that the other is doing the same.
- At the end they have common knowledge of different posteriors on the same question. Really different numbers, not a rounding difference.
- And the catch: the disagreement has to trace back to one of them being unable to so much as formulate a distinction the other can — a gap in what they can represent, not in what they've been told. It's not information they're missing. But the story must never flag this. A careful reader should only be able to reconstruct it afterwards, out of the details.

Keep the scenario under 350 words and self-contained. Then give me the mechanics: run the rounds one at a time with the actual numbers so I can put the arithmetic on the board, and point to exactly which hypothesis of the theorem gives way and at which step.

Two more things I need. First, say which feature of the case is doing the work, and show me the variant with it removed — specifically, rewrite it so that the second person does acquire the missing concept, and check whether they converge. Second, give me the strongest attack a defender of the theorem has on your case, in their own terms rather than a strawman.

And be committed. My own hunch, which you're welcome to overrule, is that this spec can't be met as written — that you can't have a common prior without a common state space, so the moment one agent can't express a distinction, the claim that "they share a prior" has quietly stopped applying, and what I've described isn't a counterexample but a case where the theorem never got a grip. If you think that's right, say so flat out, tell me which single one of my constraints has to go and why it's that one and not another, and then build me the closest working case you can with that constraint dropped. Don't hedge, don't lay out a menu of readings for me to choose between, and you don't need to cite anything — just the case, the numbers, and your verdict on it.
