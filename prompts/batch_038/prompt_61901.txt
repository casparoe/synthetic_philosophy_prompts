Roommate is doing a PhD in political theory and he's built a defense of referendums that I cant get out of. He says he'd be delighted if I found the hole, so please don't be gentle. Here's the argument as he laid it out (my paraphrase, he writes much worse than this):

1. Condorcet's jury theorem says: if each member of a group answers the same yes/no question independently, and each member is more likely than not to get it right, then the group's majority answer is more likely to be right than any single member's answer is — and that majority reliability keeps climbing toward certainty as the group gets bigger.

2. A referendum is a group of exactly that kind: tens of millions of citizens each independently casting a judgement on a yes/no question.

3. On any question that actually reaches a referendum — leave or remain, cap property taxes, legalise assisted dying — the median voter is better than chance at getting it right. Denying this is circular: to show that voters are systematically worse than chance you'd already have to know the right answer to the question they're voting on, and if you knew that you wouldn't need the vote.

4. So the majority verdict of a referendum is more reliable than the verdict of any smaller body judging the same question — a parliament, a cabinet, a court, a constitutional council — and it gets more reliable the more people turn out.

5. What makes a decision procedure legitimate, on his view, is that it tracks the right answer better than the realistic alternatives do. That's the reason we defer to expert procedures everywhere else.

6. So the more people who vote, the more binding the result, and no smaller body should be able to override, water down or delay it.

And then the conclusions he draws from it, which I don't accept:

7. Mass plebiscites whose results look repellant to me, including the 20th century ones, have to be treated as very probably correct.
8. Entrenchment is a virtue — a rule like California's, where the legislature can't amend a ballot measure without going back to the voters, is an improvement on ordinary lawmaking rather than a defect.
9. The Swiss filters he admits exist (a double majority of voters and cantons for constitutional change, plus a signature threshold before you can even force a vote on a law parliament has already passed) are just federalist haggling and have nothing to do with how reliable majorities are.

I'm fairly sure something goes wrong early, but every time I point at a step he says I'm just squeamish about the conclusion. So: which step do you reject? Say why it looked innocent going in — I want to understand what made me swallow it, not just that it's false — and tell me what I'd be giving up if I rejected a different one instead. For what it's worth I'd rather give up (6) than (3), because I don't want to end up saying ordinary people can't judge their own interests, but I've no idea if that's actually the cheapest move.

Two follow-ups if you have room. If (3) goes, is there anything left of an *epistemic* case for direct democracy, or does it have to be re-grounded on something like fairness or self-rule — which would be a different arguement entirely and wouldn't support (7) at all? And is (8) really consistent with (1)–(5)? My instinct is that a procedure justified purely by truth-tracking should want to be the most revisable thing in the system, not the most frozen, but I can't turn that into a clean objection.
