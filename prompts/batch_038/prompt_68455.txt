Help me settle an argument with myself before I write the first section of a chapter on consent. I've been reading away at accounts of when consent counts as valid — deception, coercion, competence — and every one of them is answering a slightly seperate question, and I don't want to spend the whole chapter saying so. So instead: what would an account of valid consent actually have to deliver in order to count as adequate? Write me the spec.

Cases it has to get right, at a minimum: the lie about something the consent-giver would have cared about but that isn't the thing being consented to (and the mirror case, where nothing false is said and they're still misled); the offer that isn't a threat but that you want to treat like one — the surgeon who charges what the traffic will bear for a life-saving operation; the drunk nineteen-year-old; the dementia patient who said yes a year ago and is now, in every sense that matters, someone else; the patient who signs the form without reading it, and who is neither obviously bound nor obviously excused; the person who says yes because every alternative is worse and who nevertheless isn't wronged by the person who offered. And the tacit-consent move: somebody falls asleep aboard a ship, wakes at open sea, and is told that staying put was agreement — the account should say whether anything of that shape can ever be consent, and if not, what it is instead of consent.

Distinctions it can't collapse: consenting vs. submitting vs. merely not objecting; consent as an inner attitude vs. consent as a performance with uptake conditions; consent as a condition on permissibility vs. consent as one reason among others; and whether the account can stay content-neutral or has to build in limits on what may be consented to at all — if it has to, say so plainly, that's a concession with consequences and not a detail.

Now here's the constraint I want shaping the whole list, because it's the thing the literature usually papers over: the account has to be operable by non-philosophers, inside institutions, under time pressure, in front of an audience that is partly adversarial. A nurse with four patients waiting. A Title IX panel deciding whether a coach should have been fired. A judge with a docket and a record that is mostly silence. So third-party checkability isn't a bonus feature tacked on at the end, it's one of the requirements, and I want you blunt about what it costs and who pays.

Mark each requirement as non-negotiable or as a camp preference, and say whose camp. (My suspicion: "consent must be revocable right up to the last moment" looks like a principle and functions as a preference. Argue with me if you disagree.) Then tell me which single requirement does the most work knocking candidates out, and which pair you think can't both be satisfied. My own nominee is will-tracking against checkability — if validity has to track what somebody actually wants, no third party can ever know it, and if validity has to be checkable, we've stopped talking about autonomy and started talking about ceremony. I want that argued, not asserted, including what exactly is lost if you just bite the bullet and go ceremonial.

Finish with a very short scorecard, a paragraph each and no more, of the three shapes on offer: attitude-only, performance-only, and the hybrid where the performance is necessary but not sufficient. And please, no bibliography — I've got one, its two pages long and I don't need a third.
