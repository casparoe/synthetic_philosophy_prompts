A fictional national government has established an Office of Public Confidence, and after a badly handled retraction its Director of Communications circulates a laminated desk aid for press officers: house rules on dealing with inconvenient facts. Write that desk aid as a sustained parody — a short foreword, eight to ten numbered rules with headings, and one worked before-and-after example showing a statement being revised until it concedes nothing. Around 700 words, in British English.

The comic engine must be Hannah Arendt's account of lying in politics, rendered entirely in management language. The rules should show officials treating factual truth as uniquely fragile precisely because it is contingent and could always have been otherwise; drifting from concealing a secret into replacing the reality altogether; preferring a coherent alternative account to a messy one on grounds of plausibility; treating an unwelcome fact as merely a hostile interpretation of events; and finally coming to believe the image they set out to project. Do not name Arendt, any of her essays, or any other author, and do not quote or cite anything — the document should read as though written by people who have never encountered the ideas they are enacting.

The target is the genre of official communications guidance and the self-deception it authorises, not any real government, party or individual. Keep the tone uniformly helpful, upbeat and procedural throughout; a reader should be able to see what is being ironised without you ever flagging it, and a specialist in Arendt should judge the satire fair rather than caricatural.
