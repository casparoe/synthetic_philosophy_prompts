I am working through the opening pages of Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals" (Book I, chapter XXX in the numbering of Charles Cotton's translation as edited by William Carew Hazlitt), and I want help understanding them properly. Almost all of the commentary I encounter fastens on the essay's later, much-paraphrased claim that each people calls barbarous whatever departs from its own custom, and that Europeans exceed the Tupinambá in cruelty. I am deliberately setting that material aside. My interest is in the opening sequence, which is usually skipped and which I suspect does a great deal of unnoticed work: it begins not with America at all but with Pyrrhus and the Roman camp, then moves to a servant who lived in Villegaignon's Antarctic France, then to Plato's Atlantis, then to the Dordogne and the encroaching sands of the Médoc coast, and then to a Carthaginian island reported in a text of doubtful Aristotelian authorship. I want to understand why an essay about cannibalism opens with geology and the epistemics of second-hand report, and what standard of judgment the opening announces and then applies.

Here is the text (bracketed notes are the editor's apparatus, not Montaigne's; elisions marked by square brackets are mine and cut only the Latin verse quotations and their renderings):

> When King Pyrrhus invaded Italy, having viewed and considered the order of the army the Romans sent out to meet him; "I know not," said he, "what kind of barbarians" (for so the Greeks called all other nations) "these may be; but the disposition of this army that I see has nothing of barbarism in it."—[Plutarch, Life of Pyrrhus, c. 8.]—As much said the Greeks of that which Flaminius brought into their country; and Philip, beholding from an eminence the order and distribution of the Roman camp formed in his kingdom by Publius Sulpicius Galba, spake to the same effect. By which it appears how cautious men ought to be of taking things upon trust from vulgar opinion, and that we are to judge by the eye of reason, and not from common report.
>
> I long had a man in my house that lived ten or twelve years in the New World, discovered in these latter days, and in that part of it where Villegaignon landed,—[At Brazil, in 1557.]—which he called Antarctic France. This discovery of so vast a country seems to be of very great consideration. I cannot be sure, that hereafter there may not be another, so many wiser men than we having been deceived in this. I am afraid our eyes are bigger than our bellies, and that we have more curiosity than capacity; for we grasp at all, but catch nothing but wind.
>
> Plato brings in Solon,—[In Timaeus.]—telling a story that he had heard from the priests of Sais in Egypt, that of old, and before the Deluge, there was a great island called Atlantis, situate directly at the mouth of the straits of Gibraltar, which contained more countries than both Africa and Asia put together; and that the kings of that country, who not only possessed that Isle, but extended their dominion so far into the continent that they had a country of Africa as far as Egypt, and extending in Europe to Tuscany, attempted to encroach even upon Asia, and to subjugate all the nations that border upon the Mediterranean Sea, as far as the Black Sea; and to that effect overran all Spain, the Gauls, and Italy, so far as to penetrate into Greece, where the Athenians stopped them: but that some time after, both the Athenians, and they and their island, were swallowed by the Flood.
>
> It is very likely that this extreme irruption and inundation of water made wonderful changes and alterations in the habitations of the earth, as 'tis said that the sea then divided Sicily from Italy— [verse quotation elided] —Cyprus from Syria, the isle of Negropont from the continent of Beeotia, and elsewhere united lands that were separate before, by filling up the channel betwixt them with sand and mud: [verse quotation elided]
>
> But there is no great appearance that this isle was this New World so lately discovered: for that almost touched upon Spain, and it were an incredible effect of an inundation, to have tumbled back so prodigious a mass, above twelve hundred leagues: besides that our modern navigators have already almost discovered it to be no island, but terra firma, and continent with the East Indies on the one side, and with the lands under the two poles on the other side; or, if it be separate from them, it is by so narrow a strait and channel, that it none the more deserves the name of an island for that.
>
> It should seem, that in this great body, there are two sorts of motions, the one natural and the other febrific, as there are in ours. When I consider the impression that our river of Dordogne has made in my time on the right bank of its descent, and that in twenty years it has gained so much, and undermined the foundations of so many houses, I perceive it to be an extraordinary agitation: for had it always followed this course, or were hereafter to do it, the aspect of the world would be totally changed. But rivers alter their course, sometimes beating against the one side, and sometimes the other, and some times quietly keeping the channel. I do not speak of sudden inundations, the causes of which everybody understands. In Medoc, by the seashore, the Sieur d'Arsac, my brother, sees an estate he had there, buried under the sands which the sea vomits before it: where the tops of some houses are yet to be seen, and where his rents and domains are converted into pitiful barren pasturage. The inhabitants of this place affirm, that of late years the sea has driven so vehemently upon them, that they have lost above four leagues of land. These sands are her harbingers: and we now see great heaps of moving sand, that march half a league before her, and occupy the land.
>
> The other testimony from antiquity, to which some would apply this discovery of the New World, is in Aristotle; at least, if that little book of Unheard of Miracles be his—[one of the spurious publications brought out under his name—D.W.]. He there tells us, that certain Carthaginians, having crossed the Atlantic Sea without the Straits of Gibraltar, and sailed a very long time, discovered at last a great and fruitful island, all covered over with wood, and watered with several broad and deep rivers, far remote from all terra firma; and that they, and others after them, allured by the goodness and fertility of the soil, went thither with their wives and children, and began to plant a colony. But the senate of Carthage perceiving their people by little and little to diminish, issued out an express prohibition, that none, upon pain of death, should transport themselves thither…

Please work through the following, in this order.

1. First, the sentences themselves. Several constructions are opaque to me, and I want them unpacked clause by clause rather than summarized: what force "there is no great appearance that" carries here (likelihood? evidence? semblance?); what "for that almost touched upon Spain" is doing syntactically and which "that" refers to what; the sense of "that it none the more deserves the name of an island for that"; the antecedent of "her" in "These sands are her harbingers"; and what the pairing "the one natural and the other febrific" imports, given that "febrific" is a medical term for fever-producing. Also note the alternation between "sometimes" and "some times" in the river sentence and say whether it marks a distinction or is merely orthographic.

2. Then the function of the Pyrrhus anecdote. Why does an essay about cannibals open with a Greek word for foreigners applied to a Roman army? I want the point of the anecdote pinned down: is it semantic (the label "barbarian" carries no descriptive content beyond "not us," so it cannot bear the weight the Greeks put on it), or evidential (a label can be overturned by observation of the thing itself), or both? Note that Montaigne triples the anecdote — Pyrrhus, the Greeks on Flaminius's force, Philip on Galba's camp — and say what the repetition adds beyond a single instance. Does the opening thereby promise a correction of naming practices, or a procedure for judging?

3. Then the announced standard. "We are to judge by the eye of reason, and not from common report" is the essay's methodological slogan, yet nearly everything in the pages that follow is report: a servant's recollections, a story Plato attributes to Egyptian priests, a Carthaginian island from a text Montaigne himself flags as possibly spurious, and the affirmations of the inhabitants of the Médoc coast. Please sort the sources in this opening into kinds — first-hand observation by Montaigne, second-hand testimony from an identifiable witness, textual testimony of contested authority, and anonymous local report — and say whether he treats them alike. I would find it especially useful if you located his practice against the familiar distinction in the epistemology of testimony between reductionist accounts, on which testimony merits credence only where we have independent positive reasons for trusting this witness, and anti-reductionist accounts, on which testimony is presumptively credible absent specific defeaters. Which does the opening's practice resemble, and does the "eye of reason" slogan commit him to a reductionism he then declines to exercise?

4. Then the epistemic weight of the servant. He is the sole channel by which the Tupinambá will reach the reader at all. What does Montaigne's announced standard require of a reader who cannot check him? And how should the adjacent remarks be read — that there may yet be another such discovery, "so many wiser men than we having been deceived in this," and that "our eyes are bigger than our bellies, and that we have more curiosity than capacity"? Is this a claim about the inexhaustibility of the world, a claim about the limits of our cognitive apparatus, or a hedge that pre-emptively discounts whatever errors the essay's own reports may contain? Distinguish those possibilities and say which the sentence structure favors.

5. Then Atlantis. Reconstruct "But there is no great appearance that this isle was this New World so lately discovered" as an argument with explicit premises and conclusion. I read at least three: the implausibility that an inundation should have displaced so prodigious a mass over twelve hundred leagues; Atlantis's supposed proximity to Spain, which the New World lacks; and the navigators' finding that the New World is not an island but continuous land. Assess each, and then answer a question that strikes me as the crux: is Montaigne treating the Atlantis story as a live hypothesis to be tested, or as a foil whose dismissal licenses his own fallibilism? And is "our modern navigators have already almost discovered it to be no island" an exercise of the eye of reason or an appeal to precisely the common report the opening warned against — note in particular the qualifier "almost"?

6. Then the geology, which I find the most puzzling and the most interesting. Montaigne observes that the Dordogne has in twenty years undermined the foundations of many houses, calls it "an extraordinary agitation," and then blocks the obvious extrapolation: had it always followed that course, "the aspect of the world would be totally changed," but "rivers alter their course." What exactly is the blocking move? Is he denying that present rates may be projected across long intervals, asserting that rates are not constant, or introducing a distinction between ordinary and pathological change that the "natural / febrific" analogy is meant to supply? Say what work the medical analogy of a body with normal motions and feverish ones does in the argument, and whether it gives him a premise the extrapolation would otherwise lack. Then address the direction of the inference: the digression was introduced to support the deluge hypothesis, yet it seems to reason from small observed changes toward the intelligibility of very large ones — lands joined and severed, channels filled with sand and mud, an estate swallowed by dunes. Does the passage amount to a methodological claim that great alterations in "the habitations of the earth" are to be explained by causes of a kind still observable, and if so, how does that sit with his refusal to extrapolate the Dordogne? I want the tension stated plainly rather than smoothed over, together with an account of why Montaigne might want both halves of it.

7. Finally, the payoff. Given only this opening, does it prepare the ground for a normative relativism — a claim about whose values are correct or incommensurable — or for an epistemic one: a claim about who is entitled to judge, on what evidence, and with what correction of inherited labels? The Pyrrhus anecdote, the distrust of vulgar opinion, the ranking of witnesses, and the geology all seem to me to push toward the second. Argue the point either way, and tell me whether the geological and Atlantidean digressions are load-bearing for the essay's later argument about barbarism or ornamental. If you think the answer cannot be settled without the rest of the chapter, say so explicitly and state what in the later text would decide it, rather than supplying a confident reconstruction.

Two constraints on your reply. First, no reading lists, bibliographies, or citations beyond what the passage itself contains; I want exegesis and argument, not pointers. Second, do not pad the answer with Montaigne's biography or the general context of French expansion — bring in historical or textual background only where it changes the reading of these particular lines, and where a reading depends on the Cotton translation rather than on Montaigne's French, flag the dependence instead of silently relying on it. Where a sentence genuinely admits of two readings, give both and say what would discriminate between them. And if any of my framing above misdescribes the text — if, for instance, the Pyrrhus anecdote is not doing the work I attribute to it, or if the Atlantis material is not a hypothesis at all — correct me at the outset rather than answering within a frame you think is wrong.
