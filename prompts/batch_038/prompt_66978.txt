hey, I'm in week 4 of a course on societal collapse and I think I've spotted something that makes the whole reading list kind of collapse itself (sorry). Bear with me though.

We did the Maya. Standard story: the southern lowland cities — Tikal, Copán, Palenque — stop raising dated monuments in the 9th century and get abandoned, and everybody calls that a collapse. But then in seminar someone pointed out that the people didn't go anywhere. The north took off right afterwards, Chichen Itza went on to dominate the region, and there are still millions of Maya people alive today speaking Maya languages. My prof didn't really push back on it, she just said most people now prefer rupture or transformation as the word.

So here's what I don't get. If a society collapsing is compatable with its population surviving, its cities being rebuilt a few hundred km away, and its calendar, gods and language carrying on uninterrupted, then what event would ever count as a collapse? Rome doesn't collapse either on that standard — the east carried on another thousand years, and Tainter himself says a lot of western peasants were arguably better off after. It looks like the term has been quietly redefined until nothing falls under it, and any candidate case can be waved away as just a transformation.

Which is why I cant see how the diminishing-returns-on-complexity stuff is supposed to be an actual theory. You can't build a general explanatory theory on an empty category, can you? Is the field just having a very long argument about a word, or is there content to collapse that survives the Maya objection — and if there is, why doesn't the survival of the Maya count as evidence against it?
