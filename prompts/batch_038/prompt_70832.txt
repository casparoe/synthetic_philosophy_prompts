I'm an engineer, not a philosopher, and I've been trying to pin down when I ought to defer to my own future judgment. I've written down my intuitions about a set of cases, and I don't think they hang together.

1. I estimate a database migration at six weeks. A month from now, having done half the work, I'll revise that estimate. If I learn today that the revised figure is three weeks, I think my estimate today should simply become three weeks.
2. Same setup, except the revision arrives at the end of a thirty-hour incident. I know from experience that sleep-deprived me declares things finished when they aren't. I think I should keep my current estimate and treat the 3 a.m. revision as noise, even though I'm confident it's coming.
3. Within a year I expect to have absorbed my company's conviction that our platform is best in class, and to assign that roughly 0.9. I don't think I should assign 0.9 now, even conditional on being certain I'll assign it then.
4. In six months I'll have forgotten most of the detail of this incident and will put the root cause at the cache layer about even odds. Today I have the full trace in front of me. I'm genuinely torn here: future me is calm and unbiased, merely less informed, and I want to say deference still fails, but I can't articulate what differs from case 1 except the direction of the change in information.
5. I expect that in five years, having encountered arguments I've already read and dismissed, I'll be convinced that a certain popular design pattern does more harm than good. If the convincing comes from material I can't yet reconstruct, I think I should shift toward that view now. But if I can already write out the argument that will eventually move me, and it doesn't move me, I think I shouldn't budge. Those are meant to be two distinct situations, and I suspect they're one situation described twice.

My provisional rule has been: defer to the extent that the change is produced by evidence I currently lack and will process competently. Case 3 and case 4 seem to break it, and case 5 makes it look circular, since judging whether my future self will process things competently is just the original question in new clothes.

Is there a principle that gets these right, or nearly all of them? Please state it precisely, indicate which case motivates each clause, and tell me what it says about 4 and 5. Then give its verdicts on two situations I haven't considered: expecting to be persuaded by a colleague I trust but cannot myself evaluate, and expecting to hold a view for which I presently lack the vocabulary. Finally, be explicit about which of my five verdicts the principle forces me to give up.
