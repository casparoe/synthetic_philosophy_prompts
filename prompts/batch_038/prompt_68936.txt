I've spent way to long in the money pump literature and I still can't tell what's actually being disputed, so I want a diagnosis rather than another walkthrough of the argument forms.

Here's the shape as I see it. Side A: if your preferences cycle, there's some sequence of swaps that walks you back to where you started minus a dollar, so cyclical preferences are ruled out and transitivity is a genuine requirement of rationality. Side B: those constructions only bite on an agent with no foresight — a sophisticated agent who sees the pump coming simply declines the first trade and never gets exploited, so at best you've proven something about naive agents. Side A then replies that the sophisticated agent is either paying a real cost to protect their cycles, or turning down a trade they'd take in isolation for reasons that have nothing to do with their preferences, and either way they're in violation of some norm about not frustrating your own later choices.

Every few years this gets recycled on the rationalist blogs and nobody moves an inch, which makes me suspect the impasse is structural. So what kind of disagreement is it? Is it about which idealizations are legitimate (foresight, costless refusal, costless precommitment)? About whether "you can be made worse off by your own lights" is even the right criterion, versus rationality being a property of each individual choice? Or about a diachronic self-governence principle that one side treats as bedrock and the other rejects, with no argument available to either?

My own hypothesis: the whole thing turns on the unit of assessment — whether you grade the agent choice-by-choice or over the whole sequence — and both camps slide between the two without noticing. Test that. And tell me what would count as settling it: a pump that survives full sophistication, a case that isolates the diachronic principle from everything else, or nothing at all — in which case explain why the argument is unfinishable rather than merely unfinished.
