So I just spent about four hours arguing with my friend Raj about the Great Divergence and I've come away way more confused about the *shape* of the argument than about the actual history. He's very much a "compare England to the Yangzi Delta, look at coal and wages and empire" guy, and I'm more of a "you're describing Britain's good luck and then calling it a cause" guy. We got nowhere. But the thing I kept throwing at him and he kept just not registering is a pattern I don't have a name for, and I want to know whether it has one. I'm a data analyst, not an academic, so go easy on me if this is all super well known.

The pattern: somebody asks "why did X happen and not Y?", and it turns out the answer depends completely on how you write down the X — not the Y, the X. Same historical event, different perfectly reasonable description of it, different set of near-misses that count as the relevant alternatives, different causes, and the causes you get can straight up contradict each other. Concretely, with Raj: "why England and not the Yangzi Delta." If the thing-to-be-explained is "mechanised cotton spinning shows up in Lancashire in the 1780s," then the live causes are cheap coal sitting next to navigable water, oddly high wages relative to fuel, and slave-grown cotton. If the thing-to-be-explained is "output per person starts rising every year and never stops," then it's property rights, courts, the scientific culture, and high wages barely register at all. If it's "escape from an organic, land-constrained economy," then it's basically energy and ghost acres and the institutions story becomes noise. All three are supposed to be the same question. Each one silently makes a different contrast class the interesting one (France, the Song, India, the Netherlands, Rome) and each one promotes different facts to causes and demotes others to background. And Raj would grab whichever description made his favourite cause load-bearing, and when I pushed on it he'd slide to a different description without seeming to notice he'd done it, and the argument just kept regenerating because there was never a fixed thing being explained.

It's not only history, which is why I think it's a general pattern and not just "historians are sloppy." Same structure in an argument I was in about a startup beating its rival: "beating" as in didn't go bankrupt → timing and a lucky funding round; "beating" as in ended up with 80% of the market → network effects and a distribution deal; "beating" as in created a category that didn't exist → the product. Three explanations, mutually pretty hostile, all answering "why them and not the other guy." Also: my brother and I once had a genuinely nasty conversation about "why did he end up a doctor and not me," and it turned out I meant "in medicine" and he meant "financially secure," and once we noticed that the whole two-year resentment sort of dissolved, which was annoying because I'd kind of been enjoying it.

So — is there an established concept for this, and if not, what would you call it? Here's what I've already bumped into and why none of it quite fits, because I'm not looking for a bare label, I want the edges. Contrastive why-questions and foils: that's about the hidden "rather than Q" term and about relevance, but here the instability is in the P side, and the Q shifts only as a downstream consequence. Grain-sensitivity of explanations, and macro-vs-micro: close, but that's usually about how much detail an explanation ignores, whereas mine is about two explanations at *different* grains each claiming to answer one question. Screening off, INUS conditions, causal selection, the normal/abnormal-condition business: these tell you how to pick among causes once the explanandum is fixed, and my problem is that it isn't. Reference class problem: that's about estimating probabilities, and this happens even when nobody's putting numbers on anything. Equivocation: no, because nobody's using one word with two meanings — "industrialised," "rich," "escaped Malthus" are all honourable descriptions. Motte and bailey: no defensible core and no indefensible extension. Moving the goalposts: the standard of evidence isn't being changed, the thing being explained is. What I most want carved out is the *sliding* version specifically — using description-shifts to keep a pet cause relevant — because a natural reply is "well, every explanation is relative to a description, that's just how explanation works, so what's the complaint?" and I can't articulate why the sliding case is worse than the ordinary case. Is there some requirement you could put on a why-not question for it to be well posed, something like the answer having to be stable across the descriptions of the fact that the questioner would accept? Where does that requirement break down, and what are the boundary cases between a legitimate re-specification ("actually I was asking about growth rates, not the spinning jenny, let me be precise") and the bad slide? Two or three made-up examples of each would help me more than definitions.
