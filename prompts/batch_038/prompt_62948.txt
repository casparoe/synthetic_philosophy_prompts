I am three-quarters of the way through a story collection for a small press, and my editor's note on the last two pieces amounted to the observation that they read as essays wearing costumes. Fair enough. What I want now is a story that carries a philosophical problem entirely in scene and behaviour, and I want it written to a specification, because I have been circling this idea for months without getting anywhere. The problem is whether what you believe can change what you see — not what you notice, or remember, or are disposed to say, but the character of the experience itself. The familiar anchors cut both ways, which is precisely the trouble. An arrow-headed line still looks longer than its twin after you have laid a ruler along them and know better; a photograph of a dress once split the internet into people who saw white and gold and people who saw blue and black, and neither camp could argue the other across; and yet in that old tasting experiment where a white wine was coloured red with an odourless dye, experienced tasters went on to describe it in the vocabulary of red wine — describing, apparently, an aroma they were not having. From the outside one can dispute which of these cases is perception and which is judgment. From the inside, the difference is invisible, and that first-person blindness is what I want the story to be about.

Here is the material I have assembled; use it, alter it, discard pieces of it as the story requires. The narrator is a colour matcher at an automotive refinish shop — twenty-six years at the bench, mixing by formula and correcting by eye, spraying test cards, judging panels under a light booth with several sources in it: daylight tubes, tungsten, the flat white of the shop's LEDs, and, out in the yard after closing, sodium vapour, under which two panels that matched at noon can part company. Metamerism is her daily bread; a match is never absolute but relative to a light, an angle, a mood of the eye. Her standing in the shop rests on the claim that she can split a difference nobody else can see, and she keeps two private touchstones. The first is a pair of chips she knows to be identical: she made them herself in her apprenticeship, was told by a supervisor as a joke that one was last year's leftover stock, watched the two diverge before her eyes, learned the truth that same week, and a quarter of a century on still cannot make them look the same, though she has handled them a thousand times. The second is the case the story turns upon. She has signed off a customer's refinished door as an invisible repair; a day later an adjuster mentions in passing that the shop pulled the door overnight and resprayed it in the other bay, so that what she signed off was not the panel she had mixed for. From the moment she is told, the mismatch is glaring — she can point to where the metallic flake lies wrong, she can say which quarter of the panel floats darker — and the job cards and the mixing logs then establish, to everyone's satisfaction but hers, that no door was ever swapped and nothing about the object changed between her two looks. Now she must give a deposition in the customer's small-claims action against the shop and say, under oath, what she saw and when she saw it.

What I want is roughly three to four thousand words, first person, past tense, with section breaks if you need them, and a title. The philosophy must never be named: no philosophers, no terminology, no one at the counter explaining that her perception may be leaky, no interior monologue laying out two rival hypotheses in tidy clauses. Let the reader assemble the question out of what she does — the little tests she builds and rebuilds, the way she starts avoiding the yard light, the spectrophotometer she keeps on the bench and finally uses, getting back a reading that falls inside tolerance, which settles nothing, because tolerance is a threshold somebody chose and not a fact about the world. Include one passage in which she describes the same two surfaces twice in plainly incompatible terms and lets the contradiction stand unremarked. Give her a scene in which she tries to construct a proper control — a colleague to shuffle the cards, to label them, to keep from her which is which — and the control fails, and show us why it must: she cannot un-know what she knows, so there is no double blind that works against yourself. Please do not resolve it with a psychiatric reveal, a late confession that the adjuster lied, or a twist in which she was looking at the wrong car all along; I am far less interested in whether she is reliable than in what it costs a person whose entire trade consists of her own reports to discover that she has no privileged access to whether they are reports of seeing. End somewhere that leaves the question genuinely open and locates it in the hands rather than the head. No preface and no afterword — just the story.
