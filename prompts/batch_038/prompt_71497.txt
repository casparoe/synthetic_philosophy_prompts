I chair the Research, Evaluation, and Accountability Committee of a mid-sized urban school district. The committee reports to the superintendent, and the full board votes on what we send it. At Thursday's meeting we have to decide two things: what the district's annual public report card should show about a two-year literacy pilot, and whether to fund its expansion next fall at a cost of roughly $1.8 million.

The pilot ran in nine elementary schools. The comparison group is fourteen schools that continued with the district's standard program, in which children are identified for special education or English-learner services only on referral. The nine pilot schools adopted universal screening in the fall and organized small-group instruction around the screening results; as a side effect, they identified far more children for services than the comparison schools did.

The outcome measure is the share of third graders meeting the proficiency cut on the state's end-of-grade reading assessment. Our staff analyst's figures for last spring:

Pilot schools — 6,000 third graders, 2,980 proficient (49.7%)
· not identified for services: 3,500 students, 2,380 proficient (68.0%)
· identified for services: 2,500 students, 600 proficient (24.0%)

Comparison schools — 10,000 third graders, 5,520 proficient (55.2%)
· not identified for services: 8,000 students, 5,120 proficient (64.0%)
· identified for services: 2,000 students, 400 proficient (20.0%)

So the pilot schools do better in both subgroups, by about four points each, and worse overall, by about five and a half points. The committee is split over what that shows, and the split is not really a statistical one. Two principals from pilot schools insist the subgroup figures are the only ones that describe what the program does to a child. Our CFO says the overall figure is the only one a parent or a reporter can check without first accepting someone's modeling choices — and it is the figure the state's accountability index uses, which means the pilot schools' ratings will drop this year whatever we publish. Our psychometrician raises a complication that I find the most troubling: because the pilot's own screening is what drove the higher identification rate, being "identified" was not a pre-existing feature of those children but partly an effect of the program. Separately, the pilot schools volunteered for it and serve somewhat higher-poverty neighborhoods than the comparison schools, and that gap persists inside the subgroups. A union representative has asked that whatever we publish not be usable in staff evaluations at the pilot sites.

Please draft the memo I will circulate before the meeting, roughly 1,000 to 1,300 words, with the recommendation up front. It should tell the board: what the annual report card should present and how it should be annotated; whether to fund the expansion, and if so on what conditions and timeline; and what, if anything, to change about screening and identification. Most importantly, it should say which of these tables — if either — answers the question the board is actually asking, and why; if the honest answer is that neither does, say what evidence would. And give the strongest version of the CFO's case for the overall figure, not a straw man, since I need to be able to sit across from him after reading it.

You may name a technical distinction where it genuinely earns its place, but explain it in the memo's own terms: board members will not look anything up, and I do not want a citation to the methods literature doing work the argument should do itself.

Be candid about how much weight the recommendation can bear. Separate what the figures settle from what turns on an assumption about how identification works or about why those nine schools volunteered, and tell me which one or two assumptions I should press the analyst on before Thursday. If a conclusion is only weakly supported, write it as weakly supported — I would rather bring a hedged memo to the board than a confident one I cannot defend.

Finally, if you think my framing is off — if the question I am asking the committee to answer is not one these data could settle even in principle, or if the memo worth writing recommends something quite different from the three decisions I have listed — say so plainly at the top of your reply, before or instead of drafting the document. I have no use for a polished memo that quietly accommodates a confused charge.
