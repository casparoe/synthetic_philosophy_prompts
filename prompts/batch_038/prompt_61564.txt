Title-ish: **Right but early: when a failed prediction isn't a failed theory**

The dodge is familiar. A long-run theory predicts something, decades pass, the thing doesn't happen, and the defender says the theory wasn't wrong, it was early. Malthus is the standing example: he argued in 1798 that population, unchecked, grows geometrically while food supply grows only arithmetically, so wages get pinned near subsistence. English living standards did stay ugly for a long time after that — economic historians have a name for the stretch from roughly 1790 to 1840 where output per head climbed but working-class real wages barely moved — and then they took off and never came back down. So: was Malthus wrong, or right-but-early? People have been having this exact argument for two centuries and I think it's mostly been a waste, because nobody says what "early" would have to mean for the plea to be more than a redescription of failure.

The default philosophical worry is the usual one about auxiliary hypotheses: any prediction can be saved by adjusting a parameter you never pinned down, so a theory that can always declare itself early can never be wrong. I accept that as far as it goes, but it's too blunt. It implies the earliness plea is *always* a dodge, and that's clearly false — the germ theory of disease was early for a lot of specific outcomes, and we don't think that makes it ad hoc, because the lag was attributable to things we could measure separately. So there has to be a line, and the line should be drawable in advance.

My proposal is that an earliness plea is legitimate only when three conditions hold.

1. **The mechanism is visibly running.** During the silent period, the causal process the theory posits must be observable operating, with the predicted sign, even though the aggregate outcome hasn't moved yet. Not "we assume it's still running" — we can see it running.

2. **The lag is a variable, not a constant.** The length of the delay has to be derivable from parameters estimated somewhere other than the failed prediction — a capital lifetime, a generational turnover time, an adjustment speed — and it has to have been pinned *before* the miss. If the only thing that fixes the lag is the size of the miss, the plea is a free parameter wearing a costume.

3. **The error is quantitative.** What went wrong has to be the magnitude of that lag parameter, not the absence of an effect the theory said would be there in some form.

If all three hold, "early" is a claim about a number, and numbers can be non-ad-hoc-ly wrong. If any one fails, "early" collapses into "wrong, politely."

Two consequences I want to develop, and I'm least sure about the second.

First, earliness isn't a property of the theory alone. The usefulness of a prediction decays with lead time, and the decay curve depends on how fast the decision it informs can be adjusted. So a theory can be right-but-early for a 200-year question and useless-but-right for a 20-year one, and both verdicts are correct simultaneously. "Early" should be indexed to a decision, not to a calendar. This is why I think the Malthus fight is unresolvable as usually posed: the two sides are scoring the same theory against different horizons and neither admits it.

Second — and this is the part I'm most attached to but also most suspicious of — there's a mirror case that nobody talks about. A theory can be **wrong but late**: it keeps passing tests after its mechanism has stopped operating, because the outcome variable carries inertia. Demography hands you the cleanest version. Population momentum means a country can keep growing for decades after fertility has already fallen to replacement, purely from its age structure. So a doomster who predicted continued growth in 1980 collected a string of confirmations that had nothing to do with the correctness of his account of *why* growth would continue. His theory would have predicted the same thing in a country that had already solved the problem. If earliness pleas need policing, so do runs of successful predictions, and for the same reason: the outcome is lagged relative to the mechanism, and lagged outcomes let dead theories cash checks.

Applying it back: Malthus scores well on (1) for the 1790–1840 window — the mechanism looks like it was still binding, wages really were pinned while productivity rose. He scores badly on (2). The geometric-versus-arithmetic comparison implies a ceiling but no timetable, and nothing in the 1798 Essay lets you derive how long the ceiling holds; notably he kept rewriting the book for three decades, through a much expanded second edition under his own name in 1803 and several more after that, without ever producing a lag estimate. So the honest verdict isn't "wrong" and isn't "early." It's that he had a decent model of an equilibrium and literally nothing to say about how the equilibrium breaks — and the break is exactly where he got tested. Right about the regime, silent about the transition.

Where I think this is weakest, for what it's worth: condition (2) may just be unsatisfiable for any theory with a genuinely long horizon, which would mean my test is one only mid-range theories can pass, and that I've smuggled in a preference for short horizons and dressed it as a criterion. Also (1) is gerrymanderable, since most mechanisms are latent and "we can see it operating" usually means "we can see a proxy our theory licenses us to call the mechanism." And a referee could reasonably say the whole thing is Quine–Duhem with extra steps.
