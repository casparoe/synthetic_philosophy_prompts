I'm trying to pin down where I actually stand on whether beliefs get into perception itself, and the only way I can do it is to list the cases I keep noticing and say what my gut verdict is on each, then see if there's a principle underneath. Verdicts are about whether the belief is genuinely changing the experience, as opposed to just changing what I'm disposed to report afterwards.

1. Rope on the path at dusk. I've stepped over it twice already, I know exactly what it is, and it still looks like a snake for a split second every time. Not penetration.
2. My mum's face after I found out she'd lied about my dad for twenty years. It looks colder now, in a way I cant break down into features — not "I notice the mouth more", just different. Penetration, clearly.
3. A much stronger chess player I play tells me he sees a weak pawn structure roughly the way I see an expression. He isn't scanning and concluding. Penetration, though maybe a different species of it.
4. A banana under odd lighting in a shop. I know it's greyish in that light and I know what it's "really" coloured, and it still looks straightforwardly yellow. Not penetration — I'd say that's constancy machinery doing its job, not a belief pushing in.
5. Read the back label ("blackberry, wet stone"), then genuinely taste blackberry. Penetration, and the one that bothers me most, because it makes the tasting useless as evidence.
6. Shown the same ambiguous face photograph and told beforehand it's an angry face, or a frightened one, and it looks whichever I was told. I want to call this penetration but I half suspect I'm just being agreeable about my own reports.
7. Shadow on the bedroom wall, checked it three times, still looks like a spider. Not penetration.
8. Since learning the names for two shades I used to lump together, I can't unsee the difference between them. Feels like penetration, but also like my visual system got rebuilt rather than talked into something.
9. My flatmate is convinced I see her kitchen as messier than it is because I disaprove of how she lives. Now, looking at it, I discount a good chunk of what I seem to see on the grounds that it's probably my disapproval doing the talking. I want to say a belief about my own bias can only change my trust in the seeing, never the seeing — but from the inside I genuinely can't tell.

The pairs I can't reconcile: 4 and 5 look like the same structure — a bit of knowledge that fails to change what it looks like in one case and a bit of knowledge that changes what it tastes like in the other — yet I give opposite verdicts. And 6 and 7 are worse: a thirty-second cue from a stranger penetrates, while a fear I've had since childhood and have thoroughly out-thought doesn't.

My tentative principle is two clauses. (a) It only counts if the belief is about the thing being perceived, not about perception in general. (b) It only counts if the effect survives your knowing that you have it. Together these give me the wrong answer on the rope, the banana and the shadow, which all look like penetration by (a) and (b) and which I'm sure aren't. So it's broken, but I don't know how.

What I want: the principle, or a short list of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I haven't thought of. For each clause, tell me which of my nine cases forced it in. Then run it over 4 and 5 and over 6 and 7 and say which of my verdicts it overturns — I'd rather have a simpler principle that costs me two verdicts than a baroque one that saves all nine, but tell me what the price of each option is. Give me its verdicts on a couple of cases I haven't listed, and finish by saying which of these experiences I should stop treating as evidence at all.

Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence — the principle you'd defend, in one go — and then give me the reasoning. No preamble and no restating the question back at me.
