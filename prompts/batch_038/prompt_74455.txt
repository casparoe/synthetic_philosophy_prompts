I co-teach a foundations-of-probability seminar with a colleague who has final say over which worked examples go into the problem sets. Before I propose the next one, I want to predict how she will rule on it, because her rulings this term follow a pattern I can describe case by case but cannot yet state as a rule.

Her verdicts so far:

1. A hoop-mounted pointer. A circular hoop is clamped in a jig, a pointer is spun twice, and the manufacturer certifies that the resting angle is uniformly distributed. The chord is the one joining the two resting points. Asked for the probability that this chord is longer than the side of an inscribed equilateral triangle, she said one third and called the item well posed.

2. A dart. A dart is thrown at a circular target whose hit distribution the thrower's coach certifies to be uniform across the face; the chord in question is the unique chord bisected at the point where the dart lands. She said one quarter, well posed, adding that the exact center is no embarrassment because a single point carries probability zero.

3. A gauge on a marked radius. A slider moves along one radius of a disc and stops at a distance from the center certified uniform along that radius; the chord is drawn through the stopping point at right angles to the radius. She said one half, well posed, and remarked that nothing turns on which radius was marked.

4. A textbook line reading only that a chord is chosen at random from a circle, with no apparatus described. She refused to mark any number correct and told the students the item was defective rather than difficult.

5. An advertisement for a machine shop: cubes with sides between 0 and 2 centimeters. The exam item asked for the probability that a cube from this shop has volume below one cubic centimeter. She refused it, commenting that the advertisement told her about one quantity and the question asked about another, and that nothing she had been given connected the two.

6. The same advertisement with one sentence added: the die setting is chosen by turning a knob to a position uniform on a calibrated dial, and the dial position fixes the side length directly. The same question about volume. She said one half, and observed that a single added sentence had converted a defective item into a sound one.

7. A shuttle bus due at some time between 2:00 and 2:20, with nothing else known about its schedule; the item asked for the probability that it arrives by 2:05. She said one quarter and defended it against a student who objected that this was the same sort of unsupported guess she had just refused in item 5. Her reply to the student: in the shop's case she had been told about one quantity and asked about a different one, whereas here she is told about the arrival time and asked about the arrival time.

8. A sealed envelope containing a slip marked with a number known only to be one of 1 through 100. Asked for the probability that the number is at most 50, she said one half; pressed on how the slip came to be written, she said that this did not matter, since she had been told what the possibilities were and had been told nothing that distinguishes among them.

The example I now want to propose is this. A varnish is made from linseed oil and turpentine and nothing else. The tin's label records only that the mixture contains at least as much oil as turpentine by volume, and at most nine times as much. The item asks for the probability that the ratio of oil to turpentine is at most three to one.

What I would like from you:

Give at least three candidate rules, each stated as a general policy about when a probability item has an answer and what that answer is — not as a restatement of the eight verdicts — and each consistent with all eight. Each rule should be sharp enough that some conceivable item would falsify it; say for each rule what kind of item that would be.

(b) For each candidate rule, state what it implies for the varnish item: a number if the rule yields one, an explicit refusal if it does not. Where a rule yields a number, show the calculation in two or three lines so that I can check it. If two of your candidate rules yield the same verdict on the varnish item, say so and identify what other feature of the item, or what other item, would tell them apart.

(c) Say which rule you take to be hers and why. In particular, state which of the eight verdicts carries the most evidential weight for your choice, which carry the least, and which of her stated remarks — the comment in item 5, the reply in item 7, the remark in item 8 — you read as most diagnostic and which as most likely to be a rationalization.

(d) Suppose she answers the varnish item in a way that fails to settle matters between your two leading candidates, for instance by giving a number with a hedging clause, or by declining for a reason that either rule could accommodate. Name one further item I could put to her that would separate those two, and give each rule's verdict on it.
