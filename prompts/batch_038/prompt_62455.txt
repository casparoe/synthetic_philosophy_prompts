I am a junior and the founding president of the behavioral economics club at my high school. Twice each year we run a choice experiment on roughly fifty student volunteers and present the findings at a regional student research symposium, where faculty from two nearby colleges serve as judges. Our submission last spring was returned with the criticism that our conclusions had outrun our design, so this semester we are trying to build something that can withstand scrutiny. Our faculty advisor is a mathematics teacher with no training in decision theory; she will not sign the protocol until she can follow each step and satisfy herself that we are not overreaching. Sessions run forty minutes, our entire incentive budget is about fifty dollars, and no member of the club has taken statistics beyond an introductory course.

Our subject is the independence axiom of expected utility theory and the pattern of choices associated with the Allais paradox. We intend to use the familiar four options: a certain one million dollars; a 10 percent chance of five million dollars, an 89 percent chance of one million, and a 1 percent chance of nothing; an 11 percent chance of one million dollars; and a 10 percent chance of five million dollars.

What I need is a numbered procedure the club can actually carry out. It should tell us how to assemble a pair of tasks that is genuinely capable of detecting a violation of independence, and how to check that what we have assembled has the intended structure; what to ask each participant, in what order, and with what comprehension checks; which response pattern counts as a violation; what rival explanations we are obliged to rule out before our report may state that a participant violates expected utility theory; and what to do with participants who either keep or abandon the pattern once the two choices are placed in front of them together. Please be exact about how much of this reasoning depends on knowing a participant's utility function and how much does not, because that is the point I understand least well and, I suspect, the point on which we will be judged. Keep the mathematics within reach of a capable high school student with a calculator, but supply enough of the underlying theory that my advisor can audit the procedure on her own.

Mark plainly the places where the procedure runs out and leaves us with a judgment call rather than a method, and state what our study cannot establish even if everything goes perfectly — including whether a clean, repeated pattern of the relevant kind entitles us to describe our volunteers as irrational.

Then apply the procedure to three designs. First, both pairs presented to all fifty participants within a single session, in sequence, for hypothetical payoffs. Second, the same pair split between two groups, one task per group, with a real token payment for whichever choice we implement. Third, a version we devised ourselves in which every dollar figure is scaled down from millions to single digits, on the reasoning that our volunteers have never handled sums that large and so cannot have settled intuitions about them. For each, say what the procedure requires us to do, what it permits us to conclude, and what it forbids us from concluding. If it condemns one of the three, I would much rather learn that now than in front of the judges.

Please write your response in American English.
