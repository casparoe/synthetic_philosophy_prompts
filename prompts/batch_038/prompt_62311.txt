I did transport physics in metals for thirty-eight years and retired four years ago, and somewhere in there I fell into philosophy of science headfirst, mostly by accident. My old department has now asked me to give forty minutes to the first-year materials grad students in their professional development seminar. They know their physics cold. Not one of them has ever sat through a philosophy course. I want to talk to them about structural realism.

So please dont explain structural realism to me — I've done a fair amount of the reading and I more or less know what Worrall is up to. What I need is a plan for how I explain it, in what order, and where the thing is likely to go wrong.

First, the example. The book I started with uses the ether, Fresnel's equations surviving while the medium doesn't, and I get why that's the standard move, but it's the wrong hook for this room. My audience has never met the luminiferous ether except as a piece of trivia and they won't feel any loss when it goes away. I want to use something they already own, the free-electron theory of metals. Drude in 1900 treats the conduction electrons as a classical gas bouncing off fixed positive centers and gets Ohm's law out of it plus a version of the Wiedemann–Franz ratio, and the model is a disaster in the details: the electronic specific heat comes out about a hundred times too big, the Hall coefficient has the wrong sign for a lot of metals, and the mean free paths it needs are absurdly long with no microscopic story for why. Then in the late twenties Sommerfeld keeps essentially the whole conductivity structure and swaps Maxwell–Boltzmann statistics for Fermi–Dirac, and the specific heat problem disappears, while the mean free path puzzle has to wait for electrons being treated as waves, which is a different fix entirely. Tell me honestly whether that case is strong enough to carry forty minutes on its own, and where it's going to invite a lesson I don't want. I'm specifically worried about a smart kid saying that Drude wasn't approximately right, it was just wrong, and that Sommerfeld isn't a refinement but a replacement out of a different conceptual world — which I think is a fair reading and which, if I can't answer it, guts the talk.

Then the order. My instinct is that the history has to come before the thesis, that they need to feel the record of confident theories that are now dead before "what exactly survives a revolution" is even a question with any force for them. But maybe that's backwards and starting in the graveyard just makes them cynical before it makes them curious. Lay out the sequence for me with the reasons, what has to be in place for each next piece to mean anything, and roughly how much of the forty minutes each piece should get.

The misconceptions will be physicist-shaped and I want each one headed off at the point in the sequence where it would otherwise form, not mopped up in the Q&A at the end. The ones I'm braced for: that this is instrumentalism wearing a realist's coat, i.e. shut up and calculate with footnotes; that I'm telling them electrons aren't real; that this is just what working scientists have always done, keep the equations and retire the cartoons, so why is it a seminar; and the one I actually dread, which is some student who's read a little saying that if all a theory commits you to is the structure, and structure just means whatever relations some true description says obtain, then you can satisfy the thesis by re-describing anything you like provided it has the right number of elements, so it's either trivial or it isn't a thesis at all. I believe that last one is old and has somebody's name attached to it, but I'm no good at that from memory and I'm not going to guess. Whatever it is, tell me whether a materials audience can absorb it in ninety seconds and where in the order it belongs, and if it genuinely can't be absorbed at this level, tell me what to say instead that isn't a dodge.

Give me one question I can put to the room near the end that would tell me whether the idea landed rather than whether they're being polite to the retired guy. And tell me what to leave out and say out loud that I'm leaving it out — the Ramsey-sentence machinery, and the split between the moderate version and the radical one that claims there aren't objects at all, only structure. On that last one I have opinions and I don't buy it, but I refuse to strawman it either, so tell me how to give it two honest minutes without turning my own seminar into a fight I would lose.

Write it to me like a letter, please. Continuous prose, paragraphs are fine, but no headings, no bullet points, no bold, nothing laid out in a table. I read this stuff on a tablet out on the porch and I don't want to be scrolling around a formatted document.
