I'm trying to work out whether my intuitions about biological function add up to anything, or whether they're just mush, and I'd rather test them against a real account than argue with myself any longer. Verdicts first, then what I want from you.

1. A healthy frog heart pumps blood — that's its function, full stop. And if I anaesthetise the frog and stop the heart for ten minutes during surgery, I still think the function is there the whole time. It's just not being exercised.
2. That same heart makes a thumping noise. It does that reliably, the noise is a real causal consequence of the thing working, and people listen for it constantly. But I flatly don't think making noise is the heart's function. Side effect.
3. Now a hypothetical: some species has a little duct that concentrates a particular salt, does it reliably, and the organism would die without it. But suppose we've got the phylogeny nailed down and the duct is a pure byproduct of some folding event, never selected for anything. I want to say concentrating that salt IS a function of the duct. Notice this is case 2 with the opposite verdict and I cannot tell you what the the difference is. That's the pair I most want sorted.
4. Feathers in some lineage started out being selected for keeping warm and only later got used for flight. Once the animal is properly flying, I think flight is a genuine function of the feathers and not a mere current use — even though nothing about the flight performance explains why the lineage grew feathers to begin with.
5. Conversely: suppose a snake lineage where the pelvic nubs have lost even the muscular attachment and do literally nothing at all. I think they have no function any more. I don't want to say they're malfunctioning at holding up hind legs.
6. A newborn with a congenitally defective heart that has never once pumped a drop: I still say its heart has the function of pumping blood, and thats precisely why we call it a defect rather than a novelty.
7. A lab prints an organ from a patient's own cells and installs it. The organ has functions, obviously, and I'd say they're the same ones a natural organ would have — despite there being no selection history for this particular object and no designer with any intention beyond copying something.
8. Artefacts, for contrast: a resin lump manufactured as a paperweight that I use as a doorstop. Stopping the door isn't its function, it's a use. But I don't think being a paperweight is its function either, since it hasn't left my hallway in years. My intuitions about made things seem to be leaking into the biology and I can't tell whether that's a bug.

My own tentative principle, which I suspect is broken: a trait's function is whatever it does that explains why the thing is still around — where "explains" gets cashed out either by selection or by somebody deliberately keeping it in place. Tell me whether that's a real position or a fudge that papers over 2 and 3.

What I actually want:

- a principle, or a small set of candidates, stated properly — precise enough that I could apply it to a case I've never seen before. Go clause by clause and say which of my verdicts each clause is there to capture.
- which of my verdicts it can't keep. If one has to go, say so and say why; don't quietly reinterpret it until it fits.
- its verdict on two cases I haven't listed: (a) a duplicated gene where the copies have split the ancestral job roughly in half so that neither does what the original did; (b) a behaviour — some greeting ritual in a social mammal, say — that demonstrably lowers group conflict and that the group would lose if it vanished, but which nobody would call an organ.
- and whether the two big families here, the selected-effects people and the causal-role people, are genuine rivals or are answering different questions while I've been confusing myself.

Don't send me off to read anything — I just want the reasoning worked through.
