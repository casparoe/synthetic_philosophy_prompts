My roommate and his friend Owen got into a long argument last night about the Pasadena game — the one where you flip a fair coin until heads comes up, and if heads first lands on flip n you win (−1)^(n+1) · 2^n/n dollars. So the payoffs are +2, −2, +8/3, −4, and so on. Because the 2^n cancels the probability of 1/2^n, the expected value works out to the alternating harmonic series, which sums to ln 2. But that series is only conditionally convergent, so you can reorder the terms to make it sum to anything at all.

Here's how their exchange went. Nadia said the expectation is just undefined, full stop: a conditionally convergent series doesn't have a genuine expected value, so expected utility theory goes silent here and you can't claim the ticket is worth ln 2 or any other number. Owen said that's overreading the mathematics — the coin flips arrive in a physically fixed order and you never actually get to rearrange anything, so in that order the sum is ln 2 and a rational person should pay up to just under 70 cents. Nadia came back that you could implement the exact same payoff table with a different physical setup, say payoffs indexed to a shuffled deck of cards, and get a different sum; so if ordering is doing all the work, the value of the game depends on arbitrary details of how it's built, and there's no fact about what it's worth. Owen said fine — then take the setup with the natural ordering as the real question, and note that a theory choking here isn't broken, it's telling you something: unbounded payoffs are a limit case, every actual gamble is bounded, so the Pasadena game is an idealization nobody ever faces. Nadia replied that this dodges the point, because the whole reason to study the idealization is to see what happens as the bound is pushed outward, and "real games are bounded" doesn't tell you what to do as they approach it. Neither budged and I got lost somewhere in the middle.
