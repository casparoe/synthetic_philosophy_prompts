I teach an intermediate seminar on the philosophy of well-being — eighteen students, one seventy-five-minute meeting in week five, mostly philosophy majors with a few from psychology and public policy. That week we read Nozick's experience machine, and for the third year running the discussion has collapsed in the same way: within about twelve minutes every student has announced, with complete confidence, that they would not plug in; three or four of them have recited the same handful of reasons (we want to do things rather than merely have the experience of doing them, we want to be a certain kind of person, the machine confines us to a reality we built ourselves); and the rest of the hour becomes a polite standoff between people who are certain and cannot say why. I want to replace that discussion with a structured activity in which the students' own choices, rather than their stated intuitions, become the object of study. I have a whiteboard, a document camera, and roughly fifteen dollars for materials.

The structural idea I want you to build around — improve on it if you can, but do not drop it — is decomposition. No student should ever face a single, clearly labelled decision corresponding to plugging in. Instead the session runs as a sequence of short rounds in which each student repeatedly chooses between two ways of earning the same small reward: one tied transparently to what they actually do or produce, the other delivering an equal or better payoff with the performance requirement quietly removed — a score assigned rather than earned, a compliment generated rather than elicited, a puzzle delivered already solved. Each individual choice should look unremarkable, even prudent. At the reveal, students should be able to compute from their own record sheets that the aggregate of their local decisions is structurally the machine, and that they spent most of the hour inside it. The reveal must be arithmetic rather than rhetorical: a tabulation they can check, not an accusation. Hard constraints: no deception of any kind, since these are students I grade and everything about the setup must be on the table from the first minute — which is exactly why the force has to come from accumulation rather than surprise; a currency that is not grade-adjacent but is small, immediate, and genuinely wanted; participation optional, with an opt-out role that constitutes real work rather than a penalty box; two comparison conditions whose results we can display side by side, one in which each student allocates for an anonymous classmate instead of for themselves, and one staged on the inversion, where students begin already inside, are told what they have given up, and may leave at a price; and a photocopyable record sheet that students fill in as they go and that generates the class-wide data set used in the reveal.

For the deliverables: a minute-by-minute timeline for the full seventy-five minutes; the exact wording you want me to read aloud for each round's instructions, since ambiguity there will contaminate the data; a debrief that maps each round onto hedonism, desire-satisfaction (including the distinction between the desires a person has at the moment of choice and the desires they have about their life as a whole), objective-list theories, and achievement or hybrid views, and that says explicitly which member of Nozick's cluster each round actually puts pressure on and where the activity cannot tell them apart. The debrief must engage rather than deflect the objection I expect within sixty seconds of the reveal — that of course they took the free points, it was a game, and nothing follows about their lives — and it should not resolve into a verdict about who is refuted; what I want them to leave seeing is that the intuition is a bundle, that the theories disagree about which strand does the work, and that their revealed choices may or may not be evidence about their values. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the student who games the reveal, the one who is genuinely delighted to have taken the easy route and refuses to be embarrassed, the round that overruns, the totals that fail to add up in front of them — with a specific fix for each; how to compress it to fifty minutes and what breaks if the section doubles to forty; a one-question exit slip whose answers I can use to open the following week; and a frank closing note on the feature of the thought experiment this activity cannot reproduce at all, which I take to be the guarantee that the person inside does not know they are inside, since mine knows it constantly — together with what I must then supply by argument to cover the gap.
