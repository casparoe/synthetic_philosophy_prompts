I run a monthly book club where we pair a play with a chunk of philosophy, and next month's pair is Ibsen's An Enemy of the People with Kierkegaard on the press and the public. It's an awkward pairing, I'll admitt — half my group thinks the connection is obvious and the other half thinks it's a category error, so I want an actual reading rather than a syllabus blurb. On the Kierkegaard side I'm working from the Two Ages review (the 1846 one, written right after the Corsair business) and the short dedication piece that usually gets anthologized as The Crowd Is Untruth; in the translation I'm reading he says that "even if every individual possessed the truth in private, yet if they came together into a crowd ... untruth would at once be let in," that "a crowd is an abstraction, which does not have hands," and that the crowd "either renders the single individual wholly unrepentant and irresponsible, or weakens his responsibility by making it a fraction of his decision." On the Ibsen side we're following Stockmann from the arrival of the lab results, through Act III where Hovstad's paper drops his article and prints the mayor's reassurance instead, into Act IV at Captain Horster's house where Aslaksen chairs the meeting, Stockmann throws away his water report and starts ranting about the compact liberal majority, and the meeting declares him an enemy of the people — and then Act V, with the smashed windows, Petra sacked from the school, Kiil having sunk the money he'd earmarked for his own daughter into Baths shares, and Stockmann refusing to emigrate while announcing that the strongest man is the one who stands most alone. Don't quote the play back at me; I've read it and I'd rather you not invent line readings. Paraphrase and point to scenes.

The reading I want has to deal with three things, and I'd like you to think hard about whether they're really three problems or one. First: Kierkegaard's diagnosis is anonymity and abstraction — the public is a phantom only the press can conjure, nobody in particular, chatter that levels everyone into interchangeable specimens. But Ibsen's press isn't anonymous at all. It's a tiny local sheet with a named editor, a subeditor who's in it for the excitement, and a printer who preaches moderation, and it flips not because it's a leveling organ but because the mayor offers it something and the shareholders' money matters. So does the play show leveling, or capture? And if it's capture, is that a rival diagnosis to Kierkegaard's or just a smaller-scale version of the same one? Related: the town is small enough that everyone knows everyone, and it's precisely thier personal ties — Kiil, Horster, Aslaksen, his own brother — that make the persecution work. Say whether that web of acquaintance is a hole in Kierkegaard's framework or an intensification of it. Second, and I think this is the crux: Act IV is literally a chaired assembly taking a vote, which sounds exactly like the case Kierkegaard has in mind when he objects to the crowd receiving "decisive, voting, noisy, audible importance" — yet the man crushed by that vote spends Act V wanting to lead, wanting to collect the town's poor boys and teach them, and he's visibly drunk on his own speech mid-rant. Is Stockmann's closing individualism Kierkegaard's single individual, or is it a would-be party leader's defiance — the crowd-shaped wish to command a better crowd? I want a verdict, not a shrug. Third: Stockmann is right about a fact, and the fact only matters if a majority pays to relay the pipes, so the play stages a truth that's impotent without the crowd, which is nearly the inverse of Kierkegaard's point, where truth goes wrong precisely by wanting the crowd on its side. Does the play end up containing a rebuttal to The Crowd Is Untruth, or does it only show what such a rebuttal costs?

Assume you're a philosopher who knows Kierkegaard's social criticism inside out and also reads nineteenth-century drama for pleasure, and start by brainstorming which scenes actually bear on which Kierkegaardian categories — leveling, the public, reflection, the crowd, the single individual — before you commit to a line of argument, because I suspect some of the obvious pairings are wrong. Fold in the biographical wrinkle too: Kierkegaard wrote Two Ages fresh from being cartooned in the Corsair, and Ibsen wrote this play fresh from the pile-on over Ghosts, so tell me whether wounded-author position-taking should make us discount either diagnosis or is the only reason either is worth reading. Where the play resists the reading — Ibsen isn't handing us an unqualified hero, the man is vain and rather enjoys being persecuted — say so plainly instead of smoothing it over, and end by telling me what the play contributes to the argument about the public that argument alone couldn't. Format: lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence, then give the reasoning, no preamble and no restating of my question; a few headed sections are fine, 1,000 to 1,400 words. No bibliography, no suggested further reading, no citation padding.
