I'm a novelist, not an economist, so bear with me. My brother-in-law teaches finance and he read the middle hundred pages of my book, which is about two antiquarian dealers, one in Providence and one in Lisbon, who spend most of the novel maneuvering toward a single swap: an autograph letter for a preparatory drawing. He says the swap at the end is impossible. Not improbable. Logically impossible — that I've built my climax on a contradiction. He says it's a real result, some no-trade theorem, with a proof behind it. Here's the arguement roughly as he wrote it out for me:

(1) My two dealers are both rational, both know the other is rational, both know that the other knows, and so on as far as you want to go.

(2) Before they met they had the same beliefs about the world — same priors — and everything each of them has learned since is private to him.

(3) In the scene, one offers the swap, the other accepts, and each privately thinks he's getting away with murder.

(4) A rational man only accepts a swap like that if, given what he privately knows, he expects to come out ahead. If he didn't expect that, he'd have walked away.

(5) So the acceptance is itself information. It tells the offerer that the other man has some private reason to think he's winning.

(6) And the other man knows that the offerer knows that, and so on up the chain — so at the moment of the handshake each of them has to price in everything the other's willingness reveals.

(7) Once you've priced all that in, neither of them can expect to gain. Two men who satisfy (1) and (2) cannot agree to a swap that both expect to profit from.

(8) But swaps like mine happen every day, in that trade and in every other one.

(9) Therefore one of (1), (2) or (4) is false — and he says its (1), that no actual human being is rational in the sense the argument needs, and that this refutes the efficient markets people out of their own mouths.

He says the chain is airtight and (9) is the only honest exit. I don't believe him, but I can't say why, and I need to be able to say why, because the whole book turns on whether one of these men is making a mistake or whether they're both being perfectly sensible and I've written nonsense.

So — which step do you actually reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I want to keep (8) obviously, and I'd much rather keep (1) than he would, since my dealers are sharks and not fools, so tell me what I'm paying if that's where the break has to go. Then tell me what he's *right* about: is there a version of his point that survives whatever fixing you do, because I'd rather build the scene on the true thing than on the dramatic thing. And finally, concretely: what would have to be true about these two men — about why each of them wants the swap, or about what they know of each other, or about how the offer actually reaches the other one — for both to be rational and both to expect to win?
