I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I've been invited to contribute an essay on interpretation — how we decide what a text is about, and who gets to say. Before I commit to a position in print, I'd like a candid audit of whether the views I actually hold can all be held at once. Here they are. First, what a text means is settled by what its author intended to convey; there is no further fact of the matter beyond that. Second, I take the central lesson of Wimsatt and Beardsley: the critic's evidence is the published work, and letters, diaries, and after-dinner remarks are not where meaning resides. Third, when an author repudiates a reading decades later, the repudiation changes nothing — the book has left them. Fourth, in the piece I filed this week I allowed a novelist's on-the-record answer to close a dispute among her readers about what a much-argued chapter was doing, and I wrote the passage so that she plainly had the last word. Fifth, I hold that a novel finished in 1911 can genuinely be about a phenomenon its author had never heard of, and I would defend the claim that a certain modernist text is about the Anthropocene. Sixth, I reject Hirsch's way out — the split between a fixed meaning and a shifting significance — as a device that rescues intentionalism only by making it say nothing. Tell me which of these are genuinely in conflict and which merely look it, ranked by severity rather than listed as equally troubling, and for each real conflict state the cheapest surrender and what I lose by making it. Don't spare any of them out of consideration for me; I have no favourite I'm trying to protect. Close by saying whether some single revision would dissolve more than one of the problems at once, or whether that hope is illusory.

Please answer in continuous prose, as you would write a letter or a long email — no headings, no bullet points, no bolded phrases, no tables.
