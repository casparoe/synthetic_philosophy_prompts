I'm writing up my position on the probabilities of indicative conditionals for my reading group (mostly formal epistemology people, plus one belief-revision person) and I want to know whether the package hangs together before they tear into it. Here's what I'm committed to:

(a) Indicative conditionals express genuine propositions — events in the algebra — and P(A→B) is just the probability that the proposition is true. I'm not a suppositionalist, I defintely don't want to give up truth conditions.

(b) P(A→B) = P(B|A) whenever P(A) > 0 — and not just for my current credences, but for every credence function that's rationally permissible for me, including every one I could reach by updating on new evidence.

(c) So I'm not that worried about Lewis's triviality argument. My read is that the trouble comes from embedding: I keep the language free of nested conditionals, so the equation only ever gets applied to pairs of ordinary non-conditional sentences, and I think that blocks the derivation. Also I basically never become certain of anything — my updates are all partial, Jeffrey-style shifts across a partition — so total conditioning on an antecedent never even arises for me.

(d) I'm an imprecise-credences person. My doxastic state is a set of functions, and I require that set to be convex: any weighted average of two permissible functions is itself permissible. And (b) holds for each member of the set.

(e) For the positive semantics I want to say that the truth of A→B at a world is fixed by a chance-driven draw among the A-worlds — one draw per world, settled independently of which antecedent we happen to be evaluating — so that P(A→B) comes out as the expected chance of B given the draw. My thought is that this richer proposition is what lets (b) survive.

(f) I accept Lewis's derivations as valid, no equivocation and no hidden fallacy. And I reject the material conditional analysis: P(A→B) is generally not P(¬A ∨ B).
