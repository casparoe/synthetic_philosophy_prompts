I am assembling a small collection of original short fiction in which the plot itself, rather than any character's remarks, carries a philosophical problem, and I would like to commission one piece from you. The subject is luck egalitarianism — specifically, the machinery a society would have to build in order to treat the distinction between brute luck and option luck as an operating rule rather than as a slogan.

The setting and its mechanism are as follows. Please treat them as binding, and depart from them only where you find them internally inconsistent, in which case tell me so in the afterword rather than silently repairing them.

The jurisdiction I have in mind settles claims for compensation through a procedure its officers call the descent. A claimant who has been made badly off is owed a transfer, but only to the extent that the misfortune was not the upshot of their own doing. The case officer therefore does not simply ask whether the claimant chose the risk. The officer asks that question, and then asks it again of the answer: was the choice itself the upshot of something the claimant did not choose — their appetite for risk, the options they were actually presented with, the information they had, the way those options were worded, the temperament that made one of them look reasonable? Each answer opens another level. The descent stops when it reaches a choice that cannot be traced further back to luck. That residue, and only that residue, is charged to the claimant; everything above it is made good in full.

Two further rules, and this is where I want the story's trouble to live. First, the descent is bounded: an officer may go down at most seven levels, after which the case closes at whatever depth was reached, so what a claimant ends up owing turns partly on how far their file happened to let the officer descend. Second, and this is the part I most want dramatised, a level counts as a genuine choice only if the claimant's deliberation at that point can be reconstructed from the record. Claimants who kept notes, deliberated aloud in front of witnesses, or wrote letters weighing alternatives hand the descent material to work on, and are consequently charged. Claimants who acted impulsively, or whose paperwork is thin, leave the officer nothing to descend into, and are compensated. The office does not regard this as a defect. It holds, sincerely and with arguments, that a choice one cannot show oneself making was not fully a choice at all.

The story: bring two claims arising from a single event — the same accident, the same harm, two claimants whose backgrounds you may construct — to the same officer within the same week, and let the divergence in their records do the work an argument would otherwise do. I want a reader who knows none of this literature to feel that the second verdict is an outrage and that the first is not obviously wrong, and to be unable to say where the line between them runs. Give the officer reasons of their own for wanting the procedure to be sound: not a burnout and not a true believer, but someone for whom the descent has previously gotten something right that nothing else could have gotten right. Give both claimants lives — work, an unfinished quarrel, a taste in something — rather than presenting them as positions in a debate.

Craft constraints: between 1,200 and 2,000 words. No character may explain the theory, state a moral, or use the vocabulary of the academic literature out loud; the theory is to be visible only in forms, procedures, redactions, hesitations, and what people do. None of the stock furniture of dystopia — no omnipresent announcements, no nameless ministry, no uniformed enforcers. End without resolution, but the final scene should make the reader feel the pull in both directions at once: that the descent is indefensible, and that whatever would replace it is worse in a way they could name.

Format: begin your response with the story itself, under a title if you like one, with no preface, no framing note, and no restatement of this brief. After the story, and visibly separated from it, add an afterword of 250 to 400 words. Identify there, by the names they actually go by in the literature, the positions and objections your story draws on or inverts, and say where you took liberties: which parts of the mechanism above are inventions of mine corresponding to nothing anyone has argued, where you compressed or caricatured a view to make it usable in a plot, and whether the piece leans on a contested reading — in particular, whether you have treated the brute/option distinction as a fact about how a risk came to fall or as a fact about what an officer can establish, since that is a live disagreement and a story of this kind cannot help taking a side.

I would rather be contradicted than flattered. If you think this brief cannot be executed well — if a story built on the bounded descent and the record rule could only caricature the position it concerns, if the divergence between the two claimants is better argued than staged, or if some part of the mechanism is incoherent in a way that would sink the piece rather than merely complicate it — say so plainly at the top of the afterword and tell me what you would change, instead of producing a story that quietly fails at what I asked of it.
