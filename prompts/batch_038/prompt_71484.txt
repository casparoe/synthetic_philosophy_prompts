Two members of my history reading group — neither of them an academic, though both well read — got into a sustained argument last week about whether long-run political cycles can really be forecast, and asked me to settle it. I have tried to set their exchange down faithfully.

(1) The first began by noting that a researcher had built mathematical models of recurring instability in agrarian states, published in advance a warning that the coming decade would bring mounting turmoil in the United States and western Europe, and been broadly borne out. That, he said, is what a genuine forecast looks like: the numbers were fixed before the events, so the confirmation cannot have been arranged.

(2) The other replied that the confirmation is worthless, because the quantities the models run on — an oversupply of aspirants to elite positions relative to the posts available, stagnating wages for ordinary workers, pressure on public finances, and the decay of what the theory calls collective solidarity — are each measured by indicators chosen by the modeller. Give yourself that many dials and you can produce a society under strain in whichever decade you fancy.

(3) He answered that this is merely the ordinary problem of turning a concept into a measurement, shared by every social science, and that it does not touch his real point: the timing and length of the cycles fall out of the internal structure of the model — how fast a population grows, how fast a ruling group multiplies — rather than being fitted to the particular episode that later happened.

(4) She said the cycle lengths are fitted all the same, since they were derived by counting crises in the very handful of well-documented societies that also supplied the data for calibration; and that once a hit is allowed to land anywhere within a decade or two on either side of the target year, there is no stretch of the calendar that could count as a miss.

(5) He then proposed what he took to be a decisive test: choose a society the modellers never examined, feed in the indicators up to some early date only, and see whether the model reproduces the crisis known from the record to have followed. If it does, the charge of hindsight is simply false.

(6) She said this changes nothing, because whoever selects which societies enter the sample, and decides which event in each one counts as the crisis, already knows the answers; and because the theory permits a society to escape its cycle if its elites strike a bargain and restrain their own numbers, it can accommodate any sequence of events whatsoever — which is precisely what makes it untestable.

Please work through the six moves in order, saying for each whether it succeeds, partly succeeds, or fails, and what exactly it does or fails to establish. Then tell me which of the two has the better of the exchange overall; whether her move (6) advances a real objection or merely restates the familiar complaint about unfalsifiability; and whether there is something important about forecasting from historical data that both of them overlooked. Please write your answer in British English.
