My prospectus chapter has to specify what I will require of an account of causation before it compares any accounts, and I would rather get the specification right than get it written quickly. I am in a seminar on causation cross-listed between my philosophy department and the statistics faculty, so the chapter will be read by people who think an account that cannot be operationalised is idle, and by people who think an account that only licenses a modelling formalism has changed the subject.

The purpose fixes the target. The account must serve two uses at once. It must support token-level reasoning about particular cases of the kind that comes up in ascriptions of responsibility, where what matters is whether this event brought about that one. And it must underwrite applied modelling in public health and social policy, where the variables are coarse aggregates (a recession, a school system, a racial category), where the interventions of interest are frequently impossible, unlawful, or unethical, and where the working machinery is graphical and probabilistic. An account that serves only one of those uses is not what I need.

Against that purpose, write the desiderata. For each requirement give the requirement itself, the case or consideration that motivates it, and a mark of whether it is non-negotiable or a standing preference of one camp. I want coverage of, at minimum: the phenomena an account has to get right (early and late preemption, symmetric overdetermination, prevention and double prevention, causation by omission, probabilistic and threshold cases); the distinctions it may not collapse (type versus token causation, cause versus mere background condition, causation versus explanation, causation versus nomic or lawlike connection, causation versus constitution, difference-making versus productive connection, the having of an effect versus the magnitude of that effect); and the formal and empirical results it cannot contradict (the reversal of an association under conditioning, the fact that observational data alone fixes causal structure only up to an equivalence class, the constraints relativistic spacetime places on influence between spacelike-separated events, the fact that exceptionless generality is neither necessary nor sufficient for a claim to support intervention).

Three cases of my own construction should be handled explicitly, since I suspect they do most of the excluding.

First, a dose of some drug lowers stroke risk by lowering blood pressure, while also raising a hormone that independently raises stroke risk. In a model whose only variables are dose and outcome, the generalization is invariant under interventions on dose, and the drug comes out protective. In the finer model that adds blood pressure and the hormone, the same drug has two opposing pathways to the outcome, and the coarse verdict can be reversed by an intervention the finer model licenses but the coarser one cannot represent. Both variable sets look legitimate; policy needs the coarse claim and mechanism needs the fine one. I want a requirement governing how causal claims at one level of description stand to claims at another, and a requirement that the theory tell me which descriptions are admissible without consulting my intuitions about this very case.

Second, a claim about the early universe: that a particular pattern of density variation led to the galaxies forming as they did. No intervention on the putative cause is possible even in principle, and if the cause is described finely enough, altering it would leave it a different cause. Note that the preemption cases seem to push toward individuating events finely enough that the backup fails to fire, while fine individuation is exactly what makes an intervention on the cause impossible; I want the list to acknowledge that pull.

Third, an omission case whose verdict turns on which possibility is nearest: whether the closest world in which the gardener does not water the plant is a world in which it rains instead decides whether the omission caused the death, or whether the death would have happened anyway. I want a requirement of independence, such that whatever the theory uses to rank the relevant possibilities is not itself calibrated on the causal verdicts it is supposed to generate. And I want a further requirement that the theory be licensed to convict some of my firmest intuitions of error, on the grounds that an account which saves every intuition about every case has not said anything.

I have also drafted three candidate requirements of my own, and I want each one accepted, rejected as the prejudice of a particular camp, or restated so that it survives: that the account be extensional, so that two variables picking out the same state of affairs yield the same causal verdicts; that every true causal claim be reducible to a claim about what would have happened had the cause been absent, holding the laws fixed; and that the account owe a verdict of presence or absence in every case, with no residue where it declines to answer.

Say plainly which subsets of the requirements cannot be jointly satisfied, with the reasoning, since if the list turns out to be an impossibility result rather than a checklist I would rather know that now. Say which single requirement eliminates the most candidates. Then, and only then, score the counterfactual-dependence account, the interventionist account, the process account, and a primitivist or powers account against the list, briefly. No citations and no reading list; I have those.
