So — I've somehow been tapped to give the graduation address at my own college this summer. It isn't a fancy place: mostly nursing, primary teaching, civil engineering, and about fifteen maths students a year who've been reassured it counts as a maths degree. Room will be maybe 400, nearly all of them families, plus the engineering and computing faculty and three colleagues from my department who will absolutely catch everything I do.

I'm a constructivist. Not the sort who thinks it's a bit quirky, the sort who's spent twenty years refusing to accept a proof that only shows something can't fail to exist. And I've decided I want to give the whole address in that spirit rather than do the usual thing where you mention your odd specialism for ninety seconds and then read out a generic speech about the future.

The trouble is that basically every stock line in a graduation speech is, if you actually look at it, an unearned existence claim. There's a place out there for every one of you. You'll find your way. The world needs what you've got. Nobody has a proof of any of these — they're asserted because it'd be cruel not to. Same with the 'either you'll change the world or the world will change you, and honestly both are fine' business: that's an excluded middle doing the emotional work, and if I can't say which of the two I've got no business saying it at all. And this is a town where the biggest employer shut four years ago and a third of the students are the first in their family to get here, so those lines aren't just unsupported, they're close to an insult.

Can you write the speech? Six to eight minutes said out loud. The rules I'm holding myself to:

- I only promise what I can build or point at. Any claim I make about their futures has to be one I could hand them a method for.
- I do want to say the honest weak version instead of the comforting strong one — that it isn't true that they'll come to nothing is something I can stand behind, whereas 'you'll all flourish' isn't — but the speech should own the fact that this is a smaller promise, not slip it in disguised as the bigger one. Someone's got to notice that I gave them less than they expected and that I did it on purpose.
- Never name the doctrine. No Brouwer, no intuitionism, no 'constructive mathematics', no footnote-y asides. My three colleagues should recognise what I'm up to; a nurse's gran should just hear a good speech.
- Near the end I want to actually hand them something constructed rather than promised — a specific, doable thing, not a metaphor for one.
- One joke that works on both levels at once.
- No quotations from famous people, living or dead. I can't fact-check from a podium and I'd rather say nothing I have to attribute.

It can't come out as a lecture or a sermon or a lecture wearing a sermon's coat. Funny and warm in places. British English please, and write it the way I'd say it — with the pauses marked and the bits I'd ad-lib flagged — rather than as an essay.
