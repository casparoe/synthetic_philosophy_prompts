I want a case analysis of the Bitburg cemetery affair of May 1985 read through Karl Jaspers's four categories of guilt in *The Question of German Guilt* (1946) — criminal, political, moral, and metaphysical. The salient facts, so you need not reconstruct them: Reagan and Kohl had arranged a joint visit to a West German military cemetery on the fortieth anniversary of the end of the war in Europe, as a symbol of reconciliation between the two countries; it then emerged that 49 of the roughly two thousand buried there were members of the Waffen-SS, an organization declared criminal at Nuremberg; under mounting pressure an unplanned stop at Bergen-Belsen was inserted before the cemetery, leaving Reagan perhaps eight minutes on site; and Reagan, defending the visit, said of the SS dead that "They were victims, just as surely as the victims in the concentration camps," pointing out that most of the graves belonged to boys of about eighteen. Kohl himself was fifteen when the war ended, and refused to cancel.

Do the mapping before the verdicts. For each of Jaspers's four kinds of guilt, say who in this episode could bear it, in what form, and whether it transmits to the living Germans and Americans of 1985 or stops with the participants — and then say which category each head of state was in fact operating in, and whether the ceremony is intelligible on Jaspers's terms at all. Two things I especially want pressed. First, Reagan's victims remark: treat it as an appeal to metaphysical guilt, the solidarity of all human beings in every wrong, and tell me whether Jaspers's scheme allows that category to carry the exculpatory weight Reagan put on it, or whether softening criminal guilt by dissolving it into universal human solidarity is exactly the confusion he was trying to prevent. Second, Jaspers holds that political liability is borne as a consequence imposed by the victor rather than confessed by the guilty party — what does that imply about a reconciliation ritual that the defeated state demands as its due, and that a majority of its public backs?

Finally, run the analysis back against the theory. Jaspers was writing about a task Germans had to undertake on themselves, from inside their own community. Does an atonement performed jointly with the former enemy, four decades later and largely by people who were children or unborn at the time, confirm his account, expose a gap in it, or describe a situation he had no category for? Be explicit about where the fourfold scheme goes silent rather than stretching it to speak.
