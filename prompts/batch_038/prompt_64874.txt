I'm working on something about Fear and Trembling and I keep hitting a wall when I try to apply it to cases Kierkegaard couldn't have imagined. Here's the case I actually care about:

A scientist leads a team doing work he privately thinks has a small but real chance of wrecking everything — not just his field, civilization. He keeps going anyway, partly because he figures if he stops someone less careful picks it up, and partly because he thinks the astronomical value of a long human future outweighs basically any present cost you care to name. When a journalist asks him to defend the decision he sort of can't. He says the calculation is the best he's got but admits it doesn't convince anybody, and on bad days doesn't convince him either. To two friends he's said that he feels called to it, that he's prayed about it, and that he can't explain that part without sounding insane.

Variant, same person: instead of continuing his own work he commits a serious crime to shut down someone else's project, then refuses the defense his own lawyers are offering him — the one that would frame the act as necessary to prevent greater harm — on the grounds that arguing it that way would be a lie about why he did it.

So what would Johannes de Silentio make of this? Not a summary of the book and not a lecture on the stages of existence — I want the verdict and the reasoning behind it. Which of his categories does each version land in: knight of faith, knight of infinite resignation, tragic hero of the Agamemnon sort, or the hiddenness/disclosure material from the third problem? The crux I most want you to get right is whether an appeal to the far future can even be a teleological suspension of the ethical on his terms, or whether it collapses back into something else entirely — and if it collapses, into what exactly, and what in the case is doing the collapsing. Then tell me what would have to change about the scenario for him to call it Abraham-shaped rather than a counterfeit.

And then, briefly and seperately: how would the much later Kierkegaard, the one going after the state church and the crowd, react to this guy? My guess is he'd be considerably harsher than the pseudonym, but I want to know why, not just that he would be.
