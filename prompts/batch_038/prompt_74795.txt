I've got a standing gig hosting a salon in my living room — mostly other novelists, plus two physicists who turn up for the wine. Next month the theme is Newton's bucket and the whole substantivalism vs. relationism fight, fourty minutes, whiteboard only, and I'm the one who has to run it. Don't give me the talk, give me the plan for it. What has to be in place before the bucket can do any work at all — does the climbing water come before or after I ever say the word "substance," and what has to be true about the audience's picture of motion by then? Which single running case should carry the whole thing, and is the bucket actually the right one or would something else (Newton's own two spheres joined by a cord, say) do the job better and why? Then the misconceptions: I'm braced for "relationism is obviously the humbler, more sensible view," "Mach fixed it," and "relativity settled it" — tell me where in the sequence to head each off, and what novelists in particular will get wrong that a physicist wouldn't, since my crowd's reflex is to treat space as a setting or a character and to hear the dispute as a metaphor rather than a claim about the world. Also: where does Leibniz's slide-the-whole-universe-a-few-feet-over objection belong, before or after the bucket itslef, and what should I flat out refuse to get into at this level — and say so out loud to the room? Close with one question I can put to the group that tells me whether the thing actually landed. American English, please.
