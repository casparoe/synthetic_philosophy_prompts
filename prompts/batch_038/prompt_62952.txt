You're a philosopher who does a lot of applied animal-ethics consulting, and you're good at writing dialectic that doesn't strawman anybody. Think carefully before you write — first jot down (bullets are fine, keep it brief) the two strongest points on each side plus the one place you think the argument is going to get stuck, then write the dialogue.

Setup: a working group is drafting the definition of "protected animal" for a new welfare statute, and the definition has to be administrable — a vet or an inspector should be able to apply it without having read any philosophy. Two members end up fighting about whether neural measures (brain mass, brain-to-body ratio, total neuron count) belong in the definition at all. One thinks they're the least terrible available proxy for the capacity to suffer, so pretending otherwise is just squeamishness; the other thinks the whole family of proxies is self-undermining, because the only reason we trust them is that they correlate with behavior we recognise in ourselves, which means behaviour is doing the real evidential work and we should say so. Give them something concrete to collide over: the statute has to come down somewhere on cephalopods and somewhere on insects, and each of them thinks the other's criterion gets one of those two cases badly, embarrassingly wrong.

Write it as genuine back-and-forth, maybe 700–900 words, where each one responds to what the other actually just said rather than delivering parallel speeches. Neither should win. But I want them to end up somewhere slightly different from where they started — some distinction should get forced out of the argument that neither of them had at the begining, and they should notice it.
