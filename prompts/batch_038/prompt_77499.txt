I'm a firmware engineer at a company that makes progressive cortical implants — the kind used for degenerative conditions, where synthetic modules take over the work of failing tissue in stages rather than all at once. We have an internal Continuity Review Board that issues a certification for each procedure, completed or proposed: whether the patient who comes out is the same person, legally and morally, as the patient who went in. It reads like a formality and isn't. The certification governs advance directives, consent, insurance continuity, and who gets to make decisions if something goes wrong. Our general counsel sits on the board, so does our chief medical officer, and so do I, as the only member who actually reads the device logs. I've been asked to draft the recommendation for next month's filing. I have no formal philosophy training, so I'm going to give you our record in operational terms and ask you to supply the structure.

The board's rulings to date:

1. Fourteen-month staged replacement of cortical tissue; patient awake and conversing for most sessions; no interruption of any kind; at the end no biological neurons remained in the replaced regions; memory, temperament, and self-report continuous throughout. Certified.
2. High-fidelity scan of a healthy volunteer; emulation instantiated and run for eleven days; volunteer alive, unharmed, and in the same room as the emulation on day three. Not certified — the board's finding was that the emulation was a different individual from the volunteer.
3. Terminal patient; brain destroyed during the scan; emulation started immediately afterward and functionally continuous with the pre-scan record. Not certified.
4. Staged replacement identical to (1) except that one session required forty-one minutes of complete neural silence — no measurable activity at all — midway through a twenty-six-month program. Certified.
5. A region was duplicated rather than replaced: two functionally equivalent instances ran in parallel for twenty-two days while the surgical team decided which to keep; the patient reported no doubling of experience; one instance and the tissue it duplicated were then decommissioned. Certified, with an advisory.
6. Staged replacement in which the resulting person's processing speed, memory organization, and temperament changed substantially over the following year; her husband's phrase at the hearing was "the same person, differently arranged." Certified, but the board required fresh consent for her advance directive.
7. Staged replacement with an archival snapshot of the pre-operative state retained for ninety days and then destroyed. Certified. The board also adopted a standing rule that archived states may not be instantiated.
8. Routine replacement of an implant's controller board — eleven seconds, no state carried on it that the patient's tissue did not also carry. Certified.

Now the filing I have to write about. An eighteen-month staged replacement, patient awake throughout, no silent intervals, otherwise like (1). The difference is where the work happens. From month three onward, the modules replacing her tissue are not entirely inside her skull: a wearable unit takes an increasing share of the computation, bidirectionally linked at roughly two milliseconds of round-trip latency, going from ten percent of the workload in month three to ninety-three percent by month eighteen. Her skull retains the interfaces, the brainstem, and a residual biological population. By every measure we can log, she has been continuous throughout, and she describes the unit the way I describe my laptop: part of the setup, not part of her. In month nineteen the vendor must replace that wearable with a new model. The proposed method is a hot swap: the new unit runs in parallel with the old for thirty seconds, taking state directly from it over the link rather than from a scan, after which the old unit is powered down. Separately — and I wrote this part, so discount my judgment accordingly — the unit records a restorable snapshot every night and keeps the last four hundred, any of which can be instantiated on a spare unit in about four minutes. Her family has asked that the snapshots be kept indefinitely, on the reasoning that if the wearable fails catastrophically she can be brought back from last night. She has asked for the same.

What I need from you:

- Which principles are consistent with all eight rulings? I can gesture at three myself — something about the same physical process continuing, something about there never being two candidate persons, something about whether an earlier state can be restored — and I suspect (3) and (7) between them rule out any principle built purely on memory and character. But I want this done properly, including principles I haven't thought of.
- What each such principle implies about the month-nineteen hot swap, and about indefinite retention of the snapshots. I expect them to disagree.
- Which precedent is the closest analog to the hot swap, and why it beats the others. (2), (3), (5), and (8) all look relevant to me and pull in different directions.
- A recommendation I can put in front of the board: certify, decline, or certify with conditions. If conditions, state them concretely and say what they cost this patient.
- An honest account of what principle the board would be committing itself to by ruling either way, since we will be cited on this filing for years.
- One further filing, described concretely enough that I could recognize it if it landed on my desk, that would settle which principle the board has actually been applying all along.

Please don't give me references or a literature review; nobody on the board would read them and I couldn't evaluate them. And don't come back with questions. If something turns on a fact I've left out, name the fact and tell me how you'd rule under each answer to it.
