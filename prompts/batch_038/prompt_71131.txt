Our regional water utility's board has to settle a spillway design standard for a new reservoir, and I'm one of the seven of us who has to sign off. The hydrologists won't give us a single flood-frequency figure — the gauge record is short and the climate projections disagree — so what lands on the table is a band: 100-year peak flow somewhere between 900 and 1,700 m³/s, and the cost curve is convex enough that building to the top of the band roughly doubles the price tag. Three of us have three different fixes. One wants to take the midpoint of the band and run an ordinary cost-benefit caluclation on it. One wants to build to the worst case in the band no matter what it costs. One is a former actuary who thinks we should settle on our own probability distribution across the band and integrate over that.

Draft the memo I'll circulate ahead of next month's vote. Pick one of the three, or propose a fourth, and give the board a rule we could actually reapply to the next such decision rather than just a verdict on this one — with the reasoning spelled out enough that the other two campds can push back on it. Take seriously the objection that refusing to commit to a single number is just squeamishness in the face of uncomfortable evidence, but be clear about whether what we're dealing with here is ambiguity or plain risk aversion, since I think the board is running the two together. And remember this goes into the public record, so it has to hold up when a ratepayer asks why their bill went up.
