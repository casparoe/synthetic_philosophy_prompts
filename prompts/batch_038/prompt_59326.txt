I edit a small English-language magazine of European political commentary. We publish in London; perhaps half our readership is North American. Around half of what we print arrives in translation — from French, German, Spanish, Dutch, Italian and Polish — and the rest is commissioned in English from writers on both sides of the Atlantic. I have no academic training of any kind. I came to this job from a trade background and have taught myself what I know by doing it, but for three years I have been settling the same dispute several times a week, and I have begun to think it through on my own account.

The dispute is always about one word: liberal.

Here is what I have arrived at, and I should like you to test it rather than accept it. It seems to me the word does two quite different kinds of work. Sometimes it names a programme — a set of commitments about individual liberty, limited government, markets, secularism, toleration — and sometimes it names a position, a place somebody occupies relative to the other forces in their own country's politics. My suspicion is that nearly all our mistakes come from rendering a position as though it were a programme, or a programme as though it were a position. The second thing I have noticed is that the word's polarity flips: in some countries it is a compliment, in others an insult, and in America it has drifted until it means much what left-of-centre means elsewhere, which is not at all what it means to a British or a German reader. If either observation is wrong, or crude, or misses something more basic underneath it, tell me so plainly, because the procedure has to be built on whatever is actually true rather than on my hunch.

What I want is a standing procedure my copy desk can run on any item containing the word: the questions to ask in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and at every step one of three verdicts — leave it, change it, or annotate it — together with the wording of the annotation where one is called for. My two sub-editors have ten minutes per item, no political theory, and no time to read around a case, so the procedure must be runnable by them and must supply, for each change, a sentence they can send to the author explaining it without sounding as though they are correcting the author's politics. It must also say where it stops and hands the item to me, because I would rather have a bright line I dislike than a judgement call my sub-editors are too nervous to make. And I want you to be honest about which of the steps are real tests and which are only judgement calls dressed up as procedure.

Please run it on five items currently in my tray: a translated German piece calling a proposed tax cut "a liberal reform", where the author sits in a free-market party; a French essayist who describes himself throughout as libéral while defending high public spending, a generous welfare state and heavy regulation of large firms; an American contributor who writes that she is "a classical liberal, not a conservative"; a Dutch correspondent referring to the main centre-right party simply as "the liberals"; and a Polish writer who uses the equivalent word as a term of abuse for the technocrats of the 1990s, where the abuse is the whole point of the passage. Work two of these through in full and the rest more briefly, and tell me which of the five the procedure cannot settle.

One prejudice of mine needs challenging. I have insisted that we always preserve an author's own self-description untouched, on the grounds that it is not our business to tell people what they are. Show me where that policy does real harm, even if it means telling me I have been wrong for three years.

Finally, give me enough of an account of how the two usages came apart to justify the steps of the procedure — but no bibliography, no reading list and no references. I cannot check any of them, and my desk would not read them.
