So I'm in a philosophy-of-mind reading group and we've now spent three weeks going round in circles on multiple realisability, and I've been volunteered to give a short talk that's supposed to actually take a side instead of re-describing the landscape. I could really use a hand drafting the argument, because my own version keeps collapsing into a list of labels. Here's roughly where I've got to. Putnam's point was supposed to be that mental kinds can't be type-identical to neural kinds, because the same mental state gets realised in octopuses, in hypothetical Martians, in whatever else you like; and basically everyone treats the upshot as a vindication of functionalism — even Putnam eventually turned on functionalism himself, which everyone files away as an eccentric late change of heart rather than as evidence about what the original argument actually showed. I want to argue the upshot doesn't licence that at all. The argument only gets you as far as: mental predicates are not type-identical to neurophysiological predicates. And once you notice *why* that's true — that sameness of functional organisation is only determinate relative to a decomposition of the system into parts, states, inputs and outputs, and the decomposition is fixed by what we care about predicting rather than by the system itself — the very same reasoning cuts against functionalism's claim to be a substantive thesis that could turn out false. The slogan I'm trying to defend is that functionalism isn't a rival *theory* of mind sitting alongside the identity theory; it's a different kind of claim wearing a theory's clothes, and multiple realisability is the giveaway rather than the foundation.

To stop it floating free I want to run the whole thing off a case one of us invented last term, and I'd like you to use it as the backbone. Imagine a tidal barrage on a small estuary. The hardware is a scatter of tide gauges, a central office where sixty-odd clerks work three shifts passing paper dockets around, a wall of levers for the sluice gates, and a phone tree to the harbour master. Nobody designed the thing to model anything: it accreted over a hundred and forty years of accident reports and fudges. But it has this property — there are stable internal states of the whole set-up (states of the docket piles, the shift rosters, the chalkboard in the office) that get set by gauge readings, that interact with one another in lawlike ways, and that terminate in gate movements; and a marine engineer can predict the barrage's behaviour across a whole season far better by attributing to it something like a standing expectation that the north channel silts up after a spring tide plus a preference for keeping the marina above a certain depth, than by writing down the mechanics of the levers. The engineer's attributions are about as successful, and about as inexact, as belief–desire attribution ever is with actual animals. Note the clerks individually aren't like that; they're bored and mostly illiterate about tides. My question for the talk is what the multiple realisability argument *commits* you to saying here, and I want the case used as the pivot rather than as a colourful appendix.

So: please argue for the thesis above. I don't fully buy it, which is why I want the strongest version rather than a balanced survey — assume the thesis is right and make the case for it, taking the barrage as your running example. Two objections have to be met head-on rather than waved at. First, the standard line that functionalism obviously can be false because the China-block style case is meant as a *counterexample*: if a system can satisfy the functional specification and still lack mentality, then functionalism is empirically vulnerable, and my "not a substantive thesis" claim is dead on arrival — say why that doesn't follow, or say what has to be conceded to survive it. Second, the richness response: that functionalism only counts systems with sufficiently rich internal organisation, so the barrage is simply excluded. I want you to press on whether any such threshold can be stated non-arbitrarily, or whether it either smuggles substrate chauvinism back in through the back door or dissolves into "we count the systems we like". Don't give me citations or a bibliography, I'll dig the references out myself — I just want the reasoning, and I don't want a potted history of the debate as a warm-up. Aim for somewhere between a thousand and twelve hundred words, write the whole thing in British English (spellings included), and then finish with two or three sentences, clearly seperated off, saying which step of the argument you think is weakest and what kind of consideration would make you abandon it.
