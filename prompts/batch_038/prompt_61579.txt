Consider the following fixed setting for an allocation problem. A society of ten million people produces a single transferable resource, provisions, in a constant annual supply. Everyone starts comfortably above subsistence. The population does not change, no one's identity or existence depends on the allocation, and welfare is measurable and interpersonally comparable with the reliability of a well-calibrated instrument: for any two people and any increment of provisions, we can know how much welfare each would gain from it. By classical utilitarianism here I mean the principle that provisions ought to be distributed so as to maximize the sum of welfare across all persons.

Within this setting, consider five variants of Nozick's utility monster. In each, M denotes the monster.

A. Unbounded. M's marginal welfare from every increment of provisions is one hundred times an ordinary person's, and M's capacity for welfare has no ceiling: the more M receives, the more M is able to gain from the next increment.

B. Capped. As in A, with the same hundredfold multiplier, except that M's welfare saturates. Once M holds a bundle equivalent to one percent of the annual supply, further provisions add nothing whatsoever to M's welfare.

C. Positional. M's welfare depends solely on M's share of provisions relative to everyone else's. M gains from a transfer only insofar as that transfer lowers others' relative standing, and the quantity of positional welfare available in the society is fixed, so that whatever M gains on this dimension, others lose.

D. Deferred. M's marginal welfare from provisions today is negligible, but M's welfare fifty years from now will be one hundred times that of anyone else then living, and the only route to that future is to divert provisions to M now, including provisions that presently keep others comfortably above subsistence.

E. Diffuse. M is not a single person but a very large collection of entities, each individually far less sentient than a person and each gaining only a negligible amount of welfare from any increment, such that the collection's aggregate welfare from any increment is one hundred times the aggregate welfare that the same increment would produce among the ten million people.

Please do the following.

1. Sort the five variants into three tiers according to how serious a problem each poses for classical utilitarianism as stated above — that is, by how difficult it is for the utilitarian to answer the variant without abandoning, restricting, or badly straining the aggregation of welfare across persons. Order the variants strictly within each tier; ties are not permitted.

2. For each variant, state in one sentence the single best reply available to the utilitarian, and in one further sentence whether that reply generalizes to the other variants or is confined to the one.

3. Allocate 100 points across the five variants in proportion to how much of the intuitive force of Nozick's original objection you think survives in each — how much of the sense that utilitarianism demands something intolerable remains once the variant is described. The allocation must sum to exactly 100, and I want the numbers, not a discussion of them.

4. Identify the one variant that in your judgment does the most damage beyond the damage already done by A, and explain why in a short paragraph, saying explicitly what feature of that variant A lacks.

5. Take the variant you ranked lowest and name the single stipulation you would alter to move it to the top, describing the change precisely enough that its effect on the utilitarian calculus is determinate.

Two constraints. Assume each variant is metaphysically possible and internally coherent exactly as described: do not resist a variant on the grounds that such a being could not exist, or that its welfare could not be measured or compared with anyone else's. And argue from the stipulations alone — no references, no reading list, no citations — though you may name familiar positions and thinkers where that is the quickest way to make a point.
