I am trying to determine what principle, if any, governs my judgments about when it is legitimate to let the choices and conclusions of others override my own assessment of a question. The cases below are meant to be comparable: in each, I have some private bearing on the answer, and I observe what others have done or concluded. My verdicts follow each case.

1. A food stall has a queue of twelve strangers. Reading the sign, my impression is that it is probably mediocre. I join the queue. Permissible — and I think my belief about the food should also shift toward "good," though only slightly.

2. The same queue, except I know each of the twelve was handed a free sample by the owner before lining up again. I join anyway, because there is nowhere else to eat. Permissible as an action; but my belief about the food should not move at all.

3. One stranger tells me the stall is excellent; my own reading of the sign says mediocre. I should go with my own impression. Deferring here would not be permissible — yet in case 1, twelve people who very plausibly influenced one another tip me over. Twelve possibly dependent people outweighing one certainly independent person does not obviously cohere with this verdict.

4. Three colleagues examined the same tissue slide separately, without speaking to one another, and all report malignancy. My own read is benign. I go with them. Not merely permissible but required; holding out would be a fault.

5. The same three colleagues, except each wrote a report only after reading the previous one's. All three say malignant; my own read is benign. I go with them, and I believe I am still within my rights — though I cannot say why, since on the standard account this arrangement carries barely more information than the first report alone.

6. Three colleagues repeat the conclusion of a single review article, none having looked at the slide. I hold to my own read. Deferring would be a failure.

Cases 5 and 6 strike me as near-identical in structure — one piece of independent evidence, relayed three times — and yet my verdicts come out opposite. I do not know which feature is doing the work.

7. Eleven fellow jurors have voted to convict. I have an inarticulate unease that I could not state as a reason. I vote to convict. Not permissible: I should have asked to speak before falling into line.

8. The twelve people in the queue are silent, but I could easily ask the one in front of me why she joined. I join without asking. Permissible. This is the verdict that most embarrasses me given 7.

9. Five members of a search committee interviewed the same candidate separately; all say no. My own impression was mildly favorable. I vote no. Permissible.

10. The same five, but I learn they conferred in a corridor beforehand and the first speaker set the tone. I vote no anyway. Also permissible — and yet I think that had I been the first to speak, I would have been obliged to say something favorable. I cannot see how both of those can be true at once.

11. A stock has risen for six days on heavy volume and everyone I know is buying it. I have no private information about the firm. Buying is not permissible; but neither, I think, is shorting it on the reasoning that the crowd must be overextended. Absent information, the right answer is to stay out in both directions.

12. A prediction market I take to be well run prices an event at roughly eighty percent. I disagree. I defer. Permissible — and this deferral feels stronger than in case 9, even though the contributors are anonymous to me and largely unknown to one another.

My provisional principle is that I should count independent signals and discount the rest: a chain of n followers is worth less than a single follower's own evidence. That handles 2, 6, and 11, but it renders 5 wrong, it cannot separate 9 from 12, it makes no sense of 3 against 1, and it says nothing whatever about 7 and 8, where the issue appears to be not the weight of the evidence but whether I could cheaply have asked for reasons first.

What I want from you is a principle, or a small number of competing candidates, stated precisely enough to be applied rather than merely admired. Tell me which variables enter it and how they combine: the number of agents observed; my estimate of how much independence there was among them; the strength of my own evidence and, separately, whether I can articulate it; whether the others acted on the evidence themselves or transmitted a conclusion; whether the question is factual, evaluative, or conventional; whether I can solicit reasons at low cost; and what is at stake for me and for them. For each clause of the principle, identify the numbered case that motivates it. Then state which of my verdicts the principle must overturn — directly, not diplomatically; I would rather surrender a verdict than acquire an epicycle for every case. If two candidate principles save different subsets of the verdicts, lay out the trade explicitly and say which you would choose and why.

Finally, construct three cases I have not listed that discriminate between your candidates — cases in which the candidates come apart — and give the verdict each delivers. And tell me what the principle makes of the judgment I keep reversing myself on: whether deferring to a single person whose judgment I have found reliable over many years, in a field where nobody else has expressed a view, is the same phenomenon as deferring to a crowd, or a different phenomenon that the principle does not cover at all.
