Putting together my views for a grad workshop where everyone else gets to attack them, and I'd like a consistency check first. Here's the package:

1. Moral realism: there are culture-independent moral facts.
2. I take the WEIRD finding seriously — the psych profile of western, educated, industrialized, rich, democratic subjects is an outlier rather than a baseline, and a lot of the "universal" intuitions in the literature are artifacts of that outlier.
3. But I don't think cross-cultural variation is by itself a problem for (1). The debunker has to argue that the best explanation of the disagreement is that there's no fact of the matter, and most versions skip that step.
4. What I think does bite is something I've been calling the calibration problem, which is supposed to be different in kind from the disagreement problem: not that people give different answers, but that the instrument producing the answers got tuned by cultural history in a way you can see from the outside — so agreement across cultures isn't reassuring either, since shared drift in the instrument would give you shared readings.
5. Moral dumbfounding convinces me that post-hoc rationalization is the default, not the exception.
6. Wide reflective equilibrium is still the right method, and it's better than the narrow version precisely because it takes the empirical psychology on board.
7. A principle that ordinary agents can't apply while making ordinary decisions isn't a real moral principle.

Which of these actually pull against each other and which only look like they do? Rank the tensions by severity instead of listing them as equally bad, and for each one say what has to give and what it costs to give it up. I half-suspect (4) is just (3) wearing a lab coat, but I don't know that that's where the real trouble is — thier might be worse ones I'm not seeing.
