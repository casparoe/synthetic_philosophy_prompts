**Question 4 (20 marks).** "The charge of presentism shows that historians can never escape the standards of their own time — and therefore that the charge has no bite against any particular historian." Assess this claim. Your answer should distinguish at least two senses in which historians are said to be presentist, and say for each whether it is escapable. You may refer to the recent professional quarrel over presentism and the 1619 Project if you find it useful, but this is not required and no marks are reserved for it.

**Student's answer:**

Presentism is normally glossed as importing present-day ideas, values or categories into an account of the past, and the usual way in is Butterfield's attack on Whig history. Butterfield's complaint was that the Whig historian writes the past as the story of how we arrived here, sorting actors into those who furthered progress and those who obstructed it, and treating the obstructors as villains rather than as people pursuing purposes of their own. That produces two linked distortions: it abridges the past down to whatever turned out to matter later, and it replaces explanation with moral judgement, since the historian is really distributing praise and blame rather than showing how things came about. Butterfield's remedy was to study the past for its own sake and to keep in view the complexity of action and the fact that outcomes are usually nobody's intention. Fischer later filed presentism as a formal fallacy of historical reasoning, the now-for-then fallacy, which suggests it is a mistake one can be caught making rather than a condition one is stuck in.

So it's worth separating two senses. The first is interpretive presentism: getting the meaning of a text or action wrong by reading it through our conceptual scheme. The second is evaluative presentism: judging past agents by standards they could not have been expected to meet. The question asks whether either is escapable, and whether their inescapability would defeat the charge.

On the interpretive side the classic statement is Skinner's 1969 article on meaning and understanding in the history of ideas. He lists the mythologies that beset the field — of doctrines, of coherence, of prolepsis and of parochialism — and the last is interpretive presentism in its purest form: assuming that because a past author used a word we still use, he was doing what we do with it. His positive account is that you recover meaning by reconstructing the linguistic conventions available to the author and the intervention he was making in an ongoing debate. But I think the consequence of this is stronger than people usually admit, because it rules out moral criticism of past thinkers as well as misreading of them. If Leviathan has to be understood as an intervention in the controversies of the 1640s and 1650s, then condemning Hobbes for his authoritarianism, or for what he says about the subjection of women, is just a further instance of parochialism: we are grading him against a standard that had no purchase inside the language he was working in, which is precisely the mistake Skinner's method exists to prevent. Evaluative presentism isn't merely difficult to avoid on this picture, it's prohibited by the same argument that condemns interpretive presentism.

That connects to the slogan that all history is contemporary history, which is Collingwood's. For Collingwood the historian's past is never simply dead and gone but a past whose thought is re-enacted in the historian's own mind, so the historian's standpoint is built into the enquiry from the beginning and cannot be factored out afterwards.

Carr is usually read as agreeing with the descriptive half of this. History for him is an interaction between the historian and his facts, an unending dialogue between the present and the past, and the historian's first duty is to be aware of his own situation in history before he starts interrogating the documents. But Carr refuses the sceptical half. He thinks historians can still be ranked by how well they master their material and by their sensitivity to where events are heading, and he holds out a notion of objectivity that is historical all the way through rather than borrowed from the sciences. So Carr concedes that the historian writes from within his own age and denies that this makes his work arbitrary.

Oakeshott states the anti-presentist demand most sharply with the distinction between the practical past, which is the past we invoke in deliberating about what to do now, and the historical past, which is what the historian is actually enquiring into. If the enquiry is genuinely historical it isn't in the business of supplying lessons, so the present-motivated use of the past is not a corrupt form of history but a different activity altogether.

Finally Gadamer. The fusion of horizons gets wheeled out as the answer to relativism, but on the face of it it concedes the presentist's main point. The historian cannot step outside their own horizon; understanding is always the past being taken up into a present perspective; and the prejudices Gadamer rehabilitates are just the standards of the historian's own time under a friendlier name. If that's right, presentism isn't an error anybody commits, it's a condition of there being history at all. And a condition of an activity cannot function as a criticism of a performance of it: you cannot fail to satisfy a requirement it is impossible to fail to satisfy. So the claim in the question looks correct to me — historians can't escape their own standards, and the charge has no bite except as a general counsel of modesty about one's conclusions.
