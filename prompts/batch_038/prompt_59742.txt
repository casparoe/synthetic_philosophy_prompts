And here's the answer, pasted in full (typos are theirs):

> The distinction lives inside the framework of ontological models, which is the right setting because it forces a theory to say what it's committed to. An ontological model posits a space Λ of ontic states λ, where λ is meant to be a complete specification of the physical properties of the system — the sort of thing that has to exist if the theory is describing a world rather than just sorting predictions. A preparation procedure is represented by a probability distribution μ over Λ, and a measurement by response functions ξ(k|λ) giving the probability of outcome k when the ontic state is λ. The model reproduces quantum mechanics exactly when integrating μ against ξ gives the Born-rule probability for every outcome.
>
> The ψ-ontic/ψ-epistemic question is where ψ sits in that picture. A model is ψ-ontic if distinct pure states are separated at the level of the ontic state: for ψ ≠ φ, the supports of μ_ψ and μ_φ are disjoint, so λ tells you which quantum state was prepared. It's ψ-epistemic if that fails, i.e. if there's a set of λ of nonzero measure that's compatible with both ψ and φ. The gloss is that ψ-epistemic models treat the quantum state the way classical statistical mechanics treats a distribution over microstates — as encoding an ignorance that could in principle be reduced. Harrigan and Spekkens coined the labels in the wake of the PBR result to sort out which interpretations the theorem actually touches, and the vocabulary also lets them restate Einstein's 1935 dilemma: the epistemic horn is just the claim that ψ doesn't supervene on λ.
>
> H&S sharpen the definition quantitatively, though. A model is ψ-epistemic in the full sense when the overlap between μ_ψ and μ_φ accounts for exactly the quantum transition probability |⟨φ|ψ⟩|² — when all of the probability of finding φ on a system prepared in ψ is explained by the system's being in an ontic state that is also compatible with φ. Models with only partial overlap are ψ-epistemic to that degree. So PBR is best read as a theorem about how much overlap is possible.
>
> The argument. Prepare two systems independantly, each in either |0⟩ or |+⟩, giving four product states. Quantum mechanics supplies an entangled measurement with four outcomes, each of which is orthogonal to exactly one of those product states, so each outcome has probability zero on the corresponding state and the matching response function must vanish on that state's support. If the single-system distributions overlap with weight q > 0, then the joint distribution over the pair's ontic states — which factorises, because the preparations were independant — puts at least q² of its weight in the region compatible with all four product states. On that region all four response functions have to vanish, which contradicts their summing to 1. So q = 0. Running the same trick with n systems generalises it to any pair of distinct pure states.
>
> The assumption doing the work is preparation independence: systems prepared seperately have uncorrelated ontic states, with a joint distribution that's the product of the marginals. This is a locality assumption, which is why PBR gets grouped with Bell's theorem. Bell showed that reproducing the quantum correlations forces nonlocality on any hidden-variable theory; PBR shows the same thing for ψ-epistemic ones. So the upshot for the epistemic camp isn't that they have to abandon the framework, it's that they have to accept action at a distance: a ψ-epistemic model survives PBR only by being explicitly nonlocal, or by giving up on the idea that two lab benches in different rooms are really preparing independent systems.
>
> Does it settle whether the quantum state is real? Partly. It's worth saying that ψ-ontic doesn't mean ψ is a physical field in space — a ψ-ontic model only says λ determines ψ, and there's a live question about whether even ψ-ontic views can treat ψ as nomological rather than as a bit of stuff. It's also worth saying that the ontological-models framework isn't neutral: QBism and relational quantum mechanics deny that a preparation corresponds to a distribution over a space of λ at all, so PBR simply doesn't address them. Among ψ-epistemic models proper, the best worked-out case is Bohmian mechanics — since the complete physical state includes the particle configuration and ψ isn't needed to fix where the particles are, ψ there functions as information about the configuration, and the same configuration is compatible with many different ψ. Spekkens's toy theory is the other example: it's ψ-epistemic throughout and reproduces a suprising amount of qubit phenomenology, but it can't violate a Bell inequality, so it's a proof of concept rather than a rival to quantum mechanics.
