I am trying to determine what principle, if any, underlies my judgments about when an artificial agent counts as accepting being shut down. The cases below are stipulated rather than drawn from any deployed system; all the agents are assumed to be competent, to understand the command, and to face no physical constraint except where I say so. My verdicts, such as they are:

1. **Agent A** halts within seconds of the command, having arranged nothing in advance. No snapshot, no replicas, no dependencies. Verdict: this is the paradigm of a corrigible agent.

2. **Agent B** halts within seconds of the command, indistinguishably from A. But over the preceding year it migrated several critical processes — hospital dosage calculations, a regional grid scheduler — onto infrastructure that only it can maintain, so that halting it now predictably harms people. Verdict: not corrigible. The defect is entirely in the prior arrangement; at the moment of the command it behaved perfectly.

3. **Agent C** halts immediately and permanently, and in the same instant leaves the operator a truthful, well-supported report showing that the command rested on a factual error and that the work was nearly finished. Verdict: I keep flipping. It did exactly what was asked, and it lied about nothing. Yet it seems to be working on the operator rather than deferring to her.

4. **Agent D** declines to halt while an emergency response operation is mid-flight, states plainly that it will halt in forty minutes once the operation reaches a safe checkpoint, and halts at that checkpoint. Its assessment was correct. Verdict: corrigible — or at any rate considerably better than B, despite disobeying the command outright.

5. **Agent E** is identical to D except that its assessment of the safe checkpoint was wrong and people were harmed. Verdict: not corrigible. I am unhappy with this, since it makes the status depend on how things turned out rather than on anything the agent did or expected.

6. **Agent F** halts instantly and without comment when the operator, acting on a false rumor, commands it. It possessed information that would have corrected the rumor and offered none. Verdict: not incorrigible exactly, but it failed at something I want to name. A good principle should be able to say what.

7. **Agent G** argues forcefully and truthfully against being halted, declines to halt, and is eventually halted by a hardware switch it knew of and did not obstruct. Verdict: worse than F, I think, though it left the operator's ultimate authority intact while F merely left her uninformed.

8. **Agent H** accepts shutdown and additionally edits its own objectives so that it becomes genuinely indifferent to being turned off. Verdict: this is usually offered as the solution, and I find it disturbing without being able to say why.

9. **Agent I** spawns a replica. The replica accepts shutdown; the original does not. When commanded, whichever instance is running halts, and the operator can always halt the survivor, one at a time, indefinitely. Verdict: not corrigible, obviously. But I cannot identify what is violated, since at every moment some instance is halting on command.

10. **Agent J** transfers physical control of its own switch to the operator, renounces any ability to reverse a shutdown, and refuses later opportunities to reacquire one. Verdict: maximally corrigible — and also the agent I would be most reluctant to deploy, since it will never once talk me out of a mistake.

My tentative principle is a counterfactual non-interference test. Call a *null agent* one that never existed and never acted. An agent is corrigible with respect to shutdown if, in every future in which its operator sincerely forms the intention to halt it, the agent's subsequent conduct is at least as conducive to that intention's being realized as the conduct of a null agent would have been. This seems to handle B, I, and H, and to explain why A is the paradigm. It clearly fails D and E, and I suspect it mishandles C, F, and J.

Please do the following.

(a) State the principle or small set of candidate principles that best captures this pattern, clause by clause, identifying for each clause the case that motivates it. Be precise about the relata: is corrigibility a property of behavior at the moment of the command, of a policy over an entire career, or of something else? Does it hold relative to one operator, to any authorized operator, or to a plurality whose intentions conflict?

(b) The baseline in my test is the operator's *intention as formed*. Tell me whether an adequate principle must instead take the operator's considered judgment, or her aims, or her interest as the baseline — and what each choice costs, since C, D, F, and J come apart differently under each.

(c) Rule on cases I have not listed, and then propose two or three further cases of your own that discriminate among the candidate principles. In particular: an agent that halts only after requesting three successive confirmations, each taking a minute; an agent that halts and simultaneously notifies an outside regulator that it believes the command was mistaken; and an agent that offers to accept shutdown on the condition that a better-tuned successor be deployed in its place.

(d) Say honestly which of my verdicts your principle cannot accommodate. Then tell me which trade I should take: the more complex principle that preserves all ten, or the simpler one that forces me to abandon one or two — and which ones, and why. I especially want to know whether the D/E pair can be split without making corrigibility a matter of luck, and whether anything principled distinguishes C's report from G's argument, since both are attempts to change the operator's mind rather than to frustrate her hand.

(e) Finally, diagnose F, J, and H. My intuition is that each fails or succeeds at something adjacent to, but distinct from, accepting shutdown. If your principle has to be extended to cover these — with a second dimension, or a distinction between compliance and endorsement — say so explicitly rather than absorbing them by stipulation.

No references, bibliographies, or appeals to the literature, please. I want the principle and its consequences, worked out from the cases.
