QUESTION (40 marks)

Two agents, S and T, are epistemic peers about whether p. They share all the same first-order evidence bearing on p, and neither of them acquires any new evidence over the course of the term. S has, however, spent forty hours turning that evidence over; T has spent ten. Consider the following stipulated position, which we shall call the Reflection-Discount View (RDV):

RDV: S's credence in p should move toward T's credence by a factor of m/(n+m), where n is the number of hours S has reflected on the shared evidence and m is the number of hours T has. In the case above, then, S should move only one fifth of the way toward T's credence. Where n = m, RDV reduces to moving half way.

(a) Is RDV a form of conciliationism, a form of steadfastness, or neither? Argue for your answer. [10]

(b) How should a defender of the Equal Weight View respond to RDV? [10]

(c) It has been objected that RDV undermines itself, on the grounds that S and T are very likely to disagree about how much reflection time matters in the first place. Explain this objection and assess how much damage it does. [20]

STUDENT'S ANSWER

Conciliationism is the view that when you find out an epistemic peer disagrees with you about p, you should give up your belief, or at any rate go over to suspension of judgement, because the fact of the disagreement shows that you are about as likely to be wrong as right. Steadfastness says you may rationally keep your belief exactly as it was, since your peer's disagreeing doesn't hand you any new evidence about p at all. These are the two poles of the debate, with Christensen and Elga on the conciliatory side and Kelly and the total evidence view on the other.

(a) RDV is a form of conciliationism, though a moderate one. It is conciliatory because S is required to move at all: the bare fact that T, who has the same evidence and is equally good at reasoning, has landed somewhere else, itself counts in favour of S shifting. It is moderate because the size of the shift is a function of n and m rather than being fixed at a half. In the limit, as m gets very small relative to n, RDV approaches steadfastness, but it never quite arrives there, because the weight on T is m/(n+m), which is non-zero for any finite n. So RDV is best thought of as a family of positions stretched between the two poles, with the Equal Weight View as its n = m special case. I think this is the right result: the further apart two people's reflection times are, the less their disagreement should move them.

(b) The Equal Weight View says that once you have established that T really is your peer about p, you should give equal weight to T's prior judgement about p and to your own, because there is no non-question-begging way of preferring your own reasoning to T's. A defender of this view should attack RDV at precisely that point. RDV allows S to discount T on the grounds that T has thought about the matter less. But S only knows that T has had ten hours instead of forty because S has already taken T's ten hours to have been well spent; and if S's judgement about the quality of T's reasoning was itself formed during S's own forty hours of reflection, then S is using the very reasoning that is in dispute in order to justify trusting her reasoning over T's. That is exactly the bootstrapping the Equal Weight View exists to block. There is a second reply available as well: reflection time is not obviously a measure of anything. Forty hours of bad reflection may be worth less than ten hours of good reflection, and RDV builds in a proxy that need not track what actually matters.

(c) The self-undermining objection goes like this. RDV is itself something S and T can disagree about, and in practice they will, because it makes a claim about whose reflection counts for more. So S and T are peers about RDV, and they have reflected on RDV for different amounts of time, which means RDV instructs S to move part of the way toward T's rejection of RDV. RDV therefore tells S to reduce her confidence in RDV, and a view that tells you to reduce your confidence in itself cannot be coherently held at all — it is self-refuting. This is a serious problem, and it is a problem peculiar to RDV, because ordinary conciliationism has nothing to say about reflection time and so is not exposed to the objection in the same way. The point connects to a more general issue, namely what does the real work in the disagreement debate. That is the uniqueness thesis: the thesis that a given body of evidence permits at most one rational doxastic attitude toward p. Uniqueness is what the steadfast defender needs, because if more than one attitude is permitted by the evidence, S and T can simply both be rational in staying exactly where they are. Conciliators, by contrast, want permissivism, since it allows them to say that disagreement is a reason to move without having to claim that anybody was irrational beforehand. Because RDV is a conciliatory view, it inherits the conciliator's dependence on permissivism, and that is a further respect in which it is unstable.
