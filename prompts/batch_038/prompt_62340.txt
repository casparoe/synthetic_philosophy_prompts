So — I'm about forty percent through a novel where the narrator is a first-person plural. Not a royal we, not a bunch of gossipy neighbours taking turns, one experiencing thing that happens to be made of the people in a small town. My friend who did a philosophy PhD read two chapters and told me I'd accidentally committed myself to panpsychism and then walked straight into the combination problem, and now I can't get either out of my head. What I want from you isn't an essay and definitely isn't a bibliography — no names, no titles, no citations, I have zero interest in who said what. I want the spec. The list of things any theory of how a lot of little subjects add up to one big subject has to be able to do before it deserves to be called a theory, with the case or consideration behind each item, because I want to be able to argue with you about individual entries rather than swallow the whole thing.

Here's the purpose, since I know adequacy depends on it: the theory has to be determinate enough that I can use it as a consistency check while drafting. It has to tell me what my narrator can notice, what it can feel, what it forgets, and when it stops existing — a metaphysics that's elegant but silent on boundaries is no use to me at all. So weight the requirements toward the ones that actually bite in cases.

To show you where I'm coming from, here's my own draft list, and part of the job is telling me which of these are genuine requirements and which are just me wanting my book to work:

- the whole has to be a real subject, not a collection with a plural pronoun glued on — there's a fact about what it's like to be the town
- it should follow, at least in principle, from what the parts are like, otherwise I've sneaked in emergence through the back door and stopped being a panpsychist
- but the whole is allowed to have qualities none of the members has on their own, because I have a scene where the town feels a specific dread and no individual feels anything of the sort
- some people are in it and some aren't (there's a kid, and a guy who moved there eight months ago, and I want them both excluded) so the theory owes me a boundary criterion, not just a composition story
- when a member dies the whole keeps going, and afterwards my narrator reports something from the dead woman's childhood that it never had access to while she was alive — I've started calling this the residue question and I honestly don't know if it's a real desideratum or a cheat I'm trying to license
- the whole goes out of existence every night when everyone sleeps and comes back in the morning, which I think has to be survivable if the theory is going to allow intermittent subjects at all

Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are the house preferences of one camp — I already suspect that demanding both of my second and third bullets is asking for trouble, and if some subset is jointly unsatisfiable I'd rather hear that now, because that's the part that would actually change the book. Then tell me which single requirement does the most damage to candidate theories. Also: if the parts-first approach and the whole-first approach (where the big subject is basic and the little ones are somehow carved out of it) fail different requirements, say which fails which, and whether the whole-first move genuinely escapes the problem or just relocates it. Finish with three or four lines each on how the main families of answer do against your list — parts-build-up, whole-first, and the ones that refuse to compose at all. I don't need a table, and I don't need you to be polite about my list. If the residue question is indefensible, just say so and tell me what I lose by keeping it.
