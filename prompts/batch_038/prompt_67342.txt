I host a fortnightly podcast on the philosophy of the social sciences, recorded before a small live audience of graduate students and interested lay listeners. Next month's guest is a philosopher of social science who has written a book about the so-called gender-equality paradox: the widely reported finding that average gender differences in occupational and educational choices, and in certain personality and value measures, tend to be larger in countries that score better on standard indices of gender equality.

Her position, as I understand it, has four connected parts. First, she argues that both sides of this debate share what she calls the fixed-preference picture: the assumption that a preference is an inner state which either faithfully reports a person's nature or has been distorted by social constraint, so that every observed pattern of choice must be evidence for one of these two. Second, she holds that preferences are formed within, and are answerable to, the option sets and evaluative standards that institutions supply, and that "chosen freely" is therefore not a property a choice has independently of a specification of which alternatives are relevant to the comparison. Third, she maintains that the cross-national correlation cannot settle anything about nature or culture, and that the standard indices make the matter worse, because they are partly built out of the very outcomes (educational attainment, representation in senior roles) that the studies then treat as caused by equality. Fourth, and normatively, she says justice is not concerned with aggregate ratios but with whether a gendered distribution is yoked to an independent hierarchy of pay, security and social esteem. Her standing illustration is a pair of imaginary countries with identical thirty–seventy splits in engineering: in one, engineers earn twice the median wage and the work is prestigious; in the other, the same people earn the median and are regarded as doing unglamorous maintenance. She claims only the first is unjust.

The empirical state of play, as best I can reconstruct it: the original and most publicised analysis drew on international pupil-assessment data and a global gender-gap index; replication attempts established that a headline result rested on an undisclosed and unvalidated measure of pupils' likelihood of entering STEM rather than the share of STEM degrees actually awarded, and a correction was issued; a follow-up paper argued that the STEM version of the hypothesis has both conceptual and empirical problems; a separate study reproduced a pattern for mathematics but attributed it to the stereotype that mathematics is male work, which is itself stronger in more egalitarian and developed countries; and other cross-national work finds gaps in personality traits and preferences widening, not narrowing, with development and equality.

She is also a formidable deflecter. Her habitual move is to reply that a question "already assumes the picture I am rejecting", which tends to end exchanges rather than advance them.

Please draft nine interview questions, in the order I should ask them, building from the empirical state of play through her conceptual claim to her normative claim and finally to policy. For each, give: the question as I would read it aloud, in no more than about fifty words; a line beginning "If she says..." stating in one sentence the reply you expect her to give; and then a follow-up keyed specifically to that reply.

At least one question each must address: the circularity charge against the indices, and whether her own account escapes it, since she too needs a standard for which alternatives count as relevant; what the correction episode should teach about how this literature was conducted and reported, without inviting her to score points; the reply that her stereotype-based account merely pushes the question back one step — why should a stereotype be stronger where the barriers are lower?; whether preference-formation talk leaves "free choice" with any content at all, or makes her view unfalsifiable; her pay-and-esteem criterion, pressed with the observation that the sorting and the hierarchy may not be independent, since the feminisation of an occupation appears to depress its pay and standing, which would collapse her criterion back into a ratio criterion; how esteem is to be measured when the surveys that would measure it are themselves shaped by the sorting she brackets; the unit of choice, where part-time work and household bargaining mean the decision is joint and time-constrained; a question that concedes her central point outright and then asks what a ministry of education should stop doing and start doing on Monday morning; and a final question designed so that the "already assumes the picture" reply cannot be given without her stating a prediction of her view that the fixed-preference picture does not make.

Two further constraints. Nothing generic — no questions about what drew her to the subject, how she responds to critics in general, or what she is working on next. And each question must be intelligible to a listener who has never read the empirical literature, so any technical notion you use has to be glossed inside the question itself rather than left to her to explain. Please write the whole response in British English.
