Here's what I'm trying to work out. I have a pile of verdicts about when shame and when guilt are the *fitting* response, accumulated over years of arguing with friends and myself, and I can't see what they add up to. Some are obvious, some I'm unsure of, and I'm fairly confident at least one pair is just inconsistent. I'd rather you tell me which than save them all with something baroque.

The cases. (F = fitting, U = unfitting. Where I list both emotions, the question is which of the two fits.)

1. A surgeon realizes, ten years later, that a diagnostic error of hers probably cost a patient years of life. Nothing in the record shows it and no one will ever find out. Guilt: F. Shame: U. She did a bad thing; she isn't thereby a bad doctor or a bad person, and shame smuggles in a claim about her worth that the facts don't support.

2. Same surgeon, but the error turns out to be continuous with something she already knew about herself: she rushes histories becuase she finds patients tedious. My verdict flips. Shame: F, and arguably more fitting than guilt, because what's been exposed is a standing feature of her rather than an episode.

3. A man with severe depression, unemployed for a year, feels deep shame about the state of his flat — the dishes, the smell. Shame: U. Not his doing; it's the illness and the situation talking. And guilt would be even more obviously unfitting, so this is a case where I want to say *neither* emotion fits.

4. The same man feels shame about his body. Here I give the opposite verdict: the shame is tracking something real, so I won't call it unfitting — and yet I'd argue him out of it, and I'd think it good for him if I won. So fittingness and whether-you-should-feel-it come apart.

I know 3 and 4 look like the same kind of case (non-chosen, standing, bodily-cum-situational feature of the self) getting opposite verdicts. That's the pair I most want you to press on.

5. A woman who was bullied at fourteen still feels shame remembering how she begged them to stop. Shame: U — and note there's no act here for guilt to attach to, so it isn't that guilt takes over. Then the second layer: she's ashamed of having been ashamed. Both layers U.

6. A man cheats on his partner and feels intense shame but no guilt at all. I think this is a recognizable profile and a bad one: shame without guilt produces concealment and self-protection where guilt would produce repair. So shame is F in the sense that it tracks the real defect, guilt is F and missing, and the whole configuration is pathological.

7. A soldier who did exactly what he was trained and ordered to do, lawfully, and who feels no guilt but profound shame. Guilt: U. Shame: F. This is the case that makes me distrust the usual story that guilt is the mature ethical emotion and shame the primitive one.

8. My father makes a racist joke at a dinner I'm hosting. I feel shame, not guilt, and I call it fitting. But if my closest friend makes the identical joke at the identical dinner, what I feel is embarrassment, and I'd call shame there unfitting. The only difference is that one of them is *mine* — family. I can't ground that and I suspect it's a prejudice, but I don't want to give it up either.

9. Someone decides against cheating on an exam, then fails it, and feels shame about having been on the verge. F. Guilt: U, no wrong was done; the shame is about what was in him.

10. Survivor guilt — say, the sibling who lived. And separately: a woman who feels a flash of relief when her mother finally dies after four years, and feels guilty about the relief. In both, guilt U and shame worse, and yet I want to keep survivor guilt around as somehow *valuable* even while calling it unfitting. That's the second place where fittingness and value come apart for me, and this time it's guilt doing it, not shame.

11. My one cultural claim, tentative. Suppose a society has the practices and vocabulary for shame but effectively none for guilt — no confession, no apology as repair, no making-amends script; instead honor, standing, face, being seen. My verdict: this changes the *shape* of moral life there (what gets done after a wrong, and who has to be involved) but not which responses are fitting. So a culture can choose its emotional repertoire but not the facts that make responses fitting. I want to know whether that's coherent, or whether "fitting" has already been smuggled in from my own culture.
