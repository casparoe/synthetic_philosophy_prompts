I build evaluation infrastructure for large language models. I have an engineering background and no formal training in philosophy, so I will state my position in my own terms and flag where I may have the vocabulary wrong.

My team gates the deployment of a model for a given task on what I will call an articulation test: the model must not only perform the task reliably, but also produce a description of the procedure it follows that is specific enough that a much weaker model, given only that description and no access to the first model's weights, reproduces comparable performance. Passing is treated as evidence that the model knows how to do the task; failing is treated as evidence that whatever it has is memorized surface behavior or luck rather than competence. Underneath this practice sits a commitment I have settled on, roughly the intellectualist one I associate with Jason Stanley and Timothy Williamson against the older Rylean picture: knowing how to do something is a species of knowing that a thing is so — knowing that some way of doing it is a way to do it, held in a practical rather than a purely theoretical mode. On that reading, an articulation test is not a crude proxy for competence. It is a direct probe of it, and a system that cannot say how it does a thing arguably does not know how.

What I want from you is an inventory of the considerations that would tell against this position, weighted by how much they would tell. In particular:

1. What would show that the articulation test can fail for systems that genuinely know how, or pass for systems that do not? I see two directions of failure: a system whose fluent self-description is not causally responsible for its behavior, and a system whose competence is real but inarticulate because the true description of it contains tacit steps no one can state. Which findings in psychology, neuropsychology, and the study of expertise actually bear on this — dissociations between procedural and declarative memory, verbal-overshadowing effects, confabulated explanations of one's own reasoning, the difference between what experts do and what experts say they do — and how strongly does each bear? Please also identify which of these are routinely invoked here but do no real work against the intellectualist thesis.

2. Does the position stand or fall with claims from outside the philosophy of mind? I am thinking of assumptions about whether explaining and predicting a system's behavior requires access to its internal states, and about what counts as one "way" of doing something rather than many. If the thesis is hostage to any of these, say which and why.

3. Which of these defeaters would a committed intellectualist concede as counting against the view, and which would they absorb by refinement — by separating full-blooded knowing how from mere disposition, or by loading more work onto the idea of a practical mode of presentation? I want an honest judgment on whether those refinements leave the thesis exposed to anything at all, or whether it has been qualified into a shape that no observation or case could contradict. If it has, tell me whether that is a defect in a claim of this kind or a perfectly reasonable way for it to survive.

4. Sort the defeaters by severity: which would be fatal, which merely embarrassing, which would only require a narrower statement of the thesis. Then tell me which single one you would put in front of my team first, on the assumption that we keep using the test but want to stop overclaiming what it establishes.

Please write in American English and introduce technical terms as you use them rather than assuming I know them. I am after the arguments and the evidence themselves, so there is no need for a bibliography, reading list, or citations.
