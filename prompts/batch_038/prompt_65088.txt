I'm TA-ing a philosophy of time seminar and our last session is Gödel's rotating universe — the 1949 solution he produced for the Einstein birthday volume, with the dust rotating rigidly and the negative cosmological constant tuned against the density. The session always goes the same way: half the students leave thinking "so general relativity proves time travel is possible," and the other half think Gödel got physics to settle a metaphysical question, and nobody can quite say what work the existence of a consistent solution is supposed to be doing in the argument. So instead of another handout I want to give them something to laugh at. Write a parody of a customer-support knowledge-base article from a boutique firm that ships you a spacetime to spec and has just released the Gödel model as a consumer product — the voice should be pure cheerful helpdesk, the way a router manual talks to you, completely untroubled by what it's describing.

The jokes have to come from the physics being right, not from being garbled. So the "bugs" in the known-issues section should be genuine properties of that solution: there's no way to slice it into a global present, the light cones tip over far enough from the axis so a direction that felt spatial starts feeling pastward, closed timelike curves run through every event so there's no neighbourhood a user can move to in order to escape them, the thing is homogeneous so every single customer is at the centre of the rotation and support should treat that as a feature rather than a ticket, it doesn't expand, the cosmological constant has to be exactly balanced against the dust and with the wrong sign for what actual surveys keep finding, and there are no singularities anywhere which the marketing copy can brag about. Deadpan the whole way, no winking, no "of course none of this is real" escape hatch at the end — and the support desk should never slip into seminar mode and explain the metaphysics, because that's what the reader is supposed to feel happening underneath. The satire should land on two things at once: the corporate genre that turns an existence proof into a product feature, and the enormous, unexamined leap from "here is a self-consistent model like that" to "therefore the objective lapse of time is unreal." Let the reader recoil at that leap without anyone pointing at it.

Structure it with these sections in this order, roughly: "What's in the box" (~120 words), "Getting started: your matter, your constant" (~150), "Known issues" (~200, as a bulleted list of ticket-style entries), "FAQ" (~200), and "Warranty and returns" (~120 — the warranty should turn on the fact that there is no moment at which the customer could have accepted the terms, and support should be perfectly calm about this). Then a final titled section, "What's real here" (~150 words), saying which bits are actual features of the Gödel solution and where you put the pressure. A few ground rules: don't quote Gödel or Einstein or anyone else, don't give citations, page numbers, or a reading list, don't name any real physicist as the complaining customer, and keep the jargon explained in-line by the helpdesk voice so a first-year undergrad can follow it.
