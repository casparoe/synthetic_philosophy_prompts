I serve as chair of the steering committee at a small independent research institute — nine voting members, myself included. For most of the eleven years I have been here we have observed an unwritten convention that everyone calls "personal call": on a defined set of matters that principally concern one member only, that member's own decision is final. It requires no approval, no justification, and no vote, and the committee may not overturn it, however lopsided the sentiment against it. The two rooms a member occupies, and how they are arranged and furnished, fall squarely inside this category.

The difficulty concerns the east wing, which is occupied by two senior fellows whom I will call Rao and Okonkwo. Each has arranged their half over the years under personal call, and the two halves now look nothing alike. Rao's is austere: bare desks, no shelving in the corridors, all reference material in closed cabinets. Okonkwo's is the reverse: open shelving everywhere, pinned drafts covering every surface, three secondhand armchairs by the window. In June a funder offered to pay for a short documentary about the institute, most of it to be shot in the east wing, and the production team told us plainly that a single coherent visual scheme would serve the film far better than either half as it stands. A consultant drew up a scheme — call it the joint plan — that would have required Rao to give up some of his bareness and Okonkwo some of her clutter.

Every one of the nine of us, Rao and Okonkwo included, ranked the joint plan above either of their two rooms as they currently stand. We said so in discussion, at length, and not as a formality. But when it came to acting on it, both fell silent: neither was prepared to be the one to instruct the other, and the convention gave each of them an unambiguous entitlement simply to decline. The film was shot in June with the wing as it was.

Since then I have been trying to draft a standing rule so that the next such case does not die the same death. My proposal was that personal call remains absolute except where every member of the committee, the member concerned included, ranks some feasible alternative above what that member has chosen, in which case the committee adopts the alternative. Rao objected on the spot. He said the exception swallows the convention: on almost any contested matter a determined committee can assemble a proposal that everyone, wearied by argument and unwilling to be the lone dissenter, will rank above the status quo, so personal call survives only at the committee's pleasure and is not personal call at all. My reply was that unanimity is the one case in which there is nobody left to protect: if the very person whose call it is agrees the alternative is better, what remains of the point of the convention?

Three ways of proceeding are now on the table.

Option A. Abandon personal call altogether. Every matter becomes ordinary committee business, decided by majority vote. The convention was a source of paralysis; a body that cannot act on what all of its members agree is better has misdescribed itself.

Option B. Keep personal call and drop the unanimity clause. When someone's call is engaged, that person decides, and the fact that all eight others — or all nine, counting the person themselves — would rank something else more highly is treated as a fact about their wishes and nothing more. The committee is not entitled to a ranking over that matter at all.

Option C. Keep both, but confine personal call to matters on which no other member has any stake, so that nobody's ordering of the alternatives is allowed to register a view of how a colleague should exercise a call that is theirs. Unanimity would then be computed over what is left.

Two further things need saying, and I cannot tell what to make of either.

The first is that our orderings over one another's protected decisions are, as you would expect, meddlesome. Ferreira finds Okonkwo's clutter indefensible but admires Rao's austerity; Lindqvist holds the exact opposite view and considers Rao's bare corridors a betrayal of the institute's founding charter; Adeyemi and Park are each partial to one of the two rooms and merely lukewarm about the other; the remaining three, myself included, care only about our own offices and rank their two rooms in different orders out of habit rather than conviction. Last month, to see what the room would do, I took an informal vote among three layouts: X, the east wing as Rao would have it; Y, the east wing as Okonkwo would have it; and Z, a compromise both principals said they would live with, though neither ranks it above their own. X beat Z by five votes to four. Z beat Y by seven to two. Y beat X by six to three. We went round the same three comparisons twice and obtained the same result, so it was not noise: there is simply no arrangement that survives a vote.

The second is that I do not know whether that result is an argument for Option A. It might show that a body whose members hold views about one another's private decisions cannot rank anything stably, and therefore that the private decisions must stop being private. Or it might be pointing at something narrower.

What I am asking for:

1. State whether my two commitments — that personal call must always win where it applies, and that the committee should adopt whatever is unanimously preferred to what has been chosen — can be jointly satisfied by any choice procedure at all, or whether they are inconsistent as I have stated them. If they are consistent, explain exactly what has to be true of the set of alternatives for the consistency to hold, and say whether that condition is met here.
2. Say what Rao's objection gets right and what it gets wrong. Does a right that yields whenever every holder of it agrees that it should yield cease to be a right?
3. Diagnose the cycling vote. Identify what feature of our orderings produces the cycle, and say whether the cycle is evidence that personal call must be abolished, or evidence for a different conclusion.
4. Rank Options A, B, and C from most to least defensible as responses to the structural problem you have identified — not as a matter of what my committee is likely to swallow, but on the merits. Justify the ordering, and say what each of the two lower-ranked options gives up.
