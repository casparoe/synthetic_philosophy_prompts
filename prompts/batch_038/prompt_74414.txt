I am in the second year of a graduate programme in the history and philosophy of science, and this term I have been sitting in on a reading group that our mathematics department runs jointly with us: the arrangement is that the mathematicians describe how a result actually gets established in practice, and we are supposed to say what that implies about knowledge. Two things came up repeatedly and I have not been able to stop thinking about them. The first is that almost everything a working mathematician believes outside a fairly narrow speciality rests on results whose proofs they have never read. Textbooks announce that an argument is too long to reproduce; papers describe steps as routine and leave them to the reader; journals publish on the strength of two or three confidential referee reports that nobody else ever sees. The classification of finite simple groups is the extreme case: the proof runs to tens of thousands of pages across several hundred articles by roughly a hundred authors, published mostly between 1955 and 2004; it was announced as complete in 1983, that announcement proved premature because of a gap in the quasithin case, and the gap was not closed until 2004, by a single paper of some twelve hundred pages. In the interval the theorem was being used as a lemma all over the subject. The second is that when Hales produced his proof of the Kepler conjecture, the referees assigned to it reported that they were "99% certain" of its correctness, the result was accepted as a theorem on that basis, and a fully machine-checked version did not appear until 2014.

What I would like from you is a reconstruction of the theory of testimony this practice presupposes. My supervisor's view is that the answer is obvious — mathematicians simply trust one another, as chemists or historians do — and I think that is too quick, because mathematics advertises itself as the one discipline in which nothing is taken on authority, in which the proof speaks for itself and every reader is invited to check each step. So please begin by asking which of the familiar positions the practice actually commits mathematicians to: the Humean view on which testimony earns credit only as it is corroborated by one's own experience and by the mutual conformity of reports, or the Reidian view on which we have a default, underived entitlement to believe what we are told, defeated only by positive reason for doubt, or something that is neither. Then take up the notion that seems to be doing the real work, namely checkability in principle: the community behaves as though a claim is known provided some competent person could verify it, whether or not anyone does. Can that be a reductionist account at all, and what would have to be true for the merely possible checking of someone else to transmit warrant to my own belief, rather than to theirs? Is the practice quietly substituting something else for testimony — a standing offer, an insurance policy, a division of epistemic labour? I also want you to say whether accepting a theorem on a confessedly probabilistic assurance shows that what happens here is belief at all, or rather acceptance for the purposes of further work, and whether that distinction changes anything about whether testimony can transmit knowledge. Finally, and this matters most to me, please separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what merely sits well with it: identify the points at which it is underdetermined, say what evidence internal to the practice could discriminate between rival accounts that license the same behaviour, and say what would concretely have to change — in refereeing, in textbook writing, in citation habits, in the training of graduate students — if the community adopted the rival view instead.

Please write this as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter, with no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis and no tables, and aim for somewhere between nine hundred and eleven hundred words. Argue the positions in your own words rather than quoting anyone, and do not end with a bibliography or a list of further reading; naming the classical views and their authors where unavoidable is fine, but the answer should stand on the argument, not on the references.
