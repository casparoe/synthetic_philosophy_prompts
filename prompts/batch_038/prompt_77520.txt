I work as a catastrophe risk analyst for a reinsurer, which means my days are spent with frequency–severity curves, exposure accumulation, exceedance probabilities, tail dependence, Value-at-Risk and expected shortfall, parameter and model uncertainty, and endless arguments with underwriters about how much capital to hold against a flood event that has never been observed. I have no academic training in philosophy — everything I know I have picked up on my own, mostly from essays people forward me — and a colleague recently sent me some material arguing that the risks humanity should worry most about are not extinction risks but what the author called suffering risks: scenarios in which civilisation survives and spreads enormously, but ends up containing staggering quantities of suffering, so that the outcome is worse than our simply ceasing to exist. The argument as I understood it runs roughly as follows. Extinction caps the total disvalue at stake, because once there is nobody left, there is nobody left to be badly off; a surviving civilisation does not cap it, and since some surviving futures could contain more suffering than extinction contains of foregone value, the expected disvalue of a bad surviving future can exceed that of extinction. Therefore we should spend resources lowering the probability of those futures even at some cost to lowering the probability of extinction, and it is a mistake to assume the long-run future will be good merely because we would prefer it to be.

For several weeks I have been trying to restate that position in the vocabulary I actually think in, and I have got partway, but I do not trust my own translation. My working version: extinction is an absorbing state — the book is wound down, the exposure base goes to zero, and no further losses can accrue, which means the maximum aggregate loss is bounded by the value of everything currently in force. A suffering risk, by contrast, is a non-absorbing state with an unbounded severity distribution: the entity continues to exist and continues to generate losses, so there is no finite reserve that would make it solvent. On that reading the position is just a severity-dominance claim — a warning that a low-frequency, unbounded-severity, wholly non-diversifiable and unhedgeable tail can outrank a high-severity-but-bounded absorbing state, which is not unlike the arguments I already make internally about why expected-value maximisation is the wrong criterion when ruin is on the table. But the translation breaks at a specific point, and I cannot tell whether the break is in my framework or in theirs: nothing in the vocabulary lets me say that the losses would be worse than the total loss of the entity that bears them, because a loss is only a loss to a balance sheet that survives to record it, and severity is denominated in a numeraire I do not have for suffering. When I tried to write "worse than never existing" as an infinitely negative reserve, the sentence stopped meaning anything at all.

So here is what I would like. Take the position as I have paraphrased it and restate it, claim by claim, in the risk vocabulary described above — exposure, frequency and severity, tail, ruin, absorbing states, capital, solvency, diversification, discounting, model uncertainty — as faithfully as you can manage, and keep the translation honest: if a step only works because you have quietly swapped in an assumption from my side (for instance, that losses must be borne by an entity that continues to exist, or that severity must be measurable in a single unit), say so at that step rather than at the end. Then give me the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no counterpart in my vocabulary at all — I am particularly suspicious that the claims about how many people might exist in the future, and about whether their existence is itself good or bad, have nowhere to go, since my exposure base is always fixed before the decision and never produced by it. For each piece of residue, tell me whether it marks a genuine limitation of actuarial thinking or a sign that the original claim was doing less work than it appeared to be doing. Finally, tell me where my own severity-dominance reading goes wrong, and whether the deflation is real: if the position, once translated, turns out to be something I would already accept as routine tail-risk hygiene, how much of its original force has been lost in the conversion, and how much of that force was never expressible in probabilistic terms to begin with? I cannot look anything up, so please do not give me citations, author names, or a reading list; where you must borrow a term from the philosophical side, define it in a single line.
