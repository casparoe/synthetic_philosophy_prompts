So my sister's book club made me read Kierkegaard's Two Ages over the holidays — the one where he reviews a novel and ends up basically ranting about his own century — and I went in expecting to hate it and instead it ruined me, specifically the part about the press and the public. As best I can remember his point (I'm paraphrasing badly, my copy is still at my sister's): a newspaper doesn't just inform a bunch of real neighbours, it manufactures a phantom audience, an abstraction that is supposedly everybody and is actually nobody, which can be flattered and outraged but can never be asked to do anything or held responsible for anything, and once people start addressing that phantom instead of each other, talk replaces action and everything gets levelled down into chatter. Problem is, I've spent eleven years feeding exactly that phantom. I write the obituaries and the Tuesday church-and-grange notes for the Ledger, a weekly in a town of 6,000, and our editor Dot has spent 38 years building the paper into what she always called "one room where the whole town talks." Under Kierkegaard's description I genuinely can't tell whether that's the achievement or the crime. The one thing that stops me from just agreeing with him is her obit policy: everybody got equal inches, the mill owner and the guy who collected roadkill for the state highway department, and she'd slash any copy that tried to summarise a life by its job title. That feels like the opposite of levelling to me, and it also feels uncomfortably like precisely what he'd call levelling.

The print edition ends next month (the chain that bought the paper in 2019 is going monthly-plus-newsletter), Dot retires with it, and I've somehow been volunteered to give the farewell speech at the party. About sixty people: former reporters and ad salespeople, two delivery drivers who've been doing the route since the Reagan administration, the mayor and a couple of selectmen she skewered regularly, her family, our intern who runs the paper's TikTok and is unironically good at it, and the chain's regional manager, who is at the head table and is paying for the bar. Nobody in that room has read Kierkegaard except Dot's nephew, who's a third-year philosophy student and will absolutely clock whatever architecture I build. She's also told me twice that she hates being called a pillar of the community and doesnt want to be thanked, and her brother is saying something sentimental anyway so I dont have to be the warm one.

Can you write the speech? Around six minutes, so 800–900 words. The hard part: it has to actually take a position on whether what Dot built was the public in his sense or an escape from it. I don't want the easy exit where the speech concedes that the national media is an abstraction but reassures everyone that real local journalism is a different animal — I stopped believing that somewhere around page sixty, and everyone in the room would be able to tell I'm saying it to be nice. It needs to work for people who've never heard of him (his name at most once, nothing of his quoted out loud, no seminar), reward the nephew, and not hand the regional manager a feel-good beat about community connection or the pivot to digital. Use the concrete material — equal inches, the standing head on her front-page column every Thursday, the letters page, the fact that I'm the guy who writes about the dead — and let the argument live inside those details rather than in abstractions, which feels like the only way to end up on the right side of the thing I read. Don't end on a toast to the paper or to newspapers in general; end on one actual person in the room or one actual reader somewhere. One practical note: she's deaf in her left ear and will be sitting front and centre, so it has to be something I can deliver facing one direction without loosing her.
