I'm in a seminar on Hume's miracles essay and our tutor runs an exercise where he hands out miracle reports — mostly invented, some loosely adapted from real ones — and rules on each without saying why. His record so far:

1. Shrine healing, hundreds of witnesses, contemporaneous medical records, unanimous. Rejected. He said the crowd was in a state of religious exaltation and the order that runs the site wrote it up.
2. Two healings the same weekend at shrines belonging to rival denominations, each with equally good paperwork. Rejected both, and he called it a wash — equal testimony for contrary claims destroys the credit of each.
3. An apparition reported by one family who later sold the story. Rejected on interest, no further discussion.
4. A report from a remote mission station, decades old, secondhand, witnesses dead. Rejected because that sort of thing only ever surfaces far from the scene and among people nobody can cross-examine.
5. A geologist publicly reports a modern human footprint in 300-million-year-old rock; three colleagues saw it; his carreer was wrecked by it; no god invoked anywhere. Here he said something interesting — he'd take the report as evidence that something very strange happened, but he refused to call it a miracle, because nothing in it contradicts a law of nature so far as we can tell, and an unknown cause is still a natural cause.
6. When someone asked whether any testimony could ever establish a genuine violation of a law, he said the proof on each side is at most even, then moved on — so I couldn't tell if that's his own view or his summary of the first part of the essay.

Next week's case. Two labs on different continents independently report the same anomaly under sealed, continuously logged conditions. Dozens of witnesses each, data fully public, no religious framing anywhere, and the only stake I can see is that both are obviously in a race for priority. Crucially they agree on *what* happened and disagree completely about *why*: one says it's a real breakdown of energy conservation, the other insists it's an ordinary process with an unmodelled leak.

Predict his verdict. Which rule is he actually running? Cases 5 and 6 seem to pull apart, and 2 might be doing far more work than he admits. Is it a balance-of-probabilities rule, a defeater-hunting rule about witnesses, a generalized version of the contrary-claims cancellation argument, or the anomaly-versus-violation distinction from 5? Say what each one predicts about this new case, give me your best guess with your reasons for preferring it, and tell me what further case you'd slide across the table to separate them. And if his record genuinely doesn't determine this one, what would he be signing up for by ruling each way?
