I'm in an animal ethics seminar this term and it has thoroughly infected my housing co-op, where we're voting sunday on whether to call an exterminator about a german cockroach infestation (real one, in our kitchen, not a thought experiment). Two members, Ruth and Emeka, have been at each other in the group chat for days and I genuinely can't tell who's ahead. The exchange, roughly in order:

1. Ruth: if there's any nontrivial chance insects are sentient, spraying is indefensible. We'd be killing maybe tens of thousands of them over disgust and inconvenience, and a small probability of sentience multiplied by that many individuals swamps how annoying it is to keep finding them in the cereal.

2. Emeka: it isn't annoyance, it's health. Roach allergen is one of the big triggers for childhood asthma and there's an asthmatic seven-year-old on the second floor. Also these aren't wild animals with a role in an ecosystem — they're commensals that only exist because of our buildings, they'd die fast outside, and their lives are probably short and nasty. Not spraying just means more of them.

3. Ruth: that last clause is doing all the work and it's a replaceability argument. If a creature's life is net-negative you may kill it, and if killing it makes room for more equally miserable ones you should — that logic would license almost anything, and you don't actually know their lives are net-negative. Also the real alternative isn't spray-versus-do-nothing, it's exclusion: seal the cracks, bait stations, dry out the sinks, get the population down without mass killing.

4. Emeka: baits kill too, only slower, and slow could easily be worse from the insect's side. And exclusion is expensive and unreliable. If it half works you get a worse infestation in six months and end up fumigating the whole building, which kills far more. Your humane option has a fat tail.

5. Ruth: so now we're arguing about efficacy rather than principle, which is progress I guess. But your step 2 proves too much — if more roaches means more suffering, you should favor preventively poisoning habitat everywhere, which you told me last week you wouldn't.

6. Emeka: I wouldn't, and the difference is that I have obligations toward the animals living in a building I'm responsible for that I don't have toward insects in a field. Plus the kid's asthma. It isn't the same question.

They've agreed to let a neutral referee sort it out and I volunteered you. Go move by move and rate each of the six from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with the reason for each rating. I especially want to know: which of these disputes are empirical and could actually be settled by finding out something about the world, versus moral ones that can't; whether step 5 catches Emeka in a real inconsistency or step 6 successfully blocks it; how much of the Ruth/Emeka fight is just the same claim relabeled; and what both of them are missing that somebody standing outside the argument would notice.
