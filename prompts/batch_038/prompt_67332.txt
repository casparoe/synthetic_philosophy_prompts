So I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy background whatsoever, and I've been volunteered to give the closing talk at our company all-hands on friday — about 20 minutes. Backstory: three weeks ago a junior dev ran a migration script against prod by accident and took down billing for something like nine hours. He'd been here four months. He's still employed, he'll be sitting in the third row, and he has defintely not made eye contact with anyone since.

Then two weeks ago our CTO opened a leadership offsite by saying that there's no such thing as an accident, only decisions somebody made earlier who isn't in the room. Everyone laughed. He wasn't joking. He's now started putting the incident owner's name on the quarterly review deck, which is new.

Separately, I've been reading something on my commute — a philosopher, essay from the 80s I think — and it's been rattling around in my head. The argument goes roughly: to be morally responsible for what you do, you'd have to be responsible for how you turned out, for the particular way your head is wired. And you can't be, because any attempt to change yourself — deciding to be more careful, say — is made by the person you already were, and the same demand applies to that decision, and then to whatever lay behind it, all the way back. So nobody is ever responsible in the deep sense. The part that got me is that he isn't running this off determinism. He thinks the argument works whether or not the universe is deterministic, which blew my mind, because I'd assumed this entire debate was free will versus physics. He also mentions that his own small son was killed in a freak accident involving a refrigerator, and insists the argument doesn't touch grief — which I read as him being honest about where his own point runs out.

Here's my problem. I want the talk to actually do something with that argument, and I have no idea how to stop it from becoming either an excuse for the kid in row three or a public lecture aimed at my CTO. So:

- Write the actual speech, roughly 20 minutes spoken.
- Walk the argument through properly, but in plain language and your own words. The room is engineers, a couple of execs, HR, and some sales folks. No jargon, don't name the philosopher or the paper, no citations, nothing that sounds like it was lifted off a syllabus. If the argument needs the self-changing step to hold together, the room needs to *feel* that step rather than be told it — maybe something they can run in their heads about their own code review habits.
- Then the speech has to handle the fact that the argument being right doesn't mean we stop holding people to things. This is where I'm stuck. I need some forward-looking reason we still name an incident owner, still do blameless postmortems, still sometimes fire people — and it has to survive the CTO's line. If all you give me is "we do it to shape future behavior," he'll say that's precisely what he means and the room will agree with him. So go more specific than that. There's a difference between an incident process that changes the system and one that just attaches a name to the damage, and the speech should be able to say what it is.
- It has to land differently for three people. The junior dev should hear the argument as something being taken off his shoulders, not as a diagnosis of what kind of person he is. The CTO should not be able to walk out thinking the talk was on his side, but he also can't feel ambushed or corrected in front of the company — he's my boss's boss. The other engineers should leave with something they'd actually do differently next quarter, not a vibe.
- Don't resolve it too neatly. I'd rather end inside the real tension than end with a bow on it.
- After the speech, add a short note addressed to me, not to the room: flag the two or three places where you think you've oversimplified the argument or bent it to fit the occasion, and say what a philosopher in the audience would object to. Also tell me which lines I should not deliver if the junior dev's manager happens to be sitting right next to him.

Keep the speech itself under about 2,000 words. And please don't make it sound like a TED talk — I'm the guy who wrote the migration tooling, I talk like a normal person.
