Im a philosopher of science and in about three weeks I'm giving a talk at an interdisciplinary workshop that's effectively hosted by an economics department. Room will be maybe twenty applied macro people who build and estimate DSGE models for a living, two econometricians who do mostly local-projection and VAR work and are openly sceptical of structural modelling, and one historian of economic thought who is the only person there who has actually read Lakatos rather than picking up the vocabulary from a methods seminar. Nobody else has encountered more than a paragraph of him.

The thesis is deliberately awkward and I want to be ready for it. Sketch: mainstream DSGE macro is a degenerating research programme in Lakatos's sense — the hard core (optimising agents, rational expectations, equilibrium as the organising device) has been shielded since 2008 by an ever-thickening protective belt of auxillary hypotheses, financial frictions, heterogeneous agents, occasionally binding constraints, news and policy shocks, that absorb anomalies without yielding genuinely novel predictions that then come out true. Theoretically progressive, empirically not. But, and this is the part that's going to annoy everybody, I argue the upshot is conservative rather than revolutionary. The heterodox people in the audience want me to say we should switch to agent-based modelling or to purely reduced-form atheoretic work, and I'm going to say no: those aren't rival programmes at all in Lakatos's terms, they're toolkits with no hard core and no positive heuristic, so they can't generate a progressive problemshift even in principle. Since on his account you only abandon a degenerating programme once a rival is there to take over, DSGE stays — as the only candidate that could ever get better. So I'll be telling a room full of DSGE users their work is degenerating and telling the critics their alternative doesn't qualify.

Give me a prep sheet, not a defence of the thesis. Specifically:

- the eight to ten objections I'll actually get from this room, not the ones a philosophy of science audience would raise, ordered by how much damage they'd do to the talk. Phrase each the way that person would actually say it out loud: an applied macro person sounds different from an econometrician, and the historian will go for the methodology-of-the-methodology jugular.
- for each objection, a reply I can deliver standing up in about two minutes, plus an honest line on where the reply is soft or is just my thesis restated more loudly.
- which two or three I should concede ground on up front, and exactly what to concede so it buys credibility without gutting the argument.
- the single reply that has to land for the talk to have been worth giving — if only one thing survives into the discussion, which is it.
- the question I'm quietly hoping nobody asks.

Also tell me if you think one of my two halves is much weaker than the other, because I suspect the "the alternatives aren't rival programmes" half is carrying more weight than I'd like. And please don't invent sources or put words in Lakatos's mouth between quotation marks — paraphrase the concepts, and mark anything you're less than sure about so I can check it myself.
