Background first, since I think it explains why I'm asking this in what I'm told is an unfashionable way. I'm 73 and spent 38 years as a limnologist — lake profiling, nutrient cycling, sediment cores, that kind of thing — and my whole working life was proxies. Chlorophyll-a is the classic one: everyone in the field knew it was a lousy stand-in for algal biomass, and we used it at four hundred stations a summer anyway, because it was the one thing you could actually get a comparable number for with a fluorometer and a filter cartridge. So I have developed a permanent allergy to any quantity that's supposed to carry normative weight but can't be measured consistently by two different people in two different labs. Since retiring I've gotten way into philosophy — animal minds mostly, plus whatever moral-status literature I can get my daughter to send me — and I keep coming back to a question nobody answers in a way that satisfies me: is brain size morally relevant, and if so, which of the dozen things people mean by brain size?

Here's where it gets into the weeds for me. There isn't one quantity, there's a whole family, and they rank the animal kingdom very differently. Absolute brain mass in grams. Raw brain-to-body mass ratio. The encephalization quotient, i.e. the residual off the allometric line. Absolute neuron count. Neuron count restricted to the associative front end — pallium or cortex in vertebrates, mushroom bodies and whatever the cephalopod equivalent is supposed to be elsewhere. The share of the resting metabolic budget the nervous system eats. Synapse count or synaptic density. Density and distribution of the nociceptive and endogenous-opioid machinery, which strikes me as being about suffering rather than cleverness. And maybe developmental ones, like how long an animal needs an expensive oversized brain before it starts working. Three cases I can't make fit: an elephant brain has roughly three times the total neuron count of a human brain but something like 97–98% of them down in the cerebellum and only about a third of our cortical number; an octopus has on the order of 500 million neurons, two thirds of which are out in the arms rather than central; and a honeybee does a shocking amount with on the order of a million. Then add the human edge cases I actually care about most — late-stage dementia, an infant born without most of the brain, and the cortical organoids people are growing in dishes — and it occured to me that no single number survives all of them intact. Our book group's resident philosopher says the whole enterprise is just nineteenth-century craniometry with better instruments, and I'd like you to say whether that's a decisive objection to ranking such metrics at all or only an objection to one particular use of the ranking.

So here's the ask, and I want it in your format rather than mine, because I am obviously making a spreadsheet. Take the nine candidate quantities above — add or drop one or two if you think I've missed an obvious one or included a dud, but say which and why — and score each 0–10 twice: once for moral relevance (how plausibly it tracks whatever actually grounds moral status, on your own best view of what that is) and once for measurability and robustness (could a competent non-specialist get comparable numbers across phyla, how much does it hinge on methodological choices, does it require fresh tissue and dissection or can you do it from a museum collection and a scale). Then sort them into tiers on the combined picture, and point out explicitly which entries are the ones where the two axes pull hardest in opposite directions and which axis should win there. Three additional things. First, I want to hold the metrics to a constraint of mine: any metric whose normal-range within-species spread would license ranking individual humans differently takes an automatic penalty, because it's the same quantity that would sort people. Tell me whether you think that constraint is right, and re-score if dropping it changes anything. Second, split the target question in two and say whether your tier list changes between them — can this organism be harmed at all / does it have valenced experience, versus how much its welfare counts relative to ours. Third, give me one number between 0 and 1 for the probability that gross brain size retains some genuine, non-derivative moral significance once you've controlled for everything else on the list, where by non-derivative I mean it isn't just doing proxy work for one of the other entries. Finish by naming the two entries you think you're most likely to have placed wrong and what kind of finding would flip them. No need for references or a reading list — I can't chase those down and I'd rather have your actual judgment than a bibliography.
