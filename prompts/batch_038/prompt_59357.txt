I serve on the integrity panel of a mid-sized international funder. Our task is to rule on practices reported at the local partner organizations we finance — deciding whether a given episode is corrupt, acceptable, or acceptable subject to conditions — and our rulings are meant to accumulate into a coherent body of precedent. The definition we inherited from our founding documents is the familiar one: the abuse of an entrusted position for private gain. Our record to date runs as follows.

1. A partner's logistics officer paid an unofficial fee at a highway checkpoint to move a shipment of therapeutic food during a famine, where no lawful alternative route existed and there was no time to escalate the matter. The payment was entered in the partner's books and reported to us. Cleared.

2. The same partner's director hosts an annual feast for the families of the district officials who approve its grants, costing roughly a local civil servant's weekly wage, paid from the partner's own overhead rather than from our funds, publicly announced, and open to the officials' political rivals. Not corrupt.

3. A village council chair who also sat on a partner's board steered a borehole contract to his brother's firm at ten percent above the next bid; the brother's firm was the only one in the region with a working rig, and it finished on schedule. Corrupt.

4. A regional official standing for reelection asked partner staff to register households in his ward for a cash-transfer program. Every household was genuinely eligible and all received their transfers; he solicited no votes and the partner did not fund his campaign. Acceptable with conditions: publication of the beneficiary list and a bar on partisan messaging at distribution points.

5. A partner's finance manager gave a goat and a small sum of cash to the family of a government auditor who had died, a friend of her late father, from her own pocket, at the funeral, in keeping with a customary practice in her community. The audit she was overseeing was already complete and clean. Cleared.

6. To preserve its operating license, a partner agreed to take on three young people nominated by a ministry director as paid interns at market wages. They were not relatives of his, they were unqualified for the roles, they did very little, the arrangement was renewed annually, and our investigators found that the same director had made the same request of seven other organizations. Corrupt.

7. Households in a partner's catchment area collected small voluntary contributions to supplement the salary of a government nurse whom the ministry was about to transfer away. Collection was open, declining carried no penalty, and the money went directly to the nurse. Cleared.

Two members of that panel have since retired, and those remaining disagree about what our own record commits us to. Three readings have been proposed: that we have been tracking the source of value, permitting private money to move while treating ours as protected; that we have been tracking publicity, permitting whatever is performed in the open, recorded, and declineable, and condemning whatever is hidden, repeated, or unrecorded; and that we have been tracking the shape of the obligation created, permitting exchanges that settle a bounded debt and condemning those that leave an open-ended one callable later against people who took no part in them. As far as I can tell, all three readings fit every ruling above, and none of us has found a precedent that separates them.

The case now before us: a partner runs a maternal health program in a district where it is customary for a family to present a gift to whoever delivered a difficult birth. After a midwifery team managed a complicated delivery successfully, the newborn's grandmother presented the midwife with a length of cloth and a small amount of cash, about two days' local wages, in front of the family and the clinic staff. The midwife refused at first; the grandmother became visibly distressed and said the family would be shamed if the gift were returned. Our code of conduct bars partner staff from accepting anything above a token value from a beneficiary, and this exceeds that threshold. Three further facts matter. The midwife has treated this family before and will probably treat them again. The family is among the poorest in the catchment area, so the sum is a real sacrifice rather than a trifle. And since the delivery, other families have begun asking whether bringing a gift improves their chances of being seen promptly. The clinic keeps no register of gifts. The midwife has offered to hand the cash over to the clinic's fuel fund.

What I would like from you is the following. Identify which of our precedents is the closest analog to this case, and say precisely where the resemblance holds and where it fails. Work out what each of the three candidate readings predicts for our ruling, and tell me whether some fourth reading fits our record at least as well while predicting something different — if so, name it and test it against all seven precedents. Then give your best guess at the decision most consistent with our own precedents, along with your reasons for preferring that reading to the others rather than merely finding it congenial. State plainly what principle we would be adopting whichever way we rule, including what either choice would commit us to about whether a standard built on separating the office from the person holding it can be carried into settings where that separation is not how people organize obligation, and who bears the cost if it cannot. Finally, describe one further case, realistic enough that it might actually come before us, that would discriminate among these readings, and say which way each reading would rule on it.

Please write your answer in American English.
