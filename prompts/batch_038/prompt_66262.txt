A friend and I spent most of last night arguing about democratic backsliding and got nowhere useful. His position is that the term has collapsed into a partisan label: any codebook that flags one government's court reforms while passing over another's is politics wearing a lab coat, and the honest thing is to stop publishing indices. My position is that there is a real distinction between a democracy that is getting worse while remaining a democracy and one that is crossing a threshold into something else, and that the distinction can be made operational rather than merely rhetorical. Neither of us could write the test down. I would like you to write it down, because I now have to use it at work.

The setting. I supervise a two-person classification team at a private grantmaking foundation. Four times a year we assign every country in our portfolio — roughly forty of them, including Hungary, Poland, Turkey, and the United States — to one of three categories: functioning democracy, backsliding, or non-democracy. The category determines which funding instrument a country office may use and, twice a year, appears in a public report. Our board includes appointees from both major American parties, and last cycle one of them formally accused us of applying a different standard to Ankara than to Austin. So the classification has to be defensible to people who are looking for asymmetry, and it has to produce the same verdict regardless of which of my two analysts runs it.

The constraints. Each analyst has an undergraduate degree in political science and no graduate training in comparative politics. Each country gets ninety minutes per quarter, working only from publicly reported facts. Where the procedure requires a judgment that cannot be resolved by evidence — a question of what democracy is for, or how much weight a value deserves — it must say so and hand the decision up to me rather than burying the judgment inside a scoring rule. And it must tell the analyst what to do when a load-bearing fact is contested and cannot be verified within the time budget.

What I want. A decision procedure, in ordered steps, that an analyst can actually run: the questions to ask in sequence, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the thresholds at which the classification changes, the points at which the procedure terminates in a verdict, and the points at which it terminates in an escalation. Ground it in whatever the study of comparative politics has that is genuinely operational here — the difference between a decline that remains above the floor of democracy and one that goes through it; the difference between changing the rules and changing how the rules are observed in practice; the fact that backsliding is usually accomplished through legal instruments rather than through coups; and the distinction between narrowing the competitiveness of elections and hollowing out the speech, association, and rule-of-law conditions without which competition means nothing. Name the concepts you rely on, but do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I want the apparatus, not the references. Tell me explicitly which distinctions you are preserving intact, which you are compressing for usability, and what the compression costs.

Then run it on three cases, invented to be difficult:

1. A government uses the ordinary constitutional amendment procedure, ratified by referendum, to reduce the size of its constitutional court from fifteen members to nine and to give the legislature authority to review the court's budget and administrative appointments. No right is textually repealed and no election is altered.
2. An electoral commission redraws all districts using a published algorithm. The opposition wins a majority of the national vote and forty percent of the seats. Independent analysts report that the resulting map falls within the range produced by ordinary partisan redistricting in several long-established democracies.
3. Following a major terrorist attack, a government declares a state of emergency that remains in force for fourteen months. Under it, press accreditation is suspended for outlets accused of spreading panic; two of the suspended outlets are restored after nine months.

For each, report the classification the procedure yields, the steps that produced it, and any step at which the analyst was told to escalate. At least one of these should come out in a way that would be politically inconvenient for my board — if your procedure never produces an unwelcome verdict, tell me why that is a defect rather than a reassurance.

Finish with a symmetry audit: state what happens when the same procedure is run on measures adopted by an established democracy, and identify any step whose trigger would in practice fire only in some countries and not others. Then give me a short, unsparing account of what this procedure cannot do — including whether it can handle a country that was never fully democratic to begin with, and whether it can detect backsliding that has not yet produced any observable institutional change.

Please write the entire response in American English.
