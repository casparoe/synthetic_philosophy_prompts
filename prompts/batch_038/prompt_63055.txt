Fourteen graduate students in a philosophy-of-science seminar, 90 minutes, roughly half of them with some exposure to general relativity and half with none. I want a hands-on activity that confronts them with the hole argument as a construction problem rather than as a lecture. They should assemble a small model world out of a finite set of named points plus a table of field values, discover on their own that two groups have produced lists which differ point by point yet agree on every fact expressible without appeal to the names, and then — asked to extend a configuration that is fixed everywhere outside a designated interior region — discover that the interior is not pinned down by anything they were given. Please specify the handout or grid, the roles and rules, any tokens or materials, and a minute-by-minute timeline, keeping the mathematics no harder than arithmetic on a table, and withholding all technical vocabulary until the debrief. The debrief should map each moment of the activity onto the corresponding piece of the argument — manifold points, diffeomorphism, Leibniz equivalence, substantivalism, and the indeterminism that follows if the two descriptions are genuinely distinct — and should set out clearly what each of the two available escapes costs. Two things I want addressed explicitly: my students will treat the point-names as arbitrary bookkeeping from the outset, so the design needs some device that makes those names feel like genuine individuals with something at stake in them; and I want an honest statement of which feature of the real argument the activity cannot reproduce — I suspect the continuum, and the fact that the freedom is a property of solutions to field equations rather than of a puzzle's rules — together with what I must supply verbally in its place.
