Here is my situation. I am a high school senior doing an independent study on Augustine's two works on lying: the early one he wrote before he became a bishop, around the mid-390s, and the much later one addressed to Consentius, which was provoked by a proposal that Catholics should pretend to be Priscillianists in order to infiltrate the sect's hiding places. I read both in an old public-domain English translation. Three of us discuss them weekly — my Latin teacher, another student, and me — and we have been stuck since roughly October. We agree on the texts. We disagree completely about what the two books add up to, and the argument has calcified into three positions that no longer even seem to touch each other.

The first position, which my Latin teacher holds, says there is one stable principle throughout: what makes a lie a lie is a mismatch between what is in the speaker's mind and what comes out of the mouth, so that a person can utter something false without lying and can utter something true while lying, and the later book changes nothing. On this reading the Priscillianist affair simply presented a harder case, and Augustine answered it by insisting that concealment, silence, and refusal to answer are available options that do not involve putting a false heart-content into words. Same criterion, more options on the menu.

The second position, held by the other student, says Augustine retreated. The evidence offered: in his retrospective survey of his own writings he admits that he had wanted the early book withdrawn and even destroyed, and kept it only because it contains things the later one lacks; the later book is noticeably more willing to talk about what a Catholic may do with silence, evasion, and non-verbal pretense; and the early book's careful ranked catalogue of kinds of lie, running from lies about religious teaching down to lies that hurt no one and shield somebody from harm, does not really do any work in the later argument. On this reading the pastoral emergency quietly narrowed the doctrine.

The third position, which I have drifted toward, says both of them are making a category mistake: the early book is conceptual work — what counts as a lie, how lies sort into kinds — and the later book is direction of conduct under persecution, and comparing their conclusions is like comparing a definition to a rule of procedure.

Two textual pivots keep coming up and never get resolved. One is that the early book opens by setting jokes aside as not counting as lies at all, because tone and manner signal that no deceit is intended; but that exemption seems to depend on how the hearer is disposed to take the words, which sits oddly with a purely mind-to-mouth criterion. The other is that the Priscillianists' own justification, as Augustine reports it, was that one may hold the truth in the heart while speaking falsehood to outsiders, and that the scriptural command to speak truth applies to one's neighbor; Augustine's counter is that this would have licensed the martyrs to deny being Christians. That looks like a substantive doctrinal argument rather than mere casuistry, and I cannot tell whether it supports position one or position three.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis I expect you to attack: the impasse is not about Augustine at all but about what would settle a claim about an author's doctrine — whether occasional writings separated by twenty-five years can be treated as a single system — and the word lie slides in the argument between a mental-act sense and a social-practice sense in which someone is owed the truth. But I may be wrong, and there may be a genuinely substantive disagreement buried underneath about whether withholding ever becomes a doubled heart.
