I am preparing a position paper for a cross-departmental research group on the measurement of violence, and before I circulate it I would like a consistency audit of the view I intend to defend. These are my commitments, stated as plainly as I can.

1. The unit of moral concern is the individual, so periods of very different population size should be compared by rates of violent death per capita rather than by body counts. A world of eight billion with a lower rate is, in the sense that matters, less violent than a world of two billion with a higher one.

2. World population is the denominator I use throughout, because it is the only series available at comparable quality across long historical spans.

3. Once a definition and a denominator are fixed, there is a fact of the matter about whether violence has declined. The substantive disagreements in the literature are empirical disagreements — about coding rules, undercount, the treatment of one-sided deaths, and which datasets to trust — and they are in principle resolvable by better data.

4. Violence includes structural violence in Galtung's sense. Harm that is built into social arrangements, that is avoidable, and that is in fact avoided elsewhere counts as violence even where no agent strikes a blow; on that footing, deaths from untreated but treatable disease, from unsafe water, and from ambient air pollution are violent deaths.

5. The best-documented long-run series we possess is the decline in interpersonal homicide in Western Europe since the later Middle Ages, and I treat that as the strongest single piece of evidence for the general thesis.

6. I accept the heavy-tail objection to the war data — that the sizes of wars are so fat-tailed that eighty-odd years without great-power conflict is a short and unrepresentative sample which licenses no confident prediction. I do not extend that scepticism to the homicide series, which I regard as statistically robust and well sampled.

7. The principal causal mechanism behind the decline in interpersonal violence is the consolidation of state coercive capacity: the state's monopoly of legitimate force crowds out private vengeance and feud. I regard this as a durable structural feature of the modern state system rather than a fortunate run of circumstances.

8. One-sided violence by states against their own populations — massacre, politicide, and famine deaths a state could have prevented at negligible cost to itself — belongs in the ledger on exactly the same footing as battlefield deaths.

9. I am no biological determinist about aggression. The decline shows that violent behaviour is highly responsive to institutions and incentives, not that human nature has altered.

10. Much of the controversy between optimists and pessimists is, I suspect, an artefact of their using different words for the same world; a good deal of it would evaporate if both sides agreed a single operational definition at the outset.

Please tell me whether this package hangs together. I want to know which pairs are genuinely in tension and which only appear to be, with the genuine tensions ranked by severity rather than listed as equally serious. For each, say what would have to be given up, what the cheapest available repair is, and what the repair costs me — including whether it costs me the headline claim the paper exists to make. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. Where I have assumed that a point of contention is empirical and settleable, say so plainly if the field has not in fact converged on it, and please distinguish throughout between what is settled among people who work directly with these datasets and what remains genuinely contested; I would rather be told that one of my commitments rests on a live dispute than have a contested position presented to me as the consensus.
