I've settled into what I think is a deflationary line about Bertrand's paradox: the 1/3, 1/2 and 1/4 answers aren't in contradiction, they're answers to three different, individually well-posed questions, and the principle of indifference never actually misfires — "pick a chord at random" just doesn't ask anything yet. Give me the generating procedure and indifference does exactly the work it's supposed to do.

Now tell me what would count against this. I want the arguments and results a well-posed reading genuinely can't absorb, not the ones it can trivially re-describe. Specifically: what would have to be true — physically or mathematically — for the diagnosis to be wrong? Is the symmetry/invariance strategy for singling out one answer without a specified procedure (Jaynes's move, roughly) a rival to my view or the same view wearing a hat, and what does it mean for me if that machinery can be made to deliver more than one of the three answers depending on which symmetries you priviledge? What would someone actually tossing straws at a drawn circle have to find to embarrass me — or does my view get to say "of course you got a determinate answer, you specified a procedure," and if so is that a dodge? Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward, say which ones a committed deflationist would concede as counting against them and which they'd wave off, and be honest about whether "the problem was ill-posed" has been stretched into something that can't be wrong — and if it has, whether that's a real problem for a diagnostic claim of this kind as opposed to an empirical theory.
