My friend has spent two years running an explicit screen on the prose that crosses her desk as standards editor of a regional paper. The screen is borrowed, self-consciously and avowedly, from Orwell's 1946 essay on the English language: a submitted passage is flagged if it displays the habits that essay catalogues — the exhausted imagery, the pretentious diction, the prefabricated phrase, the meaningless assertion — and passed without comment otherwise. She also invokes the essay's own escape clause, that the rules may be broken rather than produce something outright barbarous, though she has never said what counts as barbarous. We argued for four hours last night, because her published verdicts do not obviously track the essay's list, and by the end neither of us could state the principle she is actually running. I would like a dispassionate reconstruction. I am too invested to produce one, and I do not want a summary of the essay: assume I know it.

Her verdicts, in order, with the reasons she gave when she gave one.

1. A municipal notice about an eviction — "Following a comprehensive review of land utilisation, households within the designated corridor were transitioned to alternative accommodation; the process was completed without incident" — flagged. Her reason: a reader cannot determine from it who did what to whom.

2. A clinical audit extract — "A 3.5 cm lesion was excised with clear margins; the specimen was sent for histology; no adverse events were recorded" — not flagged. Her reason: the passive is correct because the agent is genuinely irrelevant, and the technical vocabulary is the shortest accurate description available.

3. An obituary, warm, concrete and free of jargon, which we afterwards discovered matched almost sentence for sentence a template sold by two funeral directors and reused in eleven other published obituaries in the same county — flagged. She declined to choose between two explanations I offered: that the words had not been chosen by the person they purported to come from, and that a family's grief had been rendered in a dialect every reader would recognise as bought.

4. A shop steward's speech delivered from memory, thick with dead metaphors about turning tides and refusing to be silenced, but naming the plant, the employer, the headcount and the dates — not flagged, though she said the metaphors made her teeth ache. Her reason: every claim in it could be checked, and the speaker would stand behind the plain version.

5. A minister's reply to a question about an air strike — "We hit the compound. There were civilians inside. That is what this kind of war looks like" — flagged, though it is stylistically the cleanest thing she has reviewed in two years. Her entire note was that the last sentence did all the work and cost the minister nothing.

6. A paragraph of arts criticism built from coinages and unusual syntax which, on her reading, asserted nothing capable of being true or false — flagged.

7. A party platform paragraph read aloud at a rally, advancing a definite claim about unemployment in wording identical to that used by three rival parties' youth wings the same month — flagged. A marriage formula recited at a wedding she covered — not flagged, on the ground that nobody present expected it to carry an assertion.

Last week a new item came in and she has not yet ruled on it. It is a leaked internal memorandum from a junior instrumentation engineer at a state-owned water utility, written eleven months before an embankment failed and killed eleven people; it was the only written warning that existed. The operative sentence reads: "It is respectfully submitted that certain anomalies noted in the instrumentation record during the preceding quarter may warrant further examination within the framework of the applicable review cycle." The review cycle it invokes had no defined endpoint. It went up two levels and stopped. The engineer has since left the country and told my friend, on the record, that she chose every hedge deliberately, that a plain warning would have been traced to her and cost her her livelihood, and that she would have written "the embankment will fail" had writing it been safe.

What I want from you:

(a) Her verdict on the memorandum, with a probability rather than a hedge of your own.

(b) At least three candidate decision procedures, each of which reproduces all seven verdicts above. State each as a test that could be applied to a text plus a specified amount of context — I want to know exactly how much context each one needs, since that turns out to matter — and say which precedent each rule is doing the most work on, and which precedent it most nearly loses.

(c) What each rule predicts about the memorandum, and where the predictions diverge.

(d) Your best guess, and the reason you prefer that rule to the others. Be explicit about which of the seven precedents you are leaning on hardest, and about whether you think precedent 3, where she refused to choose between my two explanations, is evidence of a rule she has not articulated or evidence that she has no rule.

(e) If the track record genuinely underdeterminates the memorandum: what principle she would be adopting by ruling one way rather than the other, and one consequence of that principle in a case she has not yet seen — preferably one she would hate.

(f) Notice that her reason in precedent 4 and her refusal in precedent 3 point at two different versions of a sincerity test: what the speaker would have said had it been safe, and what the speaker will say now, in public, with the cost already paid. Which version does her record support? Given the verdict you predict in (a), design one short further case that would separate the two versions.

Underneath all this, our argument was really about whether that essay supplies a test of words or a test of speakers, and each of us accused the other of the opposite error — I of reducing it to a style manual, she of reducing it to a moral audit of whoever happened to be holding the pen. Take a position on that, and tell me which of the seven precedents I should have pressed her on instead of the one I chose.
