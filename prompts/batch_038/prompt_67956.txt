I am putting together a session for a graduate bioethics seminar on the ethics of selecting and designing future people, and I have assigned Ishiguro's Never Let Me Go (2005) alongside the usual argument set. I do not want the reading the novel normally gets in these settings, which treats it as a story about cloning, commodification, and the repugnance of harvesting organs. I want it read against one quite specific strand of the liberal eugenics debate, and I want the reading to be rigorous enough that I can build a seminar discussion out of it.

The strand is this. Habermas's objection to liberal eugenics is not that designed people would lead worse lives, or that enhancement is unfair to those who cannot afford it. It is that an irreversible prenatal programming of a person's characteristics destroys the symmetrical relations that free and equal persons presuppose: the programmed person cannot regard themselves as the undivided author of their own life, and so cannot meet the person who programmed them as an equal in moral and legal discourse. Set against that the actual situation of Ishiguro's students, which strikes me as philosophically interesting precisely because it does not fit. They are not designed; they are copied, and the novel is conspicuously vague about whether anything about them was selected for at all. What is fixed in advance is not a trait but a whole biography: a vocation, a schedule of donations, and an age at death. So I want to know whether Habermas's objection survives that transposition. Is assigning a purpose to a person a stronger or a weaker version of programming their capacities, and is the difference one of degree or of kind? Does the asymmetry that bothers Habermas even obtain here, given that no one in the novel is identifiable as the students' designer — the guardians are their advocates, and the beneficiaries are anonymous? If the objection depends on there being a programmer to resent, does the novel show us a case where the wrong is fully present but the structure Habermas uses to diagnose it is missing, and what should replace it: recognition, instrumentalisation, something about who gets to tell a life story?

Second, the non-identity dimension. Hailsham is presented by its own founders as a harm-reduction project: the alternative was worse, the students would not otherwise have existed, and on the whole they had a good childhood. Kathy's calm seems partly to endorse that accounting. Does the novel side with the better-off-existing reasoning my seminar usually rejects, and can a Habermasian explain what is unsatisfying about that reasoning without appealing to a comparison between existing and never existing that there is no way to make? Relatedly: is Kathy's acceptance of her fate the cultivated second nature that the eugenics debate worries about, or is it a distinct phenomenon — adaptive preference formation, or a quietism the students were trained into at Hailsham itself, as the scene where Miss Lucy is removed for telling them the truth suggests? I want to know which description is more honest, and whether the enhancement literature simply lacks the vocabulary for the second one.

Third, the Gallery. The art was collected to prove to the outside world that the students had souls. Say what the novel thinks about a moral-status regime in which the created must furnish evidence of interiority before being admitted to the moral community, about the fact that the evidence on offer is aesthetic rather than rational, and about the students' apparent willingness to be judged by it. Who bears the burden of proof in that economy, and does the novel ever question it?

One hedge. My recollection is that the scandal which destroys the Hailsham experiment involves a scientist supplying genetically enhanced children to wealthy parents, so that what kills the humane-treatment movement is public revulsion at commercial enhancement rather than at organ harvesting. If I have that wrong, tell me plainly and set the point aside. If it is right, I want to know what the novel makes of the substitution — whether it implies that the line between tolerable and intolerable interference in human reproduction is drawn by disgust, money, and timing rather than by anything morally real — and whether the 2010 film's more explicit handling changes any of that.

Finally, tell me where this reading strains. The novel is a memoir of affection and loss narrated by someone who never moralises, and the things it dwells on are a cassette tape, a stranded boat, and a field in Norfolk where lost things collect. I would rather be told where the lens distorts the book and what it has to leave out than have the book flattened into a case study. End by saying what the novel shows that the arguments do not — the feature that ought to make a Habermasian revise something, rather than the reverse.

Please do not quote from the novel; describe scenes and narrate them as you remember them, and I would rather have an accurate paraphrase than a misremembered passage. No bibliography and no further reading — I want the reading itself. Write it as continuous prose throughout, with no headings, no bullet points, no bold, and no tables: the way one would write a letter.
