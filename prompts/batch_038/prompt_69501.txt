I keep coming back to this wasp thing, the one where the wasp drags its paralyzed prey to the burrow mouth, goes in to check, and when you shift the prey a few inches it comes back out and starts the whole routine over from the top, forever. Someone apparently coined a word for that flavor of behavior and I've been sort of collecting examples ever since, plus a rule for sorting them, and the rule isn't holding up. Here's my list, in no order:

- My commute. Identical route, identical lane, even when the traffic app is showing green somewhere else. But if the bridge closed I'd reroute in about four seconds. I don't want to call this the wasp.
- My mum and her neighbour doing the same "mustn't grumble" exchange word for word, which they've kept up unchanged since the neighbour's husband died in spring. This one I do call waspish, and I think that's partly a moral failing, not just a cognitive one.
- A friend reading his father's eulogy off a card, word for word, unable to get through it without stopping. Not waspish at all. If anything the opposite.
- A surgeon I know who runs the same checklist every single time, no exceptions, never varies it. Not waspish — I'd call it the best thing about her.
- A chess player rattling off fifteen moves of memorized theory: fine, that's craft. The same player still rattling two moves later, after the opponent deviated into something he'd spot as bad on sight if he looked up: wasp.
- A call centre agent who's clearly excellent at de-escalation, adapting her tone and pace to the caller in real time, genuinely getting him calmer — but everything she does is inside the trained range, and when a caller says something off-menu she loops back to the beginning. Wasp. Even though she's the most visibly flexible person on this list.
- A judge whose sentences swing with his mood and how long the hearing's been running. Flexible, and somehow worse than the surgeon's checklist, though I can't say why.
- Any chatbot. No idea, which I think is the point of half my examples.

My working rule was: it's waspish if you'd keep doing it after learning it no longer accomplishes anything. That seems to break immediately. The surgeon would keep running the checklist even if you showed her a study saying it saves nothing — by my rule she's the wasp, and I flatly refuse that verdict. The call centre agent would drop her script the moment she was told it was making things worse — by my rule she's clean, and I also refuse that one.

So: can you give me a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without squinting? I want to see which case forces each clause into existence, what it says about the two I can't reconcile (the eulogy and the call centre agent are both scripted; one is rigid and one is fluid, and my verdicts run backwards from that), and be straight with me about which of my judgements it simply can't save.

Then two things I havent listed. Someone who deliberately installs loops in themselves — gym bag always packed by the door, phone charging in the other room, same lunch every day to save decision-making for things that matter: is that freedom being exercised or freedom being handed over? And underneath all of it, does any of this touch free will? Is being free just the ability to break the loop on demand, or does it matter how the loop got installed in the first place? Whichever way your principle lands, tell me what it costs.
