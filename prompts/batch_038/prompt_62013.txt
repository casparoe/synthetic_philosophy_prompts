Six months ago my first monograph appeared. Its preface carried the usual sentence conceding that a book of that length would contain errors and that any which remained were mine alone. In the spring a reviewer found one — a genuinely bad inference in the fourth chapter — and closed the review by observing that, on my own confession, I had known before the book went to press that it contained mistakes, so its publication was a failure of epistemic responsibility rather than a stroke of bad luck. That sentence has lodged in me far more firmly than the error did. It has also driven the paper I am presenting at a departmental colloquium in three weeks, and I would like to be prepared for the discussion period.

The paper defends what I call the settled-premise view of belief. Its steps are these, and I want the objections aimed at them rather than at some neighboring position:

1. To believe that p is not to assign p a credence above some fixed threshold. It is to have p available as a settled premise — a proposition treated as closed for further deliberation — within the reasoning context one is actually in.
2. Premise-availability does not aggregate. Nothing about a context licenses conjoining arbitrarily many settled premises into a new settled premise, because the conjunction is almost never a premise any real stretch of reasoning needs. Commitment tracks the premises in play, not their logical closure.
3. So my preface sentence was not a confession of contradictory belief. Each chapter's claims were settled for the purposes of writing the next chapter; the conjunction of the whole book's claims was never a premise in any reasoning I performed. I was not committed to a contradiction, because I never held the two conjuncts in a single reasoning context.
4. The lottery is different, and this is where the view does real work. A stability condition is required: a proposition counts as settled only if it remains available under conditionalization on any admissible refinement of the context. "This ticket will lose" fails, because the natural refinement — that the winner is among the tickets actually in the draw — destroys it. The claims in my book survive the refinements that matter for writing and revising it. The upshot is that the threshold theory of belief is false, and the lottery paradox is what shows it, while the preface paradox is no paradox at all.

The room will be about twenty-five people: mostly epistemologists who think natively in credences and will want a formal statement of "admissible refinement"; one person who works directly on lotteries and has strong priors about what a solution must look like; a knowledge-first type who will press on assertion rather than belief; a philosopher of science who cares about how modeling programs actually run and is impatient with idealized agents; and several graduate students, one of whom has just absorbed the literature on stability theories of belief and will be alert to the charge that I have reinvented a view with known problems. Nobody in the room is a psychologist, and no one will be interested in the pop-rationalist framing of these puzzles.

Please write my preparation sheet. I want roughly eight objections — the ones this room will actually raise, not the ones a general audience or an online forum would raise — ordered by how much damage each would do to the paper if I handled it badly. For each: phrase it the way the person raising it would phrase it, so that I can recognize it mid-sentence; give me a reply I can deliver in about ninety seconds of speaking; add one sentence naming where that reply is softest and what I would have to concede to hold it; and mark any pair of objections that are really the same objection in different clothes, so that I can dispatch both with a single answer. Then tell me two things I should concede in my opening five minutes to take the wind out of the room, and identify the one question I am hoping nobody asks — the one whose force I cannot currently see my way around — together with whatever fallback answer I should have ready in case it comes anyway.

Do not give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list; everything needed is above, and I would rather you reason from the view as I have stated it than gesture at the literature. Finally, and importantly: tell me how confident you are that the settled-premise view as I have described it is internally coherent, and rate your confidence in each of your diagnoses of where my replies are soft. Single out explicitly the one point in the whole sheet about which you are least sure — the objection you suspect you may have invented rather than predicted, or the reply you think may be worse than you have represented it as being. I would rather hear that plainly than receive a uniformly confident document.
