I am in the final year of a philosophy PhD, and the second chapter of my dissertation defends a view I have been calling lexical threshold negative utilitarianism. The rough shape is this: suffering has an intensity threshold τ, and bad states at or above τ are lexically prior — no quantity of positive experience, however intense, however long, and however widely distributed, can outweigh any amount of above-threshold suffering. Below τ, by contrast, suffering and pleasure are commensurable on a single scale at some finite exchange rate, so mild and moderate bads can be compensated by goods in the ordinary way. I adopted the threshold partly to block the familiar objection that a purely negative view licenses painlessly extinguishing all sentient life in order to eliminate suffering, and partly because I want to insist that factory farming is not a matter of taste or economic efficiency. My advisor read the draft and told me, mildly but unmistakably, that my case verdicts are unsystematic and that the threshold may be doing nothing except marking where my own sympathies run out. The chapter goes to a workshop in three weeks and I would like to walk in knowing whether there is a principle underneath what I actually believe.

Here are my verdicts, as I report them in the draft. A world containing one person who endures an hour of above-threshold agony and then a hundred thousand years of the most intense joy available to any creature: worse than a world containing no sentient life at all; the joy never gets a hearing. One person's hour of above-threshold agony alongside a billion lives of supreme joy: also bad on balance, and something we ought not bring about if we can help it; other people's happiness does not compensate the sufferer. But a single person who at thirty endures an hour of above-threshold agony during a life-saving operation and then lives sixty more years of a life she describes as wonderful and would not trade: that life is worth living, and better than her never having existed — I am unwilling to condemn it, and her own retrospective endorsement seems to matter. A billion people each carrying a chronic ache for a decade at an intensity just below τ, in otherwise good lives: a genuine moral catastrophe, and in my gut worse than the single hour of above-threshold agony in the first case. One minute just below τ followed by an otherwise wonderful life: fine to bring about. One minute just above τ followed by the very same wonderful life: not fine — though I confess the pair feels arbitrary, and I cannot explain why a hair's difference in intensity should change the verdict so completely. A choice between one person at intensity ten (τ is five) for an hour and ten people each at intensity one for the same hour: dividing it is much better — which worries me, because it suggests a lexical evil can be laundered by spreading it thin enough to slip under the line. Painless extinction of all sentient life to spare everyone a lifetime of pinpricks and mild disappointment: clearly wrong; a world of modest pleasures and minor discomforts is better than an empty one. Nonhuman animals: τ must be drawn by the felt character of the state rather than by species, verbal report, or neuroanatomy, and I think a great deal of industrial animal production sits above it, which commits me to saying that no amount of human culinary pleasure could offset it — a commitment I accept. There is also a hypothesis I keep circling and have not put in the draft: that the threshold is an artifact, and what is really going on is that pleasure is capped at some finite maximum while suffering is unbounded above, so that for any amount of joy there is some intensity of agony that outweighs it, producing threshold-like behaviour without any genuine discontinuity. And the case I cannot settle: is an eighty-year life of dull, sub-threshold discomfort interspersed with modest pleasures better or worse than a two-year life containing one hour above τ and otherwise sublime joy? I have changed my mind about it four times.

What I would like is a principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough that I could tell whether my view is coherent — the ordering rule for above-threshold states, the aggregation rule for sub-threshold ones, and an explicit account of what τ is a threshold of. For each candidate, please say which of my verdicts motivates each clause, so I can see what work each clause is doing, and which of my verdicts the candidate cannot accommodate. In particular I want to know what to make of the two collisions I can see: the intrapersonal surgery case against the interpersonal billion-lives case, and the billion chronic aches against the single hour of agony. If the way to save both is to say that lexicality governs tradeoffs between persons only, while tradeoffs within a life run on the finite exchange rate, please assess that move honestly, including what it costs and whether personal identity can bear that much normative weight; and if instead the simpler thing is to give up lexicality altogether in favour of a steeply convex weighting of intensity, or in favour of the capped-pleasure and unbounded-suffering hypothesis, say so and tell me which verdict I would have to withdraw. Please also say whether that last hypothesis is a real alternative to a threshold or a threshold wearing a different hat. Finally, give me the resulting view's verdicts on three cases I have not listed: a choice between a small number of people above τ and a vastly larger number just below it; a case where above-threshold suffering is certain to occur either way and the only question is whether to concentrate it in one person or divide it among many; and a case where the sub-threshold suffering is not spread across a billion contemporaries but accumulated in a single life over a very long duration. And if the answer is that my position collapses into ordinary aggregative utilitarianism with a nonlinear badness function, I would rather hear that now than at the workshop.
