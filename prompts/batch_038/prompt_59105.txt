I am working through a question about first-person survival in a many-worlds setting, and I would like help getting my various intuitions into a single account. Please grant the following stipulations rather than contesting them: Everettian quantum mechanics is true, the universal wavefunction never collapses, branch weights are given by the Born rule, persons are physical systems whose futures are spread over many branches, and decoherence makes branching effectively continuous and ubiquitous, so that every macroscopically distinct outcome with nonzero weight is populated. Where a device below is idealized, idealize it further in whatever direction makes it coherent; I care about the structure of the cases, not the engineering. And please do not recommend readings or cite the literature — I want the argument worked out, not located.

Here are my verdicts.

1. **One round.** A man builds a device that measures a spin prepared so that the two outcomes have equal weight; one outcome fires a painless, instantaneous, total killer, the other does nothing. He presses the button once. *Verdict:* he does not experience the trigger firing. From where he sits afterward, nothing unusual happened. I am confident of this.

2. **Twenty rounds.** The same device, fired twenty times in succession; the branches in which he is alive at the end have total weight about one in a million. *Verdict:* he is still there at the end, exactly as in case 1. But I notice that I hold this verdict noticeably less firmly than I hold case 1, and I cannot find any structural difference between them that would justify the difference in confidence.

3. **Ordinary mortality.** A man of fifty-five has a heart attack; the clinical mortality figure is about one in three. Among the branches are ones in which he recovers, including ones in which he recovers against long odds. *Verdict:* he is not thereby guaranteed to recover; his family would be right to expect a funeral and right to grieve. Yet there are people with his memories sitting in those recovery branches, so I cannot say what it is I am denying.

4. **Anaesthesia.** I am about to undergo a routine operation carrying a small but nonzero risk of death on the table. *Verdict:* I will wake up or I will not, and the bare existence of waking branches gives me no reason at all to expect to be in one. As far as I can tell, the physical structure here is the same as in case 1.

5. **Degraded survival.** A three-outcome device: weight 0.5 on nothing happening; weight 0.49 on his surviving with global amnesia and the loss of most of his cognitive capacities; weight 0.01 on instantaneous death. *Verdict:* I would refuse this, and it is worse than case 1 — even though the total weight of "someone psychologically continuous with him goes on" is 0.99 here and only 0.5 there.

6. **Weight-preserving fission.** A device that, on triggering, redistributes the weight of the single continuing him across a thousand continuations, each with one thousandth of the original weight, each awake, healthy, and psychologically continuous with him. *Verdict:* nothing bad happens. Each of them is him in every sense I care about, and none of them should dread being the low-weight one, since from the inside none of them is low-weight. Set this against case 5, where low weight and dread seem to belong together.

7. **The drain.** A device fires ten thousand times over a year, each firing carrying a tiny chance of killing him; suppose the branch in which he survives all of them has total weight about one part in 10^30, and he is sitting in it, healthy, remembering everything, noticing nothing. *Verdict:* nothing has gotten worse for him. And yet I want to say that he has faded, and I cannot say what that would mean or what could make it true.

8. **The inert device.** He runs case 1, and afterward learns that the trigger had been secretly disconnected and the apparatus was inert throughout. *Verdict:* his terror beforehand was appropriate and his relief afterward is appropriate, and nothing about his first-person prospects differed from the live case. But I also think the live case is genuinely different from the inert one, and I cannot locate the difference in anything he could have known or in anything about his future experiences.

9. **The observer.** His friend watches case 2 from outside the room. *Verdict:* the friend should have expected a corpse, and the man at the end of the twentieth round should have expected himself. I am willing to assert both, and I do not think they contradict each other — but I cannot say what makes them compatible rather than merely unobjectionable.

My tentative principle is this: *anticipation is self-location, and one ought to distribute one's expectation over one's future branches in proportion to their weights. Because no continuing branch has weight exactly zero, one never encounters one's own death; but because the weight can be arbitrarily small, that is not immortality, only the observation that there is always somewhere to be.* The principle is attractive because it seems to give the same verdict for cases 1 and 8, and to explain case 9 as a difference between self-locating and third-person evidence. But it appears to predict for case 7 that he should expect to be among the dead, since they carry essentially all the weight — which flatly contradicts my verdict there — and it seems silent on why case 5 should be worse than case 1 when case 5 has more continuing weight.

Please deliver the following.

(a) One or two candidate principles, each stated in two or three sentences before you unpack it, precise enough to be applied mechanically. For every clause, say which of the cases above motivates it and what it is doing there.

(b) An explicit account of the pairs I have flagged as hard to reconcile — 1 against 2, 1 against 4, 5 against 6, and 1 against 8. If your principle dissolves a pair, explain what I was conflating; if it refuses to dissolve one, say which member it discards.

(c) Your principle's verdicts on at least three further cases that you construct, of which one must be the case you regard as most damaging to your own account, together with your judgment about whether it survives the damage.

(d) A frank list of which of my nine verdicts your principle cannot accommodate. Where you think I should give one up, say what it costs me to do so. And tell me directly whether you would rather have a baroque principle that saves all nine or a spare one that overturns two of them, and why — I have no prior commitment either way, but I want the trade-off stated rather than dodged.
