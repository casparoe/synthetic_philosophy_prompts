I build the plumbing under elections for a living — voter registration, tally servers, the cryptographic audit trails that let an outside observer verify a result without having to trust the operator. I've never taken a philosophy course. I sit on my company's Public Trust Panel, six of us: three engineers, two lawyers, and a former election commissioner who chairs it. Our charter says that no bid goes out where the client is not a national electoral authority, or where a bid could collide with an existing contract, without a written recommendation from the panel. The panel meets in nine days and I've been asked to circulate that recommendation beforehand, so I need your help writing it.

The case, as far as I can state it neutrally. A regional government inside a democratic state has tendered for our full stack — registration, ballot delivery, tally, and a signed, attested statement of the result — for a referendum on independence. The central government will not consent to it and the constitutional court is widely expected to suspend it. The region says that if the binding referendum is blocked it will run the same ballot anyway as a consultative exercise with no legal force, and it wants our attestation regardless, because it intends to annex our signed statement of the result, plus live turnout figures, to a declaration before its own parliament. The proposed franchise is residency-based: anyone resident in the region for twelve months and aged sixteen or over, including foreign nationals, while people born in the region who now live elsewhere in the state are excluded. One sub-region inside it, whose councils have formally said they would seek to remain in the state, is folded into the aggregate count. The region is offering unrestricted international observation and full end-to-end verifiability, and argues its vote will be more auditable than plenty of authorized ones. We also hold a multi-year contract with the central state's electoral commission, whose general counsel has told us informally that a vendor supporting the regional vote will not be shortlisted again.

For grounding, since I don't trust my own recall: the 2014 Scottish referendum was authorized by agreement between the devolved and UK governments, ran on a residency franchise that enfranchised sixteen- and seventeen-year-olds living in Scotland while excluding Scots living in the rest of the UK, and asked simply "Should Scotland be an independent country?"; Canada's highest court held in 1998 that a clear majority on a clear question would create a political obligation on the rest of the federation to negotiate, and Parliament then legislated in 2000 to let the federal House of Commons judge after the fact whether the question and the majority were clear; and in Catalonia in 2014 a planned consultation was suspended by the constitutional court, rebranded as a non-binding participation process, and run anyway. Please treat my region as a composite and don't invent further facts about it.

What I can do myself is the legal and commercial exposure analysis, and counsel already has. Assume the vote is probably unlawful under the state's constitution and assume our central contract is genuinely at risk — those are inputs, not conclusions. What I can't write is the normative reasoning, and that's the part I need: whether stripping the ballot of legal force changes what we would be doing, given that the number is what the declaration will rest on; whether the regional government is entitled to define who counts as the voting self, and if it isn't, what standing a vendor has to make that an issue; whether the absence of any grievance matters at all here, given that the region's citizens are treated as full equals, which seems to turn on whether leaving requires a serious standing wrong or whether a territorially concentrated group may simply choose to go, and what whichever of those positions the memo relies on implies for the sub-region that wants to stay; whether our refusal is neutral, or is itself a way of taking the central government's side by making the vote harder to run; and whether helping a group convert the threat of exit into bargaining leverage degrades the polity for everyone else in a way that is our business rather than the state's.

Draft the memo I'll circulate. It should recommend one course — decline, bid on the full scope, or bid on a narrowed scope with the carve-outs stated specifically enough that procurement could act on them, for instance whether the signed attestation is in or out — and it should say so in the opening sentences rather than building to it, with a timeline keyed to the nine days and the five weeks until the tender closes. It should give the strongest honest case for the option you reject, not a straw man. Explain any term of art the first time you use it, since three of us will be reading it cold, and don't hand me citations, a reading list, or invented authorities — I want the argument, and I can't check references anyway. Seven hundred to a thousand words. And please no headings, bullet points, numbered lists, bold, or tables: continuous prose only, the way you'd write a careful letter to a colleague. One recommendation, not a menu of considerations.
