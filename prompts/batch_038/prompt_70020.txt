I am in the second term of a philosophy-of-mathematics seminar, and we have just spent three weeks on intuitionistic logic. Away from the syllabus I have been reading a trade book on proof and certainty in mathematics, whose chapter on Brouwer opens with this passage:

"For Brouwer, mathematics begins in an activity of the mind that comes before language, and a theorem is true only once someone has carried out the proof of it; an object exists only once it has been constructed. That is why he rejected the law of excluded middle. For a question nobody has yet answered, there is nothing that makes one side of it correct, so the statement is neither true nor false, and a logic that means to be honest about this has to make room for a third possibility. Two consequences follow in practice: the intuitionist will not accept a proof by contradiction, and she will not treat an infinite collection as a finished thing over which she can quantify. What she gains is a discipline with a payoff the classical mathematician cannot match. If she proves A or B, you may ask her which, and she must be able to tell you. If she proves that something satisfying a condition exists, you may ask her for it, and she must hand you a way of computing it. It is no accident that constructivism outlived its marginalization in the computer, and Gödel pointed the way when he showed that arithmetic contains sentences that are true yet unprovable — truth outrunning proof, just as Brouwer insisted. In the end, though, intuitionism is classical mathematics under a self-denying ordinance, and the working mathematician's verdict — fewer theorems, no gain — is not easy to answer."

My instinct is that this is a better summary than most I have encountered, and one thing in it struck me as matching what our lecturer said: that from an intuitionistic proof of a disjunction you can always recover which disjunct was proved. But I also remember her saying flatly, without explanation, that intuitionistic logic is not a three-valued logic, and I cannot tell how much of the passage that remark is aimed at. I would like a careful audit of the passage, not a bibliography — I am not looking for sources, just the reasoning, since I want to use it in a short paper.

Could you go through it sentence by sentence and sort its claims into three piles: what an intuitionist or constructive mathematician would sign off on, what they would accept as a loose but forgivable way of putting things, and what they would deny outright? Where a claim belongs in the third pile, please say what the correct claim is rather than merely flagging the error.

Four points I would particularly like you to settle:

1. Does rejecting the law of excluded middle commit the intuitionist to undecidable statements having a third value, or to there being no fact of the matter? If not, what precisely is she refusing, and what does she retain? I have a vague memory that some double-negated form of excluded middle still goes through intuitionistically, and I cannot see how that is consistent with the gloss in the passage.

2. The blanket statement about proofs by contradiction seems too strong to me, but I cannot locate the boundary. Where exactly does it fall, and which familiar patterns of argument remain legitimate?

3. The passage slides from "you may demand a construction" to "you will be given a way of computing it." Is that slide licensed, or does its legitimacy depend on which strand of constructive mathematics one has in mind? If the passage is conflating different positions under the single label "constructivism," please say how they differ.

4. The appeal to Gödel. Does his incompleteness result support the intuitionist, embarrass him, or is it simply beside the point?

Finally, even if the earlier claims need repair, does the closing verdict survive — that intuitionism amounts to classical mathematics proving less for no gain? I have in mind whatever can be said about translating classical results into intuitionistic frameworks, and about intuitionistic logic serving as the internal logic of structures that classical mathematicians study for their own sake. And please tell me which single claim in the passage does the most damage, in the sense that correcting it would force the author to rewrite most of the rest.
