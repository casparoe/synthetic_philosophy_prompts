I serve on the board of a mid-sized development funder. In six weeks we must decide whether to renew an eleven-year governance programme in our largest partner country, a lower-middle-income state where we fund courts, land registries, procurement oversight and municipal budgeting. The programme absorbs about a quarter of our country envelope. The board is split three ways, and the split is not about whether the country has improved over the period. It has, appreciably. It is about what we are entitled to attribute that improvement to, and hence whether renewal amounts to a bet on a causal claim we cannot test.

Here is the evidence situation, as precisely as I can state it. Our evaluation unit's final report compares 84 districts. Thirty-one received the programme in two waves, in 2014 and 2018; the remainder did not. Using a difference-in-differences design with matched controls, the report attributes most of the measured gain in firm formation and tax compliance to the programme. The matching variables include pre-existing administrative capacity, distance to the nearest deep-water port, and whether the district lies in the coastal lowland or the interior highland. Two members object. The first is an economist who says the matching variables are downstream of the very thing under test: administrative capacity is partly a product of our own earlier interventions and of the same historical processes the report appeals to, so the design conditions on something affected by treatment and the estimates are not identified. The second is a historian who thinks the whole enterprise mistakes a correlation for a story about the past. Her point is that in this country the coastal lowland carried the heavier disease burden, later and thinner European settlement, and a different legal tradition inherited at independence, while the highland had the reverse on all three counts. Institutions, geography and the cultural-legal inheritance are therefore confounded in the historical record in a way no further history will undo, because there is no district, no period and no comparable country in the region where one of the three varied while the others held still. She adds that districts sharing an administrative lineage and a trade route are not independent observations to begin with.

The evaluator's defence is what she calls the two-clocks argument. The ecological variables moved on a slow, essentially pre-modern clock; the institutional variables moved on a fast one, changing sharply at two dated moments, a national judicial reform in 1998 and our own entry in 2014. She holds that because the economic indicators track the fast clock and not the slow one, timing alone identifies the direction of causation, and that a confound otherwise perfect is broken by the fact that the confounders did not move when the outcome moved.

Our funding rules sharpen the problem. The mandate requires that any programme above a spending threshold be justified by demonstrated causal effect established by randomised or credible quasi-experimental evaluation. Governance work cannot plausibly be randomised at the scale we operate, and the quasi-experimental route is exactly what the two objectors are attacking. One trustee therefore proposes that we let the mandate do its work and phase the programme out over three years, on the ground that we would not accept this standard of proof from an applicant and should not accept it from ourselves. Another proposes that we seek a formal waiver, on the ground that a rule which systematically excludes the interventions whose effects are hardest to measure will quietly reshape the whole portfolio towards whatever happens to be countable.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the board a week before the meeting. It should be in British English, no longer than 1,300 words, and it should be a document we can act on rather than a survey of the literature:

- Open with a clear recommendation — renew, renew on specified conditions, phase out, or refer back — and, if it involves a waiver or a change in how we apply the mandate, state exactly what we would be asking the trustees to approve and by when.
- Give the reasoning the recommendation rests on. In particular, tell us what to make of the two-clocks argument: can a difference in the timing of change break a confound that is otherwise perfect, and under what conditions would that inference fail? And tell us whether the economist's objection about matching on administrative capacity is fatal to the report's estimates, to its conclusions, or to neither.
- Address the question the historian's objection really raises: whether a variable nobody can turn — a disease environment, a settlement pattern, a legal tradition inherited at independence — can function as a reason to spend money now, and what follows for a board that must choose between two accounts of the past which the historical record cannot separate.
- Recommend a concrete evidential standard for the renewal period: what we would accept as good causal warrant for a programme that cannot be randomised, stated as criteria a future evaluation could actually be judged against, and including what the programme would have to do differently in design, phasing, data collection, or deliberate use of variation we do not control, so that the next round's question is answerable.
- Treat the strongest case against your recommendation — the trustee who insists we hold ourselves to the standard we impose on applicants — as seriously as it merits, and say what would have to be true for that position to be right and ours wrong.
- Close with three or four sentences I could read aloud in the public session when asked why we continue to fund institutional reform in a country whose history we cannot run an experiment on.

I am not asking for references or a reading list; no one on the board will look anything up, and I would rather the memo argued than cited. Where you need a technical notion, explain it in the sentence in which it appears.
