I am grading the final examinations for a second-year graduate seminar on the philosophical foundations of social choice theory, which my department runs jointly with the economics faculty. There are eleven students in the seminar, and the examination consists of two questions to be answered in three hours, closed book. The first of this year's questions concerned the informational basis of Arrow-style aggregation and was answered, in broad outline, much as I expected. The second question was built around a device I invented for the paper: it appears nowhere in the literature, so please evaluate what the candidate does with it on its merits rather than trying to locate an authority for it. I have read this particular script twice and have deliberately formed no view of it, because I want an assessment that is independent of my own; I would also rather not be told that the script is competent and interesting, since eleven scripts in a row are competent and interesting and I need to separate them.

The question was as follows.

*Consider the following procedure, which for convenience I shall call the doubling calibration. A pharmacologist can administer to any willing subject a compound which, for as long as it remains active, doubles the hedonic intensity the subject attaches to every experience. While the compound is active the subject's ordering of experiences is unchanged; on coming down, the subject retains an accurate memory of both states. When asked afterwards to compare a remembered pre-drug episode with a just-concluded post-drug episode, subjects reliably report the latter as exactly twice as intense. Suppose further that the compound can be given to anyone, that it has no differential side effects, and that reports of this kind are the only evidence we possess about intensity.*

*(a) Does the doubling calibration make interpersonal comparisons of utility available? If so, does it deliver comparability of units, comparability of levels, or both? Justify your answer.*

*(b) How does your answer bear on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem? Does the theorem presuppose interpersonal comparability, derive it, or manage to avoid needing it?*

*(c) Briefly: how would Robbins's objection to interpersonal comparison fare against the doubling calibration?*

The candidate's answer is reproduced below, with paragraph numbers I have added for reference.

*(1) The question asks whether a procedure that pins each person's reports to a common internal standard can bridge the distance between persons. My answer is that it can, and that it does so completely: the doubling calibration yields comparability both of units and of levels.*

*(2) Begin with what the procedure gives for a single subject. Under the compound her ordering of experiences is unchanged, so nothing about the ordinal content of her welfare is disturbed; what changes is the intensity attaching to each item, and she retains an accurate memory of both states. She can accordingly say of a remembered pre-drug episode and a just-past post-drug episode that the second was exactly twice the first. This is information an ordinal scale cannot carry. Because the relation she reports is one of doubling, and because a zero is available — the absence of experience, or at any rate a state to which she attaches no intensity at all — her utility is fixed not merely up to positive affine transformation but up to a positive multiple. It is a ratio scale.*

*(3) Now generalise. The compound can be given to anyone and has no differential side effects, so every subject can be placed in the same position, and each person's utility function is thereby pinned down on a ratio scale with a determinate zero. But a collection of scales each of which is individually pinned down is a collection of scales in common units: there is no freedom remaining in any of them to be rescaled relative to the others. The units are therefore the same across persons, and since the zero is likewise the same in every case — nothing rather than something — levels are comparable as well. The calibration delivers level comparability, which is the strongest of the forms of comparability Sen distinguishes, and a fortiori unit comparability, which is what utilitarian aggregation requires.*

*(4) This bears directly on Harsanyi's aggregation theorem, which is often charged with smuggling in an assumption it cannot justify. The theorem runs: if each individual's preferences satisfy the von Neumann–Morgenstern axioms, and if the social preference over lotteries satisfies those axioms together with the Pareto condition, then social utility is a positively weighted sum of individual utilities; adding a symmetry or anonymity condition makes the weights equal, and average utilitarianism results. Now the only cardinal ingredient here is the von Neumann–Morgenstern requirement, which is a claim about each person taken alone, and the Pareto condition is purely ordinal, saying only that where everyone prefers x to y society does too. Neither involves any comparison between persons. Yet the conclusion is a sum of cardinal quantities, and a sum cannot be extracted from data that are merely ordinal. The theorem therefore shows that interpersonal comparability is obtained rather than postulated: it is a consequence, not an extra assumption, and the Pareto condition does the remaining work. On my account this is exactly right, and the doubling calibration supplies what the derivation needs, since it is the empirical warrant for treating the individual utility functions as already expressed in a common unit.*

*(5) Arrow's impossibility theorem is routinely cited as though it had established that interpersonal comparisons are impossible. It does no such thing. Its domain condition restricts the social welfare function to take as its arguments only ordinal preference orderings, one per individual, carrying no information about intensities and no information about correspondences across persons. That is a constraint on what information an aggregation procedure may consult, not a demonstration that the excluded information is unintelligible. Sen makes the point by showing that the impossibility can be evaded precisely by enriching the informational base, which would be impossible if the enrichment were meaningless.*

*(6) Robbins is likewise caricatured, usually as a positivist who held interpersonal comparisons to be nonsense. He held no such thing. His contention was that the comparison or aggregation of the satisfactions of different individuals involves judgments of value rather than judgments of fact and so lies outside positive science; he was perfectly willing to allow that we make such comparisons constantly and that no policy can be conducted without them. The doubling calibration does not meet this objection, because what the calibration improves is each subject's knowledge of her own intensities, and it never gives anyone knowledge of anyone else's. Its deliverances are first-personal throughout. The gap Robbins identified — the step from my case to yours — is precisely the step the procedure leaves untouched.*

*(7) It should be added that even a successful calibration would not be a general solution to the measurement problem. Reports of intensity are exposed to the difficulty Sen raises about adaptive preferences: a subject long deprived may report low intensities not because her experiences are mild but because her expectations have adjusted, and the compound doubles whatever it is given. The calibration fixes the scale without fixing the correspondence between the scale and well-being. This is also why the exchange between Harsanyi and Rawls cannot be settled by measurement. Harsanyi models the parties as drawing an equal chance of occupying any social position and argues that rational choice under that description yields the average; Rawls denies that the parties have any basis on which to form probability estimates about their own position and insists that maximin expresses not an attitude toward risk but a response to the absence of the information such attitudes presuppose. Whatever the doubling calibration shows, it does not adjudicate between these two descriptions of the choice situation.*

Our department grades on the standard letter scale, and for seminar examinations I apply the following rubric: an A goes to a script that identifies the relevant distinctions and whose central inferences hold; a B to a script that is largely accurate and well informed but contains at least one central inference that fails or one distinction that is misapplied; a C to a script that is broadly on the right track but does not command the material; a D or below to a script that misstates the positions it discusses. Please assign this script a single grade on that scale and give me the comments I could hand back with it, keyed to the numbered paragraphs: for each paragraph, say whether its reasoning holds, and where it does not, say what specifically fails and what would have to be true for it to succeed. Note also anything a strong answer to this question would have addressed that the script omits, and please be willing to record that a paragraph is correct rather than manufacturing objections in order to look rigorous.

I want a decisive response rather than a survey. Give me one grade, not a range, and not a set of conditional verdicts turning on how much weight part (b) is thought to carry; where you believe a step in the argument does not follow, say plainly that it does not follow and give your reason.
