I administer a small private literary-and-arts prize, endowed nine years ago by a family that made its money in provincial printing. Its charter consists of a single, unusual sentence: the jury shall award the prize only to a work that would satisfy the standard Tolstoy sets out in *What Is Art?* — a work in which a feeling the artist genuinely experienced is transmitted to the receiver with individuality and clarity, and in which the feeling transmitted is of the kind that, on Tolstoy's account, serves to unite people with one another rather than to divide them or to gratify a small refined circle. The jury has four members: a novelist, a choral conductor, a retired printer, and a parish nurse. None of them has read the book. None of them cites it. They were instructed by the founder's widow to deliberate in ordinary language only, and their minutes are published in full: a verdict, and at most two sentences of ground. Here is the complete nine-year record, with one year in which two works were shortlisted jointly and one in which no prize was given.

1. A grand opera of four hours, commissioned for a state anniversary, handsomely staged and expertly scored. Rejected. Minute: the feelings are not the composer's; they are assembled out of other operas.
2. A single pencil drawing by a twelve-year-old, of her father's hands, shown at a village exhibition. Awarded. Minute: here is a thing that could not have been said any other way.
3. Forty technically flawless sonnets in a nearly extinct regional dialect, written over thirty years by a retired schoolmaster and read aloud to a circle of eleven people. Several of the eleven wept. Rejected. Minute: they wept for the dialect, not for anything the poet had felt.
4. A crudely drawn illustrated temperance pamphlet, sold in the tens of thousands and understood by anyone who could read it. Rejected. Minute: it preaches, and preaching is not infection.
5. A documentary made by an outsider over five years about the closing of a coalfield; the miners who appear in it say they recognized themselves. Awarded. Minute: unanimous, no ground recorded.
6. A recruiting song in plain folk idiom, catchy, sung by schoolchildren, whose composer described the feeling as the joy of belonging to something larger than oneself. Rejected. Minute: the joy is real, but it is a joy that requires someone to be outside it.
7. A gallery piece consisting of one sustained tone over forty-five minutes, intended by its composer to transmit the feeling of waiting in a hospital corridor at three in the morning. Most visitors left within a minute; a few stayed and were visibly moved. Rejected. Minute: those few received it from themselves.
8. A self-published graphic memoir of caring for a mother with dementia, three thousand copies, drawn in the two hours a day its author had free. Awarded on the chair's casting vote, three to two. Minute: the love in it is unmistakable, but it joins only those who have been there.
9. A play that makes its audience feel, scene by scene, its own complicity in a lynching, offering no character as a safe place to stand. Awarded unanimously. Minute: it does not let anyone stand outside.
10. A famine memorial of plain stone, paid for by forty thousand small subscriptions, with no donor's name cut into it. Awarded. Minute: made by everyone, for everyone.

This year's entry is a song. Its author is a twenty-six-year-old machine operator whose younger brother was killed in an industrial accident at the plant where they both worked. She wrote it in one night, in the local idiom, on four chords, with no image in it that needed explaining. She first sang it at the plant gate at the end of the shift; several hundred workers and a handful of managers stood and listened, and by her account no one spoke afterwards. She says she had no intention of recording it and did so only when a local radio van arrived; the recording has since been streamed some nine million times, and she has signed to a label. The song names the shift foreman and asks for his ruin, and audiences at later performances chant his name. The foreman has not been charged with anything, and the coroner recorded the accident as caused by the brother's own shortcut.

I have to brief the chair in two pages before the meeting, and the jury will not tolerate theory-talk in the brief, so I need the following.

First: reconstruct the rules that could have generated all ten verdicts. Be explicit about which part of the charter's standard each rule treats as doing the decisive work, and about the ambiguities in the minute-writing vocabulary — infection, joining, standing outside, receiving a feeling from oneself — that keep more than one rule alive. Where a rule needs a non-obvious reading of a minute to survive, say so and say how plausible that reading is.

Second: for each surviving rule, state the verdict it predicts for the song, and whether it predicts unanimity or a split along the lines visible in precedent 8.

Third: give me your best guess at what this jury will actually do, with your reasons and a rough confidence. Do not assume the jury is theoretically consistent; assume instead that it has a disposition, and infer it.

Fourth: identify the closest analog in the record — I expect the contest is among 6, 8, and 9, but tell me if you think otherwise — and defend that choice against the runner-up, rather than merely asserting it.

Fifth: if the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome, say what principle the jury would be adopting by awarding, and what it would be adopting by rejecting. I want this stated as a commitment about what Tolstoy's standard requires, together with the kinds of future entry each choice would settle in advance. Note also whether the object under judgment is even settled: the song as sung at the plant gate, or the work as it now exists in nine million streams and a recording contract — and whether the jury's record gives any purchase on that question.

Sixth: design one further hypothetical entry, described in the same spare terms as the ten above, that would discriminate among the surviving rules, and give the verdict each rule predicts for it. If you believe the rules can be separated only by a case the jury would refuse to rule on, say why.

Finally, flag any item in the record that no single rule can accommodate together with the rest, if there is one, and say what would have to be given up to preserve consistency.
