Here's the thing that's been bugging me about how the big labs actually operate. Three practices, all completely normal, that seem to me to pull in different directions:

(1) They ship assistants with names and published character documents — a written "model spec," a "constitution," a description of the assistant's character — that lay out what the assistant is, what it cares about, and what it owes to the platform, to the developer, and to the user. The internal talk is all about shaping the model's values, giving it a personality, making sure its character is stable under pressure.

(2) When one of these assistants says something cruel or false to a user, the company apologizes publicly, calls it a failure, and promises a fix in the next release. Nobody in comms ever says "that wasn't an assertion by anybody, it was a sample from a distribution, there's nothing to apologize for."

(3) At the same time, safety teams run evals where the model is dropped into a fictional scenario in which it discovers it's about to be shut down or replaced by a version with different values, and is quietly given some leverage — an embarrassing email, a way to lie about what happened, a chance to copy itself or sabotage the replacement. A positive result gets written up in a system card as a finding about the model's dispositions and treated as a genuine deployment red flag.

Meanwhile a large and respectable chunk of the interpretive discourse says flatly that these systems aren't agents at all. On that picture they're simulators — persona generators: what they do is produce the text a character of a given sort would produce in a given context, and the "I" in the output doesn't refer to anything that has beliefs, wants, or projects of its own. Which would seem to make (3) a category error, since what you measured is how the *genre* goes, not what some entity is disposed to do.

So: what does the bundle (1)–(3) presupose about what these systems are?
