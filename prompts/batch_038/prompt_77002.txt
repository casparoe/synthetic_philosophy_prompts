Since retiring from a career in X-ray crystallography, I have been auditing a tutorial on the ethics of belief, and the tutor has asked me to mark a batch of the end-of-term papers. The question set was: "Clifford holds that it is wrong to believe on insufficient evidence even when the belief turns out to be true and does no harm. James replies that our passional nature not only lawfully may, but must, decide an option between propositions, whenever it is a genuine option that cannot by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds. Is this a genuine disagreement, and if so, who is right?" One paper reads as follows:

"Clifford's case rests on the shipowner who sends emigrants to sea in a vessel he knows to be old and much-repaired, stifling his doubts until he sincerely believes her seaworthy; she goes down, and he collects the insurance. Clifford concludes that he is guilty of their deaths. The lesson is general: a belief is wrong to hold when the evidence for it is weak, because beliefs tend to produce actions, and the believer owes it to society not to act on a proposition that has not earned its place in his mind. His standard is thus a consequentialist one — proportion belief to evidence in order to minimise the harm that credulous action causes — and that is why he insists that the wrongness survives even when the belief is true and no one is hurt, since the harm in that case lies in the habit of credulity the believer has reinforced in himself and in others.

James's answer is a form of voluntarism: where the evidence runs out, we are free to believe as we wish, and it is sometimes better to do so, since the comforting belief may bring about the very state of affairs it represents. He narrows this licence by the notion of a genuine option, which he takes to mean an option that is undecidable on the available evidence and of great personal consequence; when an option is genuine in that sense, the intellect has no purchase and the will may fill the gap, and indeed must, since James holds that refusing to choose is a choice with consequences of its own.

So the two are not really in conflict. Clifford is telling us how to believe when the evidence is sufficient to settle the question, which is the situation in science, while James is telling us what to do when it is not, which is the situation in religion and in life generally. Each governs a separate domain, and neither need be wrong."

I would be grateful for a grade out of ten together with the specific comments that justify it: what the paper gets right, where it misstates either author, which of its steps do not follow, and whether the concluding division of territory between the two is available to it on the premises it has offered. Please also draft the marginal note I should return to the student, indicating the one or two points on which the paper most needs to be revised.
