Quick favor, and it's a slightly odd one. I'm about three quarters of the way through a novel whose narrator is a semi-retired moral philosopher, and in the next chapter he's at a crowded faculty dinner, lamb on the table, everyone studiously not thinking about it, and he tells a joke. Its the sort of man who tells jokes badly and knows it, which is most of why people like him. I've been staring at the page for a week and everything I write comes out as a groaner, so I'm outsourcing.

The constraint is what makes it hard. It has to land for people who actually know the material, and the material is Nozick on animals — specifically the stretch in Anarchy, State, and Utopia where he floats the two-tier option, treating animals as utilitarian subjects whose happiness you're supposed to maximize while keeping Kantian side constraints for people so that no person ever gets used merely as a means, and then worrying out loud about how badly the two halves sit together. Same neighborhood as his utility-monster worry, the idea that a creature with a vastly greater capacity for satisfaction could morally swallow us whole and we'd be obliged to hand over the knife. So the joke should be structural, not a philosophers-are-nerds gag. My instinct is that the comedy lives in the asymmetry: somebody has to be the Kantian and somebody else gets to be the utilitarian, and the animals never had a vote about which side of the line they landed on. A lion being a perfectly coherent utility maximizer about my thigh while I'm barred from being one about his feels like it should collapse into a punchline, ideally one that also quietly indicts the eleven people at the table eating the lamb.

Practical notes. Tellable in one run, somewhere between 120 and 250 words, with a real setup and a punchline that retroactively changes what the setup meant — I don't want a witticism, I want the little fold-over. It has to work read aloud in a room, so no hedging and no stopping to explain the theory mid-joke; the target is that a reader who's read the book laughs and a reader who hasn't still follows the story and grins. Don't put the name of any book or the word deontology inside the joke itself — he can gesture at the ideas in ordinary language and it'll be funnier that way. And I'd rather you didn't make a real living philosopher the butt; an invented one, or an animal, is fine.

Format-wise: give it to me as one continuous run of prose, the way you'd write it in an email or a letter. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no italics, no tables, no bracketed stage directions, and don't put quotation marks around the joke — just the words he says. If you want to tell me afterwards why you picked the punchline you picked, do it in two or three plain sentences right below, same unformatted prose. I wouldnt mind a second, shorter version either if one occurs to you, but again as prose, not as a list.
