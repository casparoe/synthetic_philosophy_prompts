I run a small independent podcast on uncertainty and money — a few hundred regular listeners, mostly tradespeople, amateur investors, and card players. I have no formal training in economics or physics; I have spent the better part of two years working through this material on my own, largely with a spreadsheet. Next month's guest is an economist with a physics background who argues that the standard expected-utility framework should be set aside in favour of an account built on how wealth actually evolves over time: that what matters for a person is the long-run growth rate of their own wealth rather than an average across imagined parallel versions of themselves; that the Kelly criterion is not one betting strategy among many but the growth-optimal rule; and that utility functions are redundant, because the correct transformation of the variable is fixed by the dynamics of the situation rather than by the temperament of the person.

Please draft nine interview questions for the hour, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer I think they are most likely to give. Arrange them as a plausible conversational arc: the first should let them state the view in their own terms for an untrained audience, and the pressure should build from there. Include one question I expect them to enjoy answering — I want to be a fair host, not a prosecutor.

The following must each get at least one question:

- The charge that maximizing the growth rate of wealth is simply maximizing expected utility with a logarithmic utility function, so that nothing has been gained except a new name for the logarithm.
- Their own framework's treatment of situations where payoffs are added rather than multiplied. If the growth-optimal rule there reduces to maximizing expected value, does the theory really instruct someone short on rent to take a small positive-average gamble?
- The one-shot case. They will reply that no decision is ever truly one-shot, since a life is a sequence. Build a run of two or three questions that makes that reply progressively harder to rest on, ending with the point that the same bet looks different to a person of twenty-five and a person of ninety.
- Unknown odds. Kelly assumes you know the probabilities; practitioners bet a fraction of it. Where does the fraction come from, if not from a judgement about one's own ignorance — which begins to look like the very risk aversion the theory calls an illusion?
- Several concurrent bets and the correlations between them, and whether the framework has anything to say about diversification beyond adding growth rates.
- People who insure small risks that cannot ruin them. Is the theory simply committed to saying they are confused?
- In a multiplicative game wealth never actually reaches zero, only tends toward it. Is the claim that the typical path collapses a statement about a limit no real person ever arrives at?
- What observation would count against the view, given that it largely reinterprets behaviour rather than predicting new behaviour?

Work my own example into at least one question: a game that raises a stake by 60% or cuts it by 40%, each with equal likelihood. The average change is up 10% a round, yet when I run it out the typical path falls by roughly 2% a round, and the doubling-or-halving version is exactly neutral over time despite an average gain of 25%. I want a question that makes them explain that to a listener who has never seen a logarithm.

Every question must carry its own premise in plain language and unpack any technical term it uses. No generic questions about their career, their influences, or what surprised them along the way.
