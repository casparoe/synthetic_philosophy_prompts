I spent thirty-three years directing a radiocarbon laboratory, and eighteen months ago I retired, enrolled in an MA in philosophy, and found myself unable to stop thinking about a problem that my trade handled without ever examining. This autumn I am co-teaching an interdisciplinary seminar on memory and the historical record — final-year undergraduates and MA students together — and I have been asked to set the final paper. I should like your help drafting one essay question, its model answer, and its marking scheme.

The topic is the conflict between a community's memory and the documentary record: when the two disagree, which gives way, and on what principle? I want a single essay question, sat in ninety minutes, worth twenty-five marks, with about twelve hundred words expected. It must not be answerable by reciting the opposition between history and memory, or by summarising Halbwachs, though the seminar has read him; the difficulty should come entirely from adjudicating two cases that pull in opposite directions.

The first case is a composite monument. A river-valley town keeps a memorial on the bank, erected in the nineteen-thirties by the millworkers' benefit society, whose inscription records that eleven named men of the parish drowned in a great flood in March 1908. The municipal burial register and the parish register show six drownings in a flood in March 1911 and five deaths in a boiler explosion at the mill in October 1908; the eleven names match the eleven dead across both events. The inscription is therefore wrong as to date, as to number, and as to cause: it has fused two catastrophes into one. Local memory supplies a reason for the fusion — the flood is said to have killed so many because the watchman's hut on the weir, burned in the 1908 explosion, was never rebuilt and so no warning was given. The registers say nothing about the hut; a survey of the weir from 1909 lists no hut but does not record whether one stood there before. The council is re-laying the riverbank and asks whether to re-inscribe the stone, to add a second panel setting out the documentary record beside it, or to leave it as it is.

The second case is a silent archive. A hamlet in the same region maintains that soldiers took a group of men away in a certain year and that none returned; the memory is carried by an annual observance and by recorded interviews with people who were children at the time. The state archives contain nothing: the district's records for those years were destroyed by fire, and the men were, on the community's account, taken as unregistered labour. A professional historian writing the standard regional history omits the episode for want of evidence. Some details are demonstrably off — the year is probably three years early, and the number of men exceeds what surviving census fragments allow for the hamlet's adult male population — but the central claim has neither corroboration nor refutation anywhere else.

The question should require candidates to state and defend a single principle capable of deciding both cases, and to say what follows for the inscription and for the regional history. I also want the question to hand candidates a distinction I have been pressing in seminar without resolving: whether commemorative distortion behaves like systematic bias, which is directional and correctable, or like random error, which is simply a reason to distrust the reading. In my former life the discovery that radiocarbon ages diverged from calendar ages in a patterned rather than scattered way did not discredit the method; it produced a calibration. Candidates should be asked whether that way of thinking transfers, where it transfers, and what it costs.

Please deliver: the question as it will appear on the paper, with both case materials polished and self-contained (each under two hundred and fifty words, with invented places, so that no real town is implicated); a model answer of about eight hundred and fifty words; a marking scheme totalling twenty-five marks, distributed over five or six named moves with marks apportioned and one sentence per move on the way candidates typically forfeit it; an explicit statement of the boundary between a first-class and a good second-class answer, naming the moves that separate them; and a short list of responses that should cap the mark. For that last item I have in mind the relativist shrug, on which history and memory are incommensurable discourses neither answerable to the other, and the positivist reflex, on which the archive is simply the facts — but tell me which others you would add.

Three constraints. First, nothing in the question, the model answer, or the scheme may cite, recommend, or presuppose any particular book or article: candidates sit with the case materials alone, and I want a first-class answer reachable by someone who has never encountered the literature. Second, my co-marker is a historian who did not teach the philosophical half of the course, so every move in the scheme must be described in terms of what appears on the page rather than which position the candidate espouses. Third, I have a professional prejudice I should like the question to be able to embarrass: my instinct is that where record and memory conflict, the record wins. I suspect this instinct is an artefact of a career spent with instruments that can be re-run and with events that nobody's identity depends upon. Please construct the question so that a first-class answer may conclude that I am wrong, and the scheme so that it does not quietly reward candidates who flatter me.
