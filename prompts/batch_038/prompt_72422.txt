I sit on the bioethics committee of an academic medical center, and next month we vote on a single referral that has divided us. A surgical team proposes a human head transplant: a thirty-four-year-old man whose degenerative disease will destroy his body within the year but has spared his nervous system, and a brain-dead donor with an otherwise healthy body. We are not being asked whether the operation is scientifically sound — the surgeons have assumed that risk and the family has been counseled. We are being asked a narrower question that has proved much harder: if it succeeds, who is our patient?

Three features make the question operative rather than academic.

First, the surgeons intend to keep the patient's discarded original body perfused and mechanically supported for thirty days, as a fallback should the transplant fail. For that month there will be two bodies in two units of this hospital, one with his brain and one without. His wife has already asked which of them the family should keep vigil at, and whether, if things go badly, that belongs at one, the other, or both.

Second, the man signed an advance directive eighteen months ago, while in his own body. It names his wife as surrogate, refuses prolonged mechanical support, and directs that his remains be interred in his family's plot. No one on the committee can say what each clause now picks out. The donor's mother, whose consent was given on the express condition that a part of her son goes on living, holds that her family retains an interest in the body that walks out of the hospital, and asks for visitation rights and for a voice if the patient becomes unresponsive.

Third, and most unusually: the patient has read Dennett's thought experiment about a brain kept alive and supported in a laboratory while its body, controlled remotely, goes on living an ordinary life, and he raised it himself at his pre-operative interview. He asked us to enter a standing instruction that if, after the operation, he reports seeming to be somewhere other than where his brain is, or reports not recognizing the vantage point from which he is speaking, we are to treat that report as bearing on the question of who survived rather than as a symptom to be medicated away. Two members think this request is entirely reasonable and that we owe him an answer in advance; two think it dereliction to weight first-person testimony on a matter settled by biological fact.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the vote. It should:

1. State a clear recommendation — clear the referral, clear it subject to conditions, or decline — with a timetable: what must be settled before the operation, what is settled at the moment the patient wakes, and what happens on day thirty.
2. Say which body is our patient for each purpose the institution must actually act on — surrogate authority, the scope of the advance directive, visitation, and eventual disposition of remains — and explain why. If the answer differs across those purposes, say so and justify the split rather than papering over it.
3. Give the committee a test for weighing the patient's post-operative testimony, rather than a ruling in advance on whether to believe him.
4. Take the donor family's condition of consent as seriously as the patient's directive, and state what we should do if the two cannot both be honored.
5. Set out the strongest consideration on the side your opinion comes out against, in terms its proponents would accept, and explain why it nonetheless does not carry the decision.

I would rather the memo commit to a defensible position and own its costs than split the difference. Assume readers who are intelligent, have not studied personal identity, and will have to explain this decision to two grieving families.
