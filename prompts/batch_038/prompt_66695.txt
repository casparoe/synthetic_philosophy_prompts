My housemate and I have had the same argument in three different outfits this year: first it was whether she should get the bigger share of the grocery money because she'd been laid off, then whose family makes Thanksgiving more miserable, and most recently who deserves the good room in the new flat. She's a nurse and she keeps saying you cant actually compare how much two different people enjoy or suffer something — that it's a category error, like comparing my height to my weight — and I keep saying obviously you can, we do it constantly and mostly get it right. She told me I'm just assuming my own scale is everybody's scale. That got under my skin, so I sat down and wrote out the cases where I think the comparison clearly works and the ones where I think it clearly doesn't, and the pattern isn't at all what I expected going in.

Here's the list. Headaches: if you and I both have a headache and yours is worse, I think that's just true, and if there's one painkiller left it goes to you, no debate. Ice cream: when my niece and nephew are both eyeing the last slice of cake, I decide by who'd get more out of it, and I think that's a fact about the world and not a figure of speech. Grief: a friend's breakup versus another friend whose dog died — I don't think I can compare those at all, and I'd feel gross even attempting it. My grandmother: she has arthritis and is in pain every single day, but she says she's perfectly content and would rather we spend the money on my cousin's tuition; I don't think her being used to the pain makes the pain count for less, but I also don't want to tell her she's wrong about her own happiness, and I can see those two pull apart. The loud one: in every shared house I've lived in, whoever complains loudest gets the good room, and I'm sure that's wrong, but I honestly can't tell whether I think loud people are lying about how much they want it, or whether wanting-it-loudly just isn't the same thing as getting-more-out-of-it. My daughter: I'd take a migraine on her behalf any day and I don't think that makes me irrational or selfish, but I also don't think my headache is objectively worse than hers, and I don't think I'm allowed to say both of those.

Is there a principle underneath that predicts which comparisons I'm allowing and which I'm refusing? My own guess is something like "you can compare changes but not starting points," which feels like it handles the headaches and maybe the grandmother, but grief seems to blow it up and the daughter case definitely does. So: give me the best one or two candidate principles, say precisely what each one is committed to, run them through every case above including the ones that embarrass me, and be straight about which of my verdicts they can't save — I'd rather be told one of them has to go than have everything fudged into place. And tell me what the principle you land on says about a case I haven't thought of yet, like my friend who is dramatically more expressive than me insisting her one bad day was worse than my bad week. I want to know whether I should call her a better self-reporter, a worse one, or someone who's confusing two different things entirely.
