You are a philosopher of mind who works on the temporal structure of experience and who has taught this material many times to mixed cohorts of scientists and humanities students; please adopt that role and think carefully before answering, beginning with a short diagnosis rather than jumping into a plan. I run one seventy-five-minute session in a third-year interdisciplinary module on the science of consciousness. The cohort of roughly thirty is about a third neuroscience students, a third computer science students, and a third philosophy students; they have had a week on apparent motion and a week on the neural-correlates debate, and they are comfortable with the vocabulary of both, but only the philosophy third has ever been asked what would have to be true for a question to have a determinate answer. I have a whiteboard, a projector, and no lab equipment. The session's topic is the color-phi phenomenon — two differently colored spots flashed in quick succession at nearby locations, such that subjects report a single spot travelling between them and changing color partway along a path on which, in the world, nothing occurs but two static flashes — together with the two ways of explaining that report that Dennett and Kinsbourne label the Orwellian and the Stalinesque: either the subject genuinely undergoes an experience of the first color moving, and the arrival of the second stimulus revises the record of that experience before the report is issued, or the processing of the first stimulus is held open past its usual deadline so that the experience eventually had already contains the mid-path color change. The payoff I want, and the part I have failed to land in both previous years, is why the impossibility of telling those two apart from the subject's own reports is supposed to be more than a curiosity — why it is meant to show that there is no determinate fact about the moment at which the content became conscious, rather than merely a fact that is very hard to establish.

I do not want an explanation of the material, and I do not want any readings, recommended or otherwise; the students get none for this session and I want the plan to work without them. What I want is a teaching plan, and I would like you to reason about the dependency structure before you commit to a sequence. Specifically:

- State first, in a paragraph, what the crux of this material actually is and which pieces genuinely depend on which others — in particular, what a student must already accept before the Orwellian/Stalinesque pair is even intelligible to them, and what must be withheld until after the demo has been run.
- Then give the session as a block-by-block plan with rough timings, and identify the single case that should carry the whole thing. Argue for your choice, and say what that carrying example must have and must lack — whether color phi itself is the right vehicle, or whether a homelier timing case should bear the weight and color phi should be introduced only once the structural point is in hand.
- Tell me where to run the demonstration I have built: a browser animation of the two flashes with adjustable interval and separation, replayable on demand, so the students can watch it live and then watch it again knowing what to look for. I see two failure modes and cannot decide between them. Run it in the first ten minutes and the students' own reports feel to them like direct evidence about the order of their experience, so they arrive at the distinction already committed to a reading on which the report simply transcribes what happened; run it at minute forty and half the room has picked a side from the description alone and spends the demo confirming it. Say which risk is worse, which placement you recommend, and what I should say at that moment to defuse the risk I have accepted.
- Give me a misconception map keyed to specific points in the sequence, with the exact sentence or single question I should use at each point to head it off. Please include at least the four I expect and add any I have missed: the neuroscience students treating the fact that the brain runs on a processing delay as though it settled the dispute in favor of the Stalinesque account, when the delay story is compatible with both; the computer science students proposing a buffer with a flush deadline, which quietly reinstates the very finish line the argument is meant to dissolve; the philosophy students sliding from you-cannot-tell-which to so-the-color-change-is-not-real or so-consciousness-is-an-illusion, which is a different and much stronger claim; and everyone treating the Orwellian case as an unremarkable memory phenomenon, on the grounds that we all accept that memory gets rewritten, thereby missing that what is supposedly rewritten here is an experience and not a belief about one.
- Say what I should openly refuse to explain at this level, and mark the refusal in the plan rather than leaving it implicit. I currently intend to defer the stopping-rule problem, the analogous cutaneous-rabbit and flash-lag cases, and the Libet data to next week; tell me if any of those is load-bearing here and must instead be gestured at honestly, with a sentence I can say out loud that flags the gap without opening it.
- End with one closing question, answerable by a student in two or three sentences on the spot, that separates those who understood the argument from those who memorized the two labels — and describe what a label-only answer sounds like, in enough detail that I can recognize it live rather than in marking.

For context on why I am asking rather than improvising again: last year a student in the front row announced, about twenty minutes in, that the people in the original experiments were obviously confabulating, and the seminar then dissolved for half an hour into whether confabulation is ever detectable, which is adjacent to my topic and completely useless for it. I want to know where in the sequence that remark is most likely to surface and what I should do with it when it does.
