I'm drafting a seminar paper for a course on religious epistemology and theories of punishment, comparing karmic accounts of cosmic justice with retributivist accounts of deserved suffering. My aim was an argument showing that the doctrine of karma, on any of its versions, is not a theory of moral desert at all: that it distributes harm to beings who are not the agents who earned it, and so amounts to a ledger kept at innocents' expense. My advisor read the draft and said it proves too much — that if the reasoning were sound it would also condemn the ordinary practice of punishing someone forty years after the offense, and would leave no room for resentment or gratitude directed at anyone whatsoever. I could not reconstruct her diagnosis from the steps, and I cannot find the flaw on my own either. The argument is this:

(1) For someone to deserve to suffer in virtue of an act, the suffering must fall on the very one who performed the act; desert is a relation between an agent and a harm, and it cannot be transferred to a third party, however closely connected.
(2) Karmic fruition in the rebirth frameworks of the Indian traditions always occurs at some remove from the deed, and the being who suffers it is separated from the actor by death and a new birth.
(3) Across that gap there is no bodily continuity and no continuity of memory, intention, or character; on the Buddhist reading there is not even a persisting self that could be continuous, only a causally connected series of momentary physical and mental events.
(4) So the being who suffers the fruit is not the person who performed the deed. The similes the tradition offers in reply — the flame passed from lamp to lamp, milk becoming curd and then butter and then ghee — concede this rather than resist it, since the whole point of insisting that the later thing is neither the same as nor wholly other than the earlier one is that strict identity fails to hold.
(5) Nor can causal continuity be substituted for identity. If standing downstream of a wrongdoer in a causal chain were sufficient to deserve punishment, then the children and neighbors of a criminal would deserve punishment in virtue of the harms his acts brought them or the dispositions those acts formed in them; no one thinks that.
(6) Therefore karmic suffering never lands on anyone who deserves it, the doctrine is not a theory of desert, and every karmic theodicy built on it fails at the same joint.

Of these, the step I care most about is (1): I want desert to be non-transferable, because without it retribution looks like nothing more than a tax on bad luck. But I would like to know which step you would reject and why it looked unobjectionable to me when I wrote it. I suspect (5) in particular may be doing covert work, since the children of a criminal are linked to the wrongdoer by one kind of causal chain and a reborn being to its predecessor by quite another, and I worry that (5) runs the two together; I also worry that any way of marking the difference will end up simply asserting that rebirth-transmitting chains are the relevant kind. Please price the alternatives rather than just naming a culprit: what does a karmic theorist give up by conceding (4) and insisting that desert requires a self that survives death, and what does she give up by denying (1) and treating fruition as something other than desert — therapeutic, pedagogical, or medical rather than retributive? Finally, tell me whether my advisor is right that the argument is self-defeating: if (1) and (3) are applied within a single life, through dementia, radical conversion, or merely the passage of decades, does ordinary punishment survive any better than karmic punishment does? If it does not, what is the most restricted version of the objection that still bites, and does it bite equally hard on the karma doctrines that posit an enduring self and those that deny it?
