I cover behavioural science for a magazine, and I also sit — as the token non-academic — on the scientific advisory panel of a mid-sized US education funder. Thursday we vote on a three-year, $4m proposal and I have to circulate my written opinion beforehand, which is awkard because it'll be read by people who know this literature far better than I do.

The proposal comes from a behavioural genetics group. The practical ask is a reallocation: they want to pull money out of a universal after-school reading programme and put it into intensive small-group tutoring for the bottom quartile of readers. Fine so far. It's the argument they give for it that's split the panel. They report a heritability estimate of about 0.6 for reading comprehension at age 12 from a large twin registry (Dutch, I think), and then say that since roughly 60% of the variance in reading outcomes is genetic, schools and funders can realistically hope to move at most the remaining 40% — so the money buys more if it's concentrated on fewer children. They want that 40% figure in the public-facing summary of the grant too.

Two members think this is just quantitative genetics applied responsibly, and that "heritability isn't malleability" is a tired talking point that lets funders wave away evidence. Two think the 40% line is indefensible and would torch us in the press. I'm more or less the tiebreaker.

Draft the opinion I'll circulate: a recommendation the panel can actually vote on (fund as proposed / fund with conditions / reject), with reasons the applicants could reply to, and a real go at the strongest consideration on the losing side rather than a strawman. Name the general findings you're leaning on if it helps, but don't build the thing around a reference list — I want the reasoning, not the citations.

Structure it like this, in American English:

- **Bottom line** — three or four sentences, including the recommendation.
- **What the proposal claims** — about 200 words, in the applicants' best light, no snark.
- **Where the inference breaks** — about 400 words, and this is the meat: what a heritability coefficient licenses and what it doesn't, the specific jump from 0.6 to a 40% policy ceiling, and what carrying an estimate across populations, cohorts and ages does to it.
- **The strongest case for funding it anyway** — about 250 words.
- **Conditions** — about 200 words, concrete and checkable: what would let us fund the reallocation without the 40% claim, or why no set of conditions would.
- **Where I'd most likely be wrong** — short. Tell me which single argument from the applicants, if it held up, would flip your recommendation, and which step in your own reasoning is the weakest.

Don't hedge the whole thing into mush. I need a vote, not a survey.
