I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine — mostly economics, some AI policy, some climate — and last month I spent two days sitting in on a workshop where a phrase kept getting used, "safe Pareto improvement," by maybe four different people in four slightly different senses, and nobody ever stopped to pin it down. I want to write a piece about it and I dont want to write that piece from inside their vocabulary. So before I quote anybody, I want to know what I should be demanding of the notion.

Here's roughly how one of the economists explained it to me, in case you haven't run across it: two parties who distrust each other and can't verify what the other actually values look for a change to the rules of their interaction that leaves each of them no worse off than they'd be in the default no-deal outcome, no matter what the other one subsequently does. Safe in the sense that you don't have to trust the other side's good behavior, or even know what they want, in order to accept it. The selling point is that you get some of the gains of cooperation without the trust that cooperation normally requires.

So: write me the specification for that. What would a theory or formal account of a safe Pareto improvement actually have to deliver to be worth the name — the requirements it has to meet, the case or consideration behind each one, and your view on which of those are load-bearing and which are just the preferences of particular camps.

A few things I specifically want you to wrestle with, though add whatever I've missed. The baseline: if safety is measured against the no-deal outcome, what stops a party from making the no-deal outcome worse on purpose so that more deals come out looking like improvements? I watched exactly that argument happen at the workshop and nobody resolved it. Second, "no matter what the other does" — does that have to hold against every action they could physically take, including insane ones, or only against rational ones? And if only rational ones, doesn't the theory now have to model what the other side wants, which is precisely the thing it was supposed to let you avoid? My suspicion is that those two horns make the notion either vacuous or trivial, and if you think that's right I'd rather you say so plainly than hedge. Third, composition: if a change is safe for each of five departments against whatever the other four do, does it follow that all five adopted together is safe? I have a real case where it didn't. Fourth, whether the theory owes me an existence result — some characterization of when safe Pareto improvements exist at all, rather than only what they'd look like if they did — and how it's meant to handle parties who aren't at the table, future people, and losers too diffuse to be represented by anyone. And what would actually count, in a newsprint sense, as evidence that some real policy was one, given that you never get to observe the fallback.

Then at the end, briefly, run three kinds of proposal I actually heard against your list and tell me which comes closest: enforceable contracts backed by a third-party enforcer; precommitment devices where each side ties its own hands unilaterally; and the value-mixing scheme, where the split is set by a weighted combination of both parties' utilities with the weights deliberately left unknown to everyone. Keep that part short, three sentences each at most — the criteria are what I want.

And please no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. Just continuous prose, the way you'd write an email or a letter. I'll be reading it on a train and possibly quoting bits, so write it like a person writing to another person, and if you think a requirement I've listed is bogus, argue me out of it rather than quietly dropping it.
