I'm moderating a panel for our political-philosophy reading group and the guest is a theorist who defends what she calls the reserve model of performance legitimacy. Roughly: she thinks delivering prosperity and order isn't a knockoff substitute for procedural legitimacy, it's a store of it. Good years build up a reserve the government can spend down during bad ones, so what actually matters is whether replenishment outpaces drawdown. On her view a regime that has banked thirty years of growth can absorb a rough decade without losing its right to rule, and demands for elections are a claim on the reserve rather than a precondition for having one. She also insists this is a normative claim about legitimacy, not just a prediction about survival.

Write me nine or ten questions for her. I want them to bite on the reserve model specifically, not just be variations on "can authoritarianism ever be legit." At minimum I want:

- one on how you'd ever size the reserve — what counts as a drawdown event, who scores it, and whether a theory where the units are made up by the theorist is doing any explanatory work
- one on falsifiability, i.e. the worry that any regime that survives just had a bigger reserve than we thought and any regime that collapses ran out, so the model can't lose
- one on the expectations problem (the Tocqueville thing, that improving conditions raise what people count as normal) — can good performance actually accelerate the drain instead of slowing it, and if so does her model predict that prosperity eventually becomes self-undermining
- one on reserves built out of numbers nobody can independently check — censored statistics, managed media, a press that can't report the bad years — is that a reserve or an IOU the state wrote to itself
- one on her claim that this is normative rather than predictive, and whether "nobody could reasonably reject it because they're doing fine" smuggles in exactly the consent-style standard she says she doesn't need

For each of those five, also give me the answer she's most likely to give and a follow up that doesn't let her just restate the model. And close with one question that forces her to name a single observation that would make her give the view up. No softballs, and nothing about what drew her to this work.
