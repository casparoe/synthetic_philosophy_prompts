I TA a first-year seminar called something like "How Do We Know About the Ancient Past?" and the whole back half of the term is the three usual suspects — Troy, Arthur, the Exodus — which means my job is basically to talk eighteen-year-olds down from both extremes: the "it's all made up, nothing in old stories is evidence" position and the "well there's obviously a kernel of truth" position. I've been running a Thursday film night alongside it and last week we did John Ford's The Man Who Shot Liberty Valance, and it wrecked me a little, because it turns out Ford built the perfect laboratory version of the problem I spend weeks on. We get the event, the misattribution, and the tradition-forming all in real time, with the ground truth visible to us in the theater but not to anyone in Shinbone: Tom Doniphon fires from the dark with Pompey's rifle at the same moment Ranse pulls the trigger, Ranse gets the reputation, and twenty-five years later he's a senator telling the true story to a newspaperman who hears it, decides it would ruin a great career, and declines to run it — when the legend becomes fact, print the legend.

So here's what I want out of you. First, do the actual inventory work on the distortion: which details of that night get changed as the story becomes the legend, which ones survive untouched, and — this is the part I keep circling — if the only thing that survived to a historian two centuries later were the legend as it stands at the end of the film (the senator's career, the funeral, the train conductor cheering that nothing's too good for the man who shot Liberty Valance), could anyone recover that Doniphon was the shooter? What would they get right by accident, what would they get wrong with total confidence? And notice the loose thread, which I think is the most interesting thing in the movie for my purposes: the entire town knew Ranse was a tenderfoot who had to borrow the editor's old gun and practice in secret, and Tom personally demonstrated at the ranch that Ranse would never survive a fair draw with Valance. The implausible version nonetheless held for a quarter century among living eyewitnesses who could have contradicted it. That seems to smash the heuristic I teach — the one about how a story that survives inside a community that could have refuted it probably has something real under it. Is the film a genuine counterexample, or does it secretly confirm the heuristic in a nastier form, on the grounds that the town didn't so much believe the story as need it (statehood, law beating the gun, a career to hang hopes on)? Then tell me which of my three cases the Shinbone legend is structurally closest to: Troy, where the dirt can corroborate a destroyed site without corroborating a single act by a single person; Arthur, where there's essentially no early evidence and you can watch the elaboration happen from inside the tradition; or the Exodus, where it's a charter story a community keeps alive by ritual retelling and stakes its identity on. Pick one, defend the pick, and say what the choice changes about whether the film can teach my students anything at all.

Two more things, and then I'll stop. I want to know where the movie itself lands on the editor's decision, because it reads to me like it both blesses and indicts it, and an honest account has to sit with the price tag rather than wave at it — Tom torching the room he'd built for Hallie, dying broke and unclaimed, the cactus rose on the coffin, Ranse and Hallie's silent ride back to Washington. Does Ford think the legend was worth it, merely inevitable, or actively corrosive (that conductor's line feels like the last word for a reason)? And does the fact that Ranse eventually confesses, decades late, to a man with every incentive to bury it, make this a self-correcting legend — and is a legend that generates its own eventual debunking from the inside epistemically less bad than one that only outsiders with shovels can ever pry open? Which brings me to a distinction I half-made-up with a friend over beers and cant tell if it's real or just us reinventing something under a worse name: leaky versus self-sealing legends. A leaky one leaves seams detectable from inside the tradition — the embarrassing residue, the local rival memory, the detail nobody would have invented because it undercuts the hero — so the correction is available to anyone patient enough to pull the thread. A self-sealing one is built so that doubt gets absorbed as more evidence, and each retelling makes the seams harder to see rather than easier. Is Shinbone leaky or self-sealing, and do Troy, Arthur, and the Exodus come out differently on that axis? If this isn't a thing people actually track, say so plainly and then give me the best version of it — definition, boundary cases, what it excludes. Last thing: I want real criticism of the film here, not a philosophy essay with a Western stapled on for illustration, and don't flatten it into a case study — Ford is doing his own work (elegy for a vanishing West, a eulogy for Wayne's kind of man), so tell me where my analogy strains and where the movie actively resists being read this way.
