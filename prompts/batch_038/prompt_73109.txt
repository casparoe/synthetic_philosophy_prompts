My flatmate's doing a PhD on chance and rational belief and she's been banging on about the Principal Principle all week — the rough idea being that if you know the objective chance of something, your degree of belief ought to just match it, unless you've got other relevant information to go on. I nod along politely, but honestly the thing that got me wasn't the slogan, it was imagining what it would feel like to be someone whose job that was. So I'd love a short story instead of another seminar summary.

The setup I keep picturing, please improve on it: a city where some official body publishes the true chances of specific everyday events — not forecasts, not estimates, the actual single-case chances, and we stipulate they're right. The chance this particular sea wall gives way in the next winter, that this defendant reoffends, that this patient comes through the operation. There's a whole trade built on those numbers, people who price risk for a living and who are good precisely because they never let themselves drift off the published figure. Your protagonist is one of them, mid-career, quietly proud that she has never once adjusted a number in her head. Then two things happen in the same season. She starts picking up scraps of information about individual cases — something a skipper says, a photograph, a weather report nobody else has bothered to read — that seem to bear on what will actually happen, and she can't work out whether she's entitled to use them, or whether the honest thing is to keep her belief pinned at the published 0.3 and take the bet even though some part of her already knows how it'll go. And separately, over the year the outcomes in her patch start drifting away from the published chances, in one direction, visibly, and the body refuses to revise a single figure — so now she has to decide whether all that accumulated evidence about what's really happening is the sort of thing that's allowed to move her off a number she's supposed to defer to. A colleague who did go off the numbers, and what became of him, would probably help here.

What I'm after is a real story — say two thousand words, a character with a life outside the puzzle, an actual plot — not a philosophy lecture in a raincoat. The bit I care about most is the texture of deferring to a number from the inside when the number starts to feel wrong: the self-distrust, the way it leaks into her judgement about everything else, the odd relief of not being responsible for being right. Keep the ideas buried in the events rather than spelled out, though if two characters want to row about it in dialogue, go for it. And please don't tie it up neatly, I'd much rather it ended somewhere uncomfortable.
