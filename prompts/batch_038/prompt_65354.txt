--- DRAFT ---

Everyone teaches the primary/secondary occurrence distinction from the 1905 paper as a scope ambiguity, and then everyone is puzzled by the fact that Russell has to tell you which scope to pick. Consider: The current king of France doesn't exist. Take the description with wide scope over the negation and you get that there is exactly one current king of France and that he fails to exist — not just false but self-undermining. Take it with narrow scope and you get the denial of the whole existence-and-uniqueness package, which is true. Russell says the sincere speaker means the second one. Standard textbooks report this as: the sentence is ambiguous, and only one of the readings is available to the sincere speaker. Which is a strange thing for a semantic ambiguity to be like. Ambiguities are supposed to be free; the grammar generates both and the world picks. Here one of the two generated readings is systematically never what anybody means, and it's never what anybody means for a reason that has nothing to do with what the sentence says.

My claim is that the wide/narrow contrast isn't a scope contrast at all, and that the reason it looks like one is that Russell had no vocabulary for the thing that actually controls it, so he reached for the only structural resource he had. What controls it is the discourse status of the descriptive content: whether that content is already on the table as part of the question being answered, or whether it's being newly asserted.

Run the example again. Somebody asks whether France still has a king. The descriptive content of the current king of France is supplied by the question; it is given, not asserted. What the answerer adds is a negation over that whole package. So the description ends up narrow, and the sentence is true. Now imagine the same words in a context where the descriptive content is precisely what's being put forward as news — a con-man has just been introduced as royalty and you want to say the fellow is no such thing. Now the description is the assertoric contribution, it goes wide, and the sentence says that there is exactly one current king of France and he fails to exist. Contradiction. But notice: the contradiction isn't a defect in the reading, it's a diagnosis of the context. You cannot newly assert existence-and-uniqueness and deny existence in the same breath. So the wide reading isn't a reading anyone could sincerely mean — it's what you get when you ask the analysis to do something incoherent.

This is exactly the structural role that focus plays in the semantics of particles like only and even. The particle's contribution is invariant; what varies is which material counts as background and which counts as the point. Russell's two occurrences are, I want to say, the two positions a description can occupy relative to the question under discussion, and he encoded them as two positions relative to a logical operator because operator position was the only notion of position he had.

Two things I think this buys us. First, it converts a stipulation into an explanation. Russell doesn't get to decree that negative existentials take the secondary reading; the decree follows from the fact that a topic can't be an assertion. Second, it explains the odd locality of the distinction. The wide/narrow contrast only ever does visible work in puzzles — empty descriptions, negative existentials, nonexistence claims about fictional characters. Everywhere else it's invisible. On the scope story that's a coincidence. On my story it's a prediction: the contrast only becomes visible when the speaker has no salient individual to work with and the descriptive content is doing the whole job of fixing the topic. When there's a perfectly good referent in the room, whether the description sits inside or outside an operator makes no difference to what's said, and so nobody notices.

There is also a consequence for the old quarrel about truth-value gaps. On my account Russell and Strawson are not disagreeing about the semantics of the English determiner. Russell is describing a procedure for converting a topic-comment structure into canonical form, a procedure that only issues a truth-evaluable claim once you know what the topic was supplied by. Strawson is describing what happens to the sentence when you detach it from any such supply. The one who says false and the one who says neither-true-nor-false are answering different questions, and the difference between them is not in the sentence. It's in whether there's a question under discussion.

The hard case for me, and I don't yet have an answer. The same distinction does work inside attitude reports and modals, where the two positions of the description generate a genuine de re / de dicto contrast and where both readings are perfectly available and non-contradictory. A monarch wondering whether the author of a certain novel is a particular person he suspects: description narrow gives a claim about whoever wrote it, description wide gives a claim about a specified individual. Both are coherent. Nothing there looks incoherent-by-construction, and it isn't obvious to me that givenness versus assertedness tracks the two readings. If my story has to say that attitude contexts are a different phenomenon wearing the same clothes, then the theory is narrower than I've been letting on.
