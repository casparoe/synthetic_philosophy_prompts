so im doing a third-year module that's half psycholinguistics and half philosophy of language, and the last seminar of term has this tradition where instead of an essay you bring in something — a dialogue, a diagram, a poem — and you get five minutes to say why you made it. I want to do a sestina about linguistic relativity, mostly because I think the form is basically the argument: you fix six end-words at the start and then you're not allowed to introduce a seventh, you can only re-sort the ones you were given in that set rotation (second stanza goes 6-1-5-2-4-3 and so on round to the envoi). That's exactly what the strong Whorfian claim sounds like to me — you never get a new concept, you get the same six words handed back in a different order forever, and the poem can't escape its own lexicon any more than a speaker can escape theirs. So I want the form to be the determinism and then I want something to happen inside it. The end-words I've settled on are blue, grey, north, left, empty, tomorrow. Blue and grey are for the colour-term stuff we did, Berlin and Kay's universals on one side and on the other that study where Russian speakers were quicker at telling apart two shades straddling their siniy/goluboy split than two shades the same distance apart sitting on one side of it — small effect, but a real one, and it isn't magic. North and left are for frames of reference, i.e. the Boroditsky work on Kuuk Thaayorre speakers who orient by compass points rather than by their own bodies, so the difference between north and left in my list is the difference between a grid that's bolted to the world and one that swings round when you turn round. Empty is Whorf's own best example — the drums marked empty that are more dangerous than the full ones because they're full of vapour, and the word habits people into carelessness; that's the bit of him I actually find convincing, its not about being unable to think something, its about being lulled. Tomorrow is the Hopi one, and here's where I need you to tread carefully.

Please don't let the poem assert that Hopi has no tense or no way of talking about time, because Malotki spent a whole book, Hopi Time, showing that it does, and our tutor will absolutely pounce on it. What I want is the weaker thing that survives all that — Slobin's thinking-for-speaking idea, that a language doesn't wall you in so much as make certain distinctions cheap and habitual and others expensive, so you keep noticing what your grammar makes it easy to notice. That's also the tone I want for the whole thing: nobody in the seminar thinks language is a prison any more, but nobody thinks its neutral either, and the poem should sit in that gap rather than pick a side for the sake of a punchline. Two bans while I'm at it. No snow words — I know how tempting it is, but that whole cliché was taken apart by Pullum decades ago and if I read it aloud the room will groan. And no Heptapods, no inkblots, no references to that film. Don't put Whorf's or Sapir's names in the body either; the title can carry that weight if you want it to. Finally, don't invent facts about real languages — if you want a specific word from a specific language, use the ones I've already handed you rather than making something up that sounds plausible, because I'll be reading this out to people who did the reading.

The bit I most want you to get right is the turn. Somewhere — stanza five maybe, or the envoi — one of the six end-words should quietly change its part of speech: empty stops being an adjective and becomes something you do to a thing, or north stops being a place and becomes a way of facing, or left stops being a direction and becomes the past tense of leaving. The words are fixed, the grammar isn't, and that's the only escape hatch the determinism leaves open. I want that to land as a formal event, not as a line that explains itself — if any line tells the reader what the poem means, cut it. Thirty-nine lines, six stanzas of six and a three-line envoi, keep the rotation honest because I will count it. No rhyme scheme, don't let it turn into a jingle, it should sound like a person talking and the lines can run as long as they need. Setting-wise I'd seed it somewhere aggressively ordinary — a filling-station forecourt, or a kitchen table with paint cards spread all over it, or being the passenger for someone who gives directions by compass points instead of lefts and rights. And the envoi should be where the strong claim dies and the weak one walks out, all six words still in there, feeling like a settlement rather than a victory.
