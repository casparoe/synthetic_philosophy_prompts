I'm working on a dissertation chapter about collective guilt across generations and I want to run a hard case through Jaspers's scheme from The Question of German Guilt (Die Schuldfrage) — the four categories, criminal / political / moral / metaphysical — and see whether it holds up or where it just goes quiet.

The case is the Belgian Congo, and the focal point is the June 2020 letter King Philippe sent to Félix Tshisekedi for the 60th anniversary of Congolese independence: deepest regrets for the violence and humiliation of the colonial period, deliberately stopping short of an apology or of any admission of legal responsibility. Same month, statues of Leopold II were being defaced or carted off in Antwerp and elsewhere.

The wrinkle I keep tripping over, and the one I want the analysis to turn on: the Congo Free State wasn't Belgium. From 1885 to 1908 it was Leopold's personal property, a private concession run through his own companies and the Force Publique, so Jaspers's political guilt — citizens liable for the deeds of their state — seems to have nothing to grip onto. Then in 1908 the Belgian parliament annexed it, took over the entire administrative apparatus, and on the terms of the transfer also assumed the Free State's debts (there's a whole odious-debt literature built on exactly that moment). The successor state swallowed the liabilities and the machinery but never the guilt, and a century later the head of state offers regret instead of admission.

So, can you map it properly? Who the relevant agents are at each stage — Leopold personally, the concessionaires and shareholders, the 1908 electorate and parliament, the colonial administration through to 1960, Philippe and the current dynasty, ordinary Belgians now, and the Congolese on the receiving end of the gesture — and which category each falls under, if any. Then three things I actually care about:

1. What does Jaspers license Philippe to say in 2020, and is "regrets without admission" a coherent position inside the scheme? My hunch is its a fifth thing he has no slot for — tell me if I'm wrong.
2. Does the 1908 assumption of the Free State's debts do any normative work, given that for Jaspers political guilt gets discharged materially rather than by confession? Is paying off the bonds a partial discharge, or a transaction with no guilt-content at all?
3. The dynasty point: Albert I was Leopold's nephew, so Philippe descends from the office and not from the perpetrator. Does Jaspers care about that difference?

And then run it the other way — is this episode a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case (colonialism, a private sovereign's atrocities, guilt transmitted through state succession rather than through a defeated regime) that Die Schuldfrage was simply never aimed at? Please flag where you're extrapolating rather than pretending the text settles it.
