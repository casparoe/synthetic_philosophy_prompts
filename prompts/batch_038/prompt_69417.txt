ok, weird situation and I could use a spec before I commit to anything. I co-supervise a small empirical project — people watch horror clips and play horror games in a VR headset while we log skin conductance, then they self-report afterwards — and supposedly my half of the job is to pick a philosophical account of emotional response to fiction to frame the results. Problem is every account I read looks built to win an argument rather than to fit data, so before I choose one I want to know what I should be demanding of any of them.

So: write the desiderata. What does an adequate theory of our emotional responses to fiction have to deliver — the phenomena it must explain, the cases it must get right, the distinctions it can't collapse, the findings from the psychology of emotion it can't contradict — with the case or consideration standing behind each requirement, and a tag on each saying whether it's non-negotiable or just what one particular camp happens to want.

Two cases of my own should be on your radar, because I suspect they're what breaks the tidy pictures:

1. The headset. In VR you know perfectly well nothing can hurt you, and you still duck, scream, back out of the room — in one session a subject actually pulled the headset off and refused to finish. So the familiar story, we don't act because we don't believe, has to explain why the behavioural asymmetry between fiction and real life is so clean for a novel and so leaky here. Any requirement that treats "nobody calls the police" as universal is already false.

2. The twice-through reader. She's read the novel twice, knows exactly what happens at every beat, says flatly that she feels no fear and doesn't enjoy it — but her hands sweat at the same points, and for two nights she sleeps badly and checks the locks, which is the same residue she gets from true crime about a real case. I have no idea weather my list should count the emotion as present.

Also, one of your items should be a requirement that people constantly demand of these theories but that you think shouldn't be in the spec at all — say what it is and why it's smuggled in. I keep seeing the "but why do we seek this stuff out" question fused with the "how is the response possible" one and I don't think that's innocent.

Format: exactly six numbered requirements, two or three sentences each, no introduction and no wrap-up. Inside each item give the requirement, its motivating case, and the tag. If two of the six strike you as jointly unsatisfiable, say so in the item where it bites — that's the part I most want to see. And mark, in the item itself, whichever one knocks out the most candidates. No reading list and no names of who says what; I want the criteria, not the scorecard.
