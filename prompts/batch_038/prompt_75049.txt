I've basically converted to risk-weighted expected utility — the Buchak line, where risk attitude is its own free standing parameter next to the utility function and the probability function, not something you smuggle in as utility curvature or as distorted probabilities. But I hold it in a deliberately strict form: one risk function per agent, fixed across domains. Not a separate risk attitude for card bets versus choosing public-health interventions versus career gambles — a single global parameter, on the grounds that risk attitude is supposed to be a fact about the agent and not about how a problem happens to be carved up.

What would count against the view as I hold it? I want the map of exposure, ranked from actually fatal to merely awkward, and I want you to commit to a verdict on each item rather than hedging into both-sides. Two I've already chewed on: the Rabin-style calibration pressure (reject a 50/50 lose-$100/gain-$105 bet at any wealth level and you're committed to rejecting lose-$20,000 for any gain), where the stock reply is that calibration results are about concave utility so they dont touch a rank-dependent theory — tell me whether that reply really holds, or whether something structurally analogous goes through for risk functions themselves. And the grand-world complaint, that the view is motivated by small-world cases like Allais but claims to govern decisions made against a background of massive uncertainty, where supposedly the risk-neutral and risk-averse attitudes become indistinguishable. How much bite does that have for someone who refuses context-relative risk attitudes?

Also give me the ones I havent thought about. How much damage do the dynamic-choice and money-pump arguments do specifically because of my one-global-function commitment, since I suspect allowing domain-relative risk functions would buy me out and I've refused that on principle? And the collapse worry: for which decision problems is my extra parameter doing literally nothing a suitably concave utility function couldnt do — is there a test I could run to tell those apart from the cases where it's genuinely load-bearing, or is that question itself ill-posed? Related: what does my view predict that expected utility theory doesn't, in a way that could come apart empirically rather than just in thought experiments about lotteries?

Then tell me which of these a committed defender would concede as counting against them and which they'd dismiss as question-begging, and where the view risks anything at all — what I'd have to give up on rather than reparameterize. Be honest about whether "risk attitude is whatever increasing continuous function happens to fit the pattern" has quietly made the thing unfalsifiable, and if so whether that's worse here than for other permissive accounts of rationality, given the whole selling point was a substantive constraint.

One more thing: if you think the strict version is the confused part — if a single domain-invariant risk function isn't a coherent commitment to begin with, or the position I've described isn't the one worth defending — say so plainly instead of politely answering the question I asked.
