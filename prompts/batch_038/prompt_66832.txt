I run the weekly discussion section for a first-year seminar here, 20-odd students, and next week is the empire week — Ferguson's side against Tharoor's side, roughly. I wrote up an argument to hand out, intending it as the thing that dissolves the whole debate, and I've now argued myself into a corner I can't get out of, because the conclusion ends up much bigger than the empire and I'm not willing to live with it.

Here it is:

(1) The claim that British rule was on balance good for India is the claim that India did better under British rule than it would have done without it. Either it's a counterfactual claim or it isn't a claim about good-for at all.

(2) To know a counterfactual claim about a single, unrepeatable macro-historical event, you need a defensible description of the comparison world.

(3) There isn't one here. Every baseline on offer — a Maratha successor order, a modernizing Mughal remnant, an industrialization-from-above on the Japanese model — gets picked because it delivers the verdict the picker already wanted, and all of them are equally consistent with everything we actually know about the eighteenth century, which was already a period of fragmentation and of successor states doing plenty of extracting on their own account.

(4) So the pro-empire balance sheet is not knowable.

(5) But the identical steps run against the other side. The claim that India was impoverished by colonial rule also compares the actual past to a baseline — an India that kept its revenue, industrialized its textiles and fed its own hungry — and that baseline is no less arbitrary. The nearest things to controlled comparisons (Japan, Siam, Ethiopia, Qing China) differ from India in ways that plausibly matter more then colonial status did, and the comparison people actually run — British-administered districts against the princely states — compares two kinds of subject rather than rule against no rule.

(6) So neither side knows what it's asserting, and what looked like a disagreement about the past is two unfalsifiable narratives in a trenchcoat.

(7) And nothing in any of this is special to empire. Every net-good-for / net-bad-for claim about a large one-off event has the same shape: the New Deal and the Depression, the Black Death and English wages, the railways and Indian national consciousness. We should stop pretending to know any of them either.

The thing is, I'm pretty comfortable with (1) through (4). It's (5) onwards I can't stomach. I'm not going to stand in front of twenty eighteen-year-olds and say we don't know that people died in their millions in famines under an administration that could have responded and didn't, and I'm even less willing to say we don't know that the Black Death raised English real wages.

So: which step breaks first, and is this argument actually valid, or is it the transitions between the steps doing all the work while each step individually looks unobjectionable? I'd also like to know whether the over-generalization in (7) is the refutation or just a symptom of one — i.e. whether you think (6) is genuinely untenable on its own, or only embarrassing once you notice how far it spreads. If I have to pick something to surrender, I'd rather give up (7) than (3), but tell me what I'm paying either way.
