I co-host a small podcast on philosophical method, and next month's guest defends a position she calls calibrated intuition. Her claim is that a thought experiment carries evidential weight only when the reader's responses to it have been shaped by training and sustained peer criticism; on her account, framing effects, order effects, and cross-cultural variation in lay responses show only that untutored intuitions are unreliable, not that the practice of running cases is. When pressed on the obvious disanalogy — that philosophy, unlike music or medicine, has no independent check on whether the trained responses are tracking anything — she replies that the community's own convergence under argumentative pressure is the check, and that demanding anything further already assumes the answer against her.

Draft eight to ten questions that make that reply progressively harder to sustain rather than letting her restate the view. I want at least one question on whether convergence among people who share a training history can be distinguished from a shared bias; one on what her criterion implies for the cases trained philosophers are divided about, which is to say for most of the interesting ones; one on whether the evidential work is being done by the intuitions at all or by the coherence testing they feed into; and one on whether some pumps are defective in their construction — loaded with detail that manufactures the response — so that the fault lies in the case rather than in the reader. For each of those four, add the follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give, and end with a question about what would count as evidence that her own intuitions are mis-calibrated.
