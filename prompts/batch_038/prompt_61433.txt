I'm two years into a policy master's and I've somehow become the designated carbon-pricing person in a reading group where roughly half the room are degrowthers, so I've had the same argument maybe a dozen times now — and recently I've noticed something uncomfortable, which is that I can't state my own position in a way that's actually vulnerable to anything. Whatever they throw at me, I have a reply, and I'm not sure that means I'm right. So I want you to do it properly, from the inside, rather than just listing the standard talking points from both camps.

Here's the view. A carbon price — a tax rather than a trading scheme, set high, on a legislated declining schedule, with the revenue going back out as a dividend or into cutting other taxes — is *sufficient* to put a rich economy on a compatible emissions path; you don't need to cap or shrink GDP to get there. The degrowth claim that growth as such is incompatible with the remaining carbon budget looks to me like a category error, because GDP is a money aggregate covering a wildly heterogeneous bundle of physical activity, so there's no fixed ratio between it and tonnes, and the usual showcase countries (Britain, Sweden, bits of the EU) show emissions falling while output keeps rising. The binding constraint is the *rate* of decarbonisation, not the *level* of economic activity, and the price is the instrument that operates on the rate. Everything else — degrowth's talk of sufficiency, post-growth, planned contraction — is either a restatement of that or a preference for a different society dressed up as a climate claim.

So: what would count against this? I want the whole exposure map, ranked from "this is fatal, I should genuinely consider switching camps" down to "awkward but survivable." In particular — which empirical findings should actually move me? Say, if the best decoupling cases turn out to be artifacts of production-versus-consumption accounting, or of one-off coal closures and offshoring rather than a durable trend; or if the observed price elasticities in the hard-to-abate sectors (steel, cement, aviation, freight, fertiliser) are low enough that no politically attainable price does the arithmetic. And crucially, how would I ever tell the difference between "the price was too low" and "a price can't do this job"? Which conceptual objections bite — the argument that pricing is the wrong instrument when the problem is lumpy, indivisible, long-lived infrastructure and path dependence rather than a smooth marginal cost; the point that any price presupposes a damage estimate that is itself deeply contested and possibly incoherent across sectors? And then the big one: has my view been qualified into unfalsifiability, since every failure can be absorbed by saying the price was too low, or the revenue recyling was botched, or the border adjustments weren't in place? If it has, is that fatal for a policy claim of this kind, or is it just the normal condition of arguing about a counterfactual that's never been fully implemented — and what separates that from, say, arguing that a drug would work if it had ever been dosed high enough?

I'd also like the comparison to be honest in both directions: what does my position predict that the degrowth position doesn't, and what have they predicted that I don't? My suspicion is I come out of that worse than I'd like. And tell me which of these objections a smart, non-evasive defender of carbon pricing would *concede* as counting against them rather than wave away — I want to see what the least defensive version of my own side looks like, because that's the version I actually want to hold. No reading list, please, and no hedging everything into "it depends on the details"; I'd rather get a committal answer I can argue with.

Finally, and this is the part I care most about: at the end, say what would change *your* mind about the ranking itself. Which piece of evidence or which step in the argument, if it came out differently than you think it does, would flip your overall verdict on the position — and where in your own reasoning above are you most likely to be wrong? If you catch yourself giving the position a pass because it's the more respectable one in policy circles, say so.
