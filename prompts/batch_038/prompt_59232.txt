I have been working through the accuracy-first program in epistemology — the view that accuracy is the sole fundamental epistemic good, and that the familiar norms of belief are justified because they are what accuracy itself recommends — and I have assembled an argument that the program refutes itself. I cannot tell whether the argument is sound or whether I have botched a step, and since I would much rather keep the program's theorems than its foundational principle, I want a careful diagnosis rather than encouragement.

(1) The program's master principle: a doxastic state or plan is epistemically rational just in case, among those available to the agent, it maximizes expected accuracy, where the expectation is computed from the agent's own credences.

(2) The program's central results are applications of (1). Its argument that incoherent credence functions are irrational, and its argument that one ought to update by conditionalizing on one's total evidence, are both presented as showing that the recommended doxastic state is the one that accuracy, as the agent sees it from where she stands, favors over the alternatives.

(3) But (1) delivers conclusions the program cannot swallow. Suppose I know that tomorrow I will be shown a white card under red lighting, so that I will have an experience as of redness, and suppose I know now that the card is white. Conditionalizing on that experience would raise my credence that the card is red. By (1) I should therefore plan now to move my credence the other way when the experience arrives, since that plan is the one my own current credences rank as more accurate. So (1) requires me to plan to discount evidence I know is coming — while (1) was supposed to be what generates the requirement to conditionalize.

(4) (1) also licenses reckless belief. When accuracy is scored across several propositions at once, maximizing expected accuracy can require adopting a credence I take to be probably false, because the gains I expect in the worlds I regard as likely outweigh the losses in the worlds I regard as unlikely. On (1) this is not a pathology but a verdict: deliberately accepting a chance of being flatly wrong is required whenever the expected payoff in accuracy is large enough.

(5) Worse, (1) makes every norm hostage to the agent's own opinions. If my credences are systematically bad, then the states that maximize expected accuracy by my lights are bad states, and (1) requires them of me. On (1) the miscalibrated agent is not merely excused but mandated to believe badly, and epistemic rationality collapses into prudence relative to one's own opinions.

(6) No theory that mandates believing badly can be a theory of epistemic rationality, and no theory that recommends disregarding one's evidence can be the theory that derives conditionalization.

(7) Therefore (1) is false; and since (2) says the program's flagship theorems are derived from (1), those theorems lose their only advertised justification.

I anticipate one reply and think it fails. The reply is that I have described the program's principle wrongly, because its derivations are dominance arguments rather than expected-value arguments: they require only that a rational state or plan not be beaten by an alternative that is more accurate at every state the agent takes seriously. My objection is that this retreat is a dilemma. Either the quantification over states is unrestricted, in which case every credence function is beaten at some state by some alternative and nothing at all turns out to be undominated; or the quantification is restricted to the states the agent assigns positive credence, in which case the norm is hostage to the agent's own opinions again and (5) returns untouched.

So: which step should I reject? Please tell me why the step looked acceptable when I wrote it down, and what I lose by giving it up. In particular I want to know whether the expected-value route to coherence and conditionalization and the dominance route really do come apart, and in which cases they diverge; whether the dominance results can carry the weight I assigned to (1), and what difficulties of their own they bring with them; and whether my dismissal of the dominance reply holds or collapses. If the argument is in fact sound and I must choose, tell me which to keep, the master principle or the theorems, and what the survivor then amounts to.
