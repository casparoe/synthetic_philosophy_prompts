My supervisor is a late-antique historian who has, over two years of supervision, developed a stubbornly consistent record of verdicts on which episodes actually count as the fall of Rome. I have written them down because I now need to predict one she has not yet given. Her rulings so far: Alaric's sack of the city in 410 — not a fall, because the western court, the senate, and the grain supply all carried on and contemporaries experienced it as a catastrophe inside a continuing order rather than as an ending. The deposition of Romulus Augustulus in 476 — not a fall either, since Odovacer shipped the imperial insignia to Constantinople, ruled Italy as a patrician in the emperor's name, and kept the Italian fiscal machinery intact; as she puts it, nobody in 476 knew they had had a fall. The Vandal seizure of Africa in the 430s and 440s — this one she treats as decisive for the West, on the grounds that the western empire ended when its African revenue did. Justinian's reconquest from 535 — a fall, though of Italy rather than of the empire, because in her phrase the recovery destroyed what it recovered: war, plague and the extinction of the senatorial order left a garrisoned peninsula that was not a restored Roman Italy. The Arab conquest of Egypt and Syria in the 630s and 640s — a fall, though she insists furiously that this is not simply Pirenne's thesis about the Mediterranean, and that anyone who says so has not read either. The Antonine plague and the third-century crisis — not a fall, because the system rebuilt itself into a late Roman state that was in some respects stronger than what it replaced. The volcanic winters of the 530s, the cooling that followed, the old lead-in-the-water-pipes hypothesis, and epidemic disease generally — not candidate falls at all: causes, not dates. Charlemagne's coronation in 800 — not a fall but evidence that one had already happened, since a title can only be revived once it has been lost. The fall of Constantinople in 1453 — not a fall of Rome, because that was the end of a medieval polity that had long since stopped being Roman in any sense she is willing to cash out; and the dissolution of the Holy Roman Empire in 1806, which I once raised as a joke, she declined to discuss. She has also told me twice that she is not a transformation theorist — she thinks the vocabulary of transformation is what historians reach for when they have lost the nerve to say collapse — and once, exasperated, that all of these verdicts follow from a single rule and that she would be mortified to learn she was running two.

The new case is Gregory the Great's pontificate, 590 to 604, and specifically what I want to do with it in the framing section of my first chapter. Here is the material as I would put it to her. A man from a senatorial family who had himself been prefect of the city, and who had spent years as papal envoy at Constantinople, becomes pope in a year of flood and plague. The exarchate at Ravenna still nominally governs Italy, and the emperor is still addressed as his lord, but when it comes to feeding Rome, ransoming its captives, garrisoning its walls and negotiating with the Lombards, the effective administration is the church's: the revenues of the papal estates, including the great Sicilian ones, are what keep the city alive, and in 598 it is Gregory who concludes a truce with Agilulf. Meanwhile his own writings describe a world in which the cities are emptied, the people have perished, the senate is gone and nothing is left standing, and he writes this in the vocabulary of a Roman citizen about his own republic, styling himself the servant of the servants of God and sending a mission to Kent in the confident expectation that the peoples at the edge of the world are subject to the Christian emperors. So the question I need answered is whether she will call this a fall, a post-fall arrangement, or evidence that no fall is dateable here at all.

What I want from you, in British English, is the following. First, reconstruct the candidate rules that would jointly explain her whole record, and tell me which of them genuinely survive every one of her verdicts and which are already refuted by one or two of them — I suspect at least one obviously attractive rule is killed by her verdict on Vandal Africa and another by her verdict on the reconquest, but I cannot work out which. Second, say which surviving rule she is most likely to be running, given her explicit disavowals as well as her verdicts, and give me her predicted ruling on Gregory with the reasons that rule would supply. Third, be explicit about where the surviving rules diverge on Gregory: I want to know which of them would make his pontificate the diagnostic moment and which would make it merely a symptom of an ending she has already dated thirty or forty years earlier, and what each would force her to say about the papacy's later turn away from Constantinople towards the Franks if she wanted to stay consistent. Fourth, tell me which single further episode I should put to her at the next supervision in order to separate the rules that survive, and why that one rather than any of the others I might choose. And fifth, if her precedents really do underdetermine Gregory, tell me what I would be committing myself to — as a substantive claim about what makes a polity the same polity over time, not merely as a periodising convenience — if I placed his Rome inside the Roman world in my framing, and what I would be committing myself to if I placed it outside. Please do not give me a bibliography or a list of further reading; I will supply references myself, and I am interested in the argument rather than in the apparatus.
