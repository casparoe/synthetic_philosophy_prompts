I am a junior at a public high school, the captain of our debate team, and the organizer of an informal ethics reading group that meets on Thursdays. Our team is preparing for a tournament resolution concerning whether it can be morally permissible to target enemy soldiers in war, and I have watched our members talk past one another for weeks, largely because terms such as "war crime," "just cause," and "combatant" are doing at least three different jobs inside the same conversation. I would like you to build us a sorting instrument that reveals which position a person actually holds, rather than which slogan that person is fond of. Please organize it in four parts.

Part one: lay out the logical space before writing any questions. Identify the handful of independent commitments that generate the possible views — for example, whether a combatant's moral standing depends on the justice of the side for which he fights, whether it depends on what he has personally done, whether the question at issue is who may be killed or who may kill, and whether an unjust-side fighter's permission might be an excuse rather than a justification. Then give the combinations those commitments permit, and for each combination state whether a recognizable position occupies it. Where a cell is empty, say why: because the view is incoherent, because nobody finds it attractive, or because it has simply not been worked out.

Part two: eight to ten questions, each presenting one concrete case with two or three answer options. The cases must include at least the following: a conscripted teenager fighting for the aggressor state who has personally harmed no one; a fifteen-year-old forcibly recruited into a non-state armed group; a volunteer officer on the just side who orders an attack he expects to kill dozens of civilians for a marginal military gain; a civilian engineer in the aggressor state who maintains the targeting software; a remote drone operator who has never been exposed to any risk; an aggressor-state soldier who is retreating and no longer poses a threat; and a fighter on the unjust side who is sincerely and reasonably mistaken about which side is in the right. Write these in plain language with no technical vocabulary at all in the question text — the jargon belongs only in the key.

Part three: the scoring key, mapping answer patterns onto the leaves of your space. Each leaf should carry its conventional name, one sentence naming its most awkward commitment, and the pattern of answers that selects it.

Part four: an honest limitations section. Name any pair of leaves the instrument cannot genuinely separate and state what further question would separate them, and supply one tiebreaker question for the respondent whose answers turn out to be jointly inconsistent.

Our audience is teenagers with no philosophy coursework and we have twenty-five minutes per session, so please keep the whole thing usable inside that time. Do not include a bibliography or citations; our library holds essentially nothing on this subject, and I would rather have the argument than a list of sources.
