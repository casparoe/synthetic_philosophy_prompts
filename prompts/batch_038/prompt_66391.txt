ok so — I'm a third year doing physics with a philosophy minor, and this semester I'm in a modal epistemology seminar. We've just done three weeks on whether conceivability is a guide to possibility: Chalmers on something being conceivable on a first pass vs. it surviving ideal reflection, Yablo's idea that imagining gives you evidence that can get undercut when you look harder, and Kung's split between the qualitative content of what you actually picture and the stuff you just stipulate on top of it. Its all very good but I couldn't see it biting on anything real, and then it turns out it does, in a place I wasn't expecting.

Since September I've been interning two days a week in the first-pass screening group at a patent office. Mostly mechanical and energy-related applications. My job is to read the application and write a one-paragraph recommendation on whether the thing could possibly work as described, which a senior examiner then signs off on. I get through about 25 a week with 20-25 minutes each, no lab access, no search budget, and I'm not a lawyer. My supervisor says that where a claim looks physically impossible we can ask the applicant to supply a working model, but that request is expensive for everyone and gets appealed, so I'm not supposed to recommend it just because something strikes me as weird.

Here's what I want. Take on the role of a philosopher who works on modal epistemology and who has also done real consulting work turning theory into procedures for non-specialists — someone who knows what gets lost in translation. Think carefully. Before the procedure itself, spend some space brainstorming which pieces of the conceivability literature could survive contact with a 25-minute screening slot and which ones have to be thrown out, and say what you're dropping and why. Then give me the procedure: the ordered questions I ask of each application, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the exits available (pass to full examination / send back for more detail / recommend a working-model request / refer upward to someone else) and what triggers each. Also tell me which exits aren't mine at all because they're questions of policy or proportionality rather than questions about what's possible.

The thing it has to get right is the direction of the guide. My physics training makes me want to reject anything that smells like free energy, and I'm pretty sure "I can't see how this would work" is doing illegitimate work in my head. So the procedure has to be explicit about what it licenses and what it doesn't: my guess is that it can only ever support a "this can't work" recommendation when I can actually derive a problem from the description as written, and not when the description is just too thin for me to picture anything. Those feel like they should be different exits and I have no idea how to keep them apart under time pressure.

Then run it on three applications I've got this week. Details are roughly right, identifying stuff changed:

1. A tabletop device with a flywheel, permanent magnets and a lever arm. The applicant has helpfully included an energy balance for one full cycle, and his own numbers don't balance — 100 J in, 138 J out — with the surplus attributed to "the geometry of the magnetic circuit". I can see exactly how it's meant to move, and I can also see exactly where the arithmetic goes.

2. Claims net electrical output from a "resonant coupling to the ambient quantum vacuum via a novel lattice configuration". Sixty pages, lots of detailed drawings of the enclosure and the wiring, not one equation, no composition for the lattice, no account of where the energy comes from. I can't find a contradiction in it, but mostly because there's hardly anything there to contradict.

3. A copper-tin alloy, processed in a stated sequence of steps, claimed to carry current with no measurable resistance at room temperature. Full composition, full processing route, described measurement setup, and nothing in it that violates any conservation law or any physics I know. It's just that literally every physicist I've met would laugh at it, and I want to reject it.

Be honest in a final section about what the procedure can't do: the kinds of impossibility it will miss, the places where it leans on my knowing less physics than the applicant does, and which steps are really just my own judgment wearing a procedural costume. Please no citations, no bibliography, no reading list — I can't look anything up and I've already done the seminar readings.
