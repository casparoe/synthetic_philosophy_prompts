A friend and I argued for two hours last night about whether a deliberating body ought to vote on the premises one at a time and let the conclusion fall out, or to vote directly on the conclusion. She thinks premise-first voting is procedural fetishism that lets whoever controls the list of premises control the outcome; I think conclusion-first voting throws away the only thing the group actually reasons with. Neither of us moved, and I would rather stage the dispute than continue it.

I run a 75-minute section of 18 second-years who have had one lecture on social choice and none on judgment aggregation. Design an in-class activity that gets them to run into the discursive dilemma on their own: the same people, voting sincerely on the same question, arriving at opposite answers depending on whether they aggregate premise by premise or vote on the conclusion. Better still, I want at least one of them to realize before I say so that a chair who picks the voting order could have produced that reversal deliberately.

Please supply: a decision problem with two premises whose conjunction entails the conclusion, chosen so that it reads like a real institutional choice — a disciplinary panel, a grant committee, a hiring shortlist — rather than a logic exercise; hidden role cards that guarantee the paradoxical profile across 18 players instead of leaving it to chance, including at least one cardholder who accepts the conclusion while rejecting a premise and must nevertheless sign onto the group's verdict; a distribution and secrecy procedure; a timeline for the two voting rounds; and an incentive scheme that gives students a reason to vote their card honestly rather than strategically, using something other than money. Then a debrief that maps each moment of the activity onto the distinction, and that separates the case of a court applying shared law to disputed facts from the case of a body whose members genuinely disagree about the reasoning — I want them to see the dilemma is not only a judicial artifact.

Finally, be explicit about what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, including the moment someone accuses me of having rigged the cards, which I fully expect and do not consider a failure of the exercise; and be frank about which feature of the problem the activity cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by hand — above all, what is lost when you demonstrate a single profile while the general impossibility result is a claim about all of them.
