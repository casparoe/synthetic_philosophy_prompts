For two years I have audited a graduate reading group on radical translation, working through the second chapter of Quine's *Word and Object* and his later essay on ontological relativity. The group is led by a senior philosopher I will call H., and she is unusually decisive: whenever we construct a case, she announces a verdict and defends it. I want to predict her verdict on the case we are raising next week, and to work out which principle she is actually running, since several seem to fit her record equally well.

Her verdicts so far, in the order she gave them:

1. Gavagai, uttered at a rabbit sighting. Two manuals — one taking the term for rabbits, one taking it for undetached rabbit parts, with the identity predicate and the general terms adjusted to compensate — reproduce every disposition to assent and dissent the natives could ever display. Neither is more correct; there is no further fact that could make one correct.
2. The same structure with rabbit stages in place of rabbit parts. Same verdict.
3. A member proposed settling it by interrogation: point to the same rabbit at two moments and ask whether it is the same gavagai; ask whether an ear is a gavagai. She rejected the proposal — the natives' answers are themselves sentences whose translation is revisable, and the compensating manual simply reads those answers differently too. Interrogation adds no determinacy.
4. A hypothetical language containing nothing but occasion sentences keyed to present stimulation, with no general terms, no quantification, no identity, and no theoretical vocabulary. Here, she says, translation is fully determinate: stimulus meanings pin the manual down and nothing is left over to be inscrutable.
5. Suppose the field linguist had perfect knowledge of the natives' neurophysiology — every neural event correlated with every utterance. No help whatsoever. This is not a question of missing evidence.
6. Two translators, each omniscient about all the behavioral facts, produce manuals that agree on the truth value of every sentence of the native language but assign different objects to the native terms, and each insists the other is wrong. She says they are not disagreeing about anything: the dispute has no content.
7. A term buried in the natives' theoretical vocabulary — their word for whatever they posit to explain a recurring phenomenon, embedded in generalizations far beyond observation. Here she says the indeterminacy is worse, not better: two manuals can match all the observational sentences and still disagree about which theoretical sentences are true.

Next week's case turns the apparatus on us. Take a native speaker of English who learned "rabbit" in the ordinary way, who can point, count, and ask about identity and persistence, who has no evidential gap of any kind with respect to her own usage, and who is not being translated by anyone. Is there a fact of the matter whether her term refers to rabbits or to undetached rabbit parts? Put differently: is the homophonic translation of her idiolect into itself uniquely correct, or merely one manual among equally good rivals?

Please give me:

- The candidate principles that could generate all seven verdicts — for instance, that there are no meaning facts beyond dispositions to verbal behavior; that reference is determinate only relative to a background language, so the puzzles in (1)–(3) are about relativity rather than about competing facts; that indeterminacy is a byproduct of holism plus the interpreter's freedom to redistribute reinterpretation across a language's structure; that "rival" manuals are notational variants, so nothing was ever indeterminate; and that this is ordinary underdetermination of theory by all possible evidence. State each precisely enough to be testable against the record.
- What each principle predicts for the new case, and why.
- Which of the seven verdicts are load-bearing for which principle, and where a principle has to strain: whether (4) can be squared with the first principle above, whether (6) can be squared with (7), whether (5) is compatible with the underdetermination reading.
- Your best guess at her verdict, with the reasons for preferring the principle you favor over the others.
- One or two further cases that would discriminate between the surviving principles, with what each principle predicts in them. A child raised from birth among the natives, with full mastery and no evidential gap, seems promising to me, but tell me whether it actually separates the options or merely restates the new case.
