I sit on the standards committee of a magazine that covers research culture and the sciences, and we meet Thursday to decide whether to run a story I have been reporting for five months. The piece alleges that a prominent laboratory head systematically published the analyses and datasets produced by junior staff without crediting them. Nine former members of the lab describe the practice. None of it is documented: the lab kept no contribution records, and the internal messaging channels were deleted when the relevant grants closed.

Our standards guide contains a clause I helped draft two years ago, and it is the one in play. Where a story rests on testimony alone, the accounts must corroborate structurally and not merely in content — independent witnesses should diverge on incidentals such as dates, wording, and who else was present, because near-identical testimony is a signature of coordination rather than of independent recollection. On that test the piece fails outright. The nine were first interviewed together at a gathering of former lab members, and their accounts now converge almost word for word. I think I understand why, and it is not collusion: until roughly eighteen months ago none of them had a name for what had been done, and once one of them supplied a label at that gathering, the others took it up wholesale, along with her phrasing. The convergence looks like an artifact of how long they went without a shared vocabulary, and our clause reads that artifact as evidence of bad faith. Two of the nine will go on the record; seven will not, and one of the seven now works at a rival institute and would gain professionally from the subject's fall. The subject's counsel has written to say the piece is a coordinated campaign by people passed over for authorship.

Draft the committee's decision memo: publish, publish with conditions, hold, or kill. It should say what, if anything, goes wrong in applying the clause here; propose a revision specific enough that a future editor could apply it to a case unlike this one; and propose one concrete reporting step we could complete within two weeks that would test whether the convergence survives when the borrowed label is withheld. Give the case for killing the story its full argumentative weight rather than a paragraph of throat-clearing, and keep the memo to a page.
