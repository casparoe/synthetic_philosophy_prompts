I am assembling a set of original cases for a graduate seminar on mental causation, and I need one case built to a specification rather than lifted from the existing literature. Its purpose is to determine whether the exclusion argument is special to the mental or whether it generalizes to any higher-level property that is realized by, but not identical to, a lower-level one. The case should make the generalization look as threatening as possible, but it must not smuggle in its own verdict: a case that asserts the higher-level property's causal efficacy, or that loads the description with causal vocabulary so that the conclusion is already in the premises, fails.

The case must satisfy all of the following.

1. Setting: an ordinary working practice — a municipal water plant, a food-processing line, a hospital's infection-control program, a transmission grid, a building's structural monitoring regime, a manufacturing yield problem, or something comparably mundane. No physics laboratories, no science fiction, no scenarios about the neuroscience of consciousness.

2. No mental properties: nothing in the case is a belief, desire, sensation, intention, or experience. Minds appear only in the ordinary role of the technicians doing the work, and their mental states must not form part of the causal chain in dispute.

3. Structure: an effect E that is uncontroversially physical; a lower-level configuration L that on each occasion realizes a higher-level property H; and an L such that the physical causal story for E is complete at L's level, with nothing left over for H to do.

4. Realized, not asserted, multiplicity: H must be shown to be multiply realized within the case, through at least two actual episodes in which E occurs via genuinely different lower-level configurations. The multiple realizability must be real rather than a byproduct of the describer's ignorance, and you must give the two configurations in enough physical detail that the completeness of the lower-level story is visible instead of claimed.

5. H must be a functional or role property, characterized by what it does within the system, and not a mere disjunction of the lower-level configurations realizing it. If you find you cannot meet this condition, say so explicitly and explain why, rather than quietly relaxing it.

6. Internal practice: the case must contain a practitioner who screens for or intervenes on H, who does so precisely because the L-tokens are not available to her as targets, and whose intervention is shown to work — it lowers the incidence of E across episodes in which no single lower-level configuration is held constant.

7. Banned stock material: no baseballs and windows, no pigeons pecking at scarlet rather than red, no tables and their molecules, no boiling water, no hurricanes, no droughts and crop failure, no thermostats, no genes and phenotypes.

Once the case is on the table, work through the following in order.

(a) State the three premises that drive the exclusion argument — the causal closure of the physical, the exclusion principle barring more than one sufficient cause, and the distinctness of the realized property from its realizer — in the precise forms they must take to apply to H, L, and E as you have described them. Describe the premises; do not quote anyone.

(b) Run the argument on your own case and report the verdict it delivers about H's causal status. Report it even if you think the verdict is false: the case is worth having only if the argument goes through on it as far as it honestly can.

(c) Identify which premise the case puts under pressure, and whether it does so by refuting that premise or by raising the price of holding it. Commit to one of the two.

(d) Give the defender of the exclusion argument her best rediagnosis — causal inheritance, the denial that H and L are distinct candidate causes, an appeal to proportionality or to a stricter notion of causation than the practitioner's, or the claim that your case is disanalogous to the mental one. Argue the strongest of these, not the weakest.

(e) Name the single feature of the case that is doing the work, and state what verdict the argument receives once that feature is removed.

(f) Close with whether a nonreductive physicalist can transfer the result back to the mental case, and identify the most promising disanalogy she would have to overcome.

Format: begin directly with the scenario, in one paragraph of no more than about 250 words, with no preface, no framing sentence, and no restatement of this request. Follow it immediately with a single sentence stating which premise of the exclusion argument the case puts under pressure and whether it refutes that premise or merely makes it costly. Only then proceed through (a) to (f) under those labels. No bibliography, no citations, and no references by title or date; I will supply those myself.
