I co-run a two-day crisis exercise every spring for a mixed room — about half security-studies and history people, half economists and formal theorists who do bargaining and signaling — and for four years running the same fight has broken out around hour six and never gets resolved. It's about Schelling, basically, and whether his account of deterrence is a theory or a piece of folklore that happened to be around during the Cold War.

The strategic-studies half of the room says: the point of The Strategy of Conflict and the later arms-influence stuff is that what deters isn't a threat you'd rationally execute, it's the deliberate manufacture of shared risk. You make your own response partially out of your hands — delegation, alerting, mobilization, public commitments, physically moving pieces into harm's way so that escalation could happen by accident or by someone lower down — and now the opponent has to reckon with a probability of catastrophe that neither of you controls. On this view being unable to back down is an asset, feigning or actually cultivating a hair trigger is a technique, and the fact that the superpowers ran crises right up to the edge repeatedly and never went over it is the track record. They also insist the formalists are reading Schelling as if he were solving a game when he was describing a craft.

The formal half says: a threat that is ex post suboptimal is not credible, full stop, backward induction eats it, and if you want it to survive you need a commitment technology, which Schelling never supplies — he just assumes the player can bind himself and then congratulates him for having bound himself. They say the rationality-of-irrationality move is unfalsifiable: if a leader backs down he was rational, if he doesn't he was strategically mad, so nothing counts against the theory. They say the chicken framing is doing sneaky work, since ordinary mixed-strategy and equilibrium analysis of that game already generates risk of disaster without anyone having to be or seem irrational. And they say the forty-plus years without a nuclear exchange is one sample from a process we can't rerun, so citing it as confirmation is survivorship bias dressed as evidence.

Then there's a third group that shows up sometimes with the incomplete-information models — private types, reputations, audience costs — and claims these vindicate Schelling by showing that seeming irrational is just being uncertain-about, while the formalists say those models replace him, since what's doing the work is the type space and not anything about relinquishing control.

My working hypothesis, which I want you to attack rather than flatter: the two camps aren't disagreeing about the solution to any game. They're disagreeing about what's allowed to be endogenous. The formalists fix the game tree, the payoff functions and the common-knowledge structure and then ask what's rational inside it; the Schellingians think almost all the interesting strategic action consists of changing those things — altering the tree, altering what the other side believes about your response rule, altering who's holding the leash — and they treat the ability to alter them as the strategic asset. So when the formalist says commitment is assumed, not explained, and the Schellingian says of course it's assumed, that's the whole content of the theory, the argument may just be two different definitions of what counts as an explanation, or two different objects of study sharing the word deterrence. A weaker version of my hypothesis: the disagreement is really about the information structure, and if you recast everything as a game where a player's response rule is private and possibly non-optimizing, most of the heat dissolves and what's left is an empirical question about how often leaders can actually signal a type they don't have.

So: diagnose it. Is this substantive, verbal (about rational, about credible, about theory), a disagreement over standards of evidence and what counts as data, or two research programs answering different questions under one label? Use what each side actually says, not a summary of deterrence history. Tell me whether the unfalsifiability charge lands or whether it's really an underdetermination point that cuts both ways, and whether the historical record — lots of crises, no bang — can bear on the dispute at all or is just silent. Then give me what would count as progress on your diagnosis: a distinction, a theorem, a lab experiment with real subjects and real stakes-for-them, archival evidence about specific decisions, a reframing of the question, or some combination. And if your honest read is that no progress should be expected and the two sides should just stop talking past each other, say that and say why. Last thing: I have to write next year's exercise brief, and I need to know whether to teach the relinquish-control moves as tools people can use or as stories people tell about themselves — so give me something I can act on.
