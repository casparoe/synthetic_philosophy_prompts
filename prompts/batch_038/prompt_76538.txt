I sit on a nine-person labor relations panel at a mid-size public university. We're not a court — we hear grievances and interpret the recognition agreement we signed with the grad student union, and we're explicitly told not to brief statutory labor law, just our own agreement and our own precedent. Here's the case, which has eaten two sessions already. The union, in a dispute over stipends and health coverage, told its members (all TAs or graders) to keep teaching their sections, hold office hours, and finish the semester, but to not submit final grades — no grades at all, not late ones, not partial ones — until the administration reopens bargaining. The registrar says that knocks out financial-aid disbursements for roughly 1,800 students and delays transcripts for people applying to jobs and med school. The charge we recieved from the administration says this is a strike in substance, that the no-interruption clause covers it, and that the union is deliberately aiming its leverage at students who have no seat at the table. The union's answer is that grading is a discretionary act of professional judgment rather than labor performed under the article that defines covered work, that its members are still working, and that the harm to students is caused by the administration's refusal to bargain, not by the timing of a spreadsheet entry.

Our chair wants something circulated before Tuesday, because she thinks we'll otherwise vote 5-4 along lines nobody can articulate afterwards. What makes this genuinely hard is a doctrine inherited from a prior chair's 2019 opinion that nobody has ever tried to apply to a partial action like this one. She laid out two tests, both of which have to be passed before the panel will treat a concerted work stoppage as protected rather than as improper pressure: an own-labor test (are the members withholding something that is theirs to withhold — their own effort — or are they using a position of trust over third parties as a lever?) and an avoidability test (could the employer make the harm stop at trivial cost to itself by conceding the specific demand the members are making? if it couldn't, the action is presumed to be aimed at the third party rather than the employer). I think the avoidability test does more work than anyone on the panel admits, and I also think it can be gamed, since a union can always pick a demand the employer genuinely can't meet cheaply, which would quietly turn the test into a rule against demanding much at all.

Draft the opinion I'll put in front of the panel. I need a bottom line they can actually vote on — uphold the charge, dismiss it, or something in between like a conditional ruling with a cure period, and if there's a deadline attached to the cure, say what it is and what happens when it lapses. I need both prongs applied to these facts rather than defined at me, including what we do with the grading-isn't-covered-labor argument, since that one could decide the case on wording alone and I'd rather not pretend it can't. I need criteria general enough that we can reuse them next year when somebody tries a work-to-rule slowdown, or a refusal to write recommendation letters, instead of a grades freeze. And I need a serious steelmanned paragraph on the strongest case against whatever you recommend — the union side isn't stupid, and a colleague who's been on this panel for a decade thinks the administration's framing boils down to the claim that workers may only strike in ways that don't work, which he says is a way of granting the right and taking back the weapon. Under 900 words, and don't cite law or scholarship, we'll add references ourselves if we need them. It should read like a document a body can act on, not a philosophy paper — I'm in the history department, so I know how that goes.
