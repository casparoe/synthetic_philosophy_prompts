A colleague of mine in philosophy of physics is drafting a short piece arguing that Bertrand's chord paradox is not a paradox at all but a piece of sloppy phrasing, and his framing is the familiar rhetorical question about how long a piece of string is: until you say which string you mean and how it is to be measured, there is no fact anyone could get wrong, and the question merely looks like one that has an answer. On his account, a chord chosen at random is exactly such a question — the random-endpoints, random-radius, and random-midpoint readings deliver one third, one half, and one quarter not because the principle of indifference is untrustworthy but because three different questions were hiding behind a single sentence. He then extends the analogy to the well-known symmetry response, the demand that an acceptable answer be indifferent to the size and the position of the circle, which is supposed to narrow the three readings down to one; on his telling, that demand is no different from insisting you fix a unit of length before measuring anything, a notational stipulation dressed up as a discovery, so the paradox dissolves without ever being solved.

Please audit the string analogy as he deploys it. Set out the mapping explicitly — what in the chord problem plays the role of the string, of the measuring procedure, of the person asking, and of the missing specification of how to measure — and then identify where the correspondence fails, what features of the source the analogy quietly imports into a target that lacks them (in particular, anything about whether there is a determinate fact of the matter once a physical randomizing device is actually in place, such as scattering straws over a large sheet with a circle drawn on it and taking the chords they cut out), and whether his two conclusions survive on their own merits: that the paradox is merely verbal, and that the invariance argument amounts to nothing but a choice of units. If you think some other analogy better captures what goes wrong in Bertrand's problem, name it and say where it too runs out.

Write in American English, as exactly five numbered points, each one or two sentences, with no introduction, no conclusion, and no framing remarks. Take the five points to be, in this order: the correspondence mapping; the disanalogies that bear on his use of the analogy, distinguished from those that are merely decorative; what the analogy smuggles in that the target lacks; a verdict on each of the two conclusions, including whether the analogy was carrying either of them; and the better analogy with its own limits stated.
