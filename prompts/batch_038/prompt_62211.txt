I lead a small analysis team at a grid operator. We put probability estimates on rare failure modes and our regulator scores them with a Brier-type rule, so at the end of the day we have to hand over one number. Trouble is a lot of those numbers rest on a handful of past incidents plus modeling choices a reasonable person would make differently, and I've been reading about imprecise probabilities — sets of priors, interval credence, that dilation business where getting new evidence makes your range wider rather than narrower — and I suspect we're manufacturing precision we don't have.

Write me a procedure my analysts can actually run when they build one of these estimates. When should the uncertainty live as a set or interval instead of a point; how do you carry that through updating, including the awkward cases where the data makes you less precise; how do you choose between the decision rules people use when you've got a set instead of a number; and where in the pipeline are we forced to collapse to a single figure for the scorer, what exactly gets thrown away at that step, and how should we log it so the number isn't standing naked on its own.

Then run it on a concrete case: probability that a certain class of transformer fault cascades, nine events in fifteen years, two competing failure models that disagree by about an order of magnitude, and a sensor rollout that will improve the data but also change what counts as an event.

Also flag the two or three steps that are really judgment calls dressed up as procedure. No headings, bullets, bold or tables please — just continuous prose, like an email.
