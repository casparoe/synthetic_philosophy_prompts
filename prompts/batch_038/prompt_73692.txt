Right, so I'm a third-year PhD student in a fairly interpretability-flavoured group, and in about six weeks I'm giving a talk at a joint session our lab runs with the philosophy of science department. The room will be maybe fifteen people: several circuits / sparse-autoencoder types who basically live and die by faithfulness scores and benchmark tables, two philosophers who work on causal explanation and interventionist accounts and who have very strong feelings about how causal variables get carved up, and a couple of systems neuroscientists who turned up last year and were politely baffled — they do lesion studies and think in terms of degeneracy and reverse inference. My supervisor is on sabbatical so I can't lean on her to tell me what's coming, and I've mostly been arguing with a mate who does SAE evals, whose verdict so far is that my view is "just functionalism with a rebrand", which I don't think is fair but also can't quite refute crisply.

The thesis I'm defending is that mechanism identity in mech interp should be cashed out interventionally rather than structurally. Roughly: two implementations count as the same mechanism just in case they support the same profile of counterfactual interventions — same input/output dependencies under the same set of do-operations, pitched at the same grain of variable. Two consequences follow that people in my subfield tend to resist. (i) A subcircuit can be graph-identical across training checkpoints, or across two models of very different size, and still be a *different* mechanism, because its intervention profile shifted — I think a lot of "the circuit moved" reports are actually this. (ii) More provocatively, a mechanism can be preserved through complete re-routing: a model can carry on implementing what is literally the same mechanism with different components entirely, so anomaly detection that hunts for the training-time circuit as a structural object is hunting the wrong thing, and will both miss genuine drift and fire on harmless re-implementation. My punchline is that detectors should be scored on whether they flag shifts in the intervention profile, and that graph-matching style benchmarks are measuring a proxy which comes apart from the target in exactly the cases that matter for safety. Supporting material I'll have: a small toy transformer where I've tracked a two-layer induction-ish behaviour through training and found a stretch where the component-level attribution changes substantially but the ablation-and-patch profile doesn't, plus an argument that any structural criterion either smuggles in a grain-of-variables choice or collapses into extensional equivalence over cases you'd want to distinguish.
