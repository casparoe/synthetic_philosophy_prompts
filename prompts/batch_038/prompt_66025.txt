I retired four years ago after a career in isotope geochemistry, most of it spent on provenance: determining, from isotopic fingerprints, which source a given rock or parcel of water actually came from. Only recently have I taken up philosophy in earnest, and I have become absorbed by the thesis Kripke made famous — that origin is essential, so that you could not have come from a different sperm and a different egg, any more than a given table could have been made from a different piece of wood.

Last month I gave a dinner for six, and two of my guests argued about this thesis for the better part of an hour. I have tried below to reproduce their exchange faithfully. Dr. Halloran teaches metaphysics; Dr. Mehta directs an IVF laboratory. Neither was making a fool of himself or herself, and I could not decide who had the better of it. I report the argument as a bystander and have deliberately not signalled where my own sympathies lie, which is the honest position: they moved several times over the evening.

1. **Halloran** stated the thesis and its ground. You are a living human organism; what makes you this organism rather than another is that you developed from the particular zygote you in fact developed from. A different sperm would have meant a different genome, hence a different zygote, hence a different organism and a different person — even had that person been carried by the same mother, raised in the same house, and given the same name. She added that some such principle is indispensable: if origin does not fix which individual a counterfactual situation contains, nothing else does, and we are left with no account at all of what it is for a merely possible situation to be one in which you exist.

2. **Mehta** answered with a case from his clinic. Suppose the couple had been delayed by two hours: the same egg, the same ejaculate, the same parents, the same pregnancy wanted and planned for the same reasons. A different one of the several hundred million sperm would have arrived first. On Halloran's account, the child born is a stranger, and the person sitting at the other end of my table would never have existed at all. He said that a thesis on which the existence of a particular person turns on a footrace among cells — with nothing differing about the parents' intentions, the gestation, or the life that follows — is not a deep insight into individuality but a reductio of the thesis. He noted further that nobody knows or could ever know which sperm it was, so the thesis cannot be applied even where it holds.

3. **Halloran** accepted the case and rejected the reductio. Yes: your existence was a matter of extraordinary chance, and nearly every counterfactual about your parents' evening that evening results in your never existing. She said this is not an embarrassment but something independently evident — of the immense number of people who might have been born to those parents, one was born, and there is no reason to suppose that all the others were you under slightly different genes. As to the epistemic point, she said a metaphysical thesis is not weakened by our inability to verify it; we do not know which atoms compose the table either, and that does not make the table's origin indeterminate.

4. **Mehta** then pressed on what the sperm and the egg are supposed to be. Either they are individuated by their genetic content — in which case the thesis collapses into genetic essentialism, and Halloran must hold that a single base pair's difference makes a different person, which she cannot consistently do, since each of us accumulates somatic mutations by the thousand and she surely does not think we perish every time one occurs — or they are individuated by their matter and cellular lineage, in which case the essential origin is not a determinate object. The oocyte has been arrested in prophase of meiosis since before its owner was born and has rebuilt its cytoplasmic contents many times over as it grew; the sperm was assembled from scratch within the preceding months out of whatever the man ate. On the second horn, there is no fact of the matter about which sperm it was, because a sperm is not the kind of entity that carries a fixed identity independent of how we choose to trace it.

5. **Halloran** replied that vagueness at the margins is entirely compatible with a necessity claim at the core, and that Mehta's own science supports her once read correctly. Chimerism and twinning show that a genome is not a person: two actual people can share essentially one genome, and one actual person can carry two. That is exactly why she grounds individuality not in genotype but in origin — the single zygote from which the organism in fact developed. Cloning points the same way: an individual produced by transferring my nucleus into an enucleated donor egg would have my nuclear genome and would manifestly not be me, which shows that something other than genetic constitution is doing the individuating.

6. **Mehta** turned both of these against her. Twinning cuts the other way: if one zygote can yield two persons, then origin does not determine a unique individual, and during the days before splitting there was no fact about which of the two the later person was. A principle that cannot pick out one individual from an origin has not yet been shown to be a principle of individuation. Cloning, likewise, shows only that neither genome nor origin suffices: what makes the clone not-me is not that its origin differs — every sibling's origin differs — but that it is not the same continuously existing organism. Identity is a fact about actual persistence through time, and counterfactual talk about other gametes is a way of settling how much of our description we are prepared to carry across worlds, not a discovery about what is there to be carried. He closed with mitochondrial donation: a child conceived from the intended parents' nuclear genome and a donor's mitochondria and cytoplasm has a fractionated origin, and Halloran must either say the child came from the intended mother's egg, giving up the egg as the essential item, or from the donor's, giving up the genome — with no principled way to choose between them.

I should add one contribution of my own, which neither of them engaged and which I cannot evaluate. In my old discipline, provenance is almost never all-or-nothing. A sample is modelled as a mixture of end-members with fractional contributions, and two samples from a single source are distinguished only statistically, by distributions rather than by identity. I cannot tell whether that has any bearing on this dispute or whether it is simply an artifact of a science that measures rather than individuates.
