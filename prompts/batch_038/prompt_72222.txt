I am taking a metaphysics seminar this term, and our final assignment permits a work of fiction provided it engages the philosophical problem directly rather than gesturing at it. I have chosen the dispute between endurantism and perdurantism, and I have constructed the following scenario, which I would like you to develop into a story of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words.

The setting is a near-future city that has installed public booths, colloquially called apertures, which reconstruct a high-fidelity scene from a specified earlier moment out of delayed light and other surviving traces. They are strictly observational: nothing can be transmitted, touched, or answered, and use is metered by the minute and dear. The protagonist is a widow and an unapologetic four-dimensionalist who pays nightly to revisit one particular summer afternoon from her marriage — an earlier temporal part of her husband. Her consolation is precise rather than vague: on her view that stage exists, simpliciter, and has not been annihilated but is merely not located here and now. Her husband's mother, an equally articulate endurantist, holds that what the widow watches does not exist at all, and that this doctrine quietly converts loss into distance, leaving grief unintelligible.

Please observe the following constraints. Let the metaphysics emerge from dialogue, gesture, and the texture of the reconstructed afternoons rather than from exposition; the terms of art should be all but absent. Give both positions their strongest case, so that a reader who has never encountered the debate feels the pull of each. Include one sustained scene in which the two women argue about whether the man is dead or merely elsewhere, in ordinary language. Avoid the familiar personal-identity apparatus — no teleporters, no fission cases, no transplants — and do not let the plot turn on a malfunction or on a revelation that reverses what is real; the ending should follow from a choice, and the mounting cost of the minutes should be what forces it. If the story inclines toward one side, let it do so through imagery and consequence rather than through a speech.
