In *The Querist* (1735), Berkeley advances two theses about money that seem to pull apart. The first is that money is worth nothing in itself and that its whole nature is bookkeeping: he asks whether the true idea of money is not "altogether that of a ticket or counter" (query 23), and whether, the denominations being retained, the bullion could vanish entirely while things were still rated, bought, and sold and industry still promoted (query 26). The second is that money's usefulness consists wholly in its effect on effort: "Whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour?" (query 5). He adds that "less money swiftly circulating" is "in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating" (query 22).

Please explain what conceptual work the second thesis is doing. If money is merely a ticket for transferring claims, why should the number of tickets a nation holds make any difference to the amount of labour performed? Say precisely what, on Berkeley's account, makes an increase in tickets productive rather than merely more numerous, and how that differs from the familiar claim that adding money simply raises all prices in proportion — and then explain what role the claim about swift circulation plays, given that it seems to allow that the number of tickets is what could have been varied by varying something else instead.

Finally, test the theory against a case Berkeley does not consider: a paper currency whose tickets expire, each of which must be surrendered to the issuer for a replacement within a month or become worthless. Explain whether Berkeley's commitments rank such a currency above or below durable coin, and whether that ranking reveals the industry thesis or the ticket thesis as the load-bearing element of his account of money.
