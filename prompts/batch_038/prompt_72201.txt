So my brother and I just took over our family's apple orchard — 40 acres, third generation — and somehow I ended up being the one who makes the spray calls, which I absolutely did not volunteer for. Codling moth is the main thing, some fungal stuff on top. Neighbor to the west has maybe sixty hives and has already let me know he'll find out if I do something stupid. My partner thinks we shouldn't be killing anything, my brother thinks we're farmers and the moths are the enemy and I'm being soft. I've spent about two months reading philosophy on this at night and it has not once helped me decide anything, so before I read another word I want to know what I should even be demanding of a framework.

Write me the spec. What does a framework for deciding whether and how to use an insecticide have to deliver to count as adequate for a person who is actually holding the sprayer? For each requirement give me the case or the consideration that motivates it, not just the label. Mark which ones are non-negotiable and which ones are really just the taste of one camp — I suspect "don't treat individuals as mere means" and "protect ecosystem function" are both in that pile and they might not even be the same kind of thing. And if some subset of the requirements can't all be satisfied at once, say so explicitly, because I have a feeling that's the real finding and nobody tells you.

Some of the stuff I keep tripping over that I want the list to actually cover:

- A friend of mine says there are two questions everyone runs together and that most arguments don't know which one they're answering: "may I kill these insects" and "may I arrange things so that there are fewer insects than there otherwise would have been." One is about individuals who exist, one is about populations. Does a framework have to keep those separate to be usable?

- Not spraying is not a no-kill option. If the crop goes, some other insects boom, the voles and the deer move in, my neighbor's trees get hit and he sprays harder than I would have. So whatever I adopt has to score the abstention on the same ledger as the action, and I don't think most of what I've read does that.

- I cannot estimate insect numbers and neither can anyone. A framework that needs a per-acre body count from me is asking for a number that does not exist. Is that a fatal objection to the whole expected-value way of doing this, or just an engineering problem?

- The same product applied differently — right timing, trunk band only instead of whole canopy, or a mating-disruption program instead of a kill — changes who dies and how much. So a framework that grades "spray: yes / no" is grading the wrong object, and I'd like that written into the requirements rather than left as my gripe.

- Then there's the bee guy, who is a constraint that has nothing to do with the pests at all. Where does a requirement about third parties who aren't even in the target population sit relative to the ones about the target population?

Then, briefly — a line or two each, no more — how four positions I actually run into do against the list: total abstention on principle, ordinary integrated pest management as it's practiced here, the view that insects don't matter at all so it's a pure agronomy question, and the view that we should be worrying about whether wild insect lives are net-miserable in the first place. I mostly want the scorecard so I know which one to argue with at Thanksgiving, so keep it short.

No references, no reading list, no titles — I can't chase anything down and I don't trust remembered citations anyway. And whatever you do, end with a line that is exactly "Verdict:" followed by the single requirement from your list that knocks out the most contenders, ten words or less.
