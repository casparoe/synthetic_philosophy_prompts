I am preparing a chapter on the monetary queries of Berkeley's Querist (first published 1735, and consisting of several hundred numbered questions put to the public), and in trying to state his position as sharply as I can I have produced an argument whose conclusion I cannot accept. I want a diagnosis of the argument, not a summary of Berkeley's economics.

The premises I take from the text itself, with the queries that supply them (numbering as in the collected edition):

(A1) Money is not wealth and is useful only instrumentally: "Whether money be not only so far useful, as it stirreth up industry, enabling men mutually to participate the fruits of each other's labour?" (Q5)

(A2) The instrument is therefore replaceable by anything with the same effect: "Whether any other means, equally conducing to excite and circulate the industry of mankind, may not be as useful as money." (Q6)

(A3) What matters is the circulation, not the stuff that carries it: "Whether, other things being given, as climate, soil, etc., the wealth be not proportioned to the industry, and this to the circulation of credit, be the credit circulated or transferred by what marks or tokens soever?" (Q21)

(A4) Quantity and speed of circulation are interchangeable: "Whether, therefore, less money swiftly circulating, be not, in effect, equivalent to more money slowly circulating? Or, whether, if the circulation be reciprocally as the quantity of coin, the nation can be a loser?" (Q22)

(A5) Money is a token, not a valuable thing: "Whether money is to be considered as having an intrinsic value, or as being a commodity, a standard, a measure, or a pledge, as is variously suggested by writers? And whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter?" (Q23) "And whether gold, silver, and paper are not tickets or counters for reckoning, recording, and transferring thereof?" (Q25)

(A6) The tokens may be removed altogether, provided the denominations survive: "Whether the denominations being retained, although the bullion were gone, things might not nevertheless be rated, bought, and sold, industry promoted, and a circulation of commerce maintained?" (Q26)

My own steps, which I believe follow from these:

(1) From (A1)–(A3) and (A5): no intrinsic property of the medium — its substance, weight, durability, or scarcity as such — contributes anything to the monetary work; the work is done entirely by the token's being generally received as a means of reckoning and transferring shares of a nation's industry.

(2) From (1) and (A4): quantity is never significant on its own, since any quantity can be matched by a compensating velocity, and velocity is a fact about how people behave rather than about the tokens. What matters is that the tokens pass and are generally received.

(3) From (A6): nor need the tokens be made of anything in particular; paper answers as well as silver, and the notes of a public bank as well as the paper of private issuers.

(4) From (2) and (3): the number of tickets a state puts into circulation is indifferent to the wealth of the nation. Holding industry, climate, and soil fixed as (A3) directs, doubling the tickets alters only what each ticket stands for; the proportions in which goods are reckoned and transferred are unchanged in substance, and the appetite to labour, which is what money is supposed to excite, is unchanged.

(5) From (4) and (A1): a state cannot impoverish itself by issuing tickets, and since the whole point of money on this account is the exciting of industry, a generous issue is a means of exciting industry that costs the nation nothing. There is accordingly no such thing as a loss to the nation from an increase in the quantity of money.

(6) But (5) cannot be right. The credit manias that collapsed in 1720 — Law's system in France and the South Sea scheme in England — Berkeley knew at first hand and wrote against, and his own practical remedy in these same queries is the sketch of a national bank, which is to say an issue governed by rules and securities rather than a licence to multiply counters. Worse, (5) renders his entire programme inert: if the number of tickets is indifferent, then the constitution of the bank, the securities behind its notes, and the limits on its issue cannot matter either, and the Querist has argued itself out of a job.

(7) So one of (A1)–(A6) or of my steps (1)–(4) has to be given up. But each of the Berkeleyan premises looks like Berkeley, and (4) and (5) look like nothing more than (1)–(3) applied consistently.

Which step is defective, and why did it look acceptable? I suspect I have slid from a claim about what the tokens are made of to a claim about how many of them there are, but I cannot say what licenses the suspicion, since (A4) appears to assert precisely that the number does not matter; and (4) also seems licensed by the ceteris paribus clause in (A3). Set out the reading on which the premises are true and the conclusion false, and say what Berkeley loses if the faulty step is rejected and whether the loss can be repaired from inside his own framework. Then price the alternatives: what a commodity or metallist theory of money, a state or chartalist theory, and a straightforward quantity theory each have to pay in order to block the argument at that point, and which of those prices is the cheapest. If you think the argument is in fact sound and that (6) is where I should give way, say so, and tell me what to do with the bank.

I would rather keep (A1)–(A3) and (A5)–(A6) than my own steps (2) and (4), though I do not yet see what rejecting those costs.
