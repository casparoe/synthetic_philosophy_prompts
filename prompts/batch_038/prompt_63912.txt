Got tangled up in something with my flatmate last night and neither of us could get out of it, so I'm outsourcing. We were arguing about nuclear deterrence — specifically the old line that a threat of massive retaliation is self-undermining, because once the missiles have actually landed, retaliating is pointless, and a threat you wouldn't carry out isn't a threat. He formalized it like this and I couldnt find the flaw:

(1) It's rational to make a threat only if it would be rational to carry it out once the trigger condition has occurred.
(2) After a first strike that has already levelled your cities, carrying out retaliation produces nothing you could still want: the losses are sunk, your population is dying, retaliation only adds to the total.
(3) So post-strike retaliation is irrational.
(4) So (by 1) the threat of massive retaliation was irrational to make, and a rational player would not have made it.
(5) Both leaderships were rational, knew the other was rational, and knew that this was known.
(6) So neither side actually expected the other to retaliate, i.e. neither was deterred by the threat of retaliation.
(7) But there was no direct superpower war and no nuclear use after 1945.
(8) So the peace wasn't produced by deterence; something else entirely explains it.
(9) Therefore the whole commitment machinery — Schelling, self-binding, the rationality of irrationality, launch-on-warning postures, delegating to a system you can't recall — was doing no explanatory work the entire time.

Here's the part that makes me suspicious of the argument rather than of (9): run it on ordinary criminal law. Punishing a murderer after the fact also produces nothing that undoes the murder; the harm is sunk; so by (1) the threat of punishment was irrational to make, no rational person was ever deterred by it, and prisons are theatre. Then run it on promises. Keeping a promise is usually costly at the moment of keeping, and the benefit was already secured when the promise was made, so by (1) no promise could rationally be made and contracts are impossible. An argument with that reach has got to be broken somewhere, and I'd rather find out where than swallow (9).

So: which step do you drop, and why did it look unobjectionable in the first place? If I have to pick, I'd rather keep (7) and (2) than (1) — (2) just seems like a true description of the situation at that moment — but I cant tell whether dropping (1) leaves me any way of explaining why the other side should have believed the threat to begin with, which was the entire reason for making it. And if you drop something else, tell me what it costs you in the criminal-law and promise versions, since those seem to survive whatever you do to the nuclear case.
