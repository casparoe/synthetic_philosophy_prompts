I sit on the five-member resolution committee of a long-running forecasting archive. The archive records timestamped, publicly visible predictions and settles them against outcomes; its whole credibility rests on settlement decisions looking mechanical rather than negotiable. We meet on Friday, the committee is split two–two, and my vote decides the case. I am a software engineer by trade with no formal training in philosophy — I have the rough sense that this dispute is really about falsification, but I could not articulate why, so please make the underlying issues explicit as you go rather than assuming I already see them.

The entry. In March 1994 a submitter, whom I will call R, recorded that the world's capacity to feed itself would hit a hard physical limit, and that this would manifest as mass famine with a death toll in the tens of millions, before the end of 2015. Our standing rule settles entries on the plain outcome by the stated date. No such famine occurred. On that rule the entry is incorrect.

R's defence, which the two members on his side find persuasive, has three strands. First, he says the mechanism was right and is still operative: demand and population grew faster than yields, as he projected, and what he did not anticipate was that the gap would be bridged for a few decades by two finite crutches — industrially fixed nitrogen and the drawdown of fossil groundwater — so a prediction about a finite system cannot be refuted by the temporary consumption of a finite buffer. Second, the entry also carried a subsidiary claim about a regional spike in staple prices within the following decade, which occurred and was settled in his favour at the time; he argues the entry was a package and should be settled as one. Third, he invokes Malthus, saying his prediction has the same shape as Malthus's argument in the Essay on the Principle of Population, that Malthus has been declared refuted for two centuries while the thing he described kept being postponed rather than disproved, and that this committee is now standing where the economists stood who wrote Malthus off in the decades after the Second World War. He asks that the entry be re-marked as unresolved and still open, with the date read as within two generations of 1994.

The other two members say this is precisely the move the archive exists to prevent: any date-bound prediction can be saved by declaring the date provisional and the mechanism sound, after which the archive measures nothing. They note that R's buffer argument does not appear anywhere in the 1994 entry; he advanced it in a 2016 forum post on our own site, and there the crutch he named was phosphorus, whereas in a later post he substituted groundwater. They ask what separates R's buffer argument from Malthus's addition of moral restraint as a further check in the later editions of the Essay — a modification introduced after the fact, aimed at exactly the cases being pressed against the earlier version — and they say that if Malthus's own amendment is a paradigm of an ad hoc rescue, so is R's.

The rule we must apply. The charter settles entries on the plain outcome by the stated date, but it carries a second clause, never yet used, permitting the committee to void an entry where the question as posed was malformed and a good-faith reading of what the submitter was asking is not the reading the wording supports. Everyone accepts that voiding differs from marking correct. Nobody agrees whether this entry qualifies. There is also pressure from the other direction: the archive publishes its calibration statistics, and this is one of our oldest open entries, so however we settle it enters the record permanently.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It should:

1. Give a recommendation for this entry — settled incorrect, voided under the malformed-question clause, or unresolved and still open — with reasoning a dissenting member could actually reply to, and a note on what each side should expect the decision to cost or gain them.
2. Take the Malthus question head-on, since it is doing real work in the debate: was Malthus wrong, or right but early, and does the answer depend on anything one could look up rather than argue? Take a position instead of surveying. In particular, say whether the addition of moral restraint is best read as a genuine refinement of the theory or as a rescue, and what the difference between those two consists in — the committee uses those words as though their meaning were settled, and I am not sure it is.
3. Say whether R's mechanism-and-buffer argument can be distinguished from an ad hoc rescue, and if so on grounds a future committee could apply without knowing anything about R. If it cannot be distinguished, say so plainly.
4. Deal with the fact that part of the entry came true. I do not know how a prediction that bundles a date-bound outcome with a mechanism should be treated, and I expect whatever we decide here will become the pattern for every later bundled entry.
5. Propose a short rule, in language we could paste into the charter, governing date-bound long-horizon entries, together with the one or two cases where you expect that rule to yield a result we would find embarrassing.

Please treat the two members who favour R as arguing in good faith: their worry that we are sliding from 'has not happened yet' to 'will not happen' is not obviously mistaken, and the memo should say what is right about it even if the recommendation goes against them. Write in British English, and keep it to a length a committee will actually read.
