Consider the following cluster of practices, all of them settled features of ordinary legal systems.

1. In most jurisdictions the state's power to prosecute an offence lapses once a fixed period has run: a few years for minor offences, decades for grave ones, no limit at all for homicide in some. Take a serious violent offence carrying a twenty-year period. Twenty-two years on, the perpetrator is identified beyond reasonable doubt by a DNA match; the physical evidence is intact, the witnesses are available, he never fled, and he is no longer dangerous. The prosecution is nonetheless dismissed — not on the merits, not by acquittal, but as time-barred.

2. The machinery around the bar is elaborate. In many systems the period is suspended, or does not begin, while the suspect is a fugitive, while the victim is a minor, or until the injury could reasonably have been discovered. In some systems expiry removes the court's power to hear the case altogether and must be declared by the court on its own motion; in others the bar is a defence the accused must raise and may waive, so that a prosecution of a time-barred offence proceeds validly if nobody objects.

3. The gravest offences under international law — genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, aggression — are expressly imprescriptible: no lapse of time bars prosecution, and international courts have tried defendants in their eighties for acts committed decades earlier.

4. Legislatures repeatedly attempt to undo the bar for categories of offence thought to have been under-prosecuted, with split results. When a state legislature revived an already-expired criminal limitation period for child sex offences, the United States Supreme Court held the revival unconstitutional as an ex post facto law; over the same period, several legislatures opened temporary windows in which civil claims for the same conduct could be brought however long ago it occurred, and those windows have largely survived challenge.

I would like an analysis of what this cluster presupposes about the relation between law and morality — the question at the centre of the dispute between source-based and merit-based accounts of law. Specifically:

(a) Reconstruct the implicit theory. What has to be true of a legal wrong for a calendar fact, together with the diligence of investigators, to extinguish it? Does the practice suppose that the wrong itself ceases to exist, or only that the state's capacity to respond to it ceases? Which of these do the details in item 2 commit it to — dismissal without acquittal, the waivability of the bar in some systems, the survival of parallel civil claims, and the fact that a time-barred person may still truthfully be described as guilty?

(b) Ask whether the merit-based side can absorb the practice. Could a theorist who holds that law's content depends on moral facts still make sense of prescription — for instance by locating the relevant wrong not in the act alone but in a standing institutional relation between state and offender, which the state can lose its standing to pursue? Would that be a genuine alternative account or a source-based account wearing different clothes? Conversely, does item 3 look like morality constraining law from outside, and if so do items 1 and 3 presuppose consistent things when they sit within a single legal order, or must one be read as a concession to the other?

(c) Explain the asymmetry in item 4. What does the difference in treatment between the revived criminal period and the revived civil period presuppose about what the time-bar creates: a right, an immunity, a limit on adjudicative power, or a mere procedural default available to whoever bothers to invoke it?

(d) Distinguish what the cluster strictly presupposes from what merely fits it comfortably. How much of it can be discharged by administrative and epistemic considerations — evidence decays, memory fades, the deterrent and expressive returns of late prosecution decline, people are entitled to organise their lives around a settled past — so that no account of legal validity is engaged at all? Identify, if you can, the precise point at which the practice becomes philosophically loaded rather than merely expedient, and say which of the four items passes it.

(e) Say what would have to change if the presupposed view were replaced by its rival. What would a criminal law that treated moral desert as directly constitutive of liability look like here — general imprescriptibility, abolition of the bar for grave offences only, a discretionary judicial power to lift it — and what would each version cost in the goods the current practice is usually said to serve?

(f) Finally, if you think this framing is the wrong lens — that the cluster does not in fact discriminate between source-based and merit-based accounts of law — say so plainly, and describe what feature a legal practice would need in order to be genuinely probative between them.

Citations are not required; please argue from the practices as described above.
