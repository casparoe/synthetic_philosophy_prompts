Ok, I've been turning this over for weeks and I want a reality check from someone who isn't me. I've talked myself into a fairly specific view about higher-order evidence and I genuinely can't tell whether it's defensible or just comfortable. The view: when you get good reason to think your own thinking is compromised — the stock cases, being told you were slipped something that makes you bad at the kind of puzzle you're working on, being thirty hours into a shift, finding out that everyone as informed as you came out the other way — that evidence never bears on the first-order question. It doesn't make p less likely. What it makes less likely is that you actually carried out the reasoning right, i.e. that you believe p for the reasons you take yourself to. So the correct response is always to distrust your own process, never to downgrade the conclusion itself. And if that's correct then real epistemic akrasia — flatly believing p while also believing that believing p isn't rational for you — should be straight impossible. Any apparent case is one of three things: the person is unsure what they believe, or they're oscillating between two states over time, or they're just being irrational and we shouldn't build the theory around that.

So: what would count against this? I want the defeaters ranked, fatal vs. merely annoying, and I want you to separate the ones someone holding my view should concede really do count against them from the ones they get to wave off. Some places I'm already nervous about, feel free to push harder. (a) Are there cases where the "you botched the process" reinterpretation has nothing to grab onto — I keep coming back to ones where the belief is too simple to botch, like reading a number off a dial or seeing that your friend is annoyed, and ones where you checked and rechecked and the higher-order news arrives anyway. What's the strongest case of that shape?
