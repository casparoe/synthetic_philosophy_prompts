**STUDENT ANSWER (extract, ~780 words):**

Both Kripke-style semantics and Lewis's counterpart theory try to give a truth-condition for de re modal claims like "Humphrey might have won the election". The transworld identity theorist says this is true iff there is a possible world w and an object x such that x = Humphrey and x wins in w. That is, the very same person exists in more than one world, and wins in one of them. Lewis rejects this. On his view every object is world-bound, existing in exactly one world, and "Humphrey might have won" is true iff there is some world w containing a counterpart of Humphrey who wins. The counterpart relation is a relation of qualitative similarity, not of identity. Which similarities matter is fixed by context: when we ask whether Humphrey might have won we are holding fixed his political career and his character, so the relevant counterparts are people very like him in those respects.

Lewis's motivation is the problem of accidental intrinsics. If one and the same Humphrey is seated in the actual world and standing in w, then he both has and lacks an intrinsic property, which violates Leibniz's Law. Transworld identity is therefore simply incoherent, and counterpart theory is the only option that keeps possible-worlds semantics without this contradiction. This is why Lewis needs counterparts.

Kripke's objection is usually put with the Humphrey example. When we ask whether Humphrey might have won, we do not care about the fate of some other person who resembles Humphrey in another world. We want to know about Humphrey himself. But counterpart theory's answer is that a different person, in a different world, wins. So the theory has not answered our question; it has answered a question about somebody else and offered that as an answer to ours. If I told Humphrey he might have won because someone else might have been victorious, I would not be comforting him at all.

Lewis's reply, I think, is that this objection is really terminological. Both theories agree about what the worlds are like and about which objects in them win elections; they disagree only about whether we call the winning object "Humphrey" or "a counterpart of Humphrey". On Lewis's view the transworld-identical Humphrey in w is no more literally Humphrey than the counterpart is, since it too is a numerically distinct object in a distinct world, made of different matter, surrounded by different people. Kripke's demand — that the answer be about Humphrey himself — cannot be met by either theory, so the objection collapses into a quibble over vocabulary. Lewis is surely right that the two theories are notational variants: they generate the same truth-conditions for every modal claim and differ only in the ideology used to state them.

There is, however, a consequence of counterpart theory that Lewis is not happy with. The theory makes "I might have been a poached egg" true whenever some world contains a poached egg sufficiently similar to me in the relevant respects. This looks absurd and Lewis acknowledged as much; his way of dealing with it is to require the counterpart relation to be one-to-one, so that each actual object has at most one counterpart in any given world. Once that restriction is in place the poached egg cannot be my counterpart, because I have a much better counterpart available.

My conclusion is that counterpart theory is the better view and Kripke's objection fails. The objection assumes transworld identity is an intelligible notion, but the problem of accidental intrinsics shows it is not. Since counterpart theory is the only theory that avoids that contradiction, and since it delivers all the right truth-conditions for ordinary modal claims, the Humphrey objection must be rejected: it asks for something no possible-worlds theory can give, and counterpart theory gives us everything else. What would have to be the case for the objection to have bite is that there be some intelligible relation, distinct from similarity, holding between an actual object and a merely possible one — and Lewis's argument shows there is not.
