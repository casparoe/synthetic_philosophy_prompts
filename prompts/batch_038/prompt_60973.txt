I'm on the board of a student-run giving fund — about $40k a year, roughly 25 pitches a semester, each one gets a 15-minute slot, we're not allowed to do outside research during the meeting, and anything over $5k needs two of us to sign off afterward. Half the pitches are ordinary and checkable: $3k for a mobile clinic van, with two years of receipts. The other half are straight-up Pascal's muggings — someone claims their pilot program will, through a chain of effects they can't demonstrate, avert a harm worth billions of lives or "the entire future," and asks for $6k. Right now we just vote, and the muggings lose, mostly because people find them irritating, which isn't a reason.

I want to build an actual decision procedure out of the argument — one we can run inside the 15 minutes. So: the questions in the order we should ask them, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where a number is required and where a judgment has to substitute for one, what to do when the applicant can't or won't give a probability at all (this is most of them), where the procedure just says no, and where it hands the thing to the full board because it's a values question. It has to keep whatever the argument really claims rather than collapsing into "distrust big numbers," and it has to seperate two things I think we're currently fusing: how large the claimed payoff is, versus how much credence the story earns.

One wrinkle I don't think the standard treatments cover: the same people come back. Our threshold gets learned. If the procedure says reject unless the claimed stakes clear X, next semester's pitch clears X+1 and adds a zero. So whatever we adopt can't be a fixed numeric cutoff, or it has to be one that doesn't get easier to beat by inflating the story.

Then run it on three pitches: $4k for a food pantry with audited numbers; $9k for AI governance outreach that claims some nontrivial chance of mattering to trillions of future people; and $500 from a returning applicant whose pitch is basically last year's with the claimed stakes a hundred times bigger, but who did in fact deliver the small local thing they promised last spring. Include at least one case where the procedure gives an answer I won't like — I'm the one who's voted no on the second pitch every single time — and be straight about which steps are judgment calls dressed as procedure, and what the whole thing can't decide.
