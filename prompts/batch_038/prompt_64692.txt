I am the Public Participation Lead for a regional water utility serving roughly 400,000 people in the interior American West. Between five and eight times a year I run a formal engagement process on a contested question — fluoridation, advanced metering, a change in treatment chemistry, a source-protection ordinance — and each process runs three to six public sessions across two to four months, closes with a process report, and feeds a decision by our five-member board. When a process goes badly it costs the utility credibility for a decade and stalls capital projects, so the design choices locked in during the first session matter more than anything I do afterward.

My difficulty is diagnostic. Some disagreements that arrive in my room are responsive to evidence: better monitoring data goes on the table and positions move. Others are not, and in those, each new study we produce is absorbed as further confirmation that we are the sort of institution that produces such studies. I have been running one playbook in both situations — more data, more transparency, more technical presentations — and I am increasingly convinced that in the second situation the playbook is not merely inert but counterproductive. I want to stop guessing which situation I am in, and I want the guessing replaced by something two facilitators with no philosophy training can actually execute.

The framework I believe fits is the material in Wittgenstein's late work on certainty: the thought that some propositions function not as things we hold on evidence but as the fixed points on which our evidence-giving and our doubting turn; that the giving of grounds comes to an end; that a doubt extended to everything would not amount to doubt at all; that a certainty can be a way of acting rather than a claim; and that a sentence which looks like an ordinary empirical assertion in a dispute may in fact be doing structural work for the person uttering it. I am not after exegesis, and I do not want a survey of whether this commits anyone to relativism. I want it converted into a working procedure.

Please write that procedure, subject to the following requirements.

1. Structure. Give an ordered set of questions a facilitator asks about a live disagreement, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, the conditions under which the procedure terminates in a definite classification, and the points at which it stops and hands the matter to a human being because the question is one of values or of institutional authority rather than of fact. It must also handle the case where the classification changes mid-process, and the case where it cannot be reached on the information available in the room.

2. Language. Nothing in the procedure or in any document produced under it may use the word "hinge" or any other term of art. Supply plain-language names for each classification and for each recommended move, suitable for participants and for a board consisting of an engineer, a lawyer, two council members, and a farmer.

3. Guard against abuse. The obvious failure mode is that my staff learn to declare a disagreement non-evidential whenever the other side is inconvenient, which is both dishonest and, given who holds the microphone in my processes, systematically self-serving. Build in a test that must be passed before a dispute may be classified this way, and state the asymmetry explicitly: who benefits when a question is declared unarguable.

4. Honesty. Mark every step that is a judgment call wearing a procedural costume, and give a short list of situations where the procedure will misfire — including, if it applies, the case of a party performing a conviction strategically rather than holding it.

5. My coinage. I have been informally distinguishing "load-bearing" convictions — rarely stated outright, discoverable because a great deal else a person says would collapse without them — from "decorative" convictions, asserted at length and with feeling at meetings, where removing them would leave the rest of the position untouched. Tell me whether this distinction maps onto anything the framework licenses, whether loud and frequent assertion is evidence for or against a conviction being structural, and whether I should keep the vocabulary or abandon it.

6. Durability. Say whether a structural conviction can be manufactured by the setting — a hinge that exists only inside my process, among people in a room with a banner and a microphone, and would dissolve at a kitchen table — and if so, what the procedure should do differently about that kind.

7. Applications. Run the procedure on three cases. (a) An organized group opposing fluoridation that produces its own studies, rejects ours as the work of a captured institution, and insists the real issue is medication without consent. (b) Residents of an unincorporated community who believe construction dewatering damaged their wells; our hydrogeological monitoring shows no effect, and they respond that the instruments were sited and read by us and so could not have detected the harm. (c) My proposed remedy for (b): a community monitoring program distributing test kits, co-locating gauges with residents, and publishing all raw data. I expect the procedure to endorse (c), and I want to know plainly if it does not.

8. Defense. Our general counsel will ask whether a public document asserting that certain of our residents cannot be moved by evidence is defensible and whether it is condescending. Draft the two or three sentences in which the procedure describes such residents, and explain why that formulation survives the question.

Constraints on the deliverable: I have ninety minutes of preparation time per process and the whole thing must fit on a single page the facilitator can work through while filling out a form; the completed form goes into a process design memo that my general manager signs before the first session. Close with an explicit statement of what the procedure cannot do — in particular, whether it can ever license us to conclude that a conviction is false, and what it owes to someone whose conviction we have classified as unarguable.
