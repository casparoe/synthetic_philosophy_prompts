I am organizing a two-day workshop on church and state for a student society whose members arrive from four very different jurisdictions: one with an established national church, one that collects a religious surcharge through the ordinary tax system on behalf of officially recognized religious bodies, one with an assertive separationist tradition, and one whose constitution forbids establishment while vigorously protecting free exercise. Unsurprisingly, they talk past one another: each assumes their home arrangement is simply what "secularism" or "religious freedom" means, and most arrive already convinced that they are for separation, or for neutrality, or for liberty, without being able to say what any of those commitments rules out. I want a self-diagnostic instrument they can complete in about fifteen minutes before the opening session, which sorts them by what they actually believe rather than by what they call themselves.

Please write a decision-tree quiz with exactly nine branch questions and between seven and nine leaves. It is written for law and politics students with no philosophy training.

Constraints on the questions:

- Every case must be set in an invented country. Do not set any question in a real jurisdiction, and do not ask anything about real statutes, real courts, or real controversies; the whole point is that nobody should be able to answer from national reflex or from a remembered headline. Where a case needs background, invent a self-contained fact pattern — a named public body, a specific practice, a specific complainant, a specific sum of money — and treat the facts as given as authoritative.
- Two or three answer options per question, each something a respondent could sincerely choose, and each phrased so that a respondent cannot tell which leaf it leads toward. No option may contain vocabulary drawn from the leaf names.
- No question may directly ask about separation of church and state, secularism, neutrality, or establishment as labels. The distinction has to be elicited by the case.
- Under each question, add one line stating which distinction it is tracking, so that I can prune questions if the instrument runs long.

Between them, the nine questions must probe at least the following lines of division; you may reorder them, and you may decide that a single case can probe two of them at once, provided you say so:

1. whether the state may identify itself with one religion symbolically and ceremonially while conferring no legal advantage upon it;
2. whether state funding of religious bodies is required by fairness between them, permitted as an accommodation, or forbidden by the state's own neutrality;
3. whether an individual may exit a collectively levied religious contribution, and what that exit right does to the standing of the body it is exited from — does an organization anyone can leave by filing a form at a civil office still speak for a people?
4. whether the state may restrict religious dress or insignia for its own employees while leaving private citizens wholly unrestricted, and whether those are one question or two;
5. whether a religious organization is itself a bearer of rights, or merely an association of individual believers whose rights are derivative;
6. whether a generally applicable employment or anti-discrimination rule binds a religious body in selecting the people who lead its worship;
7. whether state neutrality is a constraint on the reasons officials may act upon, on the effects policies have across faiths, or on the identity the state projects;
8. whether historic wrongs committed by a dominant faith generate present obligations for the state, and if so whether those obligations run to the victims, to rival faiths, or to the cause of having no religion at all.

For the leaves, give each a short name, a one-sentence statement of its core claim, and one sentence on its most awkward commitment. The set must include: a strict separationist position; a position on which establishment is acceptable provided it is ceremonial and confers no legal privilege; a position on which the state ought to treat recognized religious bodies as public-law partners and support them institutionally; a position treating religion as an exclusively private matter about which the state should decline to take any notice whatsoever; a position on which religious liberty is primarily the liberty of organized communities against the state, including a liberty to be internally illiberal; an Erastian leaf, on which the state may govern the church in matters of doctrine and discipline; and a libertarian leaf on which the state should neither fund nor recognize any faith and should leave all religions to compete for adherents unassisted. Note in passing which of these, if any, turn out to be unstable hybrids rather than genuine positions.

Then, after the tree:

- List the combinations of answers that your nine questions make logically available but that no leaf occupies, and for each say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is politically unattractive, or because nobody has simply developed it yet.
- Identify at least one pair of leaves your instrument cannot actually separate — two views that produce identical answers to all nine questions because they differ only in their underlying reasons — and state what further question would separate them, and whether that distinction is worth spending a question on.
- Finally, for each leaf, name at most one real jurisdiction whose arrangements most nearly approximate it, or write "none." Keep this to a line per leaf. I would much rather have blanks than confident guesses, since I intend to check these myself.

Please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, quotations, or the names of theorists; the deliverable is the instrument and the notes on its blind spots.
