A colleague sent me a general-interest essay about the culture wars that sets out the honor–dignity–victimhood typology like this: "Sociologists who study moral conflict sort cultures into three kinds by asking what counts as an offense and what the offended person is supposed to do about it. In an honor culture, your worth is only what others say it is, so a slight is a public attack you have to answer yourself, and being victimized lowers your standing instead of raising it. A dignity culture holds that worth is built in rather than earned, so insults are meant to be brushed off, and when a wrong is too serious to ignore it is pursued nonviolently, through the law rather than through retaliation. Victimhood culture is honor culture in a new vocabulary: offense is taken just as easily, but since fighting back gets you sanctioned, the duel is fought through administrators, courts, and social media, and the whole thing spreads wherever people stop believing they can withstand hardship. Because the three cultures simply replace one another in that order, the sequence doubles as a ranking — dignity culture is the achievement, victimhood culture a slide back toward what it displaced, and the remedy a matter of teaching people that their worth does not depend on how others treat them."

The typology strikes me as genuinely useful and I am inclined to accept that last sentence, but the essay never argues for it, and I cannot tell whether it follows from the description or has been bolted onto it. Please audit the passage sentence by sentence, marking for each what a sociologist of moral conflict would accept, what is a simplification that quietly changes the claim, and what the account does not actually say. Answer in American English as exactly five numbered items, one per sentence, each one or two sentences long, with no introduction and no conclusion; let the fifth item carry your verdict on whether the ranking survives once the earlier sentences are corrected.
