You are to act as a specialist in the philosophy of biology with a working command of the metaphysics of function; treat what follows as an exercise in argument analysis rather than as an occasion for a survey. Before delivering a verdict, reason through the argument slowly and in writing: for each premise, ask what would have to be true for it to hold, and whether it is being used in the same sense at every point where it is relied upon. Brainstorm at least three candidate points of failure, including at least one on which the argument is valid but has a false premise and at least one on which it is invalid, and only then commit to a diagnosis. I am not after a bibliography or a catalogue of positions — only the analysis.

I set the following to my graduate seminar on teleology. It is meant to press the dispute between selected-effect (etiological) theories of function and causal-role theories, and its conclusion is one nobody in the room accepts, yet after two sessions no one has been able to say where it breaks.

(1) On a selected-effect account, a trait has function F only if the production of F by the ancestors of its bearers explains why bearers of the trait are present in the population now.
(2) There is such an explanation only where, under the conditions in which the lineage was selected, producing F made a difference to whether its bearers were retained.
(3) A trait is dysfunctional with respect to F only if it fails to produce F.
(4) Whenever an item fails to produce F, either the conditions under which it operates are no longer the conditions under which F was selected, or the item's own history does not include the production of F.
(5) In the first case the item is not malfunctioning but out of place: the departure from the selecting conditions is a feature of its circumstances rather than of it.
(6) In the second case F is not the item's function, since by (1) functions attach to items in virtue of their having produced F.
(7) Therefore, on a selected-effect account, nothing ever fails to perform its function.
(8) But some things do: hearts that cannot move enough blood, enzymes that misfold, eyes that form no image.
(9) Therefore the selected-effect account is false, and function must be characterized without appeal to history — as causal role, say.

Four questions. First: identify the step at which the argument goes wrong, and say whether the failure is an invalid inference, a false premise, or a term whose sense shifts between the premises that introduce it and the premises that use it; if you think it is none of these, say so and defend (9). Second: explain why that step looked unobjectionable to a room of careful readers, and what it tacitly assumes. Third: price the repair. A selected-effect theorist, a causal-role theorist, and a theorist who identifies function with what the trait normally does in members of the kind must each block the argument somewhere; say where each would naturally block it and what each pays — in particular, whether the causal-role theorist is actually entitled to accept (9), given what her own account says about items that lack the capacity in question. Fourth: is there a repaired version, with (4) and (6) amended rather than deleted, that still threatens the selected-effect account, and if so, what is its conclusion?

For what it is worth, the premises I am most attached to are (1) and (8), and the one I am happiest to lose is (9). Tell me what holding both (1) and (8) costs me, and whether that cost is one I can discharge.
