Help, I've written myself into a corner. I'm putting together a seminar session on Du Bois on art, and I wanted to show that his claim in the 1926 Criteria of Negro Art talk — that all art is propaganda and always will be, because nobody creates from nowhere and the idea of art that just sits there being beautiful is a dodge — actually does critical work rather than being a slogan. So I sketched it out in my notes:

1. All art is propaganda; there is no art that fails to take a side, whether or not the artist intends it.
2. If something's function is to advance a side, the right standard for it is how well it advances that side.
3. How well something advances a side is a question about effects, not about how the thing is made.
4. So asking whether a work is good is just asking what it does politically.
5. Which means a crudely made piece that helps the cause beats a beautifully made one that doesn't, and the beautiful one is good only becuase of its effects — the craft itself counts for nothing in the verdict.
6. Du Bois plainly doesn't accept 5: he also insists the work has to be art, and he's furious that work serving the powerful never gets called propaganda while work serving Black Americans does, which only makes sense if the labelling is discriminating rather than describing a universal condition.
7. And if 1 is literally true, propaganda picks out everything and so nothing, and can't be the critical term he uses it as.
8. So either 1 is false and he's conceded the point to the art-for-art's-sake crowd, or 1 is true but idle.

Where does this go wrong, if it does? I'd rather keep 1 than 4, but I can't see the route. Tell me which step to reject, why it looked so natural going in, and what he pays for rejecting it — including whether 2 and 3 are his views at all or something I've bolted on from a crude instrumentalism. And if there's a way to hold onto 1 while still letting him say that some propaganda is bad art and some isn't, I want the mechanism, not just a label. British English please.
