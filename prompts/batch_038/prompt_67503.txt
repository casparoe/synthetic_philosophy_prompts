Okay so I keep running into a pattern I don't have a word for, and I'm hoping philosophy of action does. Two examples, both real.

One: my train stalls in a tunnel and a big suitcase comes off the rack and wedges itself against the doors. Three of us, total strangers, never say a single word to each other, each grab a bit of it and lift, it comes free, the doors close, and all three of us go straight back to our phones. If you'd asked me afterward what I did I would have said "I lifted a case," not "we lifted a case." I'd bet money the other two would say the same, and mean it.

Two: after a storm my whole street is full of downed branches, and over a couple of days basically every household drags stuff to the same corner pile. Nobody organized it. No group chat, no knock on doors, no plan. But it obviously only works because each person is doing their bit on the assumption the others will do theirs — if two families had quietly stopped on day one I think everyone would have stopped by day two. And here's the bit that confuses me: nobody would have had any standing to complain. Not a whiff of it. If the Hendersons had just left their branches in their own yard, no one was going to say a word, and yet the pile would have died.

So the togetherness is doing real work — my reason for hauling is entirely built out of what I expect you to do, and the outcome isn't reachable by anyone alone — but there's no agreement, no shared plan, no sense of owing anybody anything, no rebuke when someone drops out, and not one participant, me included, would describe the event in the plural. The denial of "we" isn't modesty or confusion either, it's just what it's like from the inside.

Does that combination have a name? I want the carving, not the label:

- What's the nearest established concept, and how is what I'm describing different from it? I can guess it sits somewhere near people's ideas about shared intention and about whether you need some kind of commitment to act together, but I don't know which account I'm actually describing a counterexample to, or whether I'm describing a counterexample at all.
- It isn't just several people happening to do similar things at the same time, because the expectations are load-bearing and mutually sustaining — but how much mutual expectation do you need before "they're doing it together" becomes true? Give me the threshold and what it's made of.
- It also isn't the ordinary case where people talk, agree, and then execute, but I want to know what exactly goes missing when the talking is skipped. Is it just the obligation, or is something else gone too?
- Most of all: is the no-blame-but-total-collapse pairing actually coherent? Any collective activity that really depends on everyone expecting everyone else to keep going seems like it ought to generate some entitlement to complain when they don't. Tell me whether a genuinely expectation-driven group activity can be obligation-free, or whether I'm describing something that can't exist and just looks like it can.
- And where does the term stop applying, if there is one? A stadium wave. A queue that forms itself at a food truck. A bucket brigade that gets going at a fire before anyone shouts. A flash mob where everybody rehearsed privately. Tell me which of those are the same phenomenon and which aren't, and why — that's the part I care about most, honestly.

If nothing in the literature covers it, say so and coin something for it: a definition, plus two or three cases that sit right on the edge where you'd argue about whether it applies. I'd rather have a sharp boundary and a slightly ugly name than a pretty name that blurs into everything else.
