You are a decision theorist who has taught both formal epistemology and the mathematics of gambling, and you are setting the final problem-set question for an MA course on rational choice under risk. Think carefully before drafting: begin with a short paragraph identifying where the real difficulty in the scenario below lies and which of the standard arguments for the Kelly criterion survive the modification. Only then produce the deliverables.

The scenario to build the question around: an agent holds a fund of £1,000,000. Once a year she is offered the same bet — a coin that lands heads with probability 0.6; she may stake any fraction *f* of her current fund, gaining the stake on heads and losing it on tails. She is also obliged to draw £20,000 in cash out of the fund every year for living costs, win or lose. Note that the numbers are deliberately poised: the long-run growth rate of the classic Kelly policy (*f* = 0.2) is almost exactly equal to the annual draw as a proportion of the initial fund, and the draw therefore destabilises the very process that was supposed to fund it.

Write one question worth 25 marks, answerable in 45 minutes, that cannot be answered by reproducing the Kelly formula, re-deriving logarithmic utility, or reciting the "time average versus ensemble average" defence of growth-rate maximisation as an objective, utility-free criterion. It must force students to name the specific assumption of the Kelly derivation that a fixed cash withdrawal violates; to say whether a long-run growth rate is even well defined once that assumption goes; and to state what the agent's objective — and her optimal stake as a function of current wealth — should now be.

Then supply (a) a model answer of about 700 words that is entirely self-contained, with no bibliography and no appeal to results a student could not derive on the spot; and (b) a marking scheme of five must-hit points whose marks sum to 25, with one line for each naming the specific mistake that should cost marks and saying how an A answer's treatment differs from that of a merely competent B answer.
