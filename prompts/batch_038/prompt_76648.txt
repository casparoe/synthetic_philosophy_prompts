> Consider the following argument.

And here's the student's answer, in full:

> The argument in the question is valid, and (3) is not in dispute: moral sentences turn up in the antecedents of conditionals, in the scope of negation, in questions and in reports of belief. The work is being done by (1) and (2) together, and I think the argument fails, because (1) is not something the expressivist need accept in the form the argument requires.
>
> Start with what Geach, following Frege, took the difficulty to be. On the expressivist picture the meaning of a moral sentence is given by the state of mind that a sincere utterance of it expresses: to say that stealing is wrong is to disapprove of stealing. But an embedded occurrence of that sentence is not a sincere utterance of anything, since in asserting the conditional the speaker does not thereby disapprove of stealing. If meaning is tied to the act of assertion in this way, the sentence seems to mean one thing when it stands alone and nothing at all when it stands inside another sentence, so the familiar inference — if stealing is wrong then telling your flatmate to steal is wrong; stealing is wrong; therefore telling your flatmate to steal is wrong — is either invalid or equivocal. This is the Frege–Geach problem, also called the embedding problem, and it is a problem about compositionality: it asks how the meaning of a complex sentence is supposed to be a function of the meanings of its parts when some of those parts are not being asserted.
>
> Now, what is truth-aptness doing in the argument as stated? Premise (1) says that moral sentences lack truth conditions, and (2) says that sentences of that kind cannot occur unasserted without changing their meaning. But the real content of (2) has nothing to do with truth at all: it is a claim about the relation between meaning and force. That matters, because the expressivist can simply concede truth-aptness. On a deflationary or minimalist account, 'is true' is a device for disquotation and endorsement, so that to call a moral sentence true is just to reassert it, and nothing metaphysically heavy is being claimed. Quasi-realists have taken this line for decades: they aim to earn the right to talk of moral truth, and they treat moral discourse as no less truth-apt than any other. Once that concession is made, (1) is false as it stands, the conditional above is composed out of the very same contents in embedded and unembedded position, and compositionality is restored. Minimalism about truth is therefore not a cosmetic addition to expressivism; it is what closes the gap Geach identified, and the expressivist is free to run modus ponens.
>
> Two further points about the state of play. Gibbard's answer to the embedding problem was to appeal to higher-order attitudes: an embedded moral sentence expresses not a simple attitude but an attitude towards a combination of attitudes, so that the conditional above expresses something like disapproval of combining acceptance of stealing with failing to disapprove of getting someone else to steal. Blackburn, who introduced the image of the ladder we may kick away once it has done its work, worked instead in terms of a plan-laden semantics, on which sentences express states of mind and complex sentences express correspondingly complex states of mind. Either way, the expressivist has the resources to say what the parts express and what the whole expresses.
>
> Finally, the negation problem is not a seperate problem. It is the same problem applied to a one-place connective, and an expressivist who has a general account of embedding has thereby already explained why accepting that stealing is wrong and accepting that stealing is not wrong cannot both be correct. Whatever else remains to be done, that much is settled.
