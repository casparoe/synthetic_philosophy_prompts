Thirty-six years of my working life were spent in a national measurement laboratory: the first decade at the bench, the remainder in charge of a small group whose unglamorous remit was to keep other people's instruments honest. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy with rather more enthusiasm than discipline, and I have drafted the essay below for a general-audience competition run by a learned society whose brief concerns the relations between the sciences and the humanities. It stands at roughly twelve hundred words. I should be most grateful for your comments on it, and I have set out at the end what I want them to bear on.

**Draft: "The Cheapest Decisive Test — a metrologist's note on Clifford and James"**

*1.* The received picture of this quarrel is tidy enough. Clifford, a mathematician, published his essay on the ethics of belief in 1877; a shipowner sends out an emigrant vessel he suspects to be unsound, talks himself out of his suspicions, and collects the insurance when she founders. Clifford's judgement is that the owner was guilty of the deaths even had the ship arrived safely, because the wrongness "has to do with the origin of his belief, not the matter of it". He had no right, Clifford says, "to believe on such evidence as was before him"; he acquired his belief "not by honestly earning it in patient investigation, but by stifling his doubts". James's reply, published in the 1897 collection that took its title from it, denies that the intellect always has the last word: where an option is genuine — in his terms forced, living and momentous — and cannot be settled on intellectual grounds, our passional nature decides, and rightly so. And so the debate is filed as evidentialism against voluntarism, duty against permission, the cold ethics of evidence against the warm psychology of faith.

*2.* Read from the bench rather than the lectern, the two cases are not opposites at all. In Clifford's story there existed a survey: a procedure that would have settled the question, whose cost the owner could have borne, and whose availability he knew of perfectly well. In James's religious cases there is no such procedure at any price whatsoever. The variable that separates them is therefore not evidence against will, but whether a decisive test is on offer within the agent's reach. I want to call this the **verification margin**: for a given question, the cheapest procedure actually available to the agent that would discriminate among the live hypotheses.

Two features of the texts encourage this reading, and I confess I found them rather thrilling when I noticed them. First, Clifford's second case does not merely involve thin evidence; it involves evidence that was cheaply obtainable. The agitators are condemned because "the evidence of their innocence was such as the agitators might easily have obtained, if they had attempted a fair inquiry". Ease of acquisition is doing explicit work in the condemnation. Second, James himself reaches for an example from my own trade, and in doing so describes an inquiry in exactly Cliffordian terms: "A chemist finds an hypothesis live enough to spend a year in its verification: he believes in it to that extent. But if his experiments prove inconclusive either way, he is quit for his loss of time, no vital harm being done." A year of labour, a recoverable loss, a decision that can be reversed: this is the profile of a test that is cheap enough to be run, and James puts it under the heading of the trivial option, saying that such options abound in the scientific life.

*3.* The reframing also makes sense of James's third condition, which is usually treated as a flourish about importance. An option is momentous, he says, when the opportunity is unique, the stake considerable, or the choice irreversible; and he adds that whoever refuses a unique opportunity "loses the prize as surely as if he tried and failed". Momentousness, on this reading, is a clause about the price of deferral: it marks the cases in which waiting for further evidence is itself a decision with an unrecoverable cost. That is not a permission to believe what comforts us; it is a statement that inquiry has a schedule, and that some questions close before they are answered.

So my proposal is that Clifford and James are not disagreeing about one case but addressing complementary halves of a single question. Clifford governs where a decisive test is cheap and within reach, and his condemnation is absolute precisely because there was no excuse. James governs where no decisive test is available at any price, and where the cost of suspension is itself a harm. Between them lies the whole ordinary business of deciding what to check next.

*4.* I can see four objections, and I want them pressed rather than flattered.

The first is that this is not a third position but pragmatic encroachment wearing a laboratory coat — the familiar thesis that practical stakes bear on whether a true belief amounts to knowledge. A philosopher with whom I correspond tells me my variable is only stakes under another name. My defence is that the price of the next decisive test is not the price of being wrong: the first is a feature of my circumstances and my ingenuity, the second of the world's indifference, and my claim is about a duty to inquire rather than about whether knowledge obtains. I am not confident the difference is substantial enough to bear the weight I put on it.

The second is worse. Clifford forbids any appeal to how things turn out: a fortunate voyage diminishes the guilt "Not one jot", since the owner "would not have been innocent, he would only have been not found out". But if the duty depends on which tests happen to be cheap for this agent in these circumstances, then the duty is contingent on luck in precisely the way Clifford rules out. I may have traded his moralism for a cost-benefit rider he would have despised.

The third concerns the social case, which is where I actually came in. In my laboratory almost nothing I believed was verified by me, and in a collaboration of a thousand authors no individual has inspected the raw records at all; belief is held on the strength of certificates, calibration histories, and other people's signatures. Whose verification margin is it then? My answer is two-tiered: a first-order duty to run the cheap decisive tests one can see, and a second-order duty to sustain the habits and institutions that make such tests visible — for the most dangerous belief in a laboratory is not the one held against a known cheap test, but the one held where a cheap test existed and nobody had the imagination to notice. Clifford gestures at this territory when he asks whether his own rule would turn us into universal sceptics, obliged to renounce an inherited body of knowledge that no single person could possibly test.

The fourth is that the margin is not a feature of the world but of the agent's field of view, so two honest people face the same question with different margins. If the duty is indexed to the margin, in what sense does Clifford's rule still hold always, everywhere and for anyone?

*5.* What I want the essay to end up arguing is modest: that the ethics of belief is better taught as an ethics of inquiry, whose first question is not whether I have enough evidence but what the cheapest thing is that I could do which would tell me I am wrong, and why I am not doing it. Any scientist recognises that question. Whether it can carry Clifford's moral weight is what I cannot decide, and I would rather say so than pretend.

**What I should like from you**

1. *Accuracy above all.* Where have I misdescribed or over-read either author? In particular: is my treatment of "momentous" as a cost-of-deferral clause a defensible reading or a distortion, and is it fair to say that ease of obtaining evidence does explicit work in Clifford's second case? Please correct me bluntly; I would much rather be corrected than encouraged.

2. *The encroachment objection.* Tell me whether it is fatal. If the distinction between the price of the next test and the price of being wrong is too thin, say what the distinction would have to be in order to hold, or advise me to drop the claim to novelty and present the essay as an application of an existing view to a scientific readership.

3. *The luck objection.* Is there a way to keep the verification margin and retain something like Clifford's universality — for instance by making the duty attach not to the margin itself but to a culpable failure to know what the margin is? Or should I concede plainly that I am offering a weaker and more workable ethic than Clifford's, and locate the essay's interest in that concession rather than in a synthesis?

4. *The distributed-belief section.* Is it the strongest part of the draft or the weakest? Does the two-tier duty earn its place, or is it an ad hoc patch? And does it simply reopen the old problem of testimony without adding anything to it?

5. *Structure and register.* Where does the argument move too fast for a reader without philosophy, which of the five sections could be cut with least loss, and does the chemist passage do the work I claim for it or am I leaning on it for decoration?

6. *An honest verdict.* Is there an essay here, or a well-arranged set of observations? Name the single objection a referee would press hardest, and tell me whether I should answer it in the text or concede it and move on.

Please do not trouble yourself with references, bibliographies or suggestions for further reading: I have both texts to hand and I intend to do my own reading, and what I am after is your judgement of the argument rather than your citations. I should be obliged if you would write your comments in British English throughout.
