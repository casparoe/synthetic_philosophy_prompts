So my reading group just spent its last session of the term on Hofstadter and Sander's *Surfaces and Essences*, and afterwards my friend Dana and I argued for basically three hours on the walk to her place and neither of us moved an inch. Her line is that the book is a gorgeous pile of anecdotes in search of a mechanism: that the deep-versus-surface distinction was already pinned down years earlier by people doing actual sorting experiments with physics problems and actual transfer failures, with a computational theory attached, and that "everything is analogy, all the time, categorization included" is the kind of thesis that can't be wrong. My line is that she's grading it on the wrong rubric — the interesting claim isn't that there are deep and shallow comparisons, it's that a concept isn't a list of features with a boundary, it's a family with no edges, and that seeing something as an instance of something else is the whole job of thinking rather than a fancy add-on. We agreed to disagree, and since I'm the one who puts together the little zine we print at the end of each term, I have to write the closing piece, and I want it to be a poem rather than another summary of the arguement.

Here's what I want, and the constraint is the point so please don't soften it. Six stanzas, four lines each, rhymed, no fixed meter. In the first three stanzas the rhymes should be perfect and cheap — words that sound alike and mean nothing to each other, so the reader gets that little hit of pleasure from the sound-match while the sense-match is empty, and the poem should let them feel how hollow that is. Then from stanza four onward the rhyme starts to fail: slant rhyme, then assonance, then something that barely qualifies, while the things being compared get truer and closer. By the last pair the rhyme should be almost gone and the match should be the real one, so that the reader has lived the tradeoff — the sounds get worse exactly as the likenesses get better. The turn needs to be findable, not smeared. Don't use the words analogy, surface, essence, structure, mapping, category, abstraction, chunk, or slippage anywhere in the poem, and don't name-drop the authors either; it has to do the thing rather than announce it. For subject matter, either Dana's roommate's first year as a radiology resident (she described it as a year of learning to stop seeing shapes and start seeing diagnoses, which is about as clean a case as I know) or a small kid calling every four-legged creature "doggie" and slowly taking it back — whichever gives you better raw material for degrading rhymes. One more thing, and this is the part I actually care about most: somewhere in there, a stanza where the sliding just stops. A brute particular that no comparison organizes — an arbitrary fact that has to be memorized, or something that matches everything and therefore nothing. Her best point all night was that a theory that can't fail isn't a theory, so the poem should have at least one place where the fluidity hits a wall and stays hard, and it should not be a triumphalist ending where all the boundaries dissolve. Leave the wall standing.

Then after the poem, give me a short note — one paragraph, not an essay — telling me where it took liberties. Specifically I want you to separate what's settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this: which parts of the surface/deep picture are decades-old results nobody disputes, and which parts are Hofstadter and Sander's own extrapolations that a lot of cognitive scientists reject. I especially want to know whether anything in the poem only works if you buy the strong thesis (categorization just is analogy-making, concepts have no boundaries) rather than the weak one Dana would happily sign, and whether the Copycat-style models from their group count as evidence for the strong version or only as existence proofs in toy domains. If something in there rests on a reading that's a minority position, say so plainly — I'd rather find out from you than get called on it at the reading, and I don't want the poem overselling a view as if it were consensus when it definately isn't.
