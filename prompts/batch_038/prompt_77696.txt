Third novel, and it's eating me alive. It's set in a made-up Central European country in the late thirties — collapsed currency, national humiliation from a lost border war, a parliament nobody believes in — and the central character is a rabble-rouser who rides all of that into a dictatorship. He's fictional, you can hear the real reference, I'm at peace with that. The problem started as a craft problem and has turned into something I don't have vocabulary for. My editor thinks the book is too much in love with him, that I've written a demon-genius who bends history around him, which is both flattering and dull; she wants him small and mean and petty and wants the country's own logic to do the heavy lifting. Then a historian friend I got into it with over dinner gave me the image I've been leaning on ever since: great men are lightning rods, the charge is already in the air, they just decide where the bolt lands — and by implication they aren't really doing anything, and certainly not anything another rod couldn't do. I've been using that to justify demoting my character, which is suspiciously convenient of it, and I've read maybe two actual history books for this project, which is probably the real problem.

So — how far does the lightning rod actually go? I want the mapping spelled out piece by piece: what's the storm, what's the charge, what's the building being protected, what corresponds to the fact that the rod is metal and grounded and installed by somebody on purpose (does that even have a counterpart?), and then where the image starts lying to me. The bits that bug me: a rod doesn't change how much charge there is or whether the strike happens at all, only where, whereas a leader seems to change what the anger gets aimed at; a rod is interchangeable, any copper will do, and yet everyone who writes about this period keeps insisting the bolt would have landed somewhere worse, or somewhere else entirely, if their man had died young — which is a claim about a specific counterfactual and not really a claim about rods at all; and a rod gets vaporised by doing its job, which would be a much better book than the one I'm writing.

Also, audit my own rival image, because I think I might just be flattering myself. I keep picturing him less as a rod than as a speck of dust in a supersaturated solution: the liquid was always going to crystallize, the tension was built into it, but nothing happens — it could sit there metastable for years, decades — until one particular speck gives it somewhere to start, and then the whole thing sets, and the shape of the crystal that ends up forming isn't predicable from the shape of the speck. Where does that hold up better than the rod, where does it break in exactly the same places, and what does each of the two quietly smuggle in about whether my character is responsible and whether he was replaceable? I've got an assassination attempt at the end of Act Two and I honestly can't tell whether the reader should feel that everything would have been different if it had landed, which means the whole moral weight of the book is riding on an analogy I picked up at a dinner party.

Give me the correspondence tables for both, then separate the disanalogies that actually matter for what I'm trying to do — plot it, assign blame, decide whether he's necessary to the story or an accident of it — from the ones that are just physics-pedantry with no bearing. And if both images are load-bearing something I don't want them to carry, tell me what a better one would be; I'll happily take a third option over either of these.
