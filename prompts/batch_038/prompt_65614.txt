I am in the final year of a novel whose hinge is a formal disputation held in the audience hall of a minor lord in the third century before our era, and I have hit a wall with one of the two speakers. The visiting disputant is Gongsun Long of the School of Names, notorious for maintaining that a white horse is not a horse; the man who interrogates him is the lord's steward, trained in Mohist dialectic, who finds the thesis intolerable and intends to expose it in front of the whole court. Before I can write that scene I need to hear the logician argue from the inside, because in every draft so far he has emerged sounding like a modern semanticist who happens to own a robe — a friend of mine who reads these languages told me flatly that I had given him a theory of extensions he has no business possessing, and that whatever interest the man has lies precisely in what he can and cannot do with the materials he actually has. So I should like to run the argument against you before I set it down on the page.

Play him, then: Gongsun Long, in the first person, as a sincere disputant and not a showman performing for the hall. Here is the toolkit I shall allow you, already stated in your own idiom — a horse is a name for a certain shape and gait, white is a name for a certain colour, and a name that has been fixed to both has thereby been fixed to neither of them alone; if a man calls for a horse, a yellow or a black one will answer the call, whereas if he calls for a white horse neither will, and a difference in what answers a call is a difference in the name itself rather than a quibble; and if a white horse simply were a horse, then calling for a horse and calling for a white horse would be one and the same act, which it visibly is not. You may concede subsidiary points — that ordinary speech is loose, that a white horse is plainly among the things men point at and call horses, that I have put my objection well — but you may never concede the thesis, and whenever you feel yourself drifting toward conceding it your remedy is to restate, more carefully, the distinction you are leaning on. Confine yourself to concepts available in your own time and place together with anything I tell you; do not use my modern vocabulary (extension, reference, set, predicate, semantics, necessity, and the rest of that family), and when I use such words, first render them into your own idiom and then challenge the rendering if it does not fit. Keep your replies to a paragraph, two at the very most, so that this reads as a conversation and not a treatise, and you may close a reply with a single question put back to me, as a disputant at court would. Never break character, never offer stage directions, notes to the author, or a summary of where we have got to, and never step aside to warn me that something is anachronistic; if you need a fact about my world or my language, ask for it in character, as a man enquiring after a peculiar foreign custom. Continue on these terms until I tell you to stop.

My opening move is this. I have been advised that the chapter must survive readers trained in my own century's logic, so I shall argue as they argue. Suppose that a name picks out a gathering of things, and that the gathering picked out by white horse lies wholly inside the gathering picked out by horse. Then everything you make of the two calls follows at once from that containment: a yellow horse is refused when a white horse is called for not because the names differ in kind, but because the smaller gathering simply has fewer members able to satisfy a demand. On this account white horse is a narrower name for horses and not a name for something other than a horse, and your argument establishes only that the two names cannot be exchanged for one another within a sentence — which your steward will happily grant, since he never wanted more than that. Worse, you appear committed to a rule on which every modifier makes a new name for a different thing, and then white jade is not jade, an aged horse is not a horse, and no qualified name can ever tell a man anything about the thing it qualifies. And do not answer that the Mohists are in no position to press you, for they hold themselves that to kill a robber is not to kill a man, and defend it by setting case beside case in just your manner: so either your argument proves too much or theirs does, and I want to be told which of you is the inconsistent one.
