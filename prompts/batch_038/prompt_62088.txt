I write historical fiction — mostly three- and four-generation family sagas — and for the last year I've been reading cliodynamics, mainly Turchin, because I'm structuring a book about an invented country falling apart over roughly 120 years and I want the shape of that collapse to come from somewhere other than my instincts. I'd call myself maybe sixty percent fluent on the model side: I can follow the argument but I definately can't do the math, and I've read the popular books rather than the papers. So I'll state what I think I've got, and you can correct me where I've mangled it.

What I think I've got: history has a quantifiable structure — the structural-demographic model, secular cycles that run a couple hundred years with an integrative phase and a disintegrative phase, each lasting decades. The real driver isn't the peasants being hungry, it's elite overproduction: too many credentialed, ambitious people chasing too few positions of power, which produces intra-elite competition and eventually counter-elites who mobilize the discontented against the establishment. Add a wage-price squeeze that immiserates ordinary people, a state that goes fiscally bankrupt trying to hold the line, and the decay of the asabiya thing he borrows from Ibn Khaldun — generalized social trust and willingness to cooperate. Plus the public prediction he made around 2010 that the coming decade would be a period of growing instability in the US and Western Europe, which he now says is arriving on schedule and which he's cashed out for the present day as an elite-overproduction story.

Here's the thing though. My native language is craft, not statistics, and I don't think in variables. I think in scene and sequel, point of view, whose head we're in, stakes escalation, the want versus the need, the midpoint reversal, dramatic irony, the Chekhov's gun that has to go off, the ending that feels inevitable in retrospect but not guessable on the page, and the rule that a character's decision has to actually matter or the reader checks out.

So: take the load-bearing claims of that model one by one — the cyclical structure itself, elite overproduction, immiseration, state fiscal breakdown, the loss of solidarity, and the predictability claim — and restate each in the vocabulary of craft as faithfully as you can. The closest available translations, not the flattering ones. For each, tell me what translates cleanly, what you had to bend to make it fit, and what has no expression in craft vocabulary whatsoever.

Three things I care about in how you do it:

Don't smuggle craft's assumptions into the translation and then report them back to me as if they were Turchin's. If a claim only survives once history has a protagonist, say so — that's the finding, not a bug in the finding. And conversely, don't quietly import the model's assumptions and congratulate yourself on a clean map.

Give me the residue report proper. Here's the question underneath the question: is the leftover a defect of narrative thinking — novels structurally cannot represent impersonal aggregate causation, so my form is what's broken and I should be willing to write something that reads weirder — or is it evidence that the model is doing something sneakily narrative already? I mean, is a secular cycle just a plot with a dataset attached: exposition, rising action, reversal, catastrophe, denouement, and then the next generation starts it over? If so, the wierd conclusion is that craft vocabulary turns out to be a rival theory of history rather than a translation target, and I want you to say plainly whether you think that's right. And if you think there's a third option — residue that neither framework can express, like a probabilistic forecast about specific people who don't exist yet — make that case too.

Then get practical. Which of those claims, translated honestly, stops being a claim I could ever dramatize as written, because it's true of an aggregate over a century and my book is four hundred pages about people in rooms? Point at anything in the model that becomes unfalsifiable or merely decorative once it's rendered as story, since my draft is probably full of exactly that. And finish by telling me which single claim I should build the book's spine around — the one that carries the most weight with the least distortion — and which one I should cut loose entirely because the only way to keep it is to lie about it in craft terms. If it helps, I'd rather hear that the answer is "your form can't hold this" than a list of ways to make a data set feel human.
