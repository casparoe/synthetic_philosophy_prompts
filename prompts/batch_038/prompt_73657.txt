I have been assembling a package of views for a chapter on metaontology, and different colleagues have each told me that a different part of it is incompatible with the rest. Rather than argue with them piecemeal, I would like a single audit of the whole set. What follows is the position as it currently stands; I am not sure where the trouble is, and I have deliberately not indicated which commitments I would be most reluctant to abandon.

(1) Criterion. A dispute is verbal if and only if the parties can express everything they are committed to in a topic-neutral vocabulary — one containing none of the disputed terms — on which they are in complete agreement, leaving no residual disagreement. Where such a restatement is available, the dispute is terminological; where it is not, the dispute is substantive.

(2) Metasemantics. What a speaker's words mean is fixed by how she is disposed to use them, and by nothing further. I reject the idea that some meanings are elite, or that a candidate meaning can be correct in virtue of carving reality at its joints; naturalness plays no role in determining what our logical vocabulary, the existential quantifier included, stands for.

(3) Metaontology. The dispute between the mereological nihilist and the mereological universalist is verbal. Both accept precisely the same claims about how the simples are distributed and arranged; they differ only in their quantificational practice, so that "there exists" is not univocal across their theories.

(4) Frameworks. With Carnap, I hold that questions about which conceptual framework to adopt are pragmatic and admit of no framework-independent answer. Frameworks are not true or false, though some are markedly better instruments for doing science than others.

(5) Ordinary truth. Nevertheless, claims made within a framework are true or false outright. That there are infinitely many primes is true, full stop; it is not true-relative-to-a-framework, and asserting it is not a disguised report on my linguistic habits.

(6) Genuine residue. Some disputes are substantive even under complete agreement about the base facts. My example is the dispute between the illusionist about phenomenal consciousness and the realist: both accept the same functional, behavioural, and neural facts, and yet they genuinely disagree about whether anything further is present. I do not regard that disagreement as terminological.

(7) Depth. That a dispute is verbal does not make it shallow or unimportant. The compatibilist/incompatibilist dispute over free will is verbal on my view, and it remains worth arguing about at length — indeed I think the compatibilist's way of speaking is the better one.

(8) Revision. For contested terms — "person," "cause," and I think "exists" among them — prior usage underdetermines what we ought to mean. Where that is so, the right move is to propose a precisification and defend it on theoretical grounds rather than to defer to how the words have hitherto been used.

What I want back is an audit rather than a survey. Please identify which of these commitments genuinely pull against one another and which only appear to. For each genuine tension, name the specific commitments that generate it, rank the tensions by severity, and say what would have to be given up and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the list. Where you judge a tension merely apparent, identify the ambiguity or the distinction that dissolves it; I would far rather be told that than have every pair flagged as problematic. Please attend especially to whether my criterion does the work I need it to do across the different sorts of dispute I discuss, and to whether the repair available for one tension makes another worse. Close by telling me which single change to the list would buy the greatest overall coherence, and what position I would be left defending if I made it.
