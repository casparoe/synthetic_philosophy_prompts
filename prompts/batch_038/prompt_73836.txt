I'm a clinical neuropsychologist at a comprehensive epilepsy center and I've just been drafted onto our hospital's consent-and-capacity comittee, mostly because we've had three corpus callosotomy patients come through in the last two years and apparently nobody else on the committee has any idea what to make of them. The thing that actually triggered this: a guy in his twenties, about four months post-op, whose family pulled me aside after a session in which his left hand kept pushing away the cup his right hand was reaching for — and he laughed, and said he had no idea why his hand was doing that. Half the committee wants to treat this as two agents sharing a body, which they think means we should be seeking assent twice and maybe revisiting his advance directive; the other half says it's a motor-level conflict plus a bit of confabulation and he's one person with a disobedient hand. My training is in testing and report-writing, not philosophy — I read some Nagel and Gazzaniga in college, I remember the left-hemisphere interpreter business, and my sister and I argued about it over Thanksgiving after she watched a documentary and wouldn't let it go. I need something I can hand to these people on Monday.

So here's the ask. Think of yourself as someone who is simultaneously a philosophy-of-mind person who has actually argued about the unity of consciousness and a practicing neuropsychologist who has to write the conclusion into a chart note, and please think carefully before you start drafting — I'd rather have one good procedure than three vague frameworks. First, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming out loud about which distinctions have to survive being translated into a committee checklist and which can be dropped, and which distinctions are tempting to philosophers but useless at a bedside. Then write me the decision procedure itself: the questions to ask in order, what data each step requires (be specific about which steps need lateralized stimulus presentation — left visual field, left-hand-only palpation out of view — versus which can be run in an ordinary exam room, and which need the full lab), what counts as a sufficient answer at each step, where it terminates in "treat as one agent" versus "divided agency, and here is what changes about consent," and — this is the part I care about most — an explicit flag on every step where the move from observed behavior to "two streams of consciousness" is smuggling in a philosophical assumption rather than reading off a finding. Be honest about the genuinely hard cases: a right hemisphere that selects correctly but never speaks, discordant emotional reactions to the same stimulus, and the difference between "the patient doesn't know why his hand did that" and "part of the patient knows and cannot say it." I want to know how the procedure distinguishes intermanual conflict and alien-hand-type behavior from anything that would license talk of a second subject.

Then run it on two cases and give me a verdict for each, with the reasoning shown step by step: (a) the cup situation above, including the laughing and the verbal denial of any intention; and (b) a case where lateralized testing shows the left hand reliably retrieving the object matching a picture flashed only to the left visual field while the patient insists out loud that he was just guessing and got lucky. Please make one of those two come out in a direction I personally will not like — I'd rather find out now than in front of the family. Finish with a short section on what the procedure cannot settle and has to hand back to a human being because it's a question of values rather than facts (I expect consent on behalf of an agent whose unity is in dispute belongs there, but tell me what else does). American English throughout, no jargon I'd have to look up, and no reading list or bibliography — I can't chase citations between clinic sessions and I don't want anything in there I can't check myself.
