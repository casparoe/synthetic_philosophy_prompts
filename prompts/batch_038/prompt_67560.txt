I cover science and ideas for a weekly and for the last six months I've basically been living inside the democratic-innovation beat — citizens' assemblies, sortition, the whole lottocracy thing. The piece I owe Friday is about a draft proposal that a couple of backbenchers and a small research outfit have been circulating (they dont want names in print yet), and it's much more aggressive than the polite assembly stuff: a 200-member legislature, all 200 drawn by stratified random sample from the electoral register, single non-renewable six-year term, a third redrawn every two years. Service is compulsory — small fine if you refuse, exemptions only for serious illness, a new baby, or active military — so the pool isn't the usual self-selected civics nerds. No parties, seats reshuffled monthly, and committees redrawn every six months on purpose so nobody builds a fiefdom or, as critics say, any expertise either. The chamber sets its own agenda and has final say on ordinary legislation; the executive can send a bill back exactly once with a written objection. There's a permanent professional secretariat that writes the briefing materials and staffs the committees, and every decision has to be published with a written justification that actually answers the objections raised in debate.

Every theorist I talk to gives me the same non-answer: it depends what you mean by better. So I'm asking you to commit. Give me your credence — an actual number — that this body would, over twenty years in a wealthy established democracy, make better decisions on the whole than the elected legislature it replaced. Before the number, say in one sentence what you're taking "better" to mean, because I suspect that's doing a lot of the work and I want it on the record. Then decompose it, term by term, each with a number and one line of justification: the odds that descriptive representation actually converts into substantive representation of people's interests better than elections manage; the odds that a randomly drawn body resists capture better than an elected one once you count the secretariat as itself a capture channel; the odds that losing electoral accountability costs less than you gain by deleting reelection incentives; the odds that ordinary people given six years, real staff, and no career to protect deliberate to a level that clears the bar; the odds that "better policy" is a determinate rankable quantity across policy domains at all rather than domain-relative; and the odds that the arrangement survives twenty years intact instead of being watered down in year four, becuase if it doesn't, my question sort of evaporates. Then show me how you combine those terms — multiply, weight, something looser — instead of just listing them side by side.

Two more things. I'm at about 0.6 and my editor, who thinks deliberation fetishizes consensus and quietly distrusts the whole genre, is at 0.15 — tell me which of your terms we're most likely disagreeing on, and whether we're having one disagreement or three dressed up as one. And tell me which single feature of the design above your number is most sensitive to: I keep getting told the compulsory-service clause is the whole ballgame, and at one panel a guy would not stop invoking the Condorcet jury theorem, which I'm not convinced applies to a legislature at all, since the better-than-chance competence assumption seems to fail for anything harder than picking a mayor. Say what would move you — not "more empirical research," but which specific result, from an assembly or a jury system or a corporate board, would make you revise which term by roughly how much. Rough numbers are fine, I'm a journalist not an economist, but no on-the-one-hand hedging and no bibliography or reading list at the end, I just want the reasoning laid out where I can see the joints.
