For about fifteen years I have followed the argument over Lewis's Principal Principle and its interaction with Bayesian conditionalization — in the journals, and even more in the informal venues where I do most of my reading: rationalist blogs, forecasting forums, and long comment threads in which somebody rediscovers the undermining problem every eighteen months and presents it as news. What strikes me now is not that the argument is unresolved but that it is unresolved in a particular way: nobody appears to lose. Every camp can absorb every objection by a small redescription, and after three decades the disagreement has produced no settled result, no agreed counterexample, and no shared statement of what is in dispute. I want a diagnosis of the structure of the impasse, not a ruling on which principle is correct, and not a survey of the positions.

Here is the case as I understand it, stripped down so we are arguing about one thing. Suppose a machine prints heads or tails forever, and suppose that in this world chances are not extra furniture: they are fixed by the total infinite pattern of prints, being whatever the best systematization of that pattern — simplest, strongest, balanced in the usual way — assigns. You know the chance hypothesis C, which says that every print has chance one half of coming up heads. Then you learn E: the next billion prints will all be heads. E is not ruled out by C; on C it has a tiny but nonzero probability. And yet if E comes true, the pattern it completes is one that the best-system machinery would not summarize by C, so C would never have been true. Meanwhile a principle that says match your credence to the chance you know tells you to stay at one half on the next print, while conditionalizing on everything you learn drives you toward certainty in heads. Something has to give.

The camps, as I read them: one says the original principle is simply false and deference must be relativized, so that your credence in a given outcome on the basis of E and C should match your credence about the chance of that outcome conditional on E — which dissolves the case, since conditional on a billion heads the chance of another is hardly one half. A second says the replacement principle has no independent bite, because once C and E are both in the background your credence about the relevant conditional chance is already determined, so the norm cannot be violated and therefore cannot guide anyone; this camp holds that the original principle can be rescued by getting the conditional-probability machinery right, or by restricting which propositions may serve as evidence at all. A third says the whole collision is an artifact of the best-system view of chance: make chance primitive rather than read off the pattern, and there are no undermining futures and no contradiction, so the formal quarrel is downstream of a metaphysical one. A fourth says the principle was always a constraint on initial credences, that the moment you learn E you are no longer in the situation it describes, and that the paradox comes entirely from applying a constraint on priors to a posterior state. Underneath all of it, the notion of admissible evidence looks to me like a placeholder each camp fills in differently and then accuses the others of filling in question-beggingly.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and not as a conclusion: three distinct questions are being answered under one name — which prior credence functions are permissible, how credence should respond to what is learned, and what makes a chance claim true — and the reason no argument lands is that each camp can translate the others' principles into its own framework without residue, so nothing is ever actually refuted. If that is right, the debate is not substantive but is also not merely verbal, and I would like to know what it is instead.

To test the hypothesis I built a small exercise for a meetup. In one world I stipulate that chance is primitive and remains exactly what C says no matter what pattern of prints actually occurs; in the other I let chance supervene on the pattern as above. In the first world an agent who always matches her credence to the chance she knows and an agent who simply conditionalizes on everything she learns make different bets on the billion-and-first print, and one of them loses money systematically. Does that divergence show the disagreement is substantive after all, since the two policies are visibly not the same policy? Or does it merely show that I changed worlds between the two halves of the exercise? I would especially like to know whether you can describe a world in which the two policies place identical bets and yet the camps would still disagree — because that seems to me the sharpest available test of whether anything beyond betting behavior is at stake.

So: tell me what kind of disagreement this is, arguing from what each side actually claims rather than from the labels; tell me whether my three-questions diagnosis survives, and if not, what replaces it; and tell me what would count as progress here — a distinction, a theorem, a change of question, an agreement about what chance is — or else argue that no progress should be expected and say why. Finally, say what would change your mind about your own diagnosis: which consideration, if it were right, would push you to a different classification of this dispute, and at which step your reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong. Please answer in continuous prose only, as one would write a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bolded terms, no tables.
