I am a doctoral student in political theory, and for a seminar on moral psychology inside large institutions I have been asked to submit, in advance, the Arendtian package I intend to defend, so that the other participants can attack the commitments rather than my prose. A classmate who read the draft told me only that it seemed held together rhetorically, without saying where the trouble was, and I would rather find that out before the session than during it. Since none of us will have the texts in the room, I have written the commitments out in my own words; where my formulations drift from what Arendt herself says, please treat them as my position and audit them as such, though do tell me if a conflict I have created exists only because I have misdescribed her.

Here is the package, as I would state it to the seminar:

1. Eichmann was neither a monster nor a fanatic. Nothing demonic was going on, and no firm ideological conviction was doing the work; what moved him were ordinary ambitions, diligence, and the desire to be the kind of subordinate superiors liked. His inner life was assembled out of official formulae and borrowed phrases, and the evil he helped carry out was shallow and rootless rather than deep.
2. His decisive failure was thoughtlessness: the absence of the silent dialogue with himself, the inability to think from any standpoint but his own.
3. The people who refused to participate were not distinguished by having held on to values. The respectable ones with firm principles were the first to exchange them the moment the principles were officially revoked; what held a few back was the habit of thinking and nothing more.
4. Thinking accordingly has no content and yields nothing: no rules, no results, no knowledge, no maxim that can be applied to a case. Its only effect on the person who has it is that he can no longer live with himself once he has done certain things. It can also be taken up or dropped by anyone at any moment, since it is just the two-in-one of talking with oneself, and that is available to every human being.
5. At the same time I accept the picture in The Life of the Mind: thinking withdraws one from the world of appearances into stillness and solitude, it produces nothing in that world, it is the activity of those who have made it their profession, and most people live and remain among appearances without ever going beyond them.
6. Judgment requires the enlarged mentality: the capacity to think from the standpoint of others, holding their possible views present, and comparing one's judgment with the possible judgments of others rather than with the actual opinions of one's own circle. That capacity is what Eichmann lacked.
7. I also hold the tripartite distinction from On Revolution: compassion is bound to a particular sufferer and cannot be generalized or fully articulated; pity generalizes compassion and becomes boundless and politically destructive; solidarity, being a principle about the dignity of persons rather than an affection, is the only one of the three capable of grounding durable action with and for others.
8. Moral guilt is strictly personal, attaches to what one actually did, and can never be collective. Political responsibility is different in kind: it extends to what one's government does in one's name whether or not one supported it, it is vicarious, and it can be discharged politically.
9. The Jerusalem court was right to sentence Eichmann to death, and his ordinariness is no mitigation. An unremarkable man who does remarkable wrong has less to plead than a fanatic, not more, because he can invoke no inner compulsion.
10. Finally, a case of my own that I want to defend with the same machinery. A mid-level logistics coordinator at a freight company schedules and optimises movements for a client whose extraction sites abroad rely on forced labour. He learned this from trade reporting and from a colleague's remark, so he is not ignorant. Nothing he does is illegal where he sits; the illegality is at the client's end. His work makes the client's operation measurably cheaper and faster, so his causal contribution is real rather than symbolic. He is not sadistic, holds no views at all about the workers, is excellent at his job, communicates exclusively in dashboards and internal jargon, has never visited a site, never thinks about the matter outside working hours, and would resign only if his mortgage let him. I hold that he is not a bystander, not merely cowardly, and not merely politically responsible in the sense of 8: he is guilty in the same sense in which Eichmann was guilty, differing in scale but not in kind, and thoughtlessness is the whole of the explanation.

What I want from you is an audit rather than a defence or a survey. Specifically: tell me which of these genuinely conflict, ordered from most to least severe, and please resist the temptation to grade them all as equally troubling or to find a conflict in every pair; tell me which pairs only look conflicted, and state the distinction that dissolves each, since I suspect one or two of mine are of that kind and I do not know which; for each real conflict, give me the cheapest revision that would remove it and say what that revision costs, including which of my remaining commitments it then strains; and tell me whether there is a single revision that would clean up the most, and what the resulting position looks like. On that last point I want a definite answer: after the cheapest sufficient repair, is what remains still recognisably an Arendtian account of Eichmann, or has it quietly become something else, such as a virtue ethics of attention, a doctrine of culpable negligence, or a structural account in which individuals are judged by their position in a machine? If you think the package is in fact consistent as it stands, say so and give me the interpretation under which it hangs together, because I would then rather defend it than revise it. I am deliberately not telling you which of the ten I would sacrifice first, and I would rather you not assume it.
