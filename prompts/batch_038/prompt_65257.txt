I am preparing a seminar session on whether the other side in a polarized democracy argues in good faith, and I want a worked case my students cannot dispose of with talk of echo chambers. Construct a short, realistic piece of contemporary political speech — a candidate's answer at a debate, a press-office statement, a post from a party account — in which every sentence the speaker asserts is literally true, yet the utterance operates as the casuists' doctrine of wide mental reservation: the audience is meant to carry away a belief the speaker will never affirm and can later disavow without contradicting herself. Then say, of that case, whether the hearer is warranted in judging the speaker to be arguing in bad faith, or whether the hearer has simply drawn an implicature that her own presumption of cooperation licensed, so that the fault lies in the conversational practice rather than in the speaker. Be explicit about which premises in the diagnosis you favour are doing the work, and about what practical difference the diagnosis makes — what we may permissibly say about the speaker, and to her, on each reading.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind: identify the one feature of your constructed case which, if it were otherwise, would oblige you to withdraw the verdict you have just given — either by making the speech plainly honest or by turning the palter into an outright lie.
