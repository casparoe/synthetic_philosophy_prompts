So I got into a two-hour argument with my roommate last night about the absent-minded driver problem — Piccione and Rubinstein, the 1997 one — and I've decided the only way to settle it is to walk a room full of people into it and see which way they get pulled. Quick reference for the numbers I want used, because I'd rather you not have to reconstruct them: driver leaves START, reaches junction X, where EXIT pays 0 and CONTINUE takes him to junction Y; at Y, EXIT pays 4 and CONTINUE pays 1. He can't distinguish X from Y and can't remember whether he's already been through one. At the planning stage, if he CONTINUEs with probability p at each junction, expected payoff is p² + 4(1−p)p, which I'm pretty sure peaks at p = 2/3 (napkin math, tell me if I botched it). My roommate's line is that this is a manufactured non-problem: the driver obviously writes himself a note, and if you stipulate that he can't, then whatever he does at the junction is just ordinary updating on the new evidence that he's standing at a junction — boring and fine. My line is that there's real bite: the plan that's best from the outside looks defeatable from the inside, and it isn't obvious which of the two viewpoints is the confused one. Neither of us budged, obviously.

Normal classroom with movable desks, and I can book the hallway outside for about ten minutes if the activity really needs it, but assume I might not get it.
