I'm a postdoc in a vision lab and our journal club has turned into a fight about method instead of results, so I want to build a decision tree we can run each other through and settle who's actually standing where.

The setup, since the tree needs to hang off something concrete: we do backward masking. On some trials the subject says afterwards, sincerely and with real confidence, that they had a brief experience of something — "there was a flash, maybe reddish" — and on those very same trials they're at chance on forced-choice orientation and at chance on colour. In our wagering version they happily bet against their own answer. So we have a transcript in our hands and nobody here agrees on what it is. Our PI says the transcript is a text you interpret, and the whole point of the exercise is to build a picture of the subject's reported world while holding off on whether anything in it is real. Our staff scientist says that's eliminativism in a lab coat, and that if you take reports as data at all you owe the subject some default authority. Two others think we should stop collecting verbal reports entirely and design the paradigm so behaviour and physiology carry the whole load.

What I want you to write:

1. First, the logical space, before any tree. Pick the three or four binary questions you think this entire argument actually turns on, lay out the table of answer combinations, and mark each cell: occupied (say by what), empty, or occupied-but-unreachable-by-any-tree. Then the tree below should be visibly a route through that table, not a separate invention.

2. Then the decision tree itself: 7 to 9 branch nodes, 6 to 8 leaves, taking someone from "I have a sincere, confident first-person report that the subject's own forced-choice performance contradicts" to a named methodological position. Three of us are experimental psychologists who have never read philosophy of mind, so every node has to be answerable by someone who has no idea where it leads. No node may contain the name of a person, the name of a theory, or anything ending in -ism. Nodes are questions about reports, subjects, and tasks.

3. These splits have to show up somewhere in the tree: whether a report is evidence about the world or evidence about the subject's take on the world; whether there is any conceivable further finding that could show a sincere and confident subject had no experience at all; whether better interview technique can genuinely improve a subject's access to their own case or whether it just manufactures tidier confabulation; what happens to the whole method when the subject can't talk (a macaque, a preverbal infant, someone with severe aphasia, and yes, an artificial network); and whether how-it-felt can come apart from every discriminative ability and every disposition to report taken together.

4. Leaves I need you to land on, each with its standard name if it has one: the view that you suspend belief about the reported world entirely and it becomes an open empirical question whether anything corresponds to it; the view that a report is the output of a higher-order judgement or detection, so being wrong about your own experience is just that mechanism misfiring; the view that some sincere reports have nothing behind them at all; the view that carefully trained first-person elicitation is a real measurement instrument with its own validity criteria; the view that reports should be engineered out of the paradigm wherever possible; and the view that the report is simply behaviour and there's no further question to ask.

5. Plus one leaf you leave deliberately unnamed, because you don't think anyone actually holds it, with a line diagnosing why the cell is empty — incoherent, repellent, or just unexplored.

6. Flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot really separate. I'd bet money you cant cleanly split the "hold off on whether it's real" leaf from the "there's nothing behind it" leaf, and probably not from the higher-order-judgement one either. If a question exists that separates them, give me the question; if not, say so out loud in the tree rather than quietly merging them.

7. Each leaf ends with one sentence on what that person does differently in the lab on Monday — a new task, a different interview protocol, a dropped verbal measure, an added condition. Not a sentence of doctrine.

Keep it to about a page and a half of tree plus the table. No references, I just want the instrument.
