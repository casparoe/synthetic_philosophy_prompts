I am drafting a chapter for an edited volume on replication failure. The other contributors are working on contemporary psychology and biomedicine; my chapter argues that the N-ray affair of 1903–1905 is the better test case for philosophical accounts of falsification, because in it the community possessed everything Popper asks for — a falsifiable hypothesis, a public test, a negative result — and the criterion nonetheless did no work. I want that argument stress-tested before I commit to it.

The facts as I have them, so that you need not reconstruct them: Prosper-René Blondlot, a well-regarded physicist at Nancy, announced in 1903 a new form of radiation, detected by a slight enhancement in the brightness of a spark gap or a faintly luminous screen viewed in a darkened room, and named for the university. On one common count, over a hundred researchers published some three hundred papers reporting the effect; the radiation was said to be emitted by most substances, including living tissue, with the striking exceptions of green wood and certain treated metals. Kelvin, Crookes, Lummer, and Rubens, among others, failed to reproduce it. In 1904 Robert W. Wood, at Nature's instigation, attended a demonstration at Nancy: in the dark he removed the prism on which the apparatus depended, and the experimenters continued to report the enhancement; on another occasion he substituted an inert piece of wood for the object said to be emitting, with the same result. He published his account in Nature. Within about a year no one outside Nancy accepted N-rays, though Blondlot himself reportedly remained convinced into the 1920s, and the Académie des sciences had meanwhile been asked to adjudicate a priority dispute among rival claimants.

My thesis has two parts, and I want both examined rather than endorsed. First, the hypothesis was falsified, if falsified it was, at a level Popper's schema does not describe: no basic statement of the form "here is a case in which N-rays are predicted and absent" did the work. What did the work was a higher-order claim about the observers — that their reports tracked expectation rather than illumination — and that claim is itself a psychological theory carrying its own auxiliaries. Second, Wood's intervention succeeded not by producing a falsifying instance but by constructing, for the first time, a situation in which the hypothesis and its negation yielded different outcomes; prior tests were not, in Deborah Mayo's sense, severe, since the method would have registered the effect whether or not the effect was there.

Give me the analysis, running in both directions. Begin with the mapping, and be exacting: what was the theory under test, what were its observational consequences, what counted as a test and who was competent to perform one, and what counted as evidence on each side. Then take falsificationism seriously and locate precisely where it bites. N-ray theory is about as clearly falsifiable as a hypothesis gets, so the demarcation criterion pronounces it scientific and then falls silent — is that a defect in the criterion, or the correct verdict with an excessive expectation attached to it? Address the asymmetry thesis directly: three hundred positive reports were outweighed by a single negative intervention, which is the reverse of what naive falsificationism predicts, and say what the Popperian can make of the basic statements the community accepted and those it refused. Then assess my two claims and tell me where they are overstated — in particular, whether a Popperian can absorb the severity point through the requirement that basic statements be intersubjectively testable, and whether my higher-order reading collapses into a Duhem–Quine holism that explains everything and therefore nothing.

Next, set the rival accounts against mine: Lakatos's diagnosis of a degenerating programme; the experimenter's regress familiar from the sociology of scientific knowledge — the circularity whereby an experiment is known to be adequate because it produces the phenomenon, and the phenomenon known to be real because adequate experiments produce it; and the category of pathological science. For each, say what it explains that falsificationism cannot, and identify at least one feature of the episode it handles badly. My own candidate is that the affair has a determinate right answer, discoverable in principle by better experiments, which sits awkwardly with accounts on which the outcome is settled by negotiation and closure.

Close with two things. Tell me where each theory is simply silent, rather than stretching it to speak. And tell me whether the chapter's framing survives: was there, between 1903 and 1905, any criterion an editor, a prize committee, or the Académie could have applied in deciding what to publish and reward, and does the fact that the episode is now a stock cautionary tale about experimenter bias vindicate the demarcation project or support the view that it was never a usable project at all. I do not want a bibliography or a survey of the secondary literature. Flag any point where you are unsure of the historical record rather than filling it in plausibly.
