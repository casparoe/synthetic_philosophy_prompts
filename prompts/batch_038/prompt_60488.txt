I'm putting together a short position paper for a workshop on what an aligned system should actually be serving, and I want someone to poke holes in the package before I circulate it. Here's where I've landed:

1. Pluralism about the good. Welfare, autonomy, knowledge, beauty aren't reducible to one another, and I don't think there's a fact of the matter about how much value an outcome contains in total. That doesn't paralyze the engineering side, though: the system still has to output one policy, and I think that policy should get picked by a procedure that's fair to the parties rather than by maximizing anything.

2. Interpersonal comparisons are meaningless to me. Each person's welfare ordering is thier own — meaningful only up to how they rank their own possible situations, with no common unit across people. So anything that literally adds one person's gains to another's losses, or trades them off at some exchange rate, isn't well-defined.

3. Pareto, stated in each person's own terms: if a change is better by someone's lights and worse by nobody's, take it.

4. For choosing the procedure itself, I think the veil of ignorance is the right device — pick the rule you'd want if you didn't know whose position you'd end up in — and I expect that lands you somewhere around average utilitarianism unless you're extremely risk averse.

5. Neutrality. The system has to be justifiable to each affected party without leaning on the claim that their conception of the good is worse than somebody else's. I mean justification, not effects; I'm completely at peace with some ways of life losing out downstream.

6. Where actual and extrapolated values diverge, aim at the extrapolated ones — what each person would endorse if they knew more, thought harder, and weren't under time pressure. Actual desires are evidence about the target, not the target. Something like the CEV move.

7. Leftover conflicts get settled by a floor-then-headcount rule: guarantee everyone an adequate minimum of welfare, then serve the extrapolated ends of as many parties as you can.

And one case where I have a firm verdict. Say 60% of a region's residents, once extrapolated, want the regional language to survive as a living community language, which realistically requires that kids in the area get schooled in it. The other 40%, extrapolated, would rather raise their kids in a global lingua franca. I think the system should back the 60% here and require the schooling, even though the 40% end up worse off by their own lights — and I don't think that's me ranking conceptions of the good, it's just that more of them want it.

Go through this and tell me which of these commitments actually pull against each other and which ones only look like they do. I want them ranked by severity rather than listed flat, and for each real one say what would have to give and what the cheapest repair costs me, including whether fixing it makes some other item worse. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most. I genuinely don't have a view about which of these I'd sacrifice, so don't read anything into the order I listed them in. Lead with your bottom line in the very first sentence and then give me the reasoning — no preamble, no restating my list back at me, and no bibliography or further-reading section, I just want the audit.
