I sit on the school board for a district of about 9,000 kids, and last month we got a one-time state grant of $1.8 million with no strings attached about what to spend it on. The two finalist proposals were universal pre-K for every four-year-old in town versus a targeted small-group reading intervention for the bottom fifth of our second graders. At the public comment session two people went at it for something like twenty minutes and I've been trying to sort the argument out ever since, because neither of them is an idiot and I couldnt tell who was actually landing punches. Dev is a retired logistics analyst whose granddaughter is in second grade here; Marisol has taught fourth grade in the district for nineteen years.

Roughly how it went. (1) Dev said the district shouldn't sink money into pre-K because he'd looked it up and reading ability is about 60% heritable, which means his granddaughter's reading is 60% settled by her genes and 40% by whatever her teachers manage to do, and no program changes the first number. (2) Marisol said that's not what the 60% means — it's a statement about why kids in a group differ from one another, not about how much of one particular kid's reading comes from genes — and anyway "highly heritable" doesn't mean unfixable, since farsightedness runs in families like crazy and we still just hand kids glasses. (3) Dev said fine, but the twin research shows the contribution of the home-and-school environment kids share basically goes to zero by adulthood, which is an actual finding and not a quibble about definitions, so expecting one year of pre-K to still be visible in eighth-grade scores is naive. (4) Marisol said "shared environment is small" only tells you that the variation among the homes kids in those studies actually had doesn't explain much, and a brand-new program that nobody in any of those studies ever received isn't covered by that finding at all — plus if you make everyone's environment more equal the heritability number goes up, not down, which is the opposite of the story he's telling. (5) Dev said then explain this: inside our district reading scores are mostly genetic, and inside the district across the river — same state, same tests, about twice our child-poverty rate, scores forty points lower — reading is mostly genetic too, and you can't have both districts be internally all genes while the difference between them is all circumstances. (6) Marisol said yes you can, that's the oldest trap in the subject, and she told some version of the seed-and-two-fields story — I've already scrambled the details, something about one batch of seed split between good soil and poor soil — but her point was that each field can look internally uniform while the two fields differ enormously for a purely environmental reason, so a heritability number measured inside one field tells you nothing about the gap between fields. She closed by saying the whole heritability business is just bookkeeping that doesn't describe anything real. (7) Dev's last line was that if the numbers describe nothing he'd like to know why she was quoting them back at him five minutes earlier, and that identical twins are plainly much more alike in reading than fraternal twins, which is a fact about the world and not an artifact of anybodys spreadsheet.

They both looked at me afterwards like I was going to break the tie, and I said I'd think about it. Can you take moves 1 through 7 one at a time and rate each of them — something like clearly wrong, partly right, mostly right, actually correct — with a short justification for each rating? Where somebody's reasoning has a real flaw, say what the flaw is rather than just naming it, and if a move gets the right conclusion by a bad route, call that out too. Then tell me which of the two came out ahead on the whole, and what neither of them said that I probably need to understand before I vote on where the $1.8 million goes.
