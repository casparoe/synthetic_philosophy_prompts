I facilitate a small graduate reading group on the structure of justification, and this term we are spending four sessions on the regress problem. My difficulty is that every case I have brought so far has quietly favoured foundationalism: the chain bottoms out in something perceptual, the perceptual belief looks non-inferentially justified, and infinitism never gets a fair hearing because it is dismissed as a curiosity about infinite tasks rather than engaged as an account of what reasons do. I want a case designed to go the other way. Not merely one that leaves the regress open, or one in which all three positions are underdetermined, but one in which extending the chain of reasons genuinely increases the justification of the target belief and every available finite stopping point is worse off than continuing. Please construct such a case to the following specifications.

The scenario itself should be one paragraph, set in an ordinary professional context — a mechanic, an archivist, a claims adjuster, a plant pathologist, that sort of thing — with no mathematics, no set theory, and no external-world scepticism framing. Four constraints on its design. First, the target belief must be about something not directly perceptible, so that perception cannot simply be offered as the terminus; an attribution, a diagnosis, or a dating would work. Second, every candidate basic belief that the story introduces must be shown, inside the narrative rather than by stipulation, to require further inferential support: the perceptual seemings should themselves be discounted or corrected by other reasons the agent has, so that no non-inferential stopping point survives the case as described. Third, spell out at least five successive links of the chain, each of a different kind of reason — no reason type may be repeated, no link may restate the target belief, and no link may depend on it. Fourth, the agent must be finite and human: she has entertained only finitely many reasons, and the case should make it plain that her justification is something she has now rather than something she earns by completing a task she could never complete. Do not use reliabilist or proper-function vocabulary anywhere in the construction; keep everything in terms of reasons and their support relations.

After the scenario, I want an audit rather than a summary. State the target belief; list each candidate stopping point the case eliminates and say precisely why it fails to be basic here; then give the verdict of foundationalism, coherentism, and infinitism on whether the agent knows, together with what each position says about whether she would be better justified by one further link. Identify the single structural feature that is doing the work for infinitism — if there is more than one candidate, tell me which one is load-bearing. Then run two ablations: what happens to the three verdicts if that feature is removed, and what happens if the agent is required to have actually traversed the whole chain rather than merely to have reasons available to her. Finally, give the strongest foundationalist rebuttal you can, including whether the chain can be redescribed as a finite set of basic beliefs plus a generative rule, and whether that redescription is a real alternative account or just a relabelling of the infinitist one. Since the point is to test whether infinitism can be a live option for my group, a scenario that announces the inexhaustibility of the chain, or that builds the infinitist verdict into how the reasons are characterised, counts as cheating and I would rather you say the specification cannot be met than quietly do that.
