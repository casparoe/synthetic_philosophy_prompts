**1.** The coffee taster case is usually read as an argument from the unreliability of introspection. The idea: Chase and Sanborn give incompatible reports about their own experience, both are sincere, both are attentive, and there's no way to adjudicate — so whatever it is they claim to be reporting isn't the sort of thing that can be reported on, and the traditional concept of a quale (intrinsic, private, directly apprehensible) is doing no work. On this reading the case is an epistemological one. It's about the tasters' access to their experience being bad, and about us having no reason to think anyone's access is better.

I think this is the wrong moral to draw, and that drawing it makes the argument weaker than it could be. The case isn't showing that introspection is unreliable. It's showing that there's no determinate fact for introspection to get right or wrong.

**2.** To see that, separate two kinds of change a taster can undergo over twenty years.

Call the first *hedonic drift*: the coffee tastes as it always did, but what the taster makes of it has changed — his palate has been educated, his standards have shifted, and the very same taste that used to please now bores him. Nothing here is mysterious, and nothing here touches qualia at all. Everyone agrees hedonic response is not part of the taste.

Call the second *discriminative drift*: the taster's ability to tell this coffee apart from other coffees, to re-identify it blind, to say whether today's cup is the same as yesterday's, has changed. This is the interesting one, because the discriminations available to a taster look like they partly fix what his experience is of, not merely how well he describes it. A taste that can be told apart from nine other tastes sits in a different place in a space of tastes than one that can be told apart from two.

Now: the standard case is usually presented as if one taster reports hedonic drift and the other reports a change in the tasting itself, with the second report being the puzzling one. But notice that neither taster is actually in a position to distinguish discriminative drift from a change in the coffee, or from a change in his tongue, or from hedonic drift plus a redescription. What's missing isn't reliability. What's missing is a fact — an identity condition for "the same taste" that survives the taster's own change. That's why the dispute is unresolvable, and it would be unresolvable even for a perfectly introspectively reliable taster.

So I want to swap the epistemological reading for a structural one: the case shows there is no fixed point of experience under change in the experiencer. The traditional properties attributed to qualia — intrinsicality, privacy, direct apprehensibility — are all ways of demanding such a fixed point, and it's the demand that fails, not our epistemic position relative to it.

**3.** The reason I think this is more than relabelling is that the structural reading predicts a third case, which the unreliability reading can't make sense of. Suppose a taster, call her Voss, has spent twenty years at the same job, has never changed her opinion of the coffee, still loves it, still says it tastes the same — but has acquired, purely through practice, a new discrimination. She can now tell this roast apart from a neighbouring blend that she would previously have called identical. Asked afterwards whether the coffee tastes the same as it did when she started, she says no: it tastes the same, she says, but it also now has a burnt-sugar edge it didn't have before. Her tongue is unchanged, the coffee is unchanged, her liking is unchanged.

On the unreliability reading, Voss's report is just a better report: she's noticing what was always there, and her earlier self was a worse witness. Fine — but then her earlier and later reports about the same unchanging stimulus are both "correct," which is exactly what the standard case was supposed to make impossible, except now without any change in the taster's tastes to explain it. On the structural reading, there's no problem: what it is like to taste the coffee depends on which coffees it can be discriminated from, so acquiring a discrimination genuinely changes the experience, and both reports are accurate reports of different experiences. The dispute in the standard case is then not a failure of witness but a disagreement about which discrimination profile fixes the identity of the taste — and there's no principled way to pick one, which is the point I wanted.

If that's right, a bonus: the inverted spectrum case dissolves rather than gets answered. A wholesale swap of the colour space changes the discrimination profile wholesale, so on my view it changes the experience, correctly reported, and it is no longer a case of same-experience-different-quale. Which worries me, because it sounds like I've conceded that there is a fact of the matter about experience — a dispositional one, individuated by discriminations — and handed my opponent exactly the thing I was supposed to be quining. I don't know yet whether that concession costs me anything.
