I am in the second half of an upper-level seminar on naturalistic approaches to religion — the stretch where we leave philosophy of religion behind and move into the cognitive science of religion and cultural evolution, having started the term with Dennett's insistence that religion be studied as a natural phenomenon like any other. What has me stuck is a phrase one of my classmates deployed twice in the same session, to close down two quite different arguments. His phrase was that religion is a spandrel. It worked rhetorically both times, and I am writing the response paper on that exchange, but I have realized I cannot say what he was actually claiming, because the sentence seems to slide between several distinct theses depending on what it is being pushed against. In the first instance, someone had just defended a costly-signaling account of communal ritual as a group-level adaptation, and my classmate said the debate was misconceived: if religion is a spandrel, then there was no function for it to have been selected for, and the real question is not what religion is for but what else in our cognition it falls out of. In the second, minutes later, he used the same sentence to a different end: because religious belief is a byproduct of machinery built for something else, he argued, it has no truth-tracking pedigree, which is a reason to withhold assent from it — not a refutation of any particular creed, but a standing discount on all of them. He plainly took the second claim to follow from the first, and nobody challenged the step.

What I want is a menu, not a summary. Could you lay out the distinct, precisely formulated claims that "religion is a spandrel" could be expressing, ordered from the ones that are little more than seminar platitudes to the ones that are substantive and defensible to the ones that are false, or at least unavailable to anyone who has thought them through carefully? I can see some of the axes along which the readings diverge but cannot articulate them, and I would like them handled explicitly: whether "spandrel" is being used in the strict architectural sense in which the term entered evolutionary biology — a byproduct that arises necessarily, with a definite form, given the rest of the structure — or in the loose sense of anything not selected for; whether the claim concerns origins or current design, since those come apart once a byproduct has been co-opted and further shaped; whether it is a claim about religion as a whole or about particular cognitive ingredients that religious thought recruits; whether it functions as an ontological verdict or as a null model and an allocation of the burden of proof; and whether it is a claim about individuals or about groups, given that something can be a byproduct at one level and functional at another. Where two formulations look synonymous and are not, say what turns on the difference.

Then, separately, tell me which of those readings — if any — would support each of my classmate's two moves, the dismissal of the signaling debate and the epistemic discount, and whether the second genuinely rides on the first or requires a premise he never stated. If there is a reading under which one move is right and the other is not, flag it prominently, since that is the case I most want for the paper. I am not after a bibliography or a survey of who holds what; I have the readings assigned and know the names. I want the analysis, and I would rather you say plainly when a given formulation of the slogan is too indeterminate to bear a truth value than assign it a verdict it has not earned.
