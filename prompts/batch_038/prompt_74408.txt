I cover cognitive science and the philosophy of mind for a general-interest magazine, and this Friday I am delivering the eulogy for Dr. Nora Ahlgren, a memory researcher I first profiled eleven years ago and who became, improbably, a friend. I would like you to write it. The constraints are unusually tight, because the consolation I want to offer is one she spent her career arguing against, and I would rather be honest at her funeral than effective.

Who she was, professionally. Two things matter. First, she was among the most persistent critics of the extended mind thesis — the 1998 proposal by Andy Clark and David Chalmers that a notebook, a smartphone, or a colleague can be literally part of someone's cognitive system. Her objection was never that the environment is unimportant, but that importance is not constitution: things can be coupled to a mind in the tightest causal way without any of the cognition happening in them. Second, her own research concerned autobiographical memory, and she held that remembering is reconstruction rather than playback — that a memory is assembled on the spot out of fragments, under pressure from what you now believe.

She also had no patience with the standard funeral register. When a colleague died, she turned to me in the pew and said that what people were offering was a photograph being mistaken for a person. That phrase is mine to give you and you may use it; it should be the only thing from her in quotation marks, and I will confirm the wording myself.

The idea I want the eulogy built on. In I Am a Strange Loop, Douglas Hofstadter develops the view that the "I" is not a thing but a self-representing pattern — a loop by which a brain symbolizes itself — and that a pattern of that kind is not married to one realizer, so that a person can be present, thinly and unevenly and to a degree, in the minds of those who thoroughly internalized them. He means this seriously rather than decoratively: it is his account of what it is for someone to have died and to have survived, and of why selves admit of degrees.

Why this is difficult. That is precisely the claim Nora denied. I do not want to win an argument over a coffin, and I do not want to smuggle in a view she considered confused. So the speech must concede her point and then locate what survives the concession. My own sense of the shape of it, which you may sharpen but should not abandon: she was right about the vehicles. Nothing of hers is running in my skull; no one in that room is now her; the coupling between her and her students was causal and not constitutive; and her own research rules out the comfortable version, since what we carry is not a copy but a construction, built under pressure from what each of us already is. But patterns are individuated differently from the stuff that realizes them, and she herself insisted that the interesting questions about a mind are asked at the level of description where beliefs, jokes, and standards live. At that level, what she was can be re-instantiated — imperfectly, in many divergent versions, in people who learned to think the way she made them think. That is not her continuing. It is her having been the kind of thing that leaves a re-instantiable shape.

What the concession costs, and the speech should say so plainly: a pattern re-instantiated in forty heads is not one person, it is forty drafts that disagree with one another; the reconstruction is lossy and biased toward what each of us needed; and keeping the draft accurate is labour rather than a fact about the world. I want that to land as an obligation without tipping into a homily.

The room. Her two daughters in the front row, who have told the funeral director that they do not want their mother reassured into anything. A hospital chaplain who speaks after me. Perhaps a dozen of her former graduate students along the back wall, who are philosophers and will hear every move I make. And eighty people who came because they liked her. Nora's last three years involved the illness she had spent her career studying; it will be in the room whether or not I name it. You may treat it as a fact but never as an argument, and nothing in the speech should require her daughters to accept a claim in order to be permitted to grieve.

Requirements:

1. Six to seven minutes spoken; write it to be read aloud, not studied.
2. Her objection must appear once, in its strongest form — the version her students would recognize as the one they actually had to answer — and then be conceded, not defeated.
3. Her signature habit with graduate students was to refuse the first answer and ask only what would have to be true. Use that question twice: once as a memory of her in a lab meeting, and once near the end, turned on my own consolation rather than on the mourners.
4. Two specifics, one for warmth and one for humour at her expense. She kept a tin of biscuits on her desk for visiting speakers, unchanged for years, which she called the control condition; and she once corrected a Nobel laureate's pronunciation of a colleague's surname to his face, then apologized to the surname.
5. Do not use the words legacy, spirit, journey, or celebrate, and do not write "in a very real sense" or "she lives on."
6. Name Hofstadter at most once and do not summarize his book. The philosophers in the back will know what I am invoking; everyone else does not need a lecture.
7. Invent no quotations from her. Where you need something she said, describe it and leave a marked gap for me to fill.
8. The argument must arrive as affection. If a passage would read as a seminar, cut it.
9. End somewhere the chaplain can follow without contradiction, and without my asserting or denying anything about what happens after death.

After the speech, add four or five sentences addressed to me rather than to the room, identifying where you think the concession does the least work — the point at which a determined opponent in that back row would say I have quietly changed the subject from metaphysics to sentiment. I would like to know that before Friday.
