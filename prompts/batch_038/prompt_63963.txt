For about eleven years I have kept a notebook of predictions about matters my neighbours must act on: whether a creek will overtop the low road within a day, whether a late frost will take the blossom, whether a repaired culvert will hold through the spring. I have no training in statistics or in philosophy and I have read nothing on the subject, so please treat the practice I am about to describe as my own invention rather than assuming I am using someone else's vocabulary.

From the start I have declined to write down a single number. I write two: the lowest chance I can honestly defend and the highest, and I treat everything between them as still open. When new information bears on a prediction I revise both ends, and I do not allow one end to cross the other. Most of the time the band narrows, which feels like learning. But on three or four occasions it has *widened* after I received information, and I have never satisfied myself as to whether that is a flaw in my method to be patched out, or an honest report that the news told me less than I hoped while simultaneously making me less sure of everything nearby. One such case is described below.

I now coordinate a volunteer group of nine that advises the county on whether to pre-position sandbags, close low-water crossings, and open the shelter. We meet for twenty minutes on a Tuesday, nobody in the group has mathematics beyond arithmetic, and the dispatcher who signs off will accept only a yes or a no. We also publish a record of how often we were right, and that record currently demands single numbers from us, which feels like a betrayal of the whole method.

What I want from you is a procedure this group could actually run each week, in ordered steps: how wide a band we may honestly write down and when we must widen it rather than pretend to precision; what to do when two members give bands that do not overlap at all; how to choose an action when the low end of the band says stand down and the high end says act; at what point we are obliged to collapse the band into one number, and what number that should be; and whether a band can be scored afterwards for accuracy at all, or whether the act of scoring quietly forces us back to a single figure. Please mark plainly which steps are measurements and which are merely our choosing how cautious to be, and tell me where the procedure will misfire.

Then run it on three cases. First: a band of 0.25 to 0.70 that the creek tops the road within thirty-six hours, where closing the road costs a forty-minute detour for the school run and six hours of county labour, and leaving it open risks a vehicle in moving water. Second: two members report 0.1 to 0.4 and 0.5 to 0.8 on the same frost, and neither will move. Third, the widening case: we held 0.3 to 0.5 on a freeze, then took a reading from a station whose reliability we have never managed to pin down and whose error has in the past run in both directions, and after revising we were left holding 0.15 to 0.75 — should we act on a band that got wider because of information, and if so on which end?

At least one of these should come out in a way I will dislike; I would rather be told that now. Finally, if what I have stumbled into has names — the widening, the two-number habit, the rules for acting from one — give me the names, since I need words to use in front of nine people. Do not give me a reading list, citations, or authors to look up; I want terminology, not homework.
