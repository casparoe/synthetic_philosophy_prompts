So I'm teaching the last week of a second-year value theory seminar right before break, and to keep them awake I've put A Christmas Carol on the list alongside the wrong-kind-of-reasons material. Everyone knows the story, half the class knows the literature, and I want to hand them something that treats the two as actually in tension rather than as a cute seasonal illustration.

Here's the thing I noticed on rereading it and can't stop thinking about, and I want you to build the reading around it. The middle staves do all the object-given work: Belle's account of how another master has displaced her, Fred's insistence on warmth as a good in itself, the Cratchits managing to be joyful in a house with nothing in it. Those are considerations that bear on whether Scrooge's way of life is any good. But the last stave gives him almost none of that — it's the charwomen flogging his bed curtains and shirts to a fence, a corpse nobody comes to look at, a grave with his name on it and no mourners, and sheer animal terror. And crucially, the conversion doesn't land until after that. So on the surface the story needs a big dose of the exact structure the demon case has: fear of what will happen to you if you don't come to feel a certain way.

Which I've been calling the Marley problem, purely as my own coinage for the class — Marley's chains are a threat of the same shape, a man who forged his own punishment in life and is now telling you to look at what you're forging. So the whole reformation is kickstarted by a paradigm wrong-kind reason, in the very first stave, before any of the object-given stuff arrives.

What I want from you:

Is the story cheating? Does Dickens use state-given considerations only causally — as the machinery that gets Scrooge into a position to finally see the object-given considerations for what they are — or does he let them carry justificatory weight, so that the terror of the grave is doing the actual work of showing that greed is bad? Take Hieronymi's idea seriously, the one where right-kind reasons are the ones that bear on the question the attitude itself answers, and ask which question each stave is addressed to. Where do the two come apart, and where does the story blur them on purpose?

The harder problem: you can't form an attitude at will, so a wrong-kind reason can't directly give you the right kind. Can it bootstrap it indirectly — can the threat buy you the attention you need to notice that Fred is right about warmth? The story seems to think yes and to make that its whole engine. Is that a real philosophical option, or is Dickens just skipping the gap because it's fiction and he gets away with it? I want you to be honest about which.

And then the last page. He wakes up, it's Christmas morning, he's ecstatic, he buys the enormous turkey and sends it anonymously, he raises Bob's wages. Is that joy and generosity for the right kind of reason at that point, or is it still the fear wearing a nicer costume? Give me your actual verdict on that, not both-sides mush.

One student has already told me this whole exercise is a category error, on the grounds that wrong-kind reasons are about reasons for attitudes like belief and admiration, and what Scrooge changes is what he wants and does, not what he thinks. Adjudicate that — is he right, and if he's half-right, say which half survives.

Two more things. Don't reduce the story to a case study; I want the places where it pushes back on the framework, where the framework makes the story look dumber than it is, and where you have to admit the reading doesn't fit. And end by saying what the story contributes to the debate that arguments alone don't — I have a suspicion it's something about what being converted by fear actually feels like from the inside, which is precisely the thing the papers have to leave out, but tell me if I'm wrong.

No quotes from the text and no bibliography, please, nobody is going to look any of it up and neither should you. Around a thousand words is fine. Also don't worry if you think the choice of story is sentimental — it kindof is, that's the point.
