Last month our department convened a four-person panel to decide whether to fund a proposed study of whether common agricultural pesticides produce aversive states in pollinators. Two of the panelists argued for the better part of an hour in front of the rest of us, and the rest of us mostly stayed quiet. I would like you to work through their exchange move by move rather than give me a general verdict at the end.

Aisha opened in favor of the grant; Marcus opposed it. Their moves, in order, were these:

1. Aisha said that even on deliberately pessimistic assumptions — a one-in-a-thousand chance that an insect is sentient, and a moral weight one hundredth that of a human — the arithmetic still comes out enormous once you multiply by the roughly ten-to-the-eighteenth insects alive at any time, and that this dwarfs anything else on our funding slate. She said that a panel which claims to take expected value seriously is not entitled to decline to do the multiplication just because one of the factors is small.

2. Marcus said the one-in-a-thousand is not a calibrated quantity but a shrug with a decimal point attached: set it at one in a million and her conclusion evaporates, set it at one in ten and it becomes overwhelming, and nothing we could ever observe would tell us which is right. He said an argument whose verdict flips on an uncalibratable parameter is not an argument, and that the same form of reasoning can be pointed at microbes, at the number of people who might exist in the far future, or at anything else that comes with an astronomically large count.

3. Aisha said that is just the generic difficulty of acting under moral uncertainty, and that it does not entitle anyone to ignore the question. She said the disanalogy with microbes is evidential: insects show nociceptor-like pathways, persistent avoidance of injured body parts, modulation of injury responses by analgesic-like compounds, and trade-off behavior in which an animal accepts a noxious stimulus to reach a reward — the same category of evidence we already rely on for mammals.

4. Marcus granted the evidence and said it cuts both ways. Raising the probability raises the number, which is precisely the mechanism he objects to. Worse, he said, if insects are sentient then the ordinary picture of wild insect life — parasitism, predation, desiccation, near-total offspring mortality — suggests that most such lives may contain more bad than good, and the same arithmetic that recommends protecting them then recommends that there be fewer of them, a conclusion he doubted she would sign.

5. Aisha said she need not sign it, because the two do not follow from the same premise. Our evidence bears on the capacity to suffer, not on the sign of a whole life; getting from the first to the second would require welfare totals across entire insect lifespans, which nobody has. She added that the harms we can actually address are harms we cause — pesticide drift, artificial light at night, agricultural practice — so there is no parity between reducing suffering and reducing numbers.

6. Marcus said that if she concedes the sign is unknown, she has conceded that a term in her own expected-value expression is unestimated, and has merely swapped a very large number for a very small one. On parity, he said the largest available lever on how many insects there are is not farming reform but whether wild populations keep declining, and that declining populations is exactly the outcome her own numbers say is a relief in expectation; she cannot, he said, take the credit for the arithmetic and disown its output.

7. Aisha said that this assumes the object of value is the number of sentient beings. It is not, she said: it is the incidence of bad states, and the two come apart as soon as a life is even slightly net-positive.

8. Marcus said that "even slightly net-positive" is another uncalibratable parameter, so the exchange has come full circle: her position rests on a guess about a sign, and his rests on refusing to let a guess about a sign determine how a department spends its money.

Please address the following.

(a) Rate each of the eight moves on a scale from clearly wrong to clearly right, with a sentence of justification for each rating. Where a move succeeds only by quietly conceding something to the other side, say what it concedes.

(b) Marcus makes at least four distinguishable objections — the calibration complaint, the complaint that large counts swamp everything, the sign-of-net-welfare complaint, and the intervention-asymmetry complaint. Treat them separately and say which of them actually do damage to Aisha's case and which only sound damaging.

(c) Say whether move 3 does the work Aisha wants from it. In particular: does evidence of that kind bear on the probability of sentience, on the moral weight conditional on sentience, or on both, and does that distinction change her calculation?

(d) Flag every point at which one of them slides between the probability that insects are sentient and the weight each insect carries once it is sentient, and say whether any such slide is load-bearing for the conclusion it supports.

(e) Identify the premise both of them rely on about summing welfare across a vast number of individuals that neither one defends, and say whether a panelist who rejected that premise would vote differently on the grant.

(f) Conclude with who was more right overall, and then, separately, whether the disagreement was ever really about insects, or whether it was a dispute in decision theory under moral uncertainty that they happened to be conducting by way of insects.

Do not split the difference for the sake of evenhandedness; if one of them is simply wrong on a given move, say so plainly. No citations or references are needed — I am interested in the reasoning, not in the literature.
