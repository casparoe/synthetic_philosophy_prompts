A research group I belong to is drafting an internal assessment of the programme often called ergodicity economics, and I have been given the section that ranks the objections to it. The claim I want the objections scored against is this: for decisions whose payoffs compound over time, the correct criterion is the time-average growth rate of the relevant outcome variable, and this criterion — growth-rate or geometric rationality, as against the ensemble-average reasoning of expected utility theory — requires no utility function and no appeal to risk preferences beyond what the dynamics of the situation already supply.

Here are the five objections as I have formulated them. Please take them to be self-contained; you should not need any literature beyond what is written here.

(1) *Domain.* A time average is defined only over a sequence of outcomes, so the criterion can govern only repeated decisions. A single-shot gamble, which is the case expected utility theory was built to handle, has no time average at all. On this reading the programme does not refute expected utility theory; it carves out a subclass of indefinitely repeated, compounding problems in which a different criterion applies, and the two frameworks coexist without conflict.

(2) *Dynamics.* A time-average growth rate is not a property of a gamble but of a gamble together with a rule for what happens between periods: whether winnings are reinvested in full, whether the agent may borrow or must hold a reserve, whether wealth may be pooled with others or passed on. Different but equally natural rules applied to the same one-period gamble yield different growth-optimal choices. The work is therefore being done by a concealed modelling choice rather than by the mathematics of non-ergodicity, and there is no unique ergodic answer available to displace the utility-theoretic one.

(3) *Relabelling.* Maximising the time-average growth rate of wealth is arithmetically the same thing as maximising expected utility with a logarithmic utility function over period outcomes. To say that the logarithm is supplied by the dynamics rather than by a value judgement is therefore not to eliminate utility but to fix the utility function by stipulation and call the result objective. What is advertised as a discovery about the world is a choice of numéraire.

(4) *Population.* The ensemble average is the right object of study whenever the agents whose welfare matters are many, interacting, and able to share risk: they insure, trade, pool, bequeath, or care about the distribution of outcomes across persons rather than the trajectory of any one of them. Actual economies consist of such agents, so the single-agent time average is the wrong unit of analysis even where it is well defined — and the aggregate puzzles the programme claims to dissolve are puzzles about populations in the first place.

(5) *Equivalence.* The programme's sharpest results depend on there being an absorbing state, a level of wealth from which recovery is impossible. But where such a state genuinely exists, expected utility theory with a utility function falling away steeply enough near it recommends the same choices; and where it does not exist — because insolvency law, insurance, human capital, or family support put an effective floor under wealth — the process is not non-ergodic in the sense the argument needs. Either way the two frameworks are extensionally equivalent in precisely the cases that would decide between them.

What I need from you:

- For each objection, a probability between 0 and 1 that it is correct substantially as stated; a fraction between 0 and 1 for how much of the central claim would be lost if it were correct (1.0 if the claim cannot survive it in any form, 0.0 if it survives intact with merely narrowed scope); and the product of the two, which I shall call the damage score.
- A ranking of the five by damage score, sorted into three tiers — fatal, seriously damaging, survivable — with one sentence saying why each lands where it does.
- One pair of objections that would be jointly more damaging than their separate scores suggest, with two or three sentences explaining the interaction.
- For each objection, a verdict on whether it receives more, less, or about as much discussion in the surrounding debate as its damage score warrants.
- A single number: your probability that within fifteen years the programme will be regarded as having displaced expected utility theory as the default framework for intertemporal choice under uncertainty, rather than as a specialised correction operating inside it. Say which of the five objections moved that number furthest from where you started.

Score the objections as stated and at their strongest. If you think one of them overreaches, note that in a subordinate clause, but do not let it depress the probability you assign. Do not treat agreement with the programme as the default position: if you think the central claim is largely right, that should show up as low values in the second column, not in a defence of it. Please do not supply citations or a bibliography — I shall add references myself. Put the numbers and the tier list first and the reasoning afterwards, and keep the whole thing to roughly 700 words.
