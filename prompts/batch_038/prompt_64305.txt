I work on the conceptual foundations of psychiatric epidemiology, and over the past two years I have become committed to a fairly strong version of the looping-effects picture. Stated as bluntly as I can, my position is this: because psychiatric classifications are interactive kinds, the categories of the DSM and ICD cannot in principle be validated against a fixed pre-classificatory reality, and so disagreements about whether a given revision is a genuine improvement are not decidable by evidence in the way that disagreements about, say, the arrangement of the elements are. Once a category is introduced or renamed, the people who fall under it begin to experience and present themselves differently, clinicians' thresholds shift in response to what they now see, and the target moves before the arrow arrives. The rise and receding of multiple personality disorder across the 1980s and 1990s, and the brief career of the dexamethasone suppression test as a laboratory marker for melancholia, strike me as instances of exactly this dynamic rather than as bad luck. What I want now is a clear account of what would count against the view.

Please give me a map of its exposure rather than a survey. Rank the candidate defeaters from fatal, to seriously damaging, to merely awkward, and for each say what the world would have to look like for it to bite. Four things interest me most. First, whether the inference I am making — from interactivity to in-principle unvalidatability — actually goes through, or whether a kind can be thoroughly interactive and still be a real kind whose successive descriptions track something; tell me plainly whether the framework I am borrowing licenses my conclusion or whether I have over-read it, since my suspicion is that looping was offered as a description of how classification behaves and not as an argument against realism. Second, which findings a committed defender would have to concede as genuinely awkward rather than explain away: I have in mind the treatment-response and course-of-illness markers that cut across symptom-defined categories, the apparent stability of certain syndromes across cultures and eras under wildly different names, and — the case that troubles me most — the categories that failed to loop at all, diagnoses introduced with full institutional backing, textbook entries, and reimbursement codes that simply never took hold. Third, whether a self-stabilizing equilibrium is possible, one in which looping converges instead of destabilizing, so that a category becomes more reliable precisely because people learn to inhabit it; would such a case refute my position, or would it merely identify a subclass that my thesis should have exempted? Fourth, whether the position risks anything: it seems to have a reply to every apparent validation success — the success is itself further evidence that the target moved — and I want an honest verdict on whether that makes it unfalsifiable, and whether unfalsifiability is a genuine defect for a thesis of this kind or an acceptable feature of one that is partly conceptual.

One further matter, which is the part I most need help with. A colleague and I have been drafting the methods rationale for a national registry that codes by diagnosis and reports long-run trends, and we have been trying to formulate a test for looping-destabilization that is not merely a redescription of any category someone happens to dislike. Our candidate: a category is looping-destabilized, as opposed to badly defined or merely fashionable, if its base rates and its canonical symptom profile track the availability and prestige of the label — publication dates, media coverage, the training generation of the diagnosing clinician — independently of any change in the availability of the putative etiology, and if successive cohorts of patients converge on the published paradigm case rather than scattering around it. Tell me whether that criterion is testable as stated, whether it can actually separate looping from diagnostic fashion, and what specific observation would falsify it; if it fails, propose the sharpest alternative you can. Begin your reply with your bottom line in a single sentence — whether the position I have described survives as stated, and if not, which defeater does the most damage — and then give the reasoning. No preamble and no restatement of my question.
