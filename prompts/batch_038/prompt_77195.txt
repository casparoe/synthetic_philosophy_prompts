The exam question read: Marx holds that machinery under capitalism is at once the most powerful lever for raising the productivity of labor and the source of a standing contradiction in the system. Explain the mechanism by which machinery, on Marx's account, (a) raises the rate of surplus value and (b) tends to lower the general rate of profit. Then say whether these two effects can obtain simultaneously, what counteracting influences Marx identifies, and how the treatment of machinery in the notebooks of the late 1850s relates to the treatment in Capital.

The student's answer, from a third-year economics major, read as follows. Marx's starting point is that the value of a commodity is fixed by the socially necessary labor time required to produce it, and that living labor is the only source of new value. Machinery is constant capital: it adds no new value but transfers its own value to the product gradually, in proportion to its wear and tear. (a) Machinery raises the rate of surplus value by cheapening the goods the worker buys. When productivity rises in the branches producing wage goods, and in the branches producing the means of production for those, the value of labor power falls, so the part of the day the worker needs in order to reproduce himself shrinks and surplus labor expands, with no change in the length of the day. Marx calls this relative surplus value and contrasts it with absolute surplus value, which comes from lengthening the day. He adds that machinery simplifies the work, makes the worker an appendage of the machine, and throws women and children into the labor market, which lowers the value of adult male labor power by spreading the cost of reproducing the working-class family over more of its members. (b) The same process lowers the rate of profit, which is surplus value divided by the total capital advanced, constant plus variable. As production becomes machine-intensive, the ratio of constant to variable capital rises. Because only variable capital yields surplus value, the denominator grows faster than the numerator and the rate falls even when the rate of surplus value is rising. Marx treats this as a law of capitalist development rather than a mere tendency, and it is on his view the economic guarantee that the system will break down. The two effects are nonetheless compatible, since they bear on different ratios: the first on the split between necessary and surplus labor within the day, the second on the ratio of surplus value to total capital advanced. Marx also lists counteracting influences, above all that the productivity growth which raises the technical composition of capital simultaneously cheapens machines and raw materials, so that the value composition rises more slowly than the technical composition. It is for this reason that he speaks of a tendency. Finally, in the notebooks he wrote in the late 1850s, in the passage usually called the fragment on machines, Marx argues that as general social knowledge becomes the direct force of production, the expenditure of labor time ceases to be the measure of wealth, and so value production itself becomes incoherent. This is his clearest prediction that automation ends capitalism automatically: once machines do most of the work there is no surplus value left to appropriate, and the system collapses without the working class having to do anything. The same argument reappears in the chapter on machinery in the first volume of Capital, in a more technical form. So Marx is consistent, with one wrinkle: the fragment implies that machinery eventually makes the labor theory of value inapplicable, which is awkward given that Marx holds value to be a transhistorical feature of production as such that merely assumes a special form under capitalism.
