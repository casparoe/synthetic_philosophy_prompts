And here's their answer, basically verbatim:

> (a) The difference is that in (ii) B isn't arguing about whether the cruelty claim is true. B can accept that he was cruel and that A's sentence accurately describes what happened, and still have something left over to object to. So what's doing the work in (ii) is A's position to make the charge at all — call it A's standing. In (i), blame is treated as a piece of evidence: taken as an argument, B's reply would be the tu quoque fallacy, since A's own behaviour tells B nothing about whether B behaved cruelly. Because it's a fallacy, B's reply can't be making a point about the truth of the judgement, so it must be making a procedural point instead — the same kind of point you'd make if someone talked over you or answered their own question in a meeting. It's a breach of how the exchange is supposed to be conducted rather than a moral fault in A. And it's not only A's right to make the charge that's in question: B is entitled to treat the charge itself as carrying less weight than the identical charge would from someone with clean hands.
>
> (b) What A has done wrong, roughly, is hold B to a standard A isn't holding themselves to. A is applying a rule selectively, and the selection is self-favouring. That's a failure of consistency in A's own practical outlook, so I'd say the primary failing isn't specifically a wrong to B — A would be doing something objectionable even if the hypocrisy were pointed out by a third party instead of by B. But B has the more distinctive complaint, because B is the one being addressed, and it's reasonable for B to say "then you don't get to say this to me." So it's unfair in a general sense, but it lands on B in particular.
>
> (c) A gets standing back by acknowledging the earlier episode as the same fault — admitting "yes, I did that, and it was wrong of me too" — and by changing their behaviour going forward. That removes the objection instead of just excusing A because A is now applying one standard to both of them, so there's no asymmetry left for B to point at. A isn't being let off the hook, A is being brought back into line with the standard A was invoking in the first place.
>
> (d) Yes, I think that follows. If standing is lost by doing X, then only people who haven't done X can blame others for it, since once you've forfeited standing it's gone unless you become someone who no longer does X. That's a demanding view, but it seems to be what (c) commits you to.
