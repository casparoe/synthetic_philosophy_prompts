My brother-in-law Marcus is a structural engineer and my sister Dana's a PhD student in evo bio, and they got into it at Thanksgiving in a way I couldn't follow and also couldn't referee. It was about Gould and Lewontin's '79 spandrels paper and whether the spandrel idea still deserves any weight. Here's roughly how it went, best I can reconstruct:

1. Dana: the paper's real point is about default posture. Adaptationists hear about a trait and immediately ask what it's for, skipping the possibility that it's a byproduct of something else that was actually selected. That posture correction was right then and is right now.
2. Marcus: the paper's showcase is architecturally wrong. Those triangular surfaces they describe at San Marco are pendentives, not spandrels, and pendentives were a chosen solution among options — corbells, squinches — picked partly because they look good. So the one example meant to illustrate a form imposed from below, necessarily, is actually somebody's design decision. Bad foundation for the whole thing.
3. Dana: the label slipped, fine, and Gould himself later said he meant the term loosely, for leftover space generally. The concept doesn't lean on the building anyway: male nipples, the human chin, the redness of blood. You don't need Venice to know byproducts exist.
4. Marcus: those are the trivial cases. Where the idea actually gets deployed — big brains, language, music — you can never separate the byproduct from the selected feature, so the interesting uses are exactly the untestable ones.
5. Then Marcus proposed what he called a spandrel audit: a trait only counts if you can name the selected feature it follows from, and show the same byproduct turning up in unrelated lineages that evolved that feature under a similar geometric or scaling constraint. Dana said that standard is rigged, because the second a byproduct gets co-opted, selection starts reshaping it, so it won't look the same across lineages; and demanding a derivation from a law is physics envy. Net effect: nothing qualifies and adaptationism wins by forfeit.
6. Marcus said if a category can't be tested it's a mood, not a hypothesis. Dana said the paper was never a test for single traits, it was a burden-of-proof claim: rule out ancestral holdover, allometry, drift and constraint before you announce a function. Marcus said flipping the burden is cheap, and a program whose only output is "you haven't shown it's an adaptation" hasn't produced anything positive in 45 years.
7. Dana's last move: the pendentive reply assumes someone choosing among designs. Organisms don't choose, there's no architect, so the move that dissolves the analogy in the building doesn't transfer to the biology.

They both looked at me and I had nothing. Can you go through it move by move and say who's right at each numbered step — right, partly right, or wrong, with a sentence of justification — and then who's more right overall? Also tell me what neither of them brought up that would actually reshape the argument.
