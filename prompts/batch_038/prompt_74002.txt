I've read the Damascus case retold on rationalist blogs for a decade — the traveler who meets Death, hears an appointment announced, and flees to the appointed city — and it almost always gets flattened into a two-way fight between evidential and causal decision theory. I'm building a diagnostic for a meetup that sorts attendees by what they would actually do, not by what they call themselves. Please write it as a decision tree of roughly seven nodes, each a variant of the case rather than a question about theory: for instance, Death as a merely fallible predictor with a stated hit rate; the announcement reaching the traveler only after the destination is booked; the destination fixed by a coin the traveler flips while forming an intention; two travelers who learn of the same appointment and must decide separately; a traveler with access to a precommitment device that binds him before he hears anything. Every node needs two or three options answerable from inside the case, and none may use vocabulary — news value, act-conditioned evaluation, ratifiability — that gives the position away.

Then give me the scoring key. The leaves must include evidential decision theory, causal decision theory, a Jeffrey-style treatment with no choice operator, updateless evaluation from the point before the announcement, some repair-by-ratifiability position, and a deflationary leaf on which the case simply admits no determinate answer. Build the space first from two axes — whether an act may be evaluated under the hypothesis that the agent performs it, and what one should do when no act survives revision — and locate the positions inside it, rather than starting from the names. Say which combinations of answers go unoccupied and why (incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored), and flag any pair of leaves the tree cannot separate, along with the case that would separate them. Please write in American English.
