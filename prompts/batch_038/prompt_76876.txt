I am drafting a novel whose moral engine is a refusal to lie, and before I write another page of casuistry I want the specification rather than the arguments. The situation the book turns on is this: a man bound by an oath is questioned in his own house by an officer who is entirely reasonable, entirely informed about the oath, and looking for a fugitive in the cellar; the officer knows the man will not assert a falsehood and has planned his questions accordingly; the man has at his disposal silence, evasion, a literally true sentence engineered to send the officer up the wrong road, and a promise he does not intend to keep. Later, the same man writes a book that is not true and expects strangers to read it for pleasure.

What I want is the list of requirements an account of lying must meet in order to count as adequate for that plot — a theory good enough both to generate the verdicts my scenes need and to be broken by them. Give me the desiderata, with the case or consideration standing behind each. At a minimum the list must cover the following.

The definitional core. Which elements are individually necessary — falsity of what is said, the speaker's belief about it, the intention that the hearer come to believe it, the addressing of an utterance to a hearer, the employment of a conventional sign — and what the theory must say when they come apart. It has to deliver a verdict on the man who says what is false while believing it true; on the man who says what is true while believing it false and meaning to mislead; on the true sentence deployed to produce a false belief; on the falsehood uttered to one person but overheard by another; on the hearer deceived not by what was said but by what it implicated; and on the promise made without any intention of performing it.

The distinctions it may not collapse. Lying against concealment; lying against misleading; lying against deception in general; the lie as an act against the harm the lie does; the permission to withhold against any commendation of the withholding; and the gravity of a lie against its wrongness at all — the theory must explain how lies can be ranked by seriousness while the prohibition on them is held to admit no exceptions, if indeed it is so held.

The test cases it must get right, each with the intuition that makes it a case. The officer at the door. The fugitive's relatives questioned separately and severally. The joke, the irony, the parable, the metaphor, the exaggeration, the invented story. The lie told to spare a feeling. The lie told to a persecutor by a man who feigns the persecutor's own creed in order to expose him from within. And the scriptural cases in which a lie appears to be rewarded — the midwives who misreport to Pharaoh about how quickly the Hebrew women give birth, and the woman who hides the spies under flax stalks on her roof — where the theory must either explain why the reward attaches to something other than the lie or confess that it cannot.

The structural requirements. It must be action-guiding under duress and under time pressure, by someone who cannot survey the consequences. It must be publicly avowable, which is to say it must survive being known to an adversary who plans around it, since my officer does precisely that. It must be operable by an agent who is uncertain what the other party already believes. It must admit degrees, because a novel lives on the distance between the trivial lie and the monstrous one. And it must declare whether it is confessional, deriving the prohibition from the purpose for which the power of signification was instituted, or secular, deriving it from the erosion of trust and the injury the liar inflicts on himself; a theory that slides between the two mid-argument is disqualified.

A requirement on its treatment of my own trade. The account must classify invented discourse without either convicting every novelist of habitual lying or exempting fiction by a stipulation broad enough to exempt the confidence trickster as well. It must explain how a sign can convey a maker's mind while its content is fabricated, and why an audience's willingness to be immersed is not a case of being deceived.

Then mark which of these requirements are non-negotiable and which belong to one camp rather than to the subject matter: my suspicion is that the demand for an exceptionless prohibition and the demand that no rule oblige its followers to be complicit in atrocity are rival commitments rather than common ground. Tell me which subset, if any, is jointly unsatisfiable. My own hunch is that an intention-based definition, a permission to conceal without asserting, and an absolute prohibition cannot all be held at once, because concealment by means of a true sentence is exactly what an officer cannot detect and exactly what a novelist does for a living; if the list is itself a result in that way, say so plainly, and say which requirement must go. Tell me also which single requirement eliminates the largest number of candidate theories.

Only then, and briefly — a page at most, since the criteria are what I am after — how Augustine's own account fares against the list, and how it stands on the requirements that do the most work beside a Kantian prohibition, a rule permitting falsehood only towards those who have forfeited any claim to the truth, and an unembarrassed consequentialist treatment.
