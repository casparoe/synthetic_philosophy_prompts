I want to work through a real episode from the philosophy-of-medicine side of things — the fight over the bereavement exclusion in the DSM — and I want it run through the naturalism vs. normativism debate, not through the clinical debate.

Facts as I have them, use these and dont hedge about them: DSM-IV would not let you diagnose a major depressive episode if the symptoms were better accounted for by bereavement, meaning they had come on within about two months of the death of someone close, unless there were extra severe features on top — suicidal ideation, psychotic symptoms, marked functional impairment, morbid preoccupation with worthlessness, psychomotor retardation. DSM-5 deleted the exclusion in 2013. The case for deleting it, Kendler most prominently: bereavement is just one severe stressor among many and we dont exclude for the others, like losing your job or your savings; people who get depressed after a death look the same on the things supposed to validate a diagnosis — symptom profile, family history, course, response to treatment; and the exclusion kept some genuinely sick people away from care. Against it: Wakefield and First, plus Frances who had run DSM-IV, arguing that a symptom checklist with no context clause converts ordinary sadness into disorder, with Wakefield's own line being that grief is an evolved response doing what it's supposed to do after a loss, so there's no dysfunction no matter how much harm it causes.

Three positions I want the episode pushed through. Boorse's biostatistical theory: value-free, function fixed by contribution to survival and reproduction, reference classes by age and sex, pathology as statistically subnormal function relative to the reference class in a typical environment, plus his disease/illness split. Wakefield's harmful dysfunction: a naturalist half about evolved mechanisms failing plus an avowedly evaluative half about harm. And a straight normativist, Nordenfelt-style, where health is the ability to reach a person's vital goals in standard circumstances. For each of the three, tell me what verdict it delivers on (a) someone two weeks after their spouse's death who meets the full symptom checklist and can't get out of bed, and (b) someone with an identical symptom picture eight months out. Be blunt about where a theory runs out of machinery and can only shrug, and about which parts of the actual fight each theory cannot even register — for instance whether the denying-people-treatment argument is addressed at the level Boorse thinks disease lives at, or only at the level he thinks is normative and therefore not his problem.

Then the part I actually care about, which is a distinction I made up for myself so you won't find it stated this way anywhere. The exclusion can be doing one of two seperate jobs. Evidential: recent bereavement is a cheap, defeasible cue that the sadness is probably normal, and the severe-features list exists to override the cue when it misfires. Constitutive: being in the context of a loss is part of what makes the state not a disorder at all, so context isn't evidence about dysfunction, it's a component of the concept. Sort the arguments on both sides by which of those two jobs they were quietly assigning to context — I'm fairly sure some of them switched jobs mid-argument and I want to know who and where. From there: was anyone entitled to both readings at once, or does the evidential reading collapse into the constitutive one once you bolt on the severe-features list? Did deleting the exclusion change the facts about who is disordered, change only our evidence about it, or do neither? And does the episode land as a confirmation, an anomaly, or a case the theory never anticipated — separately for each of the three, because I suspect they come out differently and I don't want that smoothed over.

One smaller thing to fold in: Boorse's reference classes are age and sex. Ask whether recently bereaved could be a reference class, what would have to be true for it to be a legitimate one rather than an ad hoc rescue, and whether admitting it saves the exclusion or wrecks the claim that the whole account is value-free.
