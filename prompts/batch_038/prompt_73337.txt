I was at a departmental seminar last week where a colleague, arguing against what he called the circumstantial account of the Terror, closed his remarks with the old line — traditionally put in the mouth of Vergniaud on his way to the scaffold, though I gather the attribution is itself shaky — that revolutions are like Saturn and devour their own children. He was using it as more than decoration. The point was that the killing of the Girondins, then of Hébert, then of Danton, and finally of Robespierre was not an unfortunate detour forced on the Revolution by invasion, civil war in the Vendée, assignat collapse, and the emergency legislation of the Year II, but the working out of premises already present in 1789: that a revolution founded on a unitary general will, on virtue as a qualification for citizenship, and on the identification of dissent with conspiracy has no way to stop at its own borders, and will consume whoever is nearest once the supply of external enemies runs short. On his telling the metaphor does real evidential work, and the people stressing contingency are left explaining away what the image makes vivid.

I would like an audit of the analogy rather than a defense or a dismissal of it. Set out, in running prose, what maps onto what: which element of the Saturn story stands for the Revolution, which for the revolutionaries, which for the prophecy the god is trying to outrun, which for the guillotine itself. Then separate the disanalogies that bear on my colleague's use of it from incidental features of the myth that do no argumentative work. I suspect at least three matter and I want to know whether you agree: that Saturn eats out of fear for his own survival and to preempt a prediction, which quietly supplies the Revolution with a unitary psychology, a belief, and an appetite that no committee, tribunal, or faction ever had; that he eats his children at birth, whereas the Terror arrived four years after the founding and only once the revolutionaries had already won; and that in the myth the devouring fails, since one child is swapped for a stone, hidden, returned, forced his siblings back up, and deposed the devourer — so the image, pressed consistently, promises a surviving heir and a restoration, which is not at all what my colleague wants it to say. I am also curious whether the identity of the devoured holds up: were the bulk of those killed in the Year II really children of the Revolution in the sense the metaphor requires, or does the image survive by keeping the camera on a handful of famous deputies?

Then tell me whether the conclusion he licenses survives once the disanalogies are on the table — in particular, whether the analogy can discriminate at all between an intrinsic radicalization driven by ideology and one driven by war and scarcity, or whether it only looks like a restatement of the first dressed as evidence. If it fails, say whether a better figure exists for the same phenomenon. I have a candidate of my own: something like an escalatory auction, in which each faction must outbid the last in accusations of insufficient virtue, so the price of safety keeps climbing until the bidders are themselves priced out and the auction is closed by Thermidor. Tell me whether that is genuinely better or only differently flattering, and what the Saturn image still carries that the auction does not — including the double valence of the same god as both devourer and ruler of a golden age.

Please write this as continuous prose, the way you would write a long letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, and no bibliography or list of sources for me to chase, since I have no way of looking any up. Finish by telling me what would change your verdict — which consideration, piece of evidence, or reading of the sequence from 1789 to Prairial would make you say the analogy does carry my colleague's conclusion after all — and where you think your own audit is most likely to be wrong, including anywhere you have assumed one side of the historiographical dispute rather than argued for it.
