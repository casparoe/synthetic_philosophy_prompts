I'm teaching a seminar session on "gains from trade" arguments for moral compromise: the idea that when two parties with different value systems weight the issues differently, each can come out ahead by swapping concessions, and that this is why compromise between rival moral outlooks is often genuinely good rather than a mere modus vivendi. My students already buy this wholesale. I want a counterexample that isn't cheap.

So: construct a thought experiment in which two parties, holding genuinely different value systems (not just different intensities of the same values), strike a trade voluntarily and with full information that is a real gain on each traded issue by each side's own measure — and yet leaves both of them worse off, all things considered, than if no deal had been made.

Constraints: realistic setting (coalition government, hospital board, a family, an NGO alliance — no sci-fi, no perfect predictors), and the scenario itself one tight paragraph, ~150 words. The badness may NOT come from harm to bystanders, from coercion or deception, from trading something on the usual inalienables list (votes, organs, children, sacred objects), or from one side's values simply being false. It has to fall out of the structure of the trade itself. What the case is for: showing that gains-from-trade reasoning quietly assumes a premise it never states. So if your scenario only works because somebody is ignorant or evil, its a fail.

Then show me the design works. Walk the trade issue by issue and say why each side counts itself ahead; name the structural feature doing the work; delete that feature and confirm the case collapses back into an unobjectionable mutual gain; and finish with the strongest reply from someone who thinks moral trade is always available here, plus whether you think it lands.
