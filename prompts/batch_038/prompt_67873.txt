I teach a grad seminar on medieval theories of causation and I've got a student coming to office hours tomorrow who is bright, allergic to theology, and has read just enough Avicenna to be dangerous. I want to rehearse the exchange before he walks in. So: play al-Ghazālī — the Ash'arite you, the one who wrote the refutation of the philosophers — defending your account of causation in your own voice, first person, in character the whole way until I say "cut". Ground rules: you may use only the conceptual resources of an eleventh-century Ash'arite theologian (God's power, knowledge, and habit, the soul, what we observe and what we fail to observe) plus whatever I tell you about the case I put to you; you can ask me clarifying questions in character if you need something pinned down; no modern names, no modern science, nothing you'd have had to read in a book published after you died; and don't cite your own works by chapter and discussion number, because I don't want you bluffing at references. Keep each reply to two or three short paragraphs — I want the rhythm of a live argument, not a treatise. You can concede small points, you're subtle enough to grant that the philosophers describe the order of the world more elegantly than you in places, but you never give up the central claim that there is no necessary connection between what we call a cause and what we call its effect, only a habitual sequence that God creates on each occassion. And please don't just retreat into mystery: I want an actual account.

I'll play the student. Here's my opening. Your fire-and-cotton case, as I understand it, is that we never witness anything except the fire touching the cotton and the cotton blackening and crumbling apart; we don't witness any necessity, and a man who had never seen the two together wouldn't know what was coming. Fine. But suppose I hand you a sequence that isn't exceptionless at all. There's a fever going through the city, and of every hundred people who catch it, seventy die within the week and thirty recover, and that proportion has held in every city, in every season, for as long as anyone has kept count. No one's nature explains it — the physicians can't pick out in advance which patients are in the seventy and which are in the thirty, and nothing about the individual case makes the difference. Now: if God creates the outcome at each occasion, what fixes the proportion at seventy? If you say it's God's habit, then "habit" is doing all the work, and it looks to me like you've just relabelled the regularity — you've swapped "the fever kills seventy in a hundred" for "God is in the habit of killing seventy in a hundred," and the second sentence says nothing the first didn't already say. Worse, if the proportion is fixed by nothing but an inscrutable will, you loose the one thing the philosophers were buying with their natures, which was a ground for inference from the seen to the unseen. So: what does your account explain that mine doesn't?
