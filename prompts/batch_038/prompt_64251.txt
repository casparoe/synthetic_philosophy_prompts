I am drafting a précis of a chapter on deflationary theories of truth from a recently published collection of essays, and the authors open with an executive summary of the view. Before I build on it, I would like to know how much of it I can safely carry over. The summary reads as follows:

"Deflationism about truth starts from an observation first pressed by Ramsey and later taken up by Quine: to call a sentence true is only to repeat it, so that asserting the truth of the claim that snow is white commits one to nothing beyond asserting that snow is white. On this view truth is not a property of sentences or of propositions at all, but a piece of logical machinery whose one useful job is to let us endorse claims we cannot list — as when we say that everything the witness testified to was true, or that a theory's consequences are true without stating them one by one. Since truth has no underlying nature, the deflationist is not merely failing to analyze it in naturalistic terms; there is nothing there to analyze, and so a theory of truth has nothing to consist in. This also disposes of the Liar. Because the predicate carries no content beyond the removal of quotation marks, a sentence which says of itself that it is not true fails to express any proposition; the equivalences therefore never license the contradictory conclusion, and the paradox never gets going. Deflationism thus earns its keep twice over: it tells us why truth resists analysis, and it tells us why truth can play no substantive explanatory role in an account of meaning, of belief, or of successful action."

Please work through this claim by claim and sort it into three piles: what a deflationist would recognize as their own view, what is a simplification that distorts the position it purports to describe, and what a careful minimalist would flatly deny. Two suspicions of my own I cannot yet articulate properly: I had the impression that some of the most prominent deflationists do speak of truth as a property and regard themselves as having a theory of truth rather than no theory, and that the family of deflationary views is more heterogeneous than this passage suggests; and it seems strange to me that a semantic paradox should simply evaporate as a corollary of a thesis about the function of a predicate, since I gather the equivalences themselves are what generate the trouble and that deflationists have had to add further machinery. Tell me whether these suspicions are correct, and if so, what the accurate statement of the points the passage is gesturing at would be.

Finally, does the last sentence survive correction? That is, is the claim that truth plays no substantive explanatory role a consequence of deflationism, an optional extra that some deflationists adopt, or a thesis that must be argued for against specific objections — and if the passage's treatment of the Liar is wrong, does anything else in it fall with it? I am interested in the conceptual map rather than the literature, so please do not include references or suggest further reading.
