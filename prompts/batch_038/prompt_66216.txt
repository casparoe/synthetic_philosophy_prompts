Six months ago I left a clinical social work post to take up a lay seat on a statutory professional conduct panel — the body that hears appeals from practitioners refused registration or sanctioned by their registrar. I had more or less settled my moral views over a decade of case conferences, and the job is now pressing on them in ways I did not anticipate. A fortnight ago I drafted my first decision. The legally qualified chair returned it with the observation that I had explained at length why I found the appellant's account unpersuasive, but had not stated a ground on which another panel, facing comparable facts, could have reached the same result. I could not immediately tell whether she had located a defect in my draft or in my philosophy, and I would rather not spend three years on the panel finding out the hard way. What follows is the package I want examined before I write anything further.

1. Reasons are holistic in the way particularists describe. A consideration that counts in favour of a course of action in one situation may count for nothing, or count against it, in another. I do not believe there are exceptionless moral principles of any non-trivial kind, and I do not think sound deliberation consists in bringing a case under one.

2. I nonetheless take the supervenience of the moral on the non-moral seriously, as a discipline rather than a platitude: if I judge two situations differently, I owe some non-moral difference between them. I am not prepared to let holism degrade into a shrug about how a case struck me.

3. I think the reliable judge's relation to a case is closer to perception than to inference. Someone can see what a situation calls for, and be right about it, without being able to set that seeing out as an argument. Full articulacy is not a condition of correctness.

4. I think moral explanations are genuine — that course of action was wrong because it betrayed a particular confidence, and so on — but I deny that such an explanation has to be underwritten by any general principle connecting that feature with wrongness wherever it occurs.

5. I believe there is such a thing as moral expertise, and that when I am out of my depth, deferring to someone who has it is reasonable and can leave me with something better than a lucky guess. I have done this for years, with good results, and do not propose to stop.

6. In this role I hold that fairness requires like cases to be treated alike, and that every written decision must state the considerations that decided it in a form capable of being applied to other cases. I regard this as a condition of the decision's legitimacy rather than a courtesy to the reader: a practitioner's registration and usually their livelihood are at stake, and they are entitled to know the ground on which they lost, on pain of the appeal being empty.

7. When I have a stake in the outcome, or find myself unusually moved by one side, I do not trust my own reading of the case; I apply the panel's codified standard as written and treat it as decisive rather than attempting to see the case afresh.

One thing that makes 6 feel urgent rather than theoretical. On a case heard the week before mine, a colleague I regard as at least my equal in care and information reached the opposite result on facts I have so far been unable to distinguish from mine. I do not think she was careless, or biased, or confused. Nor do I think it follows that there was no fact of the matter about which of us was right.

Two objections have already been put to me, and I want to know whether either is on target, because I do not want to spend my limited revision budget on the wrong commitment. A philosopher friend insists that 1 and 2 cannot both be held, on the grounds that a supervenience thesis with any real content already commits one to principles. My own suspicion falls elsewhere, on 3 and 5: if a competent judge's insight is essentially inarticulable, I am not clear what I am receiving when I defer, or how I could tell the expert from the merely confident.

Please treat this as an audit and not as an introduction to the debate; I know the general shape of the literature. Tell me which pairs of my commitments are in genuine conflict, ordered by severity rather than listed indiscriminately, and for each what would have to be surrendered or restricted and what that would cost — including what it would cost me specifically as someone who now has to sign appealable decisions under a duty to give reasons. Tell me separately which of the apparent conflicts dissolve, and name the move that dissolves them, saying explicitly whether my friend's objection and my own suspicion belong in that category. Where a genuine tension can be met by narrowing a commitment instead of abandoning it, say how far the narrowing can go and what the narrowed version can no longer do for me. Then identify the single revision that would clean up the most, and state what I would no longer be able to say once I had made it. If your conclusion is that the package is consistent as it stands, say so plainly and show me how each pair that looks conflicted is in fact to be reconciled.
