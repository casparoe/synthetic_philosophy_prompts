**Depth Without Edges: Conceptual Slippage in a Closed Microdomain**

1. The Copycat project models analogy-making as a form of high-level perception over a deliberately impoverished domain. The canonical problem is the letter-string analogy abc : abd :: ijk : ?, and the canonical human answer is ijl, which carries over the rule "replace the rightmost letter by its successor." A substantial minority of people instead answer ijz. On the standard account, that answer arises from a conceptual slippage: the description "rightmost letter of the string" slips along the slipnet to "last letter of the alphabet," and once the slip has occurred the rule cannot be executed, because z has no successor inside the domain. The answer is compelling because z is a deep concept.

2. My claim is that conceptual depth is here doing two jobs that the letters microdomain cannot separate. First, depth marks a landmark: z is special because it sits at an edge of the alphabet. Second, depth marks a place where an imported rule breaks down: z is special because nothing follows it. In an open space with hard boundaries those two roles coincide exactly, since the landmarks just are the points at which successorhood fails. The letters domain therefore cannot tell us which of the two is doing the explanatory work in an answer like ijz.

3. A closed microdomain separates them. Take months of the year, with the parallel problem Jan Feb Mar : Feb Mar Apr, whose rule is again "replace the rightmost element by its successor." Now pose Sep Oct Nov : ?. The canonical answer is Sep Oct Dec. But notice what has happened to the slippage: substituting the year's last month for the string's rightmost month yields Sep Oct Dec, which is the canonical answer. In a domain that wraps, the deep-concept slip is silently absorbed; it generates no divergent answer at all, and so it leaves no trace in an answer distribution.

4. And if one insists on carrying the rule through after the slip — taking the successor of December, which exists — one gets Sep Oct Jan, a composite in which the slippage and the rule cooperate rather than compete. Nothing of this shape is available in the letters domain, because there the slippage terminates the derivation. Closure thus has two consequences: it hides the slippage when the target string ends adjacent to the landmark, and it converts the slippage into a rule-composing operation when it does not.

5. From this I draw the conclusion the paper is named for. Depth is not a static property attached to a node in the slipnet; it is a property of a node relative to the topology of the space it ranges over. Where there are no terminal objects, nothing can be deep in the second, rule-breaking sense — and yet December remains a landmark month that people will surely slip toward. If the prediction in section 6 holds, then only the rule-breaking sense of depth was ever doing work in the derivation of answers, and the landmark sense is an artifact of the alphabet happening to be open.

6. The prediction is this. Prime cyclicity first — give subjects a wrapping example such as Nov Dec Jan : Dec Jan Feb before the target — and then collect answers to Sep Oct Nov : ?. If depth-as-landmark is the operative notion, answers containing Dec should be elevated relative to an unprimed control. If depth-as-rule-breaker is operative, priming should make no difference to that frequency, and the only novel signature should be low-frequency composites such as Sep Oct Jan, or Oct Nov Dec (the latter from rebuilding the string backward around the landmark). The letters microdomain cannot support this comparison, because in it the two notions never come apart.

7. I know the obvious objection. Months are rarely represented cyclically: calendars are grids, the year has a beginning and an end in school and tax life, and subjects may quietly treat January as a boundary anyway, in which case my closed domain is not closed for them. My response is that this concedes the thesis rather than defeating it, since the topology that governs slippage would then be the one the solver imposes on the domain rather than the one the domain has. I can see, though, that this response threatens to make the account unfalsifiable, and I have not found a way to state it so that it does not.
