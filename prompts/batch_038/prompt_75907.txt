I am a fourth-year doctoral student working on the ethics of punishment, and I am applying for a one-semester internal fellowship that would let me turn a chapter sketch into a journal-length article. The selection committee includes two non-specialists, so the proposal has to be intelligible without any background in the theology of atonement, and it has to look like a plan for work rather than a survey of a debate. I would like you to draft the proposal itself — roughly 1,100 words — out of the following half-formed idea.

My starting point is David Lewis's short paper on penal substitution, the one in which he argues that the doctrine as standardly preached is incoherent, because punishment in the strict sense is a harm imposed on the offender by an authority on account of the offense, and that is just the sort of thing no third party can take over; but he also argues that a restricted version survives. As I read him, the rescue turns on two moves. First, a contrast between kinds of penalty: some sanctions are dischargeable by another person — a fine, where a relative writes the check and the sanction has been satisfied — while others are not, since nobody can serve your custodial term in your place. Second, an involvement condition: substitution amounts to scapegoating rather than to a genuine bearing of another's burden unless the substitute is closely bound up with the offender (family, a friend, a member of the same body), acts from something like love rather than for a fee, and the offender repents and accepts the substitution instead of being indifferent to it. What Lewis never supplies, so far as I can see, is a general criterion; he works case by case, and the reader is left to infer what makes a burden transferable and what gives a particular person standing to bear it.

The project is to extract that criterion and press it in three directions Lewis does not go. (i) Secular law already runs a large informal market in substituted burdens: commercial bail sureties discharge appearance obligations for a fee; indigent defendants are jailed for unpaid fines that charities, family members, or crowdfunding campaigns then pay; restitution orders against minors are satisfied by parents; corporate criminal fines are absorbed by shareholders, workers, and customers rather than by the executives who acted. Some of these involve payment without involvement, and some involve involvement without payment, so they should pull Lewis's two conditions apart and show which one is load-bearing. (ii) The distributive consequence: if transferability is the exception that rescues the doctrine, then whether a penalty is in practice transferable depends on whether the offender has money and has people willing to stand with them, which makes the severity of what is actually suffered a function of social embeddedness. I want to know whether that is a bug in the criterion or a truth about punishment that the criterion should be built to capture. (iii) The mirror case: Lewis analyzes the transfer of penalties only, but theology and ordinary life also traffic in transferred credit — imputed righteousness on one side, and on the other the ghostwritten chapter, the laboratory head who is credited with a student's result, the co-author who contributed nothing. My hunch is that we resist transferred praise far more stubbornly than Lewis's fungibility story predicts, which would suggest the involvement condition is not tracking what punishment is but what it expresses.

So the central question I want the proposal to state precisely is whether the involvement condition is a claim about the nature of punishment (something metaphysical about who can be the bearer of a sanction) or a claim about its expressive function (something about what a community says when it accepts a substitute), and how one would tell the difference from the pattern of cases.
