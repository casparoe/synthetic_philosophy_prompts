Third year in a philosophy-of-AI program here, writing the chapter where I argue that iterated amplification and factored cognition idealize deliberation in two *different* ways, and I need a case that actually shows them coming apart. Not a survey of the general worry — a designed scenario built to spec.

The way I'm using the terms (flag it if I'm mangling them): factored cognition is a claim about a task — the task is tractable iff you can write it out as a DAG whose leaves are things a weak reasoner can do and whose internal nodes are compositions a weak reasoner can also do. HCH is a claim about a protocol — what the human would conclude if she could recursively consult copies of a weaker assistant without limit, each assistant also able to consult her.

What I want: one task, one competence bound, opposite verdicts. Factored cognition says no — there is no decomposition of this task into weak-reasoner-sized pieces, and not merely because nobody has looked hard enough. HCH says yes — the human consulting her assistants converges on the right answer.

Constraints:

- The weak reasoner has to be a concrete thing with a concrete bound, not an abstraction I can quietly weaken mid-argument. Give me the bound in checkable terms (what it can hold in view at once, what it's been trained on, what it reliably gets wrong). Then show the case still works when I turn the dial up a notch or two, right up to but not past the human's integrative capacity. If it only works at one exact setting of the dial, it's stipulated rather than generated and I don't want it.
- Realistic and mundane. A lab, an editorial office, a clinic, a firm — your pick. No superintelligence, no exotic hardware, no uploading.
- The human is not a genius and does nothing mysterious at the crucial step. Her advantage over any DAG has to be explained by the structure of the protocol, not by her being cleverer. She's diligent, keeps perfect notes, and always checks whatever is checkable.
- The decomposition failure has to be *located*. Point at the specific node that can't be built, specify its inputs and outputs, and argue that no re-slicing of it gets it under the bound. Appeals to judgment or holistic understanding don't count unless you cash them out in terms of what that node would have to take in and hand back.
- One paragraph for the scenario itself, everything else after.

Then show the work:

1. The scenario.
2. Why HCH gets it right — the consultation tree, roughly: what she asks at the top, what comes back, what she asks next, and where the answer gets pinned down.
3. Why factored cognition fails — the unbuildable node, with its inputs and outputs, plus the argument that no slicing fixes it.
4. Name the structural feature doing the work, and say what happens to the case if I remove that feature. I have a guess (something about the human being the only node that ever sees arbitrarily many sub-results at once) but I want yours, and I want to know if mine is wrong.
5. Best counterattack. Someone who thinks factored cognition handles this will say my unbuildable node is just a bundle of buildable ones and that I've confused a big task with an indecomposable one. Steelman that, then tell me whether the case survives it.

So you know what counts as cheating: the point of the case is to support the claim that consultation strictly dominates decomposition — that having a human at the root of every subtree does work no static DAG of weak nodes can replicate — and that this is the real content of saying HCH idealizes deliberation. A case that gets there by defining the weak reasoner to be just barely too weak for the crucial step and no too weak for anything else is worthless to me.

And if you think the constraints can't be jointly met — if you think anything HCH can do, factored cognition can decompose — argue for that instead of forcing a case. That's a better result for my chapter anyway, but then tell me which constraint I should drop first and what the closest surviving case looks like.
