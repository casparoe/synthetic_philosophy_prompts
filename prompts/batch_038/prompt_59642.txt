I would like a numerical credence on a specific claim in the philosophy of perception, together with the derivation that produces it.

First, the target, fixed as narrowly as I can manage. By disjunctivism I mean this and nothing stronger: when a subject veridically sees a yellow lemon on the table, and when a subject has a hallucination that she cannot, from where she sits, tell apart from seeing a yellow lemon on the table, there is no single mental-kind state — nothing individuated at the level of phenomenal character or of what the experience fundamentally consists in — that obtains in both cases and whose obtaining constitutes her having the experience she has. The two cases may share a negative or epistemic property, such as mutual indiscriminability. What they cannot share is a positive common factor.

Second, my own working hypothesis, which I want you to argue with rather than absorb. It has three parts.

1. The argument from illusion and the argument from hallucination are not two formulations of one argument, and conflating them has been costly. An illusion involves a real object presenting a real property that is simply not the property the subject takes it to have; a hallucination involves no such object at all. My suspicion is that once the disjunctivist is permitted to deny a positive common factor, illusion data cease to generate pressure toward one, and essentially all of the pressure comes from hallucination.

2. The hallucination argument does its work through a causal-matching premise: because the same proximal causal chain could have run in the absence of the distal object, the resulting state had better not be typed by its relation to that object. If this premise is doing the work, then the argument is about how mental states are to be individuated, not about what subjects can tell.

3. The standard disjunctivist reply — that hallucination is characterized only negatively, by its indiscriminability from a corresponding perception — is exactly as strong as its ability to avoid smuggling phenomenal character back in through the notion of indiscriminability, since what makes two cases indiscriminable for a subject looks suspiciously like a fact about what it is like for her.

Give me your credence that disjunctivism, as fixed above, is true, decomposed into at least the following terms, plus any others you think belong and minus any you think are vacuous:

(a) the probability that the illusion argument, taken on its own, fails to establish a common factor;
(b) the probability that the causal-matching premise is sound as a claim about how experiential states are typed;
(c) the probability that a purely negative or epistemic characterization of hallucination is both coherent and non-question-begging;
(d) the probability that the screening-off worry is fatal — that once the disjunctivist concedes indiscriminability as the shared property, that shared property does all the explanatory work and the disjuncts go idle;
(e) the probability that the question is indeterminate rather than unsettled, because phenomenal character or mental kind does not carve nature at a joint here.

For each term, give a number and two or three sentences of justification. Then show the combination explicitly — an expression I can check, not a narrative that gestures at one — and the resulting credence. Then answer three further questions: which term the final number is most sensitive to; which term is most likely to be the genuine crux between disjunctivists and common-factor theorists, as opposed to the term both sides advertise as the crux; and what argument, thought experiment, or empirical finding would move the final number furthest. Finally, if you think one of my three claims above is false, say which and why, and tell me what your credence would be under the correction rather than silently folding the disagreement into your numbers.

Lead with your bottom line: the first sentence of your reply should state your overall credence that disjunctivism, as defined, is true. Then the decomposition. No preamble, and no restatement of the question.
