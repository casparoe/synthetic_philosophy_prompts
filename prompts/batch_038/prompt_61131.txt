So a bit of background: I'm a backend engineer, I work on a policy/rules engine that turns compliance rules into config, and my partner's law-school friend runs a jurisprudence reading group that Ive been crashing for a couple months despite having basically zero philosophy background. Last week we did the Hart–Fuller exchange from the late 50s and I only half followed it, but two things stuck with me and I haven't been able to stop mapping them onto my day job. One is Fuller's checklist — his claim that there's a set of formal properties that make a body of rules count as law at all rather than just naked force: rules have to exist rather than be invented case by case, they have to be published, they can't be retroactive, they have to be intelligible, they can't contradict each other, they can't demand the impossible, they can't change so often that nobody can plan around them, and enforcement has to actually match what was announced. The other is Hart's answer, which is roughly: sure, but that's a checklist for effectiveness, not for morality — a system can hit every one of those marks and still be in the business of doing horrible things very efficiently. Plus the postwar German informer case they both argued over, where the court basically said the statute the woman reported her husband under was never law to begin with, and where the alternative was either letting her off or passing retroactive law to punish her.

What I want is a piece of satire that drops that argument into the one genre I know cold: a blameless incident postmortem. Call it 850–950 words, standard skeleton — TL;DR, impact, timeline with timestamps, root cause, contributing factors, a five-whys section, action items with owners and statuses. The company is invented; I'd name it something bland like Codifex or Statute Labs, a "legislation-as-a-service" vendor that generates and serves a client country's entire legal code out of a config repo. The incident: the client's government is replaced by an authoritarian regime that, and this is the whole joke, passes every single quality gate. The legality linter goes 100% green on a body of rules that is monstrous. The comedy has to come from the jurisprudence being right rather than from tech jokes, so each of Fuller's checks should be satisfied in a way that is unambiguously compliant and unambiguously awful — a rule that's perfectly general and perfectly clear about who counts as a person; the retroactivity check passing because the regime versions the config and never backfills it; the congruence check passing because enforcement does exactly what the published docs say, right down to the latency budget. The root cause section should stay genuinely contested rather than get resolved: one on-call engineer signs off that the system behaved exactly as specified, because whether a rule is valid is a question about where it came from and not about what it says, and a dissenting reviewer comment insists that a codebase which passes every one of those checks and still produces this has just proved the checks were never purely formal. I'd also like a sub-incident shaped like the informer case: a private citizen who reported a neighbor, or a spouse, under a rule that was validly enacted and correctly served at the time, and after the regime falls the two camps in the doc disagree about wether the fix is retroactive legislation, no fix at all, or a ruling that the rule never counted.

Constraints so it doesn't turn into a lecture: deadpan throughout, no winking at the reader, and no real countries, real philosophers, or real court cases — invented names only. No bibliography and no quoted passages from anyone; I want the piece itself, not a reading list. It needs to read two ways, which means keep the infra detail thin and generic and put all the weight on the legal reasoning: a lawyer who has never seen a CI pipeline should get the jokes, and a devops person who has never read jurisprudence should get them too. Two specific bits I'd love if you can land them without forcing it: an ambiguity ticket about whether an autonomous delivery robot counts as a vehicle under a park ordinance, where closing it requires the team to choose between plain meaning and the purpose of the rule and they cannot agree; and a proposed action item to add a content check to the linter, closed WONTFIX because nobody on the team can say what "unjust" compiles to. Let the five whys run all the way to a fifth why that no one on the rotation can fix.
