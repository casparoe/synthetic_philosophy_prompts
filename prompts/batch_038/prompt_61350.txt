I am the elections officer for a regional society of about sixty amateur astronomers who jointly maintain a mountaintop observatory. Every autumn we elect a chair from three nominated members, and the last two elections have left me with more questions than the bylaws can answer. Two years ago we used simple plurality. The winner took 26 votes to 22 and 12, and afterward roughly a dozen members told me privately that the third-place nominee was their genuine favorite but they had voted for one of the two front-runners to avoid spoiling the result. We then switched to instant runoff. Last year the first-choice counts were 21, 20, and 19; the third-place nominee was eliminated by a single vote, her ballots redistributed almost evenly, and the eventual winner prevailed 31 to 29 in the runoff. A week later one member wrote to me to confess that she prefers the eliminated nominee's chief rival to that nominee, but had deliberately listed the rival last so that the rival would be the one knocked out in the first round. She added, without embarrassment, that she had looked into this and understood that any published procedure with three or more options either invites that kind of calculation or else amounts to letting one person's ballot decide everything. I do not know whether she is right, and I would like to find out before the nominating committee meets.

For this year we have five proposals on the table, and I would like each one assessed rather than a general lecture on voting theory. First, keep instant runoff but require every member to sign a declaration that the ranking submitted is their sincere one, on the theory that a shared norm can accomplish what the arithmetic cannot. Second, a colleague proposes writing all ballots on identical slips, drawing one at random, and letting that member's ranking decide the election; he insists this is immune to manipulation because nobody can foresee the draw, and that it therefore escapes whatever impossibility my confessing member invoked. Third, hold a preliminary vote to narrow the field to two finalists and then take a majority of the full membership between those two, reasoning that with only two options honest voting is plainly the safe play. Fourth, a member argues that our nominees can always be arranged along a single dimension — how lavishly they would spend the observatory's equipment budget — and that everyone's preferences are peaked along that line, so we could simply adopt the favorite of the median member and be finished with the matter. Fifth, someone suggests approval voting, where each member marks every nominee they find acceptable and the most-marked nominee wins. Our bylaws impose three constraints on any procedure we adopt: every nominee must be capable of winning under some configuration of ballots; the outcome must not be fixed by any single individual's submission; and the winner must be determined by a published rule rather than by the committee's judgment. I am also conscious that we are sixty people who see each other monthly, that last year's confession became awkward gossip, and that a member who is caught manipulating faces a real social cost here that she would not face in a national election — I would like to know whether that difference touches the analysis at all, or only its practical urgency.

Please tell me, in prose first, whether my confessing member's sweeping claim is correct and what its exact preconditions are; which of the five proposals genuinely deliver a rule where misreporting one's preferences never pays; and what you would recommend we actually adopt given the three bylaw constraints and the social setting. Then close with a table, one row per proposal, using exactly these columns: "Proposal" (a short label for it); "Can misreporting still pay, and for whom?" (which bloc or voter type gains by lying, in a society of our size and shape); "Bound by the impossibility?" (whether and why the result that worries my colleague applies to this proposal or fails to); "Bylaws (i)-(iii)" (one entry per constraint, marked as satisfied, violated, or uncertain, with a phrase of explanation); and "Recommendation" (adopt, adopt with a specific modification, or reject). Keep the table's cells to a sentence or less each, and put your single-sentence bottom line immediately above it.
