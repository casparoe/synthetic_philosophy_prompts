For several years I have co-taught a graduate seminar that pairs a philosophy of historical explanation with a course in archaeological method, and this term the sentence 'absence of evidence is not evidence of absence' has surfaced in three separate sessions — each time, I notice, at the point where an argument was about to be settled rather than where one was about to be constructed. In the first instance my own argument was the target. I had claimed that for a certain class of large, long-occupied lowland settlements, the complete absence of any administrative apparatus — no sealings, no clay tokens or counting devices, no script or proto-script, no standardized storage facilities, no iconography of individual office-holders — together with a striking uniformity in house sizes and in grave goods, constitutes positive evidence that these communities lacked centralized, heritable political authority. My colleague from the archaeology department replied with the sentence above, observed that we have excavated perhaps four percent of one of the sites in question, and treated the matter as closed. The following week he deployed the identical sentence in what struck me as the opposite direction: when a student suggested that the failure to locate production debris showed that metalworking was carried out elsewhere in the region, my colleague insisted that the missing workshop tells us nothing and that on-site production remains a live hypothesis — while in my case the same species of silence was, on his account, positively diagnostic of an oral, kin-ordered polity. A third student then pressed the maxim further than either of us had, concluding that archaeology can add indefinitely to its inventory of what existed but can never subtract from it, so that every claim about what a past society lacked is immune to disproof.

I should like to bring some order to this before our next meeting, and I want a menu rather than a single favored interpretation. Please lay out the distinct precise claims the sentence can be made to carry, keeping in mind that both of its halves are ambiguous: on the one side, between evidence that was sought and not found, evidence that has never been looked for, evidence that could have existed but did not survive deposition, and evidence that the practice in question would never have left in the first place; on the other side, between proof, strong support, any support whatsoever, and a mere shift in the credence a rational agent ought to hold. Give me the pairings worth taking seriously, with a verdict on each — platitudinous, substantive and true, substantive and false, or indeterminate pending an empirical model of the record. For the strong probabilistic reading in particular, state the condition under which a failed search leaves a hypothesis exactly where it was, and be explicit about the parameters that govern how much weight a silence bears: how likely the practice would have been to leave a material footprint at all, how likely that footprint would have survived abandonment, reuse, ploughing, alluviation, and the like, how much of the relevant deposit has been sampled and by what recovery techniques, and whether the search was aimed at that footprint or merely happened to run across the ground where it might lie.

Then I have four further questions. First, which reading, if any, licenses my colleague's blocking move, and is four percent even the relevant figure, or must coverage be weighted by the way the traces of political authority would be distributed across a settlement — administrative debris presumably concentrating in a few places rather than scattering uniformly? Second, is there a single reading on which both of his moves are legitimate; and if not, which of the two must be surrendered, and why? My suspicion, which I should like either vindicated or corrected, is that the maxim is doing covert work as a shifter of priors — it smuggles in a high prior for the phenomenon being defended — rather than functioning as a constraint on inference at all. Third, how does all this bear on the stock detective-fiction situation in which the decisive clue is precisely that the watchdog did not bark, and on the use my colleagues in evidence-based medicine make of the same sentence about underpowered trials, where it evidently concerns magnitude rather than validity? Tell me whether that medical reading is the true kernel of the maxim everywhere, or whether the archaeological record is lossy in some way that makes a stronger reading correct there and only there. Fourth, give me a replacement maxim — a sentence or two a graduate student could actually apply — together with an answer for the student who believes no negative conclusion about the past is ever warranted: I want to be able to put in front of him a case in which silence genuinely is decisive, and to say what distinguishes it from mine.
