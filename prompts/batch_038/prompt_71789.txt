I've been writing down my own answers to made-up gambles for a few weeks now, mostly because I got into an argument about the Allais thing and realized halfway through that I had no idea whether I was consistent. Here's the list. What I want to know is whether there's a principle that actually captures all of it, and if not, which of my answers you're throwing out and why.

1. A million dollars for certain, or a ticket that pays $5M with probability 0.10, $1M with probability 0.89, and nothing with probability 0.01. I take the certain million. No hesitation, even though I know the ticket is worth more on paper.

2. Now: $1M with probability 0.11, or $5M with probability 0.10. I take the second one, also with no hesitation. I accept these two are supposed to be the same decision wearing different clothes. I still answer them differently.

3. Same as 1, except the "certain" million is a million with probability 0.99999 and nothing with probability 0.00001. Now I take the $5M ticket. So whatever the million-for-sure was doing, it switches off exactly at one and not gradually — 0.99999 is already enough to loose it.

4. Same two pairs as 1 and 2 but with coffee money: $1 for certain vs $5 at 0.10 / $1 at 0.89 / nothing at 0.01, and then $1 at 0.11 vs $5 at 0.10. No flip. I take the $5 ticket both times. Whatever is happening in 1–3 doesn't happen at all when the amounts are trivial.

5. Losses. A certain loss of $1M vs 10% chance of losing $5M, 89% chance of losing $1M, 1% chance of losing nothing: I take the certain loss. And 11% chance of losing $1M vs 10% chance of losing $5M: I take the 11%. So in the loss versions I pick the option with the less-bad worst case both times and there's no flip anywhere. People tell me that's not what most people do. It's what I do.

6. A coin flip: loose $100 on tails, win $110 on heads. I turn it down. I'd turn it down even if I knew I'd be offered it a hundred times, and I'm not broke. Someone once told me there's a theorem showing that refusing a small bet like that commits you to refusing some insane big-stakes bets too. I don't know whether I believe them, but I'm not giving this one up.

7. When the numbers in 1 and 2 are about the endowment of the little foundation I sit on the board of instead of my own money, my answers reverse and I take the 10% shot at $5M in both. It isn't that I feel braver. It's that "the whole million is gone" reads as a different species of event when it's a specific pot of other people's money.

8. If I take the certain million I don't want to be told what the other ticket would have paid, and I'd pay a bit to not find out. If I take the gamble I very much want to know. Related: a big part of why I take the certain million is that if I gamble and lose I get to blame myself, and if I never gamble there's nobody to blame.

9. This one may belong to a different subject entirely — I'd rather be handed the choice next month than today even when I'm certain nothing will change and certain I'll pick the same thing, and I'd give up a little money for the delay.

My own guess, for what it's worth: I'm not doing probability arithmetic first. I'm asking "is there any chance at all of the outcome I can't live with," and only when every option on the table has some such chance do I start comparing numbers. That seems to handle 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 7, since what counts as unlivable obviously moves with the stakes and with whose pot it is. But it clearly doesn't handle 6, 8 or 9, and I suspect it could be turned into a money pump by anyone who figured out how I decide.

So: state the rule, or the small set of candidate rules, as precisely as you can — actual decision procedure, not a reading list, though do say if what I've described has a standard name. For each clause, tell me which of my cases forced it in. Then give me a couple of cases I haven't thought of where your rule and the obvious rival come apart, so I can go check myself against them. And be straight about which of 1–9 it can't keep.

The one I keep going back and forth on: my brother wants me to co-sign something. Worst case I'm out $30,000, maybe a 5% chance of that, and the upside for him is real. My gut says no. But my gut also says no to a 5% chance of losing $30,000 when the upside is $500,000, and I think that's crazy. Where does thier rule put the line, and does it have to be a line at all?
