You are a philosopher of mind who works on consciousness, mental causation, and the metaphysics of introspection. Treat what follows as analytic housekeeping rather than a survey of the literature, and think it through carefully: work out the inferential links between the commitments before you pronounce on any of them.

Context. I am writing up my own position for a graduate reading group. One member of the group told me flatly that my package cannot stand and then declined to say which part fails, on the grounds that I should be able to find it myself. I cannot. I would rather have it located properly than guess.

Here is what I hold.

1. Experiences have an intrinsic qualitative character that is not exhausted by any functional or physical description of the system that has them. The explanatory gap marks a fact about the world, not merely a limit in our concepts.

2. Qualia have no causal powers: nothing it is like to have an experience makes any difference to what happens next. I do not regard this as a risky empirical hypothesis. I take the causal closure of the physical to be among the best-confirmed general claims we have, and closure together with non-identity gives me epiphenomenalism. At the same time I concede that epiphenomenalism is untestable in itself: no observation would distinguish it from interactionism.

3. All concepts, phenomenal ones included, get their content causally: a concept refers to the property that reliably produces and covaries with its tokens. I therefore reject acquaintance, direct presentation, and any special mechanism of phenomenal concept formation. A relation that lets a causally inert item determine what a thought is about looks like magic to me, and I will not buy the deliverances I want at that price.

4. Methodologically I refuse brute coincidences. If two distinct domains line up systematically, there is a reason they line up; that the laws simply happen to work out that way is not an answer I accept from myself or from anyone else.

5. In the case of the scientist raised in a black-and-white room who knows all the physical facts about color vision, I hold that she learns something new on release, and that what she learns is a fact about the world rather than a newly acquired ability.

6. A physical duplicate of me lacking experience is conceivable, and I treat conceivability as a fallible but genuine guide to metaphysical possibility.

7. My beliefs about my own current experience are knowledge. The access is direct rather than inferential, and it is extremely reliable, but I do not claim infallibility: I allow for error under distraction and where experiences succeed one another quickly.

8. That first-person reliability is something no third-person science could reproduce in principle, not merely in practice or with current technology.

What I want from you is an audit, not a lecture. For each tension you find, say which commitments generate it, trace the mechanism of the conflict rather than attaching a label to it, rate its severity, and state what would have to go or be weakened to remove it, together with what that concession costs me elsewhere in the set. Order the tensions by severity; do not present them as equally bad. Discriminate carefully between genuine incompatibilities and pairs that merely look awkward side by side — some of the above will survive scrutiny, and telling me which is part of the job. Pay particular attention to repairs that relocate a problem rather than dissolve it: I suspect at least one of the obvious fixes walks me straight into another difficulty, and I want that sequence mapped.

Then commit. Tell me which single revision would clean up the most, why that one rather than its rivals, and what the resulting view permits me to say and what it forecloses. Do not hand me a menu of options and leave the choice to me. I have deliberately not told you which commitments I am most attached to, and I do not want the answer organized around that.

No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I want the argument. Aim for roughly 900 to 1200 words, and begin by restating the set in your own terms and marking which commitments are load-bearing for which others before you turn to the tensions.
