You are a philosopher of mind working in the theory of emotion, equally comfortable with the cognitivist, perceptual, and feeling-based accounts, and you are good at auditing analogies rather than merely admiring them. Think carefully and do the analysis yourself; I am not looking for a survey or for citations. Do not give me a bibliography, a list of further reading, or references of any kind — I want your reasoning, not your sources.

Here is my situation. I am drafting a chapter on whether emotions are judgments, perceptions, or feelings, and my supervisor keeps steering me toward the perceptual model, defended today in broadly the form you find in Prinz's embodied appraisals and in Tappolet's claim that emotions are perceptions of evaluative properties. His pitch rests on an analogy he uses constantly: the emotions are a sense. Fear is to dangerousness what vision is to color — a dedicated, largely non-conceptual, world-presenting capacity that picks up a property in the environment, that can be more or less acute, that can be fooled, and that puts you in a position to know. He says this ancestry runs back to the eighteenth-century moral sense theorists, who were doing the same thing with worse physiology.

He uses the analogy to license two conclusions, and these are what I actually need audited:

1. Emotions can justify evaluative beliefs non-inferentially. If fear presents the dog as dangerous in the way vision presents the wall as white, then my fear gives me a reason to believe the dog is dangerous without my having to reason anything out, and the cognitivist's demand that emotion contain an actual judgment or construal is simply unmotivated.
2. Emotional disagreement is perceptual disagreement, and therefore tractable in principle. If two people feel differently about the same situation, the model tells us to look for differences in positioning, attention, or acuity — the way we settle disputes about color by moving into better light — which dissolves the charge that evaluative claims are mere sentiment.

He also has two standing replies to objections, and I want these treated as part of the analogy rather than as afterthoughts. When I say perceptions don't move you but emotions do, he says I have picked the wrong sense: the right analogue is not color vision but affordance perception, where to see the cup is already to see it as graspable and where a looming stimulus is perception and avoidance in one act. When I say emotions depend on beliefs and so cannot be basic, he says vision depends on lighting, prior expectations, and attention too, and that nobody concludes visual experience is therefore a judgment.

One further thing I want brought in, because it comes from my own reading rather than his. A colleague pointed out that if emotions really are a sense, then there is immediately a politics of the sense: somebody has to decide which perceivers are reliable, and the historical record is that women's anger and Black people's fear have been systematically classed as defective instruments rather than as accurate readings. Her worry is that the perceptual model, far from neutralizing this, gives it a respectable vocabulary — a malfunctioning sense is a thing you may discount, whereas a mistaken judgment is a thing you have to argue with. I want to know whether the analogy makes that problem better, worse, or merely tidier.
