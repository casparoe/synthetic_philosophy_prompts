I cover science and ideas for a weekly magazine. At a public panel last month, a philosopher of religion dispatched my question — whether a denomination's teaching that God answers petitionary prayer could be shown false by outcome data — with the remark that "religious belief isn't a hypothesis, it's a form of life, so evidence is simply beside the point," which she credited to Wittgenstein. The room applauded and I could not answer her. I want to write the piece properly, which means first taking that slogan apart.

Could you lay out the distinct, precise claims the sentence might be making, ordered from near-platitude to radical, and for each say whether it is defensible, whether Wittgenstein plausibly held it, and whether it would actually license brushing my question aside? I am particularly interested in the gap between readings that hold of religious language in general and readings that hold of this one claim, since the church in question tells its members to pray for named patients and keeps its own tally of recoveries. Tell me also which reading you think my panelist was invoking, and whether that one survives contact with what believers say when they pray for rain.

Please do not be even-handed for its own sake: where a reading is simply false, say so. And close by marking where your sorting is weakest — which reading you are most likely to have mis-filed, and what argument or consideration would change your verdict about the one she was relying on.
