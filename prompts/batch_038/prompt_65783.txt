I am preparing a session of my graduate seminar in early modern natural philosophy on the question whether Kepler's astrology is detachable from his astronomy, and I have run into an interpretive difficulty I would like help working through. What follows are the excerpts my students will read, all in Kenneth Negus's English translation; where the original is German I give both. Please treat the texts as I have set them out: assume you have no access to the secondary literature, and where you think some further passage in these same works would settle a question, describe what that passage would have to say rather than citing or reconstructing it.

A. Title page of the Tertius Interveniens (Frankfurt, 1610): "TERTIUS INTERVENIENS. Das ist, WARNUNG an etliche Theologos, Medicos und Philosophos, sonderlich D. PHILIPPUM FESELIUM, dass sie bey billicher Verwerffung der Sternguckerischen Aberglauben, nicht das Kindt mit dem Badt ausschütten und hiermit ihrer Profession unwissendt zuwider handlen," which Negus renders: "to wit, an admonition to several theologians, physicians, and philosophers, especially Dr. Philipp Feselius, that they, when quite properly rejecting the superstition of star-gazing, do not throw the baby out with the bath water and therewith unwittingly undermine their professions." A further line on the same page, which Negus's rendering passes over, advertises the book as adorned with many weighty philosophical questions never previously raised or explicated.

B. From the same work: "This curiosity [predictive astrology of a frivolous kind] flourishes, and stimulates one to learn astronomy. And astronomy is not rejected, but highly praised, as is appropriate. Now, this Astrology is a foolish daughter (as I wrote in my book de Stella Cap. XII ). But dear Lord, what would happen to her mother, the highly reasonable Astronomy, if she did not have this foolish daughter. The world, after all, is much more foolish, indeed is so foolish, that this old sensible mother, Astronomy, is talked into things and lied to as a result of her daughter's foolish pranks... The mathematician's pay would be so low, that the mother would starve, if the daughter did not earn anything. If formerly no one had been foolish enough to hope to learn of the future from the sky, then, Mr. Astronomer, you would not have gotten so clever as to think that the course of the heavens should be made known for God's honor and glory. In fact, you would have known nothing of the course of the heavens." The bracketed gloss and the mid-sentence capitalizations are the translator's and Kepler's respectively; the ellipsis marks an omission in the excerpt.

C. Also from the Tertius Interveniens: "The geometry or harmony of aspects is not between the stars in the sky, but is located rather down here on earth in the point that collects all these rays." And: "I have often expressed what an ill-considered thing it is to reject something completely because of its imperfections; for by this process even the science of medicine would not be spared."

D. From the Harmony of the World (1619): "The heavens do not act without mediation, for the vital power of the soul maintains its sovereignty as it brings its works into consonance with the heavenly harmonies, under this so-called 'influence' of the heavens. Unfortunately the term 'influence' has blinded philosophers to the extent that they would prefer to engage in mad ravings with the uneducated masses, than to think scientifically as I do."

E. From Kepler's revised delineation of Wallenstein's nativity, cast in January 1625: "In carrying out this request, I limited the scope of the mundane particularities. Of course astrologers are not lacking in techniques for satisfying peoples' curiosity. But I have put such 'rules' to the philosophical test, and I have no use for them whatsoever. Am I nevertheless supposed to let myself be used as a comedian, a performer, or some other kind of marketplace entertainer?... Philosophy, and therefore genuine astrology, is a testimony of God's works, and is therefore holy. It is by no means a frivolous thing. And I, for my part, do not wish to dishonor it."

My questions, in the order I want them taken up:

1. Begin with B and give me a sentence-by-sentence plain paraphrase, flagging every place where the sense depends on a translation choice. Then tell me what kind of argument the mother/daughter economy is. Is the daughter's usefulness to the mother offered as a reason to think astrology true, as a reason to tolerate it while withholding assent, or as an explanation of the historical conditions under which astronomy became possible at all? The three readings have quite different consequences for how I should present Kepler, and I cannot decide between the second and the third.

2. Does B license the deflationary reading on which Kepler regarded astrology as false but remunerative? Set against E, where the same author calls genuine astrology holy and refuses to play the marketplace entertainer, and against the word "genuine," which seems to be doing all the work. If the modifier is doing that work, what criteria of genuineness must B and E jointly supply, and are they consistent with one another? Note also Kepler's parenthetical cross-reference to a chapter of an earlier book: if that quip predates 1610, does it undercut or strengthen the hypothesis that B is a later, embittered rationalization of paid work?

3. C and D together. In C the aspect is not a relation among the stars but something located at the terrestrial point that collects the rays; in D the heavens act only through mediation, the soul's vital power keeps its sovereignty, and the very term influence is said to have misled philosophers. Does this amount to a rejection of celestial influx in favor of an occasion-and-response account, on which the heavens supply geometrical configurations and the sublunary soul — terrestrial and individual — supplies the activity? If so, what is the ontological status of the collecting point, and does making aspects features of a location rather than of the sky render Kepler's astrology a theory of recognition or perception rather than of causation?

4. The audience. A concedes that the superstition of star-gazing is quite properly rejected and addresses theologians, physicians, and philosophers, singling out Feselius; C's second half defends astrology by analogy with medicine. Who, precisely, is the third party standing between which two positions, and what rhetorical work does the baby-and-bathwater idiom do when the addressee is a physician whom Kepler can accuse of undermining his own profession? I want to know whether the title page frames the book as natural philosophy or as professional self-defense, since that changes how I introduce it.

5. Tell me where my own framing is overreaching. In particular, whether the contrast I am drawing between an occasion-and-response account in C and D and a mercenary instrumentalism in B is a distinction Kepler could have drawn, or one I am importing; and which of the questions above cannot be settled from these excerpts at all, together with a description of the sort of further material from these same works that would settle it.

Please write in prose, with the paraphrase in 1 set out separately. No bibliography, no reading list, and no citations: any claim about material I have not quoted should be marked explicitly as a conjecture.
