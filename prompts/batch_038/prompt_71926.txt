**Exam question (15 points; students had forty minutes):**

> Part A. State the "acquaintance principle" as it functions in current debates about aesthetic testimony. Explain what it does and what it does not imply about the epistemic standing of a professional critic's review. Part B. State and evaluate one objection to the principle.

**Student's answer:**

> Part A. The acquaintance principle says that a judgment attributing aesthetic value to a work can be warranted only for someone who has experienced that work for herself. Testimony can tell me a great deal about a novel — its length, its plot, that its sentences are long and unhurried — and it can even produce in me the true belief that the novel is beautiful. But it cannot put me in a position to *make* the aesthetic judgment, because that judgment has to rest on my own encounter with the work. The intuition is a familiar one: it is close to Kant's insistence that no amount of reasoning or reporting by others can get a subject to find something beautiful, since the judgment of taste has its ground in the subject's own feeling of pleasure. Recent defenders of the principle stress that it is a claim about warrant rather than about truth: testimony-based aesthetic beliefs are not thereby false, they are simply improperly grounded.
>
> What the principle amounts to, I think, is Russell's distinction between knowledge by acquaintance and knowledge by description carried over into art. A critic's phrases — "claustrophobic," "achingly slow" — are descriptions standing in for an experience, and the principle says that the description cannot do the work of the experience, just as knowing by description that the table in the next room is brown leaves me unacquainted with the table.
>
> Applied to criticism, this means that a professional review carries no warrant where aesthetic value is concerned. It can inform me about everything except the thing that matters, and the pessimist about aesthetic testimony is therefore committed to a response-dependent account of aesthetic value: since only the acquainted subject is in a position to judge, aesthetic claims are true only relative to how works strike such subjects, and realism about aesthetic value is not available. What is left to the review is a practical role — it helps me decide how to spend my evening.
>
> Part B. The strongest objection is that the principle proves too much. Almost everything I know about a great many works reaches me through testimony: that the poem was written in prison, that the symphony exists in two versions, even, in translation, the words on the page. If testimony is barred from the aesthetic domain, criticism — a practice we plainly credit with improving our responses to works — turns out to be a sham, and the aesthetic judgment is left with nothing to be about. This objection is decisive. Once the principle is restricted to the evaluative content of the judgment, which is what its defenders must do to avoid the objection, it has lost whatever substance it had: a thesis that says only "you have to see it for yourself" is not an epistemological position but a piece of etiquette about how to spend one's leisure time.
