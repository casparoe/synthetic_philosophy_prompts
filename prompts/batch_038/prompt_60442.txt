For the past three years I have run the two-day statutory interpretation workshop that new attorneys in our state attorney general's office complete before being assigned to agency litigation. The participants are smart and well read, and that is precisely the problem: they have all encountered the canonical materials, so when I hand out a hypothetical, half the room recognizes it within a minute. Anything built on Hart's familiar municipal ordinance banning vehicles from a park, or on a reskinned version of *Church of the Holy Trinity v. United States* (1892) or *Bostock v. Clayton County* (2020), is dead on arrival, and the exercises that survive tend to split the room along priors the participants already held rather than along interpretive commitments they are being asked to test. I am rebuilding the second day around a single constructed case, and I need you to build it to a fairly demanding specification.

Here is what I need. Draft, from scratch and modeled on no real statute anywhere, a state benefits or licensing provision in a mundane administrative setting — wage replacement, professional licensure, a tax credit, a permitting deadline; not criminal law, not civil rights, and nothing with a sympathetic-victim shape that pre-loads the reader's intuitions. The package should have three parts: the operative text of the provision (two or three subsections, set out as it would appear in the code, including any definitions section you need); a short excerpt from the legislative record — a committee report passage and a floor exchange, six sentences total at most — that states the legislature's purpose plainly and shows that the mismatch at the heart of the case was foreseen and accepted rather than overlooked; and the facts, in one paragraph, involving a single claimant whose situation the drafters knew would arise and did not specially address.

The case must then satisfy these conditions jointly:

1. The textualist reading of the enacted language is the *more generous* one: it entitles the claimant to the benefit, or brings the claimant within the protection, while the purposivist reading denies it. I want the direction that contradicts the standard classroom caricature, in which textualism is stingy literalism and purposivism the humane corrective.

2. The divergence must not come from any of the usual engines. No scrivener's error or drafting slip; no term in the operative language that is genuinely polysemous or vague; no contested term of art; no reliance on context that exists only in the record. It has to come from the architecture — the kind of condition the legislature chose to write (a status or functional test rather than a test keyed to the consequence the statute was meant to prevent, a general clause swallowing a specific one, a stipulative definition that outruns the mischief, and so on).

3. No escape hatches. The textualist must not need the absurdity doctrine, which means the generous reading has to produce a result that is merely unwise or costly rather than grotesque or self-defeating. The purposivist must not simply declare the text incapable of bearing the intended reading; their move has to be one a sophisticated purposivist would actually make and defend in print.

4. The record must cut both ways. Give the purposivist something real to work with and the textualist something real to resist. If the committee report is drafted so that only one side can use it, the case is not hard.

The purpose of all this is the exercise itself, which fixes what counts as cheating: participants receive the text, the record, and the facts, write down an outcome plus a one-sentence justification, and only afterward are told which reading was textualist and which purposivist. So the case must not smuggle in signposts — no judge names, no editorializing in the record, no claimant whose plight makes the answer obvious on affect rather than on argument — and, above all, the two verdicts must be generated by actually applying each theory to what you have drafted, not stipulated by how you have framed the scenario. If the case only works because one of the two theories is being played as a strawman, I would rather learn that now than in the room.

After the case, walk me through it. State the textualist reading and the chain of reasoning that reaches it (which canon or default does the work, and why the record cannot change the output); state the purposivist reading and its chain (what the purpose is, why the text can still bear the narrower reading, what interpretive move licenses it); then isolate the single feature of your drafting that produces the divergence and show what happens if I remove it — if the theories converge once that feature is gone, say so, and say whether the case was ever about anything more than that feature. Close with the losing side's best attack on the case as rigged and the winner's best reply. Keep the whole thing under roughly 1,100 words, and cite nothing: no cases, no articles, no bibliography. Everything a reader needs should be inside the package you draft.
