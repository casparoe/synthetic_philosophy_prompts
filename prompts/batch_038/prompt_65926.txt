I've read the rationalist-blog treatment of Hume's miracle argument more times than I can count: the Bayes reconstruction, the charge that the "uniform experience" premise begs the question, the ritual dismissal by way of mass hysteria. Nearly all of it runs on toy cases, and nearly all of it treats testimony as a single homogeneous lump. I want the argument run on a real event, with the event allowed to push back.

The event is the solar phenomenon of 13 October 1917 at the Cova da Iria, near Fátima, Portugal. So that we argue about the inference rather than the record, here are the facts I'm granting you:

- Three shepherd children — Lúcia Santos and her cousins Francisco and Jacinta Marto — had been reporting monthly apparitions of a lady since May 1917 and had announced that on 13 October she would perform a sign so that everyone would believe. Local newspapers reported the prophecy in advance; crowds had been building for months. The children were unschooled. Earlier in the summer the anticlerical republican administration had briefly detained them on suspicion that the prophecies were political.
- On the day, after heavy rain, the clouds broke. Estimates of attendance run from about 30,000 to about 70,000. For roughly ten minutes many present reported that the sun appeared as a dull opaque disc that could be looked at directly, cast multicolored light over the crowd and the landscape, lurched toward the earth, and zigzagged back into place. Others reported only the colors; others reported visions of the Holy Family; a number reported nothing unusual whatever. Several said their soaked clothes and the muddy ground dried almost at once.
- The crowd included literate professionals, government officials, and reporters from the secular Lisbon press, at least one of whom filed an account of what he saw. The local bishop opened a canonical investigation within weeks and in 1930 declared the events worthy of belief.
- Most of the surviving first-person testimony was collected decades later, in the 1940s, by a priest researching the site with obvious devotional interest.
- Treat as given that no corroborating report came from elsewhere in Europe, and note the obvious difficulty that a midday solar event would be hard to detect instrumentally in any case.

The lens is Hume's two-pronged argument in the miracles section of the second *Enquiry*: the a priori claim that a miracle is a violation of laws established by firm and unalterable experience, so that testimony establishes one only if the testimony's falsehood would be more extraordinary than the fact it reports; and the a posteriori claim about the actual quality of miracle testimony — the number, education, and standing of the witnesses, what they have to lose, the prevalence of such reports among unlettered peoples, the mutual cancellation of miracles claimed by rival religions, and the passion of surprise and wonder as a standing motive to credulity. Layer on the Bayesian reconstruction, and specifically the convergence idea: that genuinely independent testimony can in principle overwhelm any finite prior, which is where Earman locates Hume's failure.

What I want from you:

1. **Decompose the testimony.** This is not one report. Separate at least the children's private visions, the public solar phenomenon, the drying of clothes and ground, the advance prediction, and the individual visions of the Holy Family reported by some in the crowd. For each, say who the witness population is, what proposition they are actually attesting to, and what prior and what likelihood ratio attach. Flag any of these that a serious apologist would not in fact be relying on.

2. **Run Hume's own criteria**, and be honest where they cut against him. The presence of literate professionals, officials, and hostile press is exactly what Hume said would never be found in a miracle report; the unschooled children and the rural devotional setting are exactly what he said would be. Say which criterion does the real work here, and whether the hostile-witness point survives the fact that the hostile witness was himself inside the primed crowd.

3. **Do the independence arithmetic explicitly.** Convergence requires approximate independence. Given months of prophecy, newspaper coverage, pilgrimage, and shared expectation, quantify in rough terms how correlated the crowd's reports must be, and say what happens to a convergence argument once the common causes — expectation, afterimages from staring at a bright disc, social cascade — are admitted. I suspect this is where the case is actually decided, and I suspect it is decided for Hume's conclusion by a route Hume's own framework cannot articulate.

4. **Handle the prediction.** Hume's model is about testimony to a past surprising event and has no obvious slot for a publicly announced forecast. Work out whether the advance prophecy raises or lowers the likelihood ratio, and address the asymmetry I find most interesting: on the natural hypothesis the prophecy generates the very conditions — a large, expectant, emotionally aroused crowd assembled at a fixed time and place — that make the reported phenomenon likely, so a successful prediction may be self-fulfilling rather than probative. Note also how unspecific the prediction was about the *character* of the sign, and what that does to the calculation.

5. **Account for the negative witnesses.** A number saw nothing. Under a real solar-event hypothesis and under a suggestion hypothesis, what fraction of the crowd should have seen something, and why is the surviving record — retrospectively collected, non-random, curated by a devotional investigator — a poor instrument for estimating that fraction? Say how much weight Hume's number-of-witnesses criterion can bear when the witness list is itself an artifact of the collection process.

6. **Compare models.** Test Hume against at least a contagion or mass-psychogenic account and against the presumption-of-credulity view on which testimony enjoys a defeasible default warrant prior to any inductive case. The discriminating feature, I think, is the *shape* of the disagreement: colors here, dancing there, visions elsewhere, silence in the rest. A true event should produce convergent detail; a primed crowd should produce a family resemblance of loosely related reports. Say which model predicts the observed pattern, and what each model is silent about.

7. **Say where Hume goes quiet.** He offers a dichotomy — knaves and fools — and no positive account of mechanism. Where the case demands one and his framework supplies none, say so in a sentence and move on rather than making him speak.

8. **Deliver a verdict and a falsification condition.** Does this episode confirm Hume's argument, sit as an anomaly inside it, or fall outside the class of cases he was addressing? Then specify what the evidence would have to look like — instrumentation, timing, blinding, collection procedure, geography — for a crowd event of this kind to become probative on your analysis.

Constraints. No bibliography, no reading list, no padding with names of books. Do not put anything in quotation marks: if you want to invoke a line of Hume's, paraphrase it and label it a paraphrase, since neither of us can check you. Roughly 1,800 to 2,500 words, with headings matching the eight items above. I would rather have three cruxes examined properly than a survey of every objection in the literature; the standard moves — illiterate peasants, mass hysteria, low priors — I already know, and reciting them earns you nothing.
