ok so I run a small metaethics reading group, six of us, and we've spent three sessions going in circles on the embedding problem for expressivism — mostly because everyone insists they're a quasi-realist without being able to say what they'd give up to stop being one. I want to build a sorting instrument instead of another reading list. Can you write it?

Starting point for the sorter: the reader accepts exactly two things and nothing else. (i) "lying is wrong" and "lying isn't wrong" can't both be right, in the ordinary sense of not-both-right; (ii) you can reason with moral sentences in conditionals — e.g. "if lying is wrong then getting your little brother to lie is wrong; lying is wrong; so getting your little brother to lie is wrong" is a good argument, and its being good has something to do with the parts meaning what they do. From there the tree walks them to one expressivist strategy for making those two things work together.

Constraints on the nodes, please follow these pretty strictly:
- every node asks for a verdict about a specific sentence or pair of sentences, phrased so someone who's never heard the word "expressivism" can answer it. No node question may contain "proposition", "truth-apt", "cognitive", "descriptive content" or "compositionality" — those can appear in the branch labels and the leaves, just not in the questions.
- no node may ask about labels or self-identification. "do you think moral judgements are beliefs?" is banned, for the obvious reason.
- 9 to 12 nodes, no more than 8 leaves.

The nodes have to be built out of at least six of these test cases, and don't add cases that require knowing anything about metaethics to have an intuition about:
1. "lying is wrong" vs "lying isn't wrong" — is the second one just the first with a negation stuck on the front, or is it something else entirely?
2. "maybe lying isn't wrong" and "there's a 30% chance lying is wrong".
3. "is lying wrong?" — said as a real question, not rhetorically.
4. "don't lie!" — and whether it differs from "lying is wrong" only in mood.
5. "either lying is wrong or the train is late."
6. "if lying is wrong then stealing is wrong," sincerely accepted by someone who thinks neither is wrong.
7. "she thinks lying is wrong" and "I used to think lying was wrong."
8. the theorist's own sentence: "'lying is wrong' expresses disapproval of lying" — what happens when she applies her own account to that one?
9. "lying is wrong, but I don't disapprove of lying" — is that a contradiction, a confession of insincerity, or perfectly coherent?

For each leaf give me: the name of the strategy if it has one (I can name maybe half of them myself — quasi-realism and its higher-order attitudes move, planning/norm-expressivism, something involving a state I think Schroeder calls "being for", hybrid or ecumenical views that make moral sentences carry both an attitude and a belief, and just giving up on compositionality — fill in the rest, including one approach I half-remember that treats moral sentences as updating a shared conversational state rather than as reporting anything about the speaker; name it if it has a name, and if a leaf is genuinely undefended, say "unnamed" rather than inventing a label), one sentence on that strategy's most awkward commitment, and which of the nine test sentences it handles worst.

Then two more things. First, flag every pair of leaves your tree can't actually separate — I already suspect one pair, mature quasi-realism and a sufficiently deflationary realism, and I want to be told flat out whether the tree distinguishes them or whether that distinction has to come from somewhere other than sentence-level verdicts. Second, before the tree itself, give me the logical space in miniature: which independent yes/no questions generate it, how many combinations that allows, where the real leaves sit in it, and for at least three unoccupied combinations why nobody is there — incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Use "merely unexplored" sparingly and argue for it each time.

Last bit: tell me which single node does the most sorting work, and which one I should cut if I want to get the whole thing down to eight nodes. The group is mostly ex-realists, so the tree shouldn't assume anyone wants to end up an expressivist — but it also shouldn't offer a "none of the above, I'm a realist" exit, because the whole point is to make them commit to a semantic strategy and then live with it.
