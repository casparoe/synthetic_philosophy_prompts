In three weeks I'm running a seventy-five-minute session for my department's interdisciplinary reading group — roughly half philosophy graduate students, half economics students who have finished first-year micro but never taken information economics. The topic is why common knowledge makes speculative trade impossible: Aumann's agreement theorem and the Milgrom–Stokey no-trade result. I don't want the theory explained to me; I want a plan for explaining it.

First, tell me what has to be in place before either theorem sounds surprising rather than trivial. My sense is that the common prior and the common knowledge of the proposed trade are two separate pieces of apparatus, and I can't decide which to build first, or whether one of them can be deferred until the moment it's needed.

Second, give me one small concrete example — two states, two traders, coarse partitions — that carries the entire argument, and say what that example must not contain if it is to do the work.

Third, map the misconceptions each half of the room will bring and the point in the sequence where each should be defused. My guesses are that the philosophers will treat common knowledge as a harmless idealization, that the economists will hear the theorem as one more version of market efficiency, and that both will leave concluding that actual trading is irrational.

Finally, take a definite position on whether to close with the Grossman–Stiglitz point about fully revealing prices destroying the incentive to gather information, or to cut it as one paradox too many — and say what you would drop elsewhere to make room. Commit to your choices and give reasons rather than laying out options for me to weigh, and end with one question I could pose in the last five minutes that would show whether the reasoning landed rather than the theorem merely being accepted. Please write in American English.
