So I'm putting together a film night for a small philosophy meetup and I've got Memento (Nolan, 2000) down as the pick, but I'm bored of the reading everyone does with it — the one where amnesia means Leonard isn't really one person over time, so it's all Parfit and psychological continuity and the film is basically a thought experiment with a reverse-chronology gimmick. I want to run it through Buddhist no-self instead, and honestly I can't tell yet whether that's a different reading or just the same reading with better vocabulary. Help me figure that out.

The setup seems to point that way. Leonard has no continuous memory, so he's held together instead by notes, Polaroids with captions he wrote himself, and tattoos covering his body — plus the dead man's clothes and the dead man's car, which is its own little joke about how thin a self is. That sounds a lot like the chariot business in the Milinda dialogue, where the monk walks the king through axle, wheels, frame and so on and concludes that the chariot is only a name for the assemblage, and a lot like the five-aggregates picture of a person as a conditioned stream rather than a thing. So: does the film give us a conventional person held together by causes and conditions, or is it a reductio of that picture — i.e. the reason Leonard comes across as a person who's been damaged rather than a person revealed to have been a fiction all along is that the movie keeps assuming the substantial self it's supposedly illustrating? I want the honest version of this, including wherever the analogy just breaks and you have to say so.

The bit I care about most is the ending once you put it in chronological order: Leonard — knowing he won't remember doing it — deliberately steers himself toward the cop as the next John G., because he wants a puzzle left to chase. On a Parfitian account that's a present stage harming a future stage who's arguably a seperate person anyway. But Buddhist ethics doesn't run on harm-to-a-victim; it runs on skillful versus unskillful, on craving and the way craving drives the stream onward. Is what Leonard does at the end maybe the worst possible version of no-self — a man living the metaphysics without the ethics or the practice, so he gets all the dukkha and none of the liberation? Relatedly, does the Sammy Jankis story, retold until it becomes the shape of his whole life, work like a karmic narrative — a self you keep manufacturing by telling it?

And Leonard insists, roughly, that he has to believe his actions still have meaning even though he won't remember them. Is that a decent Buddhist move or a straight craving move? Where would you draw the line?

No need for citations or references, I just want a real reading that takes both the film and the doctrine seriously, doesn't turn Nolan into a sutta, and doesn't flatten the doctrine into a plot summary. Around 1200 words. Finish with the two or three scenes you'd actually screen and argue about, and say why those.
