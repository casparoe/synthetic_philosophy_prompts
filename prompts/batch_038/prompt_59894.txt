I convene a graduate seminar in the philosophy of mathematics, and our final meeting is a reading rather than a discussion: each participant contributes one poem. I would like you to write mine — a sestina about the hotel that is full and yet always has a room.

**Form.** Six sixains and a three-line envoi. If the end-words of the first stanza are numbered 1 through 6, the subsequent stanzas must run 615243, 364125, 532614, 451362, 246531, and the envoi must carry all six words, closing on end-word 1. Lines of roughly equal length; a loose pentameter is welcome but not required. Choose the six end-words from the working vocabulary of a hotel and its front desk — a key, a corridor, a register, a lamp, a bell, a door — and let each of them shift its sense at least once across the six rotations, so that what meant an actual room in the first stanza has come to mean a place in an ordering by the last. None of the six may be a term of art, and the words Hilbert, Cantor, Dedekind, bijection, cardinal, countable, aleph and infinite may not appear anywhere in the poem.

**Speaker.** The night manager, writing the entry for one night in the register he keeps by hand. He was a land surveyor before this job and has never entirely stopped being one: he measures things, and he used to hold that a house is only as large as the ground it stands on. The poem should let the reader watch that conviction loosen through the returning end-words rather than hear him announce a conversion.

**What each stanza must do.** One traveler arrives at a house with no room to give, and the manager issues the instruction that everyone move up one; the shift is then carried out, and nothing is lost, because there is no last guest to be pushed off the end. Next, the coaches — not one but a queue with no end to it — and the doubling that throws all the odd-numbered rooms open at once. Then the unease: every guest is housed, the house was full an hour ago, and yet a door stands empty. Then the diagnosis, which is the heart of the poem: "full" has been doing two jobs, meaning both that no room lacks a guest and that no guest lacks a room, and in a finite house the two come together, while here they part company — a whole can be no larger than a proper part of itself, and that is not a contradiction but the very mark of what this house is. Finally the manager signs the page, and the envoi takes up the objection he still half believes: that the hotel was never built all at once, that all he was ever given is a rule for the next reassignment, so that strictly he has never housed anyone, only known how to. Let him answer it — concede that the house is never completed while insisting that the rule was all the housing required, finite about the building and infinite about the assignment. Give me an answer, not a shrug.

**Accuracy.** The pairing must be shown, not asserted: no line may offer a slogan of the form "infinity plus one is still infinity," and if that thought appears at all it must appear as the manager counting heads and finding the same heads he started with. The register's page must run out even though the corridor does not. And if the poem glances at guests who cannot be listed at all, it must be unmistakable that this is where his method stops working, not where it triumphs.

Give me the poem alone, thirty-nine lines, with a title and no gloss. Someone who has never heard of the hotel should find it a strange night in a very large building; someone who has should find nothing to correct.
