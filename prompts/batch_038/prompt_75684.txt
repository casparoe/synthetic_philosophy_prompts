In the literature on the evolution of cooperation there is a dispute that has run for decades without either side conceding, and I want a diagnosis of it rather than a survey. One side holds that the indefinitely repeated prisoner's dilemma explains cooperation: if the shadow of the future is long enough, reciprocal strategies of the tit-for-tat family are robust, mutual cooperation becomes self-enforcing, and the practical lesson is to make interactions repeatable and partners identifiable. The other side replies that this is close to vacuous, because the folk theorem says that when players are patient enough, any feasible payoff profile that leaves each player strictly better off than the level they can guarantee themselves by punishing their opponent — their minmax payoff — can be sustained as an equilibrium, mutual defection included; repetition therefore tells us which outcomes are possible, not which will be reached, and the explanatory work must be done elsewhere: equilibrium selection, trembles and implementation error, population structure, cultural evolution. Meanwhile the first side keeps producing tournament results and keeps being cited by people designing institutions and treaties.

My hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than accept: this is not a factual disagreement but two different questions being answered under one heading — one about the stability of cooperation once it is in place, the other about which outcome gets reached in the first place — with a further, largely verbal strand about what counts as an explanation of cooperation. Say whether that is right or too tidy. Specifically: what is each side actually committed to; does the tournament evidence bear on the folk-theorem objection at all, given that a tournament fixes the pool of entrants in advance and thus assumes away selection; and is the noisy-repeated-game literature a genuine resolution or just a restatement of the selection problem in new notation? Then tell me what would count as progress for someone who wants to spend the next few years here — a distinction, a formal result, a change of question, or a reason to expect none — and be explicit throughout about which parts of your diagnosis are settled among people who work on repeated games and which remain genuinely contested. I do not want your preferred reading presented as the consensus.
