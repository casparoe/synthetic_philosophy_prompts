You are a legal philosopher with practical experience advising statutory law-reform bodies. Think carefully before answering, and begin with a short, explicitly labelled brainstorming section in which you work out which distinctions from the underlying debate have to survive translation into an administrative procedure and which can be dropped without distorting the argument; only then present the deliverable itself.

I am counsel to a law-revision commission in a common-law jurisdiction. Our enabling statute directs us to review the criminal code and, for every offence that prohibits conduct chiefly because the community regards it as wrong rather than because it demonstrably injures anyone, to recommend one of four dispositions: repeal outright; retain as it stands; downgrade to a civil or administrative penalty; or refer the file to the full commission as a question of values that the secretariat should not resolve on its own. Two clauses in the statute pull against each other. The first provides that no conduct may be criminalised solely on the ground that the community finds it immoral, indecent or offensive. The second provides that in reviewing existing offences the commission must have regard to whether abolition would tend to weaken standards that the community holds in common. We have roughly 130 candidate offences, fourteen months, and three staff — one with training in jurisprudence, two without. Each recommendation is drafted by a staff member, signed by me, and put to the five commissioners; the resulting report goes to a legislative committee and may be tested in judicial review, so every memo must carry its reasoning in two pages. Two commissioners hold that the second clause does most of the work and three that the first does, and the procedure has to be run day to day by the two staff members who have never read any jurisprudence and will not be reading any for this job.

Write the procedure as a sequence of ordered steps. For each step, state the question in plain operational language, say what would count as a sufficient answer, and say which answers route the file to which disposition and which merely advance it to the next step. It must build in the difference between a communal feeling about a practice and a communal belief about what will happen if the practice is permitted, and it must say what evidence could settle the second question, what to do when there is none — which is the usual case — and how much weight the sheer longevity and prevalence of a standard can bear without the procedure collapsing into a rule that whatever has always been illegal stays illegal. Include steps that ask about the offence as it is actually enforced, rather than about the conduct in the abstract: who is charged, what the offence makes available by way of blackmail, extortion, entrapment or entry into private premises, and whether the enforcement pattern is so selective that the rule on the books is not the rule in operation. Mark explicitly every place where the procedure must stop and hand the question to the commissioners, and flag every step that is really a judgement call dressed up as a step. Ground the whole thing in the mid-century exchange between Patrick Devlin and H. L. A. Hart, and draw on other writers in that tradition where they sharpen a step; you may name ideas and positions where a name helps a commissioner see a step's pedigree, but paraphrase rather than quote, and do not append a bibliography, reading list or citations.

Finally, run the procedure on three petitions and report the disposition for each. (a) A retained offence prohibiting sexual conduct between two consenting adults in private. (b) An offence prohibiting the desecration of, or commercial dealing in, human remains, where the petitioner's case is that the wrong is not owed to any identifiable victim and cannot be cashed out as injury. (c) An offence prohibiting the keeping of a brothel in a jurisdiction where the underlying acts are not themselves criminal, so that the question is whether the state may criminalise the organisation of conduct it has declined to criminalise. For each, state which steps did the work, where the procedure ran out of factual answers and had to fall back on something else, and whether the output is one this commission will dislike; for at least one of the three, say plainly that it is, and say why the procedure should not be adjusted to avoid it.
