Write a short story of roughly two thousand words set in an archive-city whose law of evidence recognizes only constructive warrants: a claim counts as established only when a witness, or a procedure guaranteed to produce one, has been deposited in the archive, and once established at a given state of information it remains established at every later state. The plot should turn on the gap between "it is not the case that there is no such warrant" and "there is such a warrant" — a junior clerk must decide whether a person who cannot be shown to be present and cannot be shown to be absent may be declared absent, so that an estate can be released and a lease reassigned.

Three technical requirements:

1. Someone invokes the principle that every claim is either established or refuted, and the answer must be that the principle cannot be refuted, only withheld — since even its double negation is provable. Get this exactly right; it is the story's central irony.
2. Include a document from the old classical regime that is made legible only by translating it into the archive's idiom through systematically inserted double negations, and show what the translation preserves and what it quietly costs a reader who trusts it.
3. Let a faction that accepts the step from "it is refutable that no such procedure exists" to "such a procedure exists" clash with those who refuse that step, and make the disagreement change the outcome rather than serve as local color.

Dramatize rather than expound: no lectures, no naming the historical founders of the view, no glossary. Do not make the regime merely villainous — its rule should have a justification the reader comes to feel, and the clerk's scruple should cost something.
