I teach an upper-level undergraduate seminar in philosophy of mind — fourteen to eighteen students, one 75-minute session — and I want to run an activity on pain and the badness of pain. The students have finished a unit on phenomenal consciousness and a short one on value theory, so they are at home with talk of qualia and with the distinction between intrinsic and instrumental badness. They have not encountered the clinical literature on pain, and I do not want them to hear the term "pain asymbolia," or any theory label attached to this material, until the debrief.

The insight I am after is not the familiar point that pain has a sensory-discriminative dimension and an affective one. I want them to run into something sharper: when a subject reports a sensation but shows no aversion to it, whose verdict settles whether the state is bad for them — the subject's own report, the subject's dispositions, or the outside observer's? My working hypothesis, which the activity should test rather than announce, is that the standing argument over whether the badness of pain is a felt quality or an attitude toward a felt quality is largely an argument about the direction of that authority, and that ordinary reasoners shift which authority they appeal to from case to case without noticing. If the session works, students should catch themselves making the shift in real time and be unable to say which of their own verdicts to keep.

Please design the session with these components:

1. A first round in which the dissociation appears in the room's own data before it appears in any case study. Use a mild, physically safe, wholly voluntary aversive stimulus, together with a rating procedure that separates how intense a sensation is from how much the subject minds it, and some small bidding or trade element that forces a revealed preference rather than a reported one. The crucial design problem is that if every student rates both scales high, the round produces nothing: build the stimulus set or the rating scheme so that divergence is close to guaranteed, and give me an explicit fallback for a group in which no divergence materializes. Include the consent and opt-out protocol, and a non-physical version of the round that preserves the mechanic for students who decline the stimulus or for whom it is inadvisable.

2. A second round of written patient dossiers that the students, working in small committees, must convert into a single ranked list under a scarce treatment budget. One dossier should be the asymbolic patient, with scripted lines you write for the facilitator to read aloud. Include matched foils — for instance a stoic who reports high intensity and high minding but displays nothing, and a patient who declines treatment for a state they describe as terrible — arranged so that the committees cannot use expression, report, and disposition interchangeably. Give me a payoff scheme requiring no real money in which every candidate rule for translating reports into rankings is punished by at least one dossier, and at least one dossier forces the question whether to treat a patient over their own account of not minding.

3. A minute-by-minute timeline, a materials list, group sizes within the seminar, what goes on the board and when, and the facilitator's scripts. Also a cut-down 50-minute version.

4. A debrief that names the syndrome only at the end and maps specific moments of the two rounds onto the distinction. For the clinical picture, use the standard description and nothing beyond it: such patients recognize and localize noxious stimuli but show absent or inadequate emotional and motor responses to those stimuli and to threatening gestures and to verbal threats, appear unconcerned about their deficit, fail to learn protective or escape responses, and the associated brain damage invariably includes the insular cortex. Then lay out, without naming proponents, the three readings the students' own rankings have implicitly committed them to — genuine pain that lacks the component that makes pain bad; a state that is not pain at all, the reports being an artifact of a broader loss of affect or insight; and genuine, genuinely bad pain whose badness the patient cannot act on or acknowledge — and force each committee to identify which reading its ranking rule required, and where in the game it changed authorities.

5. An account of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and how to handle it: students guessing the point early, the stimulus reading as amusing rather than aversive, a committee finding a ranking rule that escapes every dossier, the override question collapsing into quick consensus, and any distress the physical round may cause in a student with a history of chronic pain or injury.

6. A frank closing note on which features of the phenomenon the activity cannot reproduce and I must supply myself — the difference between a temporary, safely bounded discomfort that healthy subjects can end at will and a lesion-based loss of the capacity to learn protection, among others.

Two constraints. Do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations, named researchers, or paper titles; the session must be preparable from your own understanding, and the students will read nothing beforehand. Where you construct cases or details that are not part of the clinical description of the syndrome, mark them clearly in the facilitator's copy as invented for the game, and do not present them to students as clinical fact.
