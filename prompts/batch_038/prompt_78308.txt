The question, exactly as it appeared on the exam:

**Question 3 (18 points).** Vogel's case of Roxanne and her car's gas gauge is usually described as a bootstrapping argument, and Cohen generalizes the worry into a problem about easy knowledge.
(a) Reconstruct the argument: state Roxanne's epistemic situation, the reasoning she goes through, and which premises the reconstruction depends on.
(b) Explain in what sense the conclusion is supposed to be unacceptable — that is, why this is a reductio rather than a discovery that Roxanne really can come to know her gauge is reliable.
(c) Pryor's dogmatist accepts that perceptual beliefs have basic warrant. What resources does that view have for responding, and what does the response cost it?

The student's answer, transcribed from the blue book:

> (a) Roxanne drives a car with a gas gauge she has no reason to trust and no reason to distrust. She glances at it, sees the needle on F, and forms the belief that the tank is full. On a reliabilist account, if the gauge is in fact reliable and her vision is working normally, that belief is knowledge straight off — she doesn't need any prior warrant for the gauge's reliability. And she can also tell, just by looking, that the gauge reads F. So she reasons: the gauge reads F, and the tank is full; that's one case where the reading matched the fact. She does this every time she fills up, accumulates a list of matching cases, and generalizes inductively to the conclusion that the gauge is reliable. Since each step starts from knowledge and competent deduction from known premises yields knowledge, Roxanne ends up knowing that her gauge is reliable purely by looking at it.
>
> (b) This is a reductio because the conclusion is supposed to be absurd, not because the reasoning is invalid. It can't be right that anyone can manufacture warrant for the reliability of a source just by using the source and noting the agreement — that would make checking wether your instruments work a matter of using them, and it would let you run the same trick for any belief you already hold. So the reliabilist has to give up one of the ingredients: basic perceptual knowledge, closure under known deduction, or the claim that Roxanne knows what the gauge reads.
>
> (c) The dogmatist can't give up basic perceptual warrant, that's the whole view. What they can say is that the argument, though valid, doesn't transmit: the premises are warranted, but that warrant isn't what makes the conclusion warranted, so Roxanne's reasoning can't be her route to believing the gauge is reliable. That's the Wright line — deny that closure holds for arguments like this one, and insist the reliability belief needs an independent entitlement before the reasoning can get going. The cost is obvious: once you allow that deduction can fail to transmit, you owe a principled account of which arguments transmit and which don't, and if the answer is "most of the ones that start from perception," the dogmatist has backed into skepticism.
>
> One more thing worth flagging: the argument only gets off the ground because Roxanne knows that she knows the tank is full — she has to be in a position to take her own belief as data about the gauge. Reliabilism denies KK, so the reliabilist can simply say the bootstrapping reasoning requires a level-crossing belief their view independently rejects.
