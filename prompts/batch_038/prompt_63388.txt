I work in the grants office of a national arts council, and I have been asked to write the internal review of our main open-access funding instrument ahead of its triennial renewal. Before I write a single line of recommendation, I want to understand what the instrument is actually committed to, because I have become unable to explain its structure to people outside the organization without sounding evasive.

Applications are scored by two panels whose marks are then combined by a simple average. The Artistic Merit panel is drawn from practising artists and critics; its guidance tells assessors to weigh craft, ambition, originality, and the internal coherence of the proposed work with the applicant's stated intention, and it expressly forbids them from taking any account of projected audience size, ticket prices, the demographic composition of the audience, or any social, educational, or health outcome. The Reach panel is drawn from social researchers and community organizers; its guidance tells assessors to weigh projected participation numbers, the socio-economic and geographic breadth of the audience, evidence of first-time attenders, and self-reported measures of belonging and wellbeing collected by a standard post-event survey, and it expressly forbids them from considering the artistic quality of the work. Two further rules complete the picture. Our founding charter contains a neutrality clause under which the council may not pronounce on the value of any particular artwork, only on the value of programmes and organizations. And every applicant must complete a short field in which they state, in one sentence, the feeling they intend the work to leave its audience with; that sentence is never scored for accuracy or sincerity, but the application is inadmissible unless it can be mapped onto one of eight approved outcome categories: wonder, pride, consolation, solidarity, delight, unease, curiosity, and remembrance.

A colleague on the policy team insists that the averaging rule is nothing but a political settlement between two constituencies and should not be read as expressing any view at all. I want to know whether she is right, or whether the settlement itself has a philosophical shape. My suspicion comes from having recently read Tolstoy's late treatise on art, where art is treated as a means by which one person transmits a felt experience to others through outward signs, and where a work is graded both by how well the transmission succeeds and, decisively, by the worth of the feeling transmitted, measured against the moral and religious consciousness of the age. I cannot shake the impression that our instrument is a partial and possibly incoherent implementation of something like that picture, one that keeps the machinery of transmission and throws away the evaluation.

Please address the following. First, reconstruct the theory of artistic value the instrument presupposes: for the average of two panel scores to mean anything, what must be assumed about the relation between merit and reach? Are they genuinely distinct and irreducible goods, is one a proxy for the other, or is the averaging a device for distributing blame rather than for measuring value? Second, identify which rival accounts of art the rules quietly exclude, and which they could accommodate with no change to the text at all; pay particular attention to the neutrality clause, and say whether it commits the council to the view that there are no facts about the value of particular artworks or merely to the view that a public body must not act as if it knew them, and whether the panels' actual behaviour lets us tell those two readings apart. Third, evaluate the feeling-sentence field against Tolstoy: which of his conditions on successful transmission, if any, does it capture, and which does the eight-category list suppress? Can a post-event report of raised wellbeing or belonging distinguish a work that infected its audience with what Tolstoy would count as a good feeling from one that infected them just as thoroughly with a bad one, and does the approved category list presume that the moral differences among feelings have already been settled, or that they are simply beside the point of a funding decision? Fourth, mark explicitly where the instrument is underdetermined, that is, where several incompatible theories of art would license exactly these rules and nothing else. Finally, sketch what a sincerely Tolstoyan replacement would look like: what the panels would be, what each would be forbidden to consider, what would become of the neutrality clause, and which categories of currently funded activity would become unfundable; then say whether the council's stated public purposes would be better or worse served by it, and whether my colleague's deflationary reading survives the exercise.
