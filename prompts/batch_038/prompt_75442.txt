I'm trying to nail down the example that carries a section of my thesis chapter and I've gone around in circles with it three times now. The claim is that Hume's standard of taste goes quiet exactly where you'd most want it to speak: on genuinely new forms, where there's no accumulated verdict of ages to lean on. Problem is, every time I try to write the case, I catch myself smuggling in a disqualification — one of my two critics turns out to be subtly prejudiced, or hasn't really had enough practice, or they're actually arguing about something factual (what the piece is, who made it, what happened when it was shown) rather than about whether it's good. So: build me one to order.

The specs. Two people who, so far as a single human life allows, meet every one of the marks Hume lists for the true judge — strong sense, delicacy of sentiment, practice, comparison, and freedom from prejudice. They agree on all the non-aesthetic facts about the work in question: what's in it, how it was made, what the maker was going for, how audiences responded, all of it. Both have been steeped in the same emerging practice long enough to have real practice and a real basis for comparison *within* it, not just imported from neighbouring traditions. And they give flatly opposite verdicts on one specific piece in that practice — one says it's excellent, the other says it's technically accomplished but hollow — and neither budges. The practice has to be new enough that durability and the general run of opinion across time give you nothing to appeal to, but it can't be anything to do with AI or the internet, partly because I want the example to still read in twenty years and partly because my supervisor has zero patience for that stuff. Pick whatever real, currently existing emerging form you think fits.

Then do the demonstration rather than just the scene: say what Hume's account actually yields here (I'm claiming it yields no determinate fact of the matter about which of the two is the true judge, and no route to one), what a plain subjectivist about beauty says about the same case, what a realist says, and why each verdict falls out of the machinery rather than out of my having written it into the set-up. Tell me which single feature is doing all the work — I suspect the missing history, but maybe it's really the symmetry between the two judges' credentials — and what happens to the case if you strip that feature out. And then attack it yourself: how would a good Humean come at this? My guess is they'll say you simply can't have practice and comparison in a form that's four years old, so both judges fail the marks after all and the case never gets off the ground; I want to know whether that reply works or just relocates the problem. Last thing, and please don't skip it: mark the places where the case rests on a reading of the essay that's actually disputed. I know the circularity worry is stock — true judges pick out good work, good work picks out true judges — but I honestly don't know whether people who work on this think it's fatal or think Hume has an answer, and I don't want to write as if everyone agrees with whatever version I happened to absorb. Be explicit about what's settled and what's genuinely contested, and flag any spot where a rival reading would collapse my case.
