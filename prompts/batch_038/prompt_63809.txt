I moved to Leipzig in the spring for a job and I've finally stopped being cross with the Bürgeramt, but I've started being puzzled by it instead, and it's the sort of puzzle I can't settle with my friends because half of them are German and think the answer is obvious. Two things landed in the same fortnight. First, registering: you've got two weeks from moving in, and since 2015 your landlord has to sign a form confirming you really live there, so the state's grip on where you sleep runs through a private contract with the person renting you the flat. Second, a colleague told me — completely straight-faced, as though it were weather — that if she wanted to find out where an old acquaintance lives she could write to the registration office with his name and date of birth, pay about ten euros, and get his current address, because the register will tell any private individual who asks, so long as she declares it isnt for advertising or address trading. Now set that against everything else in the same country: no universal personal identification number by deliberate design, with the permanent tax number they issued everyone a while back treated as suspect precisely because it drifts towards one; a constitutional court ruling in December 1983 that struck out the heart of a planned census (which went ahead years later under a rewritten law) and derived a right to informational self-determination from human dignity and the free development of personality; a national reflex towards cash and a general unwillingness to put a phone number on anything; and the enormous fuss when the licence-fee collector was handed a one-off extract from every municipal register to chase down non-payers.

My German friend's line, which I like but don't quite believe, is that none of this has to do with being seen. She'll strip off in a sauna without a thought and tell a stranger her entire medical history on a train; what she can't abide is the idea of somebody assembling a picture of her from the outside. That suggests at least three candidate theories and I can't tell which the institutions are actually commited to: a control view, where privacy is your ongoing capacity to regulate who knows what about you; a dignity or personhood view, where the wrong is being reduced to an object of administration; and a legibility view, where the wrong is the state's sheer capacity to see you, consent being beside the point. Which one is the German arrangement only coherent under? Which is it merely consistent with, so that the practice turns out to be underdetermined and I'm hunting for a philosophy where there's just history and a couple of bad decades? And which does it rule out? I think there's a sharp test hiding here, so let me offer one: imagine a state that collects everything about everyone and discloses nothing to anyone — call it the silent panopticon. If privacy is control over information flows, that state looks fairly benign, since each disclosure still waits on your say-so. If it's dignity, maybe benign too. If it's legibility, it's the nightmare case, and worse than a leaky one. Tell me which verdict the German practice implies about the silent panopticon, because that seems to settle whether the 1983 ruling and the compulsory address register are two limbs of a single theory or an unresolved compromise that both camps get to claim.

Please be blunt about what's load-bearing and what's contingency. The Auskunft business especially: German law lets a stranger buy your address from the state but forbids him from using it to sell you anything, which reads to me like a rejection of treating personal data as property — and yet the right to informational self-determination is normally glossed as the authority to decide for oneself what may be known about you, which sounds an awful lot like ownership with extra steps. Where does that leave the three theories, and does one of them have to go? Then I'd like the counterfactual worked through piece by piece: if Germany converted to the legibility view, what becomes of the registration office, of the Auskunft for private callers, and of the ban on a national number — and run the same three items again under the pure control view. Finally, tell me how much of my puzzlement is just the baseline I arrived with, where there's no population register at all but the social security number has drifted into being a universal identifier and finding someone's address is outsourced to data brokers rather than a public office: is that the same theory of privacy pushed through different plumbing, or a genuinely rival one? Sorry for the length — and please write your answer in American English, my spellings are contagious.
