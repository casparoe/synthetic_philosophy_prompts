Right, this is a bit of a mess so bear with me. I'm the outgoing chair of a grower co-op — 26 of us, small veg farms in a river valley, we share a packing barn and a cold store. The costs are basically fixed and get split by head, so the more members the cheaper it is per farm.

Last spring a supermarket buyer went round farm by farm with cash contracts, above what our box scheme paid, but exclusive: take it and you can't send volume through the co-op. Nine took it. The levy went up for everyone left, two more walked after that, we nearly lost the barn and ended up taking a loan against it to get through the season. Now the buyer's been moved on, those contracts are up, and most of the nine want back in for next spring. The vote is at the AGM in three weeks and the handover speech is mine — outgoing chair, last thing I get to say to the room.

Two things I need.

First, three or four sentences telling me what you think we were actually in last year. My brother in law teaches economics and he has told me at every family meal since that it's a prisoners dilemma and we were mugs for cooperating. But I sat down with the numbers over Christmas and I dont think that's right. If enough of us had stayed, staying beat every other option open to me — better than the supermarket contract, better than going it alone. And going it alone turned out worse for nearly everyone who did it, the nine included, once you count the year they've just had. It wasn't that leaving paid better. It was that each of us was pretty sure everyone else was leaving, and given that, leaving was the sensible thing to do. So: name what that situation is, say whether I've got it right or he has, and say what turns it into his version — because I think it flips, and I think we were nearer the flip than I realised at the time.

Then the speech. About 900 words, meant to be said out loud, no bullet points.

The room will be around forty: growers and their partners, the incoming chair who is 31 and does not want to inherit a feud, the bank's agricultural rep who is there about the loan, at least four of the nine sitting at the back, and my brother in law, who will catch anything sloppy. So the ideas have to be in there properly but none of the words can be — no prisoners dilemma, no game theory, no free riders, no tragedy of the commons, no nash anything. If he wants proof I know what I'm doing, one line will do it, and it has to be a true line rather than a decorative one.

What the speech has to manage:

- It can't pretend last spring didn't happen and it can't shame the nine. Two of them have already said they'll walk for good if they're made to feel like the accused, and we need their volume to make the levy work for everybody, them included.
- It has to put actual readmission terms on the table, and the terms themselves have to be the argument: open to anyone who wants back, but the arrears and their share of the loan interest get settled first; nobody kept out for good; and all of it written down beforehand so nobody has to guess what triggers what.
- It has to cope with the fact that a bad year isn't a betrayal. A flooded field or a chiller that dies in July looks exactly like somebody walking out, from the outside, and if our rules treat those the same we'll be at each other's throats by August. So the terms need some way of telling couldn't from wouldn't.
- And it should answer my brother in law's best point without addressing him: he says any arrangement like ours unravels in the end, because sooner or later everyone knows it's the last season and then nobody has a reason to keep it going. I think the barn and the loan answer that, and I think nobody ever actually knows which season is the last one — but I want that felt by the growers, not followed by them.
- I want to finish by saying what I'm signing and when, and being first on the list, and the list has a number on it: we don't open the barn for next season until that many have signed, and if we don't hit it, everyone finds out early enough to do something else instead. That's the bit I think actually saves us. Make it land as practical, not clever.

Voice: I'm 64, I've farmed this valley my whole life, I don't do sentiment and I'm not thanking anybody for their journey. One joke at most. Plainspoken, a bit dry. It should sound like something said standing up in a barn with a bad microphone, not something read at a lectern.
