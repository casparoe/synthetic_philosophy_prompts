I'm in an interdisciplinary reading group — two economists, a philosopher of science, and me (I do ML) — that spent last month on Keynes on probability: the weight-of-argument material in the Treatise, plus the long-term expectation chapter in the General Theory. Last session two of the regulars, Marta and Owen, went at it and it never got resolved. Writing it up as best I can from memory. Neither of them thinks the other is stupid; they each think they're arguing about something the other isn't hearing, and I suspect that's right but I can't show it.

Some background so the moves make sense. Marta's line all month has been that there are two readings of the chapter and that everybody slides between them without noticing. Reading 1: investors are irrational — they systematically underweight vague-but-decisively-relevant evidence and overweight the confident near-term facts, and that's a cognitive failure you could in principle shrink with better disclosure, longer track records, longer horizons. Reading 2: there is no fact of the matter about a ten-year prospect at all, so conventional valuation isn't a failure to track anything, it's a stand-in for knowledge that doesn't exist, and it moves in jumps that aren't caused by information. She says the policy stakes are completely different — under Reading 1 you fix information, under Reading 2 you fix institutions — and that most self-described Keynesians don't know which one they're holding.

Roughly how it went:

(1) Owen said Reading 2 is unfalsifiable and so does no work. His argument: whenever a valuation model goes badly wrong you can say afterwards "that prospect was never comparable to begin with," and whenever it goes right you can say the convention happened to hold. No observation separates "the probabilities don't exist" from "the probabilities exist and we're bad at getting them," so Reading 2 collapses into Reading 1 plus a humility disclaimer. He said this is a general trouble with the whole Knightian-uncertainty family, not specific to Keynes.

(2) Marta said that's wrong, because the two readings predict different *time profiles* of error. Reading 1 predicts error that decays with information and experience — more data, longer histories, more analysts, gradually better forecasts. Reading 2 predicts the reverse: valuation gets dominated by whichever facts are nearest and most confident, and then shifts discontinuously when the convention shifts, with the shifts not corellated with anything you'd recognize as news. She said that's an observable difference and you can go look, and the burden is on Owen to say why the pattern we see looks like Reading 2.

(3) Owen said fine, grant the empirical difference, it still doesn't get you Reading 2, because costly-information bounded rationality reproduces the same profile: people use the near confident facts because they're cheap, not because the distant ones lack a truth value. So the diagnosis survives and the metaphysics is surplus. He said the cheapest story is always the one where the world has probabilities and the agents are cheap.

(4) Marta quoted the line near the top of the chapter, "It would be foolish, in forming our expectations, to attach great weight to matters which are very uncertain," and pointed out he goes straight on to say it's reasonable to be guided by the facts you feel confident about even when they're less decisively relevant than the vague ones. Her claim: that's Keynes *endorsing* the discounting, not diagnosing it. On Reading 1 he'd have to be calling a bias reasonable in one breath and catastrophic in the next, which she said is incoherent. Owen said "foolish" there is indexed to the agent's own epistemic situation, not to whether an answer exists — a man who can't estimate a copper mine's yield ten years out is right not to try, and that's fully compatible with there being a true answer he can't reach.

(5) Owen went after weight of argument itself. He said the Treatise makes it a balance between "the absolute amounts of relevant knowledge and of relevant ignorance," which is just a precision or confidence measure that decision theory has since recaptured — sets of priors, second-order probabilities, ambiguity aversion — so it isn't a second dimension of probability in any interesting sense, it's redundancy with sample size. Marta said whether the recapture is legitimate is the whole question, and that it cheats: all of those frameworks restore comparability at the end, because once you have a set of priors and a rule for it you can rank any two ambiguous options. Keynes's claim is that for some pairs the comparison isn't imprecise, it's absent. If your model ranks everything, she said, you've assumed away the thesis you were modeling.

(6) Owen's closer was that Keynes contradicts himself here, and he brought in the animal spirits passage and the maximin-style reconstructions people offer. If prospects are genuinely non-comparable, then any rule for acting anyway — maximin, minimax regret, whatever — requires at least an ordering of badness across states, and producing that ordering is producing the comparability Keynes denied. So the reconstruction buys the practical advice by selling the philosophy. Marta conceded this was the strongest thing he said and answered that acting under non-comparability is exactly what the animal spirits passage is describing, a spontaneous urge to act rather than a weighted calculation, so the resolution isn't a decision rule at all, and demanding one is just staying inside Reading 1. Owen said that's not an answer but a confession: it means the account can't tell an entrepreneur what to do, only describe what entrepreneurs do, which makes it psychology rather than decision theory.

Then somebody — I think Marta, might have been Owen — said the beauty contest example doesn't support either reading, because that one is a game about guessing what average opinion guesses average opinion will pick, which is strategic interaction with a perfectly well-defined payoff structure, i.e. the opposite of fundamental uncertainty. The other said that's a hit on the popular gloss of Keynes and not on Keynes. They never agreed on who said what and the thing broke up.

Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step? Not just who wins overall, though I do want that verdict at the end too. The four I most care about: (a) does Marta's time-profile argument in (2) do what she says it does, or does (3) neutralize it; (b) is the "foolish" sentence evidence for or against Reading 2, and can you say something better than either of them managed about what "reasonable" is doing in that passage; (c) has weight of argument actually been recaptured by ambiguity frameworks, or is Marta right that the recapture smuggles comparability back in; (d) is (6) fatal, or is "animal spirits isn't a decision rule" a legitimate place to stop. And tell me what they were both missing — I have a nagging feeling the whole thing turns on something neither of them said out loud, maybe whether "there's no fact of the matter" and "we can't get to the fact" are rival claims or the same claim wearing two hats, but I cant pin it down.
