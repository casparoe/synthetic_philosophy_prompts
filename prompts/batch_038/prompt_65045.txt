I'm the field coordinator for a volunteer search-and-rescue team in a rural county — fifty-some of us, mostly people with day jobs and no statistics past high school, and we run fifty to seventy callouts a year. We keep records going back twelve years, 714 callouts total, and here's the thing that eats up the first hour of almost every serious search: whatever number anyone pulls out of that spreadsheet depends entirely on which past cases you decide the current one belongs with. Last November we had a 74-year-old man with dementia who wandered off from a care home, and the family didn't call it in until nine hours after he was last seen. One of my team leaders said, out loud at the briefing table, that we find 88 percent of our subjects and 64 percent of the ones we find are alive, so let's plan for a live find. Another said that's lazy, that if you filter to people over 70 it drops to 38 percent alive, and if you filter to over-70s where the initial report mentions dementia or confusion it's 8 alive out of 41. A third said both of those are too small a slice to mean anything and we should use the "reported more than twelve hours late" group instead, which is 41 percent alive across 203 cases. All three numbers came out of the same file and all three were defensible, and we argued for an hour while it got dark. I finally googled the situation and found the phrase "reference class problem," plus something called reference class forecasting that a friend of mine who does construction budgets swears by, and I got about two pages into a philosophy paper before giving up on it. So I'm coming at you from the ground, not the library.

Here's what we actually have, so you can see how messy the raw material is. Twelve years, 714 callouts, 631 subjects located, 402 of those alive when found. Reported to us within two hours of the last known point: 118 cases, 89 percent alive at location. Reported more than twelve hours later: 203 cases, 41 percent. Subjects over 70: 96 cases, 38 percent alive. Over 70 with dementia or confusion noted in the initial report: 41 cases, 34 located, 8 alive. Over 70 with dementia noted and reported more than six hours late: 17 cases, 13 located, 2 alive. Under 25: 149 cases, 77 percent alive. Under 25 where the initial report mentions a note, a goodbye text, or suicidal language: 23 cases, 14 located, 2 alive. Overdue recreationists, meaning someone was expecting them home and reported them late: 121 cases, 88 percent alive. Overdue and reported more than eight hours past the expected return time: 31 cases, 61 percent. Overdue with a cell phone we couldn't reach: 14 cases, 4 located, 1 alive. Now the caveats, which are the part I don't know what to do with. Our "time reported" field before 2016 is when dispatch passed us the call, not when the family realized something was wrong, so those early numbers are systematically off by an unknown amount. "Dementia" wasn't a checkbox until 2013 and I coded the earlier ones myself by reading the narrative write-ups. Some subjects get found by a passerby or a relative half a mile outside our assigned perimeter and we still log them as found by us, which probably flatters every category. We got drones in 2019 and dedicated K9 teams in 2017, so a 2011 case arguably isn't the same kind of event as a 2024 case at all. And there's pressure from two directions: the county board wants one clean number in every after-action report, and a bigger organization that operates in the next county over advertises "nine out of ten missing people come home alive" in its fundraising, which our volunteers have read and believe.

What I want is a procedure, not an essay about how hard the problem is — something a tired team member can run in fifteen minutes on the tailgate with a laptop, that tells them in order what to ask, what counts as a good enough answer at each step, how to decide whether to narrow the group or widen it, what the minimum number of past cases is before a slice is allowed to override a bigger one, what to do when two legitimate slices disagree, when to hand the family a range instead of a point and when to refuse to give a number at all, and where the procedure has to stop and hand the call to a human being because it's a question of values rather than of our records. I also want you to be straight with me about which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedure as a costume, and about the ways this will misfire on the kinds of data quality problems I just described, since I'd rather know than be surprised. Then run it, showing your work, on three cases. One: the 74-year-old with dementia, nine hours late, from last November, where we have 17 past cases that seem to match exactly and two of them were alive. Two: a 19-year-old who texted a friend something like "I'm done" and walked into the national forest in July, reported thirty hours later, where the under-25 slice says 77 percent and the goodbye-message slice says 2 alive out of 23. Three: a 34-year-old hunter who told his brother he'd be back by dark Saturday and didn't come back, reported Sunday afternoon, healthy, carrying a phone we can't reach — the overdue-recreationist slice says 88 percent, but the slice for overdue subjects with an unreachable phone has 14 cases and one survivor, and I have a bad feeling about where this one lands and I'd rather hear it from you now than at the debrief. Please don't soften it if the honest output is that we should tell a brother his chances are grim before we commit forty volunteers for three days. One formatting thing: I know a procedure normally wants numbered steps and boxes and arrows, but I'm going to print this and stick it in a binder where it'll get rained on, and I want it to read like a long letter from one person to another, so no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables — just continuous prose where the order comes through in the sentences themselves, first this, then that, stop if such-and-such.
