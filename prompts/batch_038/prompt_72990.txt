This autumn my paper's reader festival has given me a two-hour slot, and I need to fill it with something that is not a talk. I cover science and ideas for a national newspaper; the room is a hired hall with six round tables, twenty-four people, adults of every age, none of them trained in philosophy, several of them the sort who read my column on consciousness and write in to say I have got it wrong. The subject is material constitution — the statue and the clay — and I want it run as a game with real, if trivial, stakes.

The outcome I am after is specific. I do not want people leaving saying that identity is puzzling; they will say that about anything. I want them to discover by their own arithmetic that they have been applying two different criteria of sameness all along without noticing, that the criteria conflict, and that any single rule they can write down in advance either delivers a verdict they are unwilling to accept or can be turned to profit by a table that has written down a different one. If, at the end of two hours, a table cannot state a rule that survives the sequence I have put it through, the workshop has worked.

Here is the shape I have in mind, which you are welcome to improve on or replace outright:

- Each table is a small workshop taking a commission, not an anonymous group with a lump of matter. The object should have a client, a name and a deadline, so that whether it is still the same one is a question somebody has an interest in.
- Before play, each table writes its own criterion of sameness in a sentence or two, on a card, in its own words. That card is then fixed, and every subsequent decision is scored against it.
- Stakes must be real and immediately payable. I intend to bring a tin of 50p pieces; every claim a table settles should cost or earn somebody something on the table, not on paper.
- The event script should run through at least gradual replacement of parts; destruction and remaking from the same matter; division of one lump into two competing claimants; and the fusion of two into one. I should also like at least one event I cannot script in advance, arising from how modelling clay actually behaves in a warm hall over two hours — drying, shedding crumbs, taking up moisture and skin — and I want that unpredictability built into the scoring rather than smoothed away.
- Kitchen scales on every table. If they have to weigh the matter to settle a claim, they will meet the question of which crumbs count with a scale in their hands rather than in a sentence from me.

What I need back: a complete materials list with quantities for twenty-four people; a minute-by-minute timeline; the full event script, including what the facilitator says and does at each transformation and which events must be run with the whole room watching rather than table by table; the betting and scoring mechanics worked through with concrete numbers, so that I can check the arithmetic on my own tables beforehand; a debrief that maps the rules each table improvised onto the views they turn out to have reinvented, and does that mapping only at that point, since I know these positions have names and I want nobody to hear one of them before the last twenty minutes; a list of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it, with a fix for each, including what I do about the participant who announces in the third minute that there are no statues, only matter arranged statue-wise, and then declines to bet; and a candid note on the feature of the dispute the game cannot reproduce.

On that last point I have a particular worry and I should like you to face it directly. A dispute settled by who can make money from it is not obviously the metaphysician's dispute, and I can see a route by which the whole afternoon collapses into a seminar on contract law and small print. Tell me what I have to supply in words to stop that happening, and where the game's incentives actively mislead.

Two further requests. I shall be writing the workshop up, so I need one moment, late in the session, that lands with the entire room at once — twenty-four separate mild discoveries are of no use to me as a writer and of no use to them as participants. And please keep the Ship of Theseus out of it. They have all met that one, and if it appears in the first ten minutes they will stop thinking and start reciting. No screens, no handouts that give anything away before the debrief, and nothing that depends on my being able to lecture.
