I'm prepping a session on von Neumann's *The Computer and the Brain* for a grad philosophy-of-mind seminar, and I want to rank his main claims about where nervous systems differ from our machines by how well they've aged. My paraphrase of the shortlist: (1) individual neurons are slower and sloppier than the components of a good electronic machine, so the nervous system has to buy its performance with parallelism rather than serial speed; (2) that sloppiness forces a statistical code -- meaning attaches to rates and aggregates of pulses rather than to single ones -- plus alot of redundancy; (3) much of the system's memory is slower to reach than artificial memory by a wide margin, and its physical embodiment may be chemical rather than electrical; (4) the brain/computer analogy is shallower than popular talk suggests, and the honest way to compare the two is through a general theory of automata rather than by structural analogy; (5) the nervous system mixes digital and analog procedure in ways the machines of his day basically didn't.

Rank them best-aged to worst-aged, one line of justification each, grounded in what we know now -- synaptic reliability, myelination, population codes, the metabolic budget, hippocampal versus cortical storage, deep nets -- and tell me which one you think he got most wrong. Then the bit I actually care about: give each a second number, 0 to 1, for how much your ranking would move if the thing being compared against were a modern GPU cluster running a large trained neural net instead of a vacuum-tube machine, and flag any thesis that only looks bad relative to the older hardware.
