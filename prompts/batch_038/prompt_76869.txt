You are a moral philosopher with roughly fifteen years in normative ethics and metaethics who has also served on grant panels and knows what the "research agenda" section of a proposal has to do to survive skeptical readers. Please think carefully before answering: I want your considered judgment about what deserves a group's effort, not a survey of the terrain and not a neutral summary of both sides.

**The situation.** A private foundation will fund exactly one five-year project on the debate between moral particularism and moral generalism: one principal investigator and about three postdoctoral researchers, no laboratory, no meaningful data-collection budget. Its board is skeptical. They have been told that this is a decades-long stalemate in which each side restates its intuitions about whether a principle can be both informative and non-trivial, and that the debate produces position papers rather than results. They will fund it only if the proposal names questions on which a small group of philosophers could plausibly make real progress inside five years, and says what would change downstream if those questions were answered. I am writing that agenda section, and I need your prioritization.

**A framing hypothesis of mine that I want you to test.** My working suspicion is that much of the stalemate is an artifact of layer-conflation: that participants slide back and forth between (i) the semantic and metaphysical layer — whether a claim about a reason must be underwritten by a true generalization, and whether the holism of reasons, i.e. the possibility of a feature flipping valence from case to case, is a thesis about reasons themselves or about the properties that bear them; (ii) the deliberative layer — whether an agent can reason well without operating with anything like a codified principle, and what the particularist's mature judge uses in place of the "crutches" she has supposedly outgrown; and (iii) the psychological and pedagogical layer — whether moral learning, moral testimony, and the very idea of moral expertise require the transmission of generalizations. Concede as much or as little of this diagnosis as you think is right. If you reject it, say why, and rank on some other basis; if you accept it, tell me whether a five-year project should attack one layer in isolation or deliberately build bridges, and what the cost of each choice is.

**The candidate questions.** I have assembled the following list. You may add items I have missed, drop items, or argue that an item is malformed and needs to be repaired before it can be ranked at all — in which case repair it and rank the repaired version.

1. Whether the disagreement is substantive once the notion of a principle is pinned down. Much of the air seems to leave of the debate when one distinguishes absolute principles (all acts of type T are overall wrong), contributory principles (that an act is T counts against it, leaving the overall verdict to further weighing), and merely defeasible generalizations with explicit "in the absence of defeaters" clauses. Is there a version of generalism that the particularist has not already granted, and a version of particularism that the generalist should not reject?
2. The "that is not a principle, that is just a list" objection and its converse: whether adding enabling and disabling conditions to a generalization makes it informative, or makes it a restatement of the case-by-case verdicts it was supposed to explain. What is the right notion of informativeness here, and is there a general account of it?
3. The grounding of holism. If a feature's valence is genuinely context-sensitive, what makes it so? Is holism a thesis about reasons, about the properties that bear them, or about the shape of the facts that obtain? Can holism be secured without positing contrastive or conditional reason-facts whose own status is more puzzling than the phenomenon they were invoked to explain?
4. Default reasons. Can a particularist admit that some features count a certain way unless something defeats them, without thereby admitting principles in anything but name? What explanatory work do defaults do that a corresponding defeasible generalization could not do?
5. The deliberative layer: what a skilled moral judge is actually doing. Does the particularist owe a positive account of perceptual or quasi-perceptual discrimination that does not smuggle in generalizations at the level of the skill itself, or does the demand for such an account already concede the generalist's point?
6. Moral testimony and expertise. If there are no codifiable principles, in what sense can there be experts, and what exactly can an expert transmit to a novice — dispositions, cases, a vocabulary, something else?
7. Portability. Does the particularist's case, built largely on paradigm features such as promise-breaking and cruelty, survive transport into domains where the background conditions are unstable and the enabling conditions are not yet understood — novel technologies, allocation under severe scarcity, sustained cross-cultural disagreement? Does holism get stronger or weaker as the reliability of background conditions changes?
8. Borrowed machinery. Whether the debate can be sharpened by importing tools from nonmonotonic and defeasible reasoning, or from the treatment of hedged and exception-filled generalizations in the philosophy of science — and whether that borrowing is a genuine shortcut or a false analogy, given that laws and reasons may differ in exactly the ways that matter.
9. The status of thick concepts and of moral perception as the particularist's positive resource: whether they can bear the weight placed on them, or whether they are best understood as a restatement of the generalist's own appeal to judgment.

**What I want from you.**

- Begin with a clearly labeled brainstorming section. Work through the candidates and the dependencies between them before you commit to an ordering — which questions cannot be answered until some other question is settled, which are independent, which are downstream and therefore cheap to leave unresolved, and which would collapse into one another if pursued seriously. This section may be unpolished; it should be visible work rather than conclusions.
- Then give a ranked agenda: the three questions the project should actually pursue, in order, with the remaining candidates explicitly placed below the line. For each of the three, explain what makes it worth five years rather than merely unresolved.
- Apply and defend your criteria. I care about four: how upstream a question is (how many other candidates' answers depend on it); tractability for a small group of philosophers working without new empirical data and without waiting on an adjacent debate to be settled; neglectedness relative to how much has already been written on it; and downstream payoff, meaning whether an answer would change anything in how ethics is practiced, taught, or applied, or would merely relabel the positions. State how you are weighting these against one another and why, and note where the weights are in tension — in particular, where the most upstream question is also the least tractable, and how you are resolving that trade-off.
- Identify the single crux: the one question on this list such that the project's entire design would flip depending on how it is answered. Explain what the two possible answers are and what each would commit the group to.
- Name one or two candidates you would refuse to fund, and say why they are overrated, pseudo-questions, or already settled in all but name. Do not hedge here; a proposal with no exclusions looks like a proposal without judgment.
- For each of the three funded questions, sketch what a good result looks like at the end of year five — concrete enough that a hostile reader could check whether it had been achieved — and what failure looks like. Include at least one milestone inside the first eighteen months that would tell the group early whether it is on the wrong question.
- Close with a short paragraph on what would change your ranking over the next eighteen months: the kinds of result, argument, or external development that would move a question up or down, and which of your three you would be most willing to abandon.

**Constraints on the response.** Aim for roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words. Do not produce a bibliography, a reading list, citations, titles, dates, or page references — the readers have no library access and I am not asking for one. Where a name is genuinely the shortest way to fix a position, you may use it, but never as a substitute for describing the position: state in your own words what the view says and what would count against it. Resist ranking by fame of the question; the board has already read the famous versions of this debate and found them unconvincing. If your honest judgment is that one of my three favored framings should be demoted in favor of something I did not list, say so plainly and argue for it.
