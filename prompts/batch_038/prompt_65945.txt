I have taught philosophy of mind to undergraduates for nineteen years, and there is one week that has never worked. The week is on panpsychism. The opening thesis is not the difficulty: my students find the claim that experience is fundamental and ubiquitous merely strange, which is a productive condition. The difficulty is the objection that is supposed to dispose of the thesis. I can set it out on the board, and by the end of the hour they reproduce it accurately and even score the standard replies. What they cannot do is feel it. They file it as a technicality about composition — a problem about parts and wholes, they say, which a better account of composition would dissolve — and they leave the room untouched. They have read James's version of the objection in *The Principles of Psychology*, the one with the hundred men who each know a single sentence and no further mind that knows all hundred, and they found it quaint.

I have decided to hand them a story instead of a fourth paper. Please write it for me.

The setting is mine; the execution is yours. The story takes place in a jurisdiction where a licensed procedure is routine: for the duration of a shift, the experiential lives of a consenting crew are pooled, so that twelve people operating a machine too large and too fast for twelve separate attentions act as a single field of awareness, with one stream of reportable experience. The procedure — call it consociation, and give the institution its own unexplained vocabulary for the stages of a shift rather than glossing them — is well regulated, unremarkable, and popular with employers. The story begins where it stops being unremarkable. During an eleven-hour shift one member of the crew is killed, and the pool goes on working for six minutes before it is unbound. There is now an inquiry. The company has filed a single deposition on behalf of the shift; the union insists there are eleven surviving witnesses and one dead one; the dead man's family insists there were thirteen participants in that work, and that the thirteenth has never been accounted for. And into the pooled account of those six minutes there has entered a detail — one is enough, and it must be sensory rather than propositional — that no member of the crew could have supplied: a vantage no body occupied, or a quality none of them had before the shift and which at least one of them has not lost since.

The story must carry the following, and must carry them as plot, paperwork, and testimony rather than as speech:

- the difference between a subject that *has* parts and a subject that is *composed of* other subjects, with at least two characters reasoning from opposite sides of that difference and both of them vindicated by the same evidence;
- the question of what became of the twelve during the fusion — annihilated, absorbed, or present throughout with a share of a single experience — which the dead man's case makes unbearably concrete;
- the requirement that the pooled experience contain something that cannot be assembled out of what the members bring to it, delivered as the one detail described above and never argued for;
- the difficulty running the other way: that a perspective genuinely unified cannot obviously be taken apart again, so that a clean unbinding is itself evidence, and evidence that reads two ways;
- the fact that the architecture of the pooled experience tracked the arrangement of the bodies rather than any single brain, which should be legible in the very form of the deposition.

Constraints. No character is a philosopher, and nobody discusses consciousness in a theoretical register; the phrase you and I would use for this family of problems must not appear anywhere in the piece. Include one short specimen of institutional writing — a clause of the operating licence, or the inquiry's own finding — in flat administrative prose that is doing metaphysical work whether or not it means to. End with the institution deciding something it cannot postpone, on evidence that equally supports the reading that someone was there for the whole of that shift and the reading that no one was ever there. Two thousand to three thousand words.

After the story, add a note of no more than two hundred and fifty words: where each of the five requirements is carried, which of them you had to compromise and why, and the single sentence you would expect a student who has never heard of panpsychism to be able to write after reading it.
