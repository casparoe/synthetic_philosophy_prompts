I am convening a graduate seminar in the philosophy of quantum mechanics and am setting the final examination paper. The paper contains four questions worth 25 marks each, candidates have fifty minutes per question, and they may use neither notes nor a calculator, so every numerical result must come out exact. I would like you to draft one of the four questions, on Bell's theorem and locality, together with a model answer and a marking scheme.

My difficulty is that any question posed in the usual way — explain the theorem and discuss whether it establishes nonlocality — is answered from memory, in the vocabulary of local realism, and marked on fluency rather than understanding. I want the candidates to have to work on a specified model rather than on a slogan. Please therefore build the question around the following toy model, which should appear, fully specified, in the question stem.

Model M. A bipartite experiment in which Alice and Bob each choose a measurement direction in a common plane, Alice from {0°, 90°} and Bob from {45°, 135°}, and each obtain an outcome in {+1, −1}. There is a single hidden variable, λ, which takes one value λ₀ with probability one and is therefore independent of both settings. Conditional on λ₀, for any pair of settings a and b, the probability that the two outcomes agree is (1 − cos(a − b))/2, and the four joint probabilities divide this evenly between the two agreeing pairs and its complement evenly between the two disagreeing pairs.

The question should have five parts, marked 5, 5, 4, 6 and 5:

(a) Derive the expectation value E(A,B|a,b) in Model M, evaluate the CHSH combination S = E(a,b) − E(a,b′) + E(a′,b) + E(a′,b′), and state the bound that any factorizable model must obey.

(b) Decide, from the probability specification alone, whether Model M satisfies parameter independence and whether it satisfies outcome independence.

(c) Decide whether Model M permits superluminal signalling, and explain what the answer to (b) does and does not settle about this.

(d) Evaluate the claim that Model M refutes Bell's theorem, because it reproduces the quantum correlations while remaining local. This part must force candidates to say which notion of locality is in play — no-signalling, parameter independence, or Bell's own criterion of local causality — and to reach a verdict.

(e) Explain what changes if the model is required to be deterministic, with each outcome a fixed function of the local setting and λ, and why Model M's irreducibly stochastic character is doing essential work.

Please deliver three items. First, the question exactly as it should appear on the paper, including the specification of Model M, the five parts, and the marks attached to each; it must be self-contained, so state in the stem any inequality the candidates are meant to appeal to. Second, a model answer of about 900 words, which performs the calculations explicitly and, in the discursive parts, commits to a definite conclusion rather than balancing the considerations. Third, a marking scheme that allocates the 25 marks across the parts, names within each part the specific moves a first-class answer must make, lists at least six identifiable errors that should cost marks — each with a one-line diagnosis of the confusion it reveals and the deduction it carries — and states briefly what separates a first-class answer from a competent second-class one.

Two further constraints. Nothing you produce should ask candidates for references, or supply a bibliography or reading list. And there is one point I want the question to leave unstated, so that only the strongest candidates will find it: because λ₀ carries no information, Model M's observable probabilities coincide with those of orthodox quantum mechanics applied to the singlet state with the quantum state treated as a complete description. The marking scheme should award marks for noticing this and for drawing out what it implies for part (d), but the question itself must not give it away.
