The question we set was this:

"Consider a world W. The complete history of W consists of 4,000 identical particles in a box observed over 100 ticks of a clock; for each particle and each tick, the history records only whether that particle was up or down. Exactly 2,000 particles are up at tick 1. Three candidate theories of W are proposed, all of them true of W and all deductively closed.
A: one axiom — every particle that is up at tick n is down at tick n+1 and vice versa — together with a statement of the configuration at tick 1. The entire history is derivable.
B: an exhaustive list of the state of every particle at every tick, with no generalisation.
C: 'At every tick, between 1,900 and 2,100 particles are up.'
(i) On a best-system account of laws, which of A, B and C states the laws of W, and why?
(ii) Now introduce the predicate UP*, defined by exhaustive enumeration: UP*(x,n) holds just in case particle x is recorded up at tick n in the actual history of W. Theory A* is the single sentence 'For every particle x and tick n, x is up at n iff UP*(x,n)', from which the whole history follows. A* is shorter than A and just as strong. Does this show that which theory is best, and hence what the laws are, depends on the notation we choose? What should the best-system theorist say?
(iii) How would a theorist who takes laws to be relations of necessitation between universals treat W differently, and what does their account gain or lose?"

The student's answer (600 words allowed; this is about 480):

"A best-system account says the laws are those generalisations that appear as axioms or theorems of the true deductive system that best balances simplicity and strength. Strength is how much a system says about the history; simplicity is how compactly it says it. In W, B is maximally strong but not simple at all, since it is the history written out longhand. C is simple but weak, since it is compatible with enormous numbers of different histories. A is both simple and strong, so A states the laws. This fits the Humean picture: there are no necessary connections in W, the laws are nothing over and above the pattern of particular matters of fact, and the direction of determination runs from the history to the laws rather than the other way round.

(ii) A* shows something important. It is one sentence long and it entails the whole history, so it beats A on both counts and its generalisation becomes the law. But A* is a cheat: UP* is defined by listing the very facts it is supposed to explain. The problem is that once definitions like that are allowed you can make any theory as short as you like, so the simplicity ranking is a fact about our vocabulary and not about W. Lewis accepts this, and holds that the tie is broken by pragmatic considerations about which vocabulary suits our interests. So on this view laws are partly conventional, and the Humean has to give up the idea that there is a single objective fact about what the laws of W are.

(iii) A necessitation theorist says the regularity in A is not enough: what makes it a law is that a second-order relation of natural necessitation holds between the universal being up and the universal being down at the next tick. This relation is contingent rather than logically necessary, but because it is a genuine relation in the world and not a summary of it, Armstrong's laws can support counterfactuals and underwrite induction in a way Lewis's regularities cannot. W therefore has a real governing law, and the Humean's laws are left merely descriptive.

The necessitation theorist also avoids a problem the Humean has with explanation. Since on the Humean view the laws supervene on the whole history, the laws cannot explain any part of that history, as that would be circular. Humeans accept this and say laws only systematise, which is why the charge that Humean laws do not govern does not worry them."
