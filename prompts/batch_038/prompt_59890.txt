I am trying to establish whether the pattern of my own moral verdicts can be captured by any single principle of the Scanlonian kind — one on which an act is wrong just in case it would be disallowed by principles that no one could reasonably reject, where the comparison between candidate principles is made pairwise, person by person, and no one's complaint is ever summed with anyone else's. I accept that framework provisionally, and I want to keep it: if the only way to save my verdicts is to concede that the framework must smuggle in an aggregative step — a summing of complaints, or an impersonal comparison of totals — I would rather be told so plainly, and told what remains of the pairwise picture afterward. What I cannot see is how the framework is to accommodate what I actually believe about cases in which many people stand to suffer a little and one person stands to suffer a great deal. The cases follow, with my verdicts. Please treat the verdicts as data.

1. Drainage. A city may either complete a drainage works, which will certainly kill one worker in a shaft collapse, or abandon it, which will leave ten thousand commuters with a mild headache each for the better part of an afternoon. I judge: abandon the works. The headaches do not accumulate into anything that competes with a death.

2. Paralysis. The same works, except that abandonment will leave ten thousand people paraplegic rather than merely headachy. I judge: complete the works. Here the many do defeat the one.

3. Hands. Abandonment will leave ten thousand people each permanently deprived of the use of one hand. I judge: abandon the works. This is the verdict I hold least securely, because the difference between it and case 2 strikes me as one of degree and not of kind.

4. Eyes. A driver can be spared total blindness only by diverting a vehicle in a way that leaves two other people each blind in one eye. I judge: divert. Two half-blindnesses defeat one full blindness, although each is substantially less bad for the person who suffers it.

Cases 3 and 4 are the pair I most want resolved. If two complaints, each markedly weaker than a single complaint, can together defeat it, I cannot see why ten thousand markedly weaker complaints cannot; and if they can, case 1 collapses, which I am unwilling to accept.

5. Monastery. A road will either flood the only access to a monastery, ending a four-hundred-year communal way of life for the twelve monks who remain, or take a working farm from a family of four, who will be healthy, together, and comfortably employed in a town twenty miles away. I judge the monks' complaint the stronger, though on any ordinary reckoning of wellbeing the family loses considerably more. I do not think the monks' complaint is stronger because it is more intense in some measurable sense; I think it is stronger because the principle permitting the road would license the destruction of something for which there is no substitute.

6. Risk. A factory may be sited so that one inspector certainly dies, or so that ten million people each incur a one-in-ten-million chance of dying. I judge the second permissible, on the ground that none of the ten million has a complaint in advance: each would accept that risk. Yet I also judge it impermissible to impose on those same ten million a certain harm of identical expected magnitude — say, a momentary sting to each. The risk behaves differently, for me, from the harm it is the risk of, and I cannot say why.

7. Statistical victims. I think it acceptable to open a stretch of highway that will, on reliable statistics, kill three drivers over fifty years, and unacceptable to commission a structure I know will crush three particular maintenance workers on the day it opens. I cannot articulate the difference beyond the fact that in the second case I can point to the people.

8. Sacrilege. A broadcast will cause a million people a distress they describe as sacrilege — no injury to health, property, or prospects, but a genuine and deeply felt wrong — while suppressing it will cost one technician a month's wages and a broken engagement. I judge the sacrilege a real complaint, admissible in the pairwise comparison rather than excluded at the threshold; and I also judge that it cannot, however many feel it, defeat the technician's loss. I want these complaints inside the game and incapable of winning it, which I suspect is not a coherent pair of attitudes.

My own attempt at a principle is a banding rule: a complaint counts against a principle only if its severity falls within some fixed proportion — call it a factor of two — of the strongest complaint generated by the alternative; within a band, complaints aggregate across persons; across bands, they do not. It appears to handle cases 1 through 4, since I can set the factor so that two half-blindnesses fall in the same band as one full blindness while ten thousand lost hands fall outside the band containing one death. It fails everywhere else, and the factor itself seems unmotivated: any fixed proportion faces a case straddling it, so the rule is either arbitrary or requires a principled way of setting the band, which I do not have.

What I would like from you:

(a) The principle or principles stated precisely enough to be applied — a decision procedure rather than a slogan — with each clause tied to the case that motivates it.

(b) An account of whether this is one principle or two, a severity rule plus a separate rule governing cases like 5 and 8; and if two, what fixes the boundary between their domains, since I suspect the whole difficulty lives on that boundary.

(c) Its verdicts on cases 3 and 4 taken together, explicitly, together with its treatment of the intermediates I have not listed: two hundred lost hands against one life, two thousand, twenty thousand. If there is a point at which I am supposed to change my mind, I want it identified; if there is none, I want to be told that my verdicts commit me to a discontinuity and what kind.

(d) A candid list of the verdicts above that your principle cannot accommodate, and, wherever it requires me to surrender one, an argument as to which surrender is costlier. I would rather be told that case 3 must go than be handed a principle that quietly explains it away.

(e) Its implications for at least two further cases of your own choosing, selected to probe wherever the principle is least stable.

I am not after a survey of positions in the debate or a history of who has argued what. I want the principle and the reasoning that supports it, argued from the cases.
