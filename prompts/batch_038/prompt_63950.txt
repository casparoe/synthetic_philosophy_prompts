I've been reading the cognitive and evolutionary science of religion as an interested outsider for about fifteen years, almost entirely through the rationalist blog-and-forum ecosystem — which means I've absorbed the arguments secondhand and in a fairly adversarial setting, and I've never shaken the suspicion that both sides of the byproduct-versus-adaptation debate have learned to survive any evidence whatsoever. I'm writing a long piece for that audience, where every move gets nitpicked, and I want to test the suspicion by commissioning a dialogue rather than an essay.

The two positions, as I understand them. On one side: religious belief and practice constitute a cultural adaptation — costly ritual, doctrinal conformity, and beliefs in morally concerned supernatural punishers were retained by group-level selection because they made coalitions more internally cooperative, demographically robust, and better at outlasting and out-absorbing their neighbors. On the other side: religion is a byproduct — the accidental convergence of independently adaptive cognitive machinery (hyperactive agency detection, intuitive mind-body dualism, promiscuous teleology, a theory-of-mind system that does not switch off) with cultural transmission biases favoring minimally counterintuitive, memorable, emotionally arousing content. On this second view, the fitness benefits of group membership are real but are not why the machinery exists; the machinery would generate gods whether or not gods paid.

Please write a dialogue between two invented researchers holding these views. Give them names, do not model them on identifiable living scholars, and do not let either one be the fool — I want the strongest version of each position arguing against the strongest version of the other.

Now the stipulated evidence. I am inventing this dataset rather than pointing you at a published one, so please treat all of it as given and not open to attack on grounds of sampling, dating error, coding decisions, or missing data. Across roughly three hundred societies drawn from a dozen world regions, the record shows the following:

1. In every society where both can be dated, social complexity — sustained population above a fixed threshold, full-time administrators, taxation or corvée labor, monumental construction — precedes the earliest secure evidence of a moralizing high god by on the order of two to three centuries. There are no documented exceptions.
2. Once such beliefs are present, the societies that hold them outlast and demographically out-reproduce closely matched neighbors over the following several centuries, and the margin is large.
3. In the subset of societies where the record permits separating costly communal ritual participation from doctrinal assent, participation carries the entire effect; assent adds nothing once participation is controlled for.
4. In those same societies, the demographic and persistence advantage continues for roughly two further generations among descendants who have stopped assenting to the doctrine but retained the ritual calendar.

The dialogue should meet these demands. First, the two characters must be explicit about whether they are arguing about the origin of the trait, its maintenance, or both, since finding 1 bears on origins and findings 2 through 4 bear on retention — and I suspect much of the real debate is a slippage between those questions that neither side admits to. Second, they must handle two objections head-on: that byproduct accounts are unfalsifiable, in that they can absorb any functional correlation by insisting the machinery would have produced the belief regardless; and that group-level selection is doing no work in finding 2 that ordinary within-group benefits of cooperation plus straightforward demographic expansion could not do. Third — and this is what I most want — each side must name the mechanism by which finding 4 occurs on their own account, because I think that finding is where the disagreement actually lives and neither standard line predicts it cleanly. Fourth, they should say which of the four findings, if any, is genuinely diagnostic rather than equally accommodable, and be willing to answer that some of them are not.

Close by having each character state the single observation that would most move them; the two answers must not be the same observation.

Constraints: no citations, no reading lists, no named studies, authors, or field-specific acronyms — the dataset above is the entire evidential base, and the arguments have to stand on their own. No stage direction beyond a sentence or two of setup. Roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words. Do not resolve into a tie, a synthesis, or a mutual-appreciation ending; if one side concedes something, make it something specific and expensive.
