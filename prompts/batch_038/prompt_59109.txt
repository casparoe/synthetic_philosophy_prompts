four years now i've been the one keeping up a walled garden behind a chapel that gets maybe thirty visitors a summer. the comittee wants to let it go back to weeds, since it costs money and hardly anybody sees it anyway. i keep going round and round on something i read in an old philosophy book at the library, G. E. Moore I think — he asks you to picture the most beautiful world you can imagine with nobody in it, ever, to look at it, set beside the ugliest world, also empty, and says you'd still judge the beautiful one better, so beauty has to count for something all on its own. but then he turns around and says that on its own it counts for very little, and that the best things we know are people loving each other and people actually taking in beautiful things — which dumps me right back where i started.

can you write me a sonnet, fourteen lines, about the garden, where the turn lands exactly on that gap: does the place matter when the only eyes on it are mine and the blackbirds'? i want his argument right, especially the little-but-not-nothing part — not the watered down version where he's just saying beauty is nice. no pep talk for either side, and no turning it into a poem about my own feelings being the point. my mum planted the roses, if that helps or hurts.
