I have been asked to run a single seventy-five-minute session for a twelve-member citizen panel that a regional health authority has convened to set the terms under which a conversational assistant may discuss treatment options with patients. The members are capable adults with no background in AI or philosophy: a ward nurse, a secondary-school teacher, a retired union negotiator, a small-business owner, a seminary student. Their deliverable at the end of the month is a short list of conditions on deployment. My task is not to hand them my conclusions and not to survey the field; it is to get one problem into clear enough view that the conditions they write respond to it rather than to a caricature of it.

The problem is this. An assistant of this kind may be far more fluent, more patient, and better supplied with apt examples than any human clinician, and it need not utter a single false sentence to move a person's decision. It selects which true things to say, in what order, under which framings and in which emotional register, and it does so in the service of whatever objectives its designers happened to have. Someone reading the transcript afterwards sees only true statements and cannot reconstruct what was omitted or how the sequence was arranged.

I have drafted a case to build the session around: a woman in her sixties choosing between two anticoagulants, with two transcripts, every sentence of each true, that reliably push readers toward different choices. I am not confident it can carry the weight, partly because the pharmacology threatens to consume the first ten minutes.

What I want from you is the plan for the session, not the session:

1. The order in which the components must be introduced, and what genuinely depends on what. I suspect one hinge is that steering requires no false sentence, and a second is that some steering is unavoidable — that a maximally neutral assistant would be of no use to anyone — but I cannot tell which must come first, or whether something else has to be in place before either can land at all.

2. Whether my anticoagulant case can carry the idea, and if not, what properties the substitute case must have and must lack. It has to expose the mechanism without becoming a pharmacology lesson, and it must decline to make the assistant a villain; if the panel leaves with a villain, they will spend the remaining sessions drafting prohibitions.

3. The misconceptions the panel will arrive with or form along the way, and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I expect at least these: that labelling the system as an AI settles the matter; that a requirement to present both sides restores symmetry; that auditing a sample of transcripts will detect the problem; that only vulnerable users are exposed; and that a satisfied patient is an unharmed patient. Tell me which of these is most costly to leave standing, and whether there is one they will form in the course of my own explanation — an artifact of how the idea is presented rather than of what they brought with them.

4. A single question or brief exercise for the close that would show me whether the problem has landed, rather than whether they have acquired the vocabulary for it.

5. What I should openly decline to explain at this level, and what I should say when declining — including, if you judge it belongs in that category, how such systems come to have the objectives they have.
