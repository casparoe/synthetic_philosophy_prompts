You are marking the final examination for a graduate seminar on the moral emotions. Candidates sat the paper for ninety minutes under closed-book conditions, and no candidate was expected to cite sources from memory. Below are the question as it was set and one candidate's answer to it. The question is worth 25 marks.

**Question as set**

A hospital group's professional standards committee is drafting a new conduct code for clinicians. In its working paper it justifies the draft as follows:

> Shame is the emotion of an audience: it exists only where someone might see. Guilt is the emotion of a standard: it exists wherever an agent has adopted a rule. A profession that wants its members to be reliably trustworthy should therefore cultivate guilt and suppress shame, since guilt survives the departure of the last observer and shame does not.

(a) [10 marks] Explain why the committee's contrast between shame-as-audience and guilt-as-standard misdescribes both emotions on Bernard Williams's account in *Shame and Necessity* (1993). Your answer must make explicit use of the notion of an internalized spectator and say what work that notion does in the argument.

(b) [15 marks] The committee assumes that a group's emotional economy can be redesigned by decision — that a profession may simply elect guilt in place of shame. State the strongest objection to this assumption that is available from within Williams's position, and say what the objection costs the committee's recommendation. Your answer should distinguish clearly between the sense in which shame is about the self and the sense in which guilt is about what one has done, and should say which of the two emotions is better equipped, on Williams's account, to respond to a bad outcome in which the agent is implicated but not at fault.

**Candidate's answer**

Williams's aim in *Shame and Necessity* is to dislodge a modern prejudice: the story according to which guilt is the mature and autonomous emotion while shame is a primitive residue, left over from societies that had not yet arrived at the idea of a person who can be held responsible. He accepts E. R. Dodds's picture of Homeric society as a shame-culture that knew nothing of guilt and had no concept of the will; his complaint is only that we have not, in fact, climbed out of that culture, since shame remains a permanent feature of ethical life and cannot be replaced by the machinery of obligation.

On the committee's contrast, much depends on separating two claims that the working paper runs together: that shame is essentially social, and that shame requires an actual onlooker. The first is right. Shame's characteristic impulse is to hide, to sink out of sight, and it is bound up with exposure; guilt's characteristic impulse is instead to confess and to make amends, since guilt concerns something one has done to someone. But the second claim is false, and this is where the internal spectator does its work. The agent who feels shame alone, in a locked room, is still before an audience: he sees himself as those he respects would see him, and the spectator he has taken in is a standing feature of him rather than a person who happens to be present. So shame does not die with the last observer. Guilt is structured the same way, since to feel guilt is to hear the reproach of the person one has wronged whether or not that person is anywhere near. The asymmetry the committee relies on therefore collapses in both directions at once: neither emotion is audience-independent, and neither is audience-dependent in the sense the paper needs. This shows that shame, rather than guilt, is the emotion a profession should build on for clinicians who work alone at night.

It also shows that the two emotions are nearer to one another than the tradition supposes. Shame is guilt turned on the self instead of on the act; the internal spectator judges in both cases, and the difference between the emotions is one of scope rather than of kind. From this the committee's practical point partly survives. Because guilt rests on a rule the agent has reflectively endorsed rather than on a social setting, it travels: an endorsed standard applies wherever the agent goes, whereas shame is tied to the particular community whose opinion he has absorbed. A code can therefore install guilt directly, simply by getting clinicians to endorse the rules and to see themselves as answerable to them. Shame is also felt about matters in which no one is wronged — clumsiness, bad taste, losing one's nerve — and this range is a further reason not to build a professional ethics on it.

Part (b). Can a culture choose? Only indirectly, and here the committee's assumption fails on its own terms. If the internal spectator is formed by the opinions of those the agent respects, then the way to change a group's emotions is to change whom it respects: what a profession can decide is who counts as a colleague whose estimation matters, not which emotion its members will feel. Emotional dispositions are not adopted the way beliefs are endorsed, and a rule accepted on paper produces no sentiment until there are people whose disappointment would sting. So the committee cannot purchase guilt without first constructing a community — which is to say it cannot avoid building shame. What this costs the recommendation is not its target but its method: guilt remains the thing to aim at, since a code can legislate it directly, while shame can only be grown slowly and as a by-product.
