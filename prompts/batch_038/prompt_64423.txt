Write me a dialogue, please. American English spelling and idiom throughout.

Setup: a review board on a station out past the Moon is deciding whether to approve a seeding mission — a ship that will release an engineered consortium of microbes into the subsurface ocean of a world that appears to be completely lifeless. Nothing alive has ever been found there after forty years of looking, but nobody can prove a negative, and the ocean is under kilometers of ice so the surveys are patchy at best.

Two characters, both sharp, both making real points — I dont want either one to be a strawman or to lose by default. One is the mission's ecologist, who thinks spreading life is close to the most consequential moral opportunity we will ever have: biology happened exactly once here, the rest of the universe is empty rock, so a sterile world stays sterile forever unless someone acts, and we're the only ones around who can. The other thinks a place that has never been alive has a value of its own that isn't just "future habitat waiting to be filled," and that seeding it is the one act out there that can't be undone or repented of — you can always send the ship in a hundred years, you can never un-send it.

Have them push on each other instead of trading speeches. Specifically I'd like these worked through:

- the irreversability asymmetry, and whether it really cuts only one way: the ecologist will point out we're already contaminating everything accidentally with our own probes, so abstaining buys nothing
- whether that "it's happening anyway" move actually works or is a dodge, and what it would take to make it work
- a seeded biosphere means predation, parasitism and animal suffering at planetary scale, designed by a committee; and whether designing an ecosystem with none of that in it is a better answer or a worse piece of hubris
- what you should do when you aren't sure the world is sterile — does the uncertainty argue for waiting or for going now, and does the answer change if this system is drifting out of reach and we may not get another one
- who is authorized to choose the starting conditions of an evolutionary lineage that will outlast the people who chose it by eons, and whether that's different from ordinary decisions with long tails

Aim for roughly 1200–1500 words. No name-dropping philosophers or books — argue from the features of the case itself. Let it end unresolved, but with the two of them having narrowed down what exactly they still disagree about, and that residue should be smaller and sharper than where they started.
