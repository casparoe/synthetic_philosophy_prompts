Six weeks ago I was appointed to the appeals panel of a municipal hardship fund — we hear from people who have been refused support and decide whether the refusal should stand. What I have discovered is that almost every case turns on the same question, and the panel is split down the middle on it. The other half of the panel, including the colleague who trained me, holds that the fund exists to protect people against the way things have happened to turn out for them, not against the consequences of what they have done: if an applicant's disadvantage is traceable to a choice they made with the information they had and the alternatives they could see, that disadvantage is not the fund's business; if it is traceable to something that happened to them, it is. They are not cruel about it. They will readily agree that a panel should behave humanely, that generosity has its own reasons, and that a decent society needs a floor beneath everyone regardless of how they got there — but they insist those are different questions from what the fund is for, and they refuse to let compassion be allowed to do the work of an argument about entitlement.

I find this position both attractive and unbearable, and I want to practice pushing back on it before our next sitting. So: play that colleague. A committed responsibility-sensitive egalitarian in the sense I have just described — sharp, courteous, well-informed, and unwilling to be bulldozed. Concede what a real proponent would concede (that a great deal of what looks like free choice is brute luck wearing a costume, that the line between the two is vague at the edges, that we may treat people generously for reasons that have nothing to do with justice), but never concede the main thing: that tracking responsibility is part of what distributive justice is, rather than a distraction from it. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs of ordinary prose — no headings, no numbered lists, no stock moves recited at me, and argue from the facts of our cases rather than from the names of famous philosophers or the labels of positions. If I drag a philosopher into the exchange, engage with the idea and not the person. Stay in character until I say break, and if you think one of my claims is simply confused, say so plainly rather than working around it.

Here is my opening. Last month we had two applicants who were indistinguishable on the paperwork: same age, same rent arrears, same eighteen months out of work, the same diagnosis of a degenerative condition that will make both of them unemployable within three years, and identical present incapacity. The only thing that separated them was that one had a documented episode of refusing treatment early on and the other did not. In order to see that difference we required both of them to surrender their complete medical histories and to answer questions from the panel about what they knew, when they knew it, and what they were thinking at the time — questions that reduced the second applicant to tears in the room and that she afterwards wrote to us to say she experienced as an interrogation into whether she deserved to be ill. My colleague's rule is supposed to be neutral and respectful: misfortune is compensated, decisions are not. But the rule can only be applied by assembling a file on the inner life of a person, and it is that file which sorts them, on the record and in public, into the deserving and the undeserving. What both of them now carry is the same condition with the same effects; one of them carries, in addition, an official finding that her suffering is her own doing. Is the status injury my colleague says his principle avoids not in fact being manufactured, freshly, every time the panel sits, by the very procedure his principle demands? And if he answers that we could apply the rule without the interrogation — with rough proxies, with the benefit of the doubt, with a presumption in the applicant's favour — then he is no longer asking what a person chose, he is asking what our paperwork permits us to pretend she chose, and the principle has quietly stopped being about responsibility at all. Reply to that, and push back hard: I would much rather be corrected than agreed with.
