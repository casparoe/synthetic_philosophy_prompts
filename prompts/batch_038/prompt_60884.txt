I am a novelist at work on a book that takes the Everett interpretation literally — no collapse, every outcome of every measurement realized somewhere in the branching structure — and because the emotional machinery of the novel depends on what my characters can rationally fear, hope for, and stake their decisions on, I have written my way into a definite position on probability in that setting. Before I commit the last hundred pages to it, I want the position audited. Please adopt the standpoint of a philosopher of physics who has worked seriously on the Born-rule problem for many-worlds; think through the entailments carefully, and generate more candidate tensions than you ultimately report. The commitments, which are mine as much as the book's: (1) branch weight, the squared amplitude, is not the probability of anything, since on a no-collapse reading every outcome simply occurs; (2) the Deutsch–Wallace decision-theoretic derivation cannot repair this, because it either presupposes what it needs to prove about the value of branches or quietly reinstates a measure of caring rather than a measure of belief; (3) envariance-based derivations of the Born rule fare no better, since they assume equiprobability among the states they then claim to have counted; (4) the appeal to self-locating uncertainty — the interval after decoherence has split the branches but before the observer registers an outcome — is illegitimate, because knowledge of the universal wavefunction exhausts what there is to know, leaving nothing for the observer to be uncertain about; (5) nonetheless my characters are to hold ordinary, well-formed degrees of belief that track squared amplitude, and it is to be rational rather than sentimental of them to grieve a low-weight branch less and to bet on a high-weight one. Tell me which of these pull against which, and how badly. I have no idea where the trouble is, so treat the order above as giving you no information about which commitment I would most readily abandon. Give me exactly five numbered items, ordered from most to least severe, each one or two sentences, naming the commitment or commitments at issue, saying whether the tension is genuine or only apparent, and stating the cheapest revision that would dissolve it together with what that revision would cost the book. No introduction and no conclusion.
