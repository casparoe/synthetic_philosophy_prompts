You are, for this exercise, a philosopher who works on formal epistemology and who has also spent real time inside the operational machinery of forecasting tournaments — someone who can hold an accuracy-dominance argument and the fine print of a question's resolution criterion in their head at the same moment. Please think carefully before writing. Spend some effort first generating candidate philosophical commitments and then discarding the ones that turn out to be mere restatements of the design in fancier language; the discarding matters more than the generating. Do that work privately and do not show me the scaffolding. The deliverable is one continuous piece of prose, written the way you would write a long letter to a colleague: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables, no section breaks. Paragraphs only; if you must enumerate, enumerate inside sentences.

For most of two decades I have read and written in the online rationalist world, where the standard move in any argument about belief is to point at proper scoring rules and declare the matter settled. I have made that move myself more times than I can count. I now run a forecasting program, and running it has made me uneasy about how much philosophy is load-bearing in the machinery.

The practice is this, and it is modeled closely on the public tournaments you will know. Questions are posed by us rather than by forecasters. Each is binary, occasionally three-way, with a deadline and a written resolution criterion that names a particular source or a particular testable condition. A single adjudicator later rules the question resolved one way or the other, applying the criterion as written; the ruling is final and is never itself scored. Forecasters enter a number between zero and one hundred for each question they choose to answer, may revise it any number of times before the deadline, and are scored on their final revision. We score with the quadratic rule, and a forecaster's standing is the simple arithmetic mean of their per-question scores over all questions they answered that got resolved. There is a leaderboard ranked on that mean, with prizes at the top. We actively encourage volume: our guidance to newcomers is that more questions is better, both because it produces useful calibration feedback and because a mean over two questions is mostly noise. Once a year we show each forecaster their reliability curve, in the Murphy-style decomposition into calibration and resolution, and tell them in effect to move their reported numbers until the curve lies on the diagonal. When someone is stuck we tell them to look at what the rest of the pool is saying and to treat the aggregate as evidence; we publish the pool median a week into every question.

Three changes are on the table, and they are what made me want the underlying commitments written out rather than assumed. Several of the better forecasters want to be scored with the logarithmic rule instead of the quadratic one, on the grounds that the quadratic rule is too forgiving of extreme error and that a serious forecaster should be willing to be crushed by a confident miss. We are also considering letting people skip questions at no cost, so that the mean is taken only over questions a forecaster felt competent on. And last season we converted a batch of binary questions into three-way questions by adding an intermediate outcome covering cases that are technically within the letter of the question but not within its evident point; our strongest forecasters' numbers improved, even though as far as anyone can tell their beliefs did not change at all. I do not yet know whether that last observation is a scandal, an artifact, or exactly what the theory predicts.

Tell me what this design presupposes about the nature of epistemic value, at the level of commitments rather than slogans. Take the pieces one at a time and say what each is intelligible only against. The quadratic rule is strictly proper, and I know the story: properness makes honest reporting incentive-compatible, and the dominance arguments in the accuracy-first literature are supposed to convert that same fact into a norm rather than a mere incentive. But say precisely what the practice commits itself to when it treats the mean of per-question quadratic scores as a measure of how good a person's beliefs are. What must be true of epistemic value for that aggregate to mean anything at all — that inaccuracy is a function of credence and truth-value at a world, that it is additively separable across questions, that questions are independent items of belief rather than fragments of a single web, that every question deserves the same share of epistemic attention because each contributes equally to a simple mean? Then draw the line I suspect I have been drawing in the wrong place for eight years: which of these does the machinery strictly require, and which does it merely happen to fit comfortably?

Go after the parts I think are doing unearned work. The adjudicator resolves questions by applying a written criterion, so what our scores actually track is distance from a ruling rather than distance from the truth; say what accuracy-first epistemology can do with that substitution and whether it can absorb it. Is the criterion best read as fixing which possible world obtains, leaving the framework untouched, or does the practice replace an alethic standard with an institutional one and then borrow the legitimacy of the first for the second? The question set is chosen by us and never scored, yet everything downstream is a function of that choice; say what view of the value of a belief is presupposed by leaving the selection of propositions entirely outside the accounting, and connect it to the fact that the quadratic rule is not invariant under refinement of a partition, which I take to be what my third observation is really about. The leaderboard ranks people by mean score, and rank is a comparative quantity rather than a truth-directed one; say whether the ranking practice is continuous with the properness argument or a different animal wearing its clothes. And work out what the volume encouragement and the proposed costless skipping each imply about whether there is any epistemic value in holding opinions on matters one has not studied.

I also want the rivals named rather than left implicit. Which views of epistemic value does this design rule out by construction, and which is it merely silent about? I have in mind a James-style picture on which believing truth and avoiding error are two distinct aims whose trade-off is set by the agent rather than by the scoring rule; views on which epistemic value attaches to understanding or explanatory structure rather than to the accuracy of individual credences, and on which a set of perfectly calibrated but mutually disconnected numerical opinions might be worth nothing; views on which credences answer to norms of assertion, deliberation, or respect for evidence rather than to expected accuracy; and the pragmatic line on which the point of a credence lies in its role in action, so that scoring it in isolation measures something that does not matter on its own. For each, say whether our practice is incompatible with it, indifferent to it, or already smuggling in an assumption that forecloses it.

Finally, tell me where the practice is underdetermined — where several quite different theories of epistemic value would license exactly the same scoring rule, the same leaderboard, the same calibration exercise — and where, conversely, a single decision I have treated as housekeeping actually commits us. Put the three proposed changes in that light: for each, say whether it is accuracy-neutral bookkeeping, a shift in the implicit theory of value, or a change whose philosophical status cannot be determined until something else is settled, and name what that something is. And if you think the whole apparatus rests on a commitment no amount of redesign can discharge, say so plainly and say which one.
