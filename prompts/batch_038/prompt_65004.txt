**Question 3 (25 points).** In lecture we distinguished two relations that can hold between experiences:

- *co-consciousness*: two experiences are co-conscious when there is a single point of view from which both are had together.
- *mutual availability*: information carried by one experience is available to the reasoning, speech, and deliberate action associated with the other.

Consider this argument.

P1. If two experiences are neither co-conscious nor mutually available, then they are not both experiences of one and the same subject.
P2. In a complete commissurotomy patient, some experiences supported by the right hemisphere (e.g. the tactile feel of an object explored out of sight with the left hand) and some supported by the left hemisphere (e.g. whatever accompanies a spoken report about something else entirely) are neither co-conscious nor mutually available.
P3. Mutual availability between the hemispheres depends on the corpus callosum, and in these patients it has been severed.
C. Therefore a complete commissurotomy patient is not one conscious subject but two.

(a) Restate the argument's structure and, for each premise, say which of the two relations it invokes — co-consciousness, mutual availability, or both. (8)
(b) Is the argument valid? If not, identify exactly where it breaks and state the further premise that would repair it. (8)
(c) Pick the premise you think is weakest, evaluate it, and say what a defender of the argument could reply. (9)

And here is the student's answer, verbatim, typos and all:

"The argument has three premises and a conclusion. P1 is the general principle about what it takes for two experiences to belong to one subject, P2 is the empirical claim about the split brain case, and P3 explains why P2 holds: no callosum, nothing gets across. The conclusion follows from P1 and P2, because the patient has pairs of experiences that fail both conditions, so those experiences aren't the experiences of one subject.

One thing tho: the conclusion says two, and P1 and P2 only show not-one. To get from not-one to two you need the extra premise that each hemisphere by itself is sufficient for a subject. I think that's obviously true — after surgery each hemisphere goes on supporting perception, learning, memory and deliberate action in its own domain, and the right hemisphere has some language comprehension even though it can't speak — so the argument is sound once you add that premise.

The weakest premise is P2, but only because it's the empirical one, and it holds just for lateralized tasks under controlled conditions. In ordinary life these patients seem basically normal: the two halves can share information through the environment (the patient can move his eyes or his head, look at his own left hand, cross-cue), and they still share the same subcortical structures and blood supply, which nobody cut. So a defender should restrict P2 to the experimental cases. Gazzaniga's interpreter results push the same direction — the left hemisphere will confidently invent a story to explain behavior that was initiated by the right, which shows there isn't even a unified self-model in charge. That's basically what Nagel argues in the brain bisection paper: the commissurotomy patient just IS two people, and that's an empirical discovery that finally settles what the old one-brain-one-subject assumption got wrong. Given all that, P3 is doing the real work in the argument and it's the hardest premise to deny."
