I'm the junior commentator on an author-meets-critics panel at a small logic workshop next month — twenty minutes of questions from me, then the author replies. Her paper is about Carroll's tortoise, but she isn't offering a solution in any of the usual shapes, and I want my questions to bite on what she's actually saying rather than on the dialectic everyone already knows.

Her claim, as near as I can put it: the regress only looks infinite because we assume the demand for a warrant has to be answered with a further premise, and no rule of inference can ever be warranted by anything of that kind. So she says the warrant is reflexive instead — any attempt to assess a rule has to be carried out using that very rule, which means the chain loops back rather than climbing — and she's explicit that this isn't a proof that deduction is justified and isn't meant to convince the tortoise on the tortoise's own terms. She rejects the Russell-style separation of implication from inference as beside the point, rejects the popular taking-condition reply (that inferring requires taking the premises to support the conclusion) on the grounds that it either restarts the regress or bottoms out in a psychological state doing no normative work, and rejects the Winch-ish line that inferring is a trained something-you-do rather than a formula, because she thinks that leaves it open whether someone who lacks the skill is making a mistake at all.

So: eight to ten questions I can actually say out loud in twenty minutes, each with a follow-up aimed at the answer she's most likely to give. She's slippery — her default is to say the question already presupposes the malformed demand and then re-explain her view — so I want the sequence to make that move progressively harder to pull off.

Please make sure I get at least one question on each of these:

- whether the paper undercuts itself. She argues from premises to a conclusion, so is she entitled to think her argument establishes anything, or is she only describing a practice we happen to be inside? And relatedly: isn't reflexive warrant just rule circularity under a new name, given that everyone agrees a rule-circular argument begs the question against anyone who doubts the rule — what's left over beyond a shrug?
- if nothing in the head marks the difference, what seperates genuinely inferring on her account from one belief sliding into another the way it does after a stroke, or under conditioning? And if the difference is entirely a matter of how other people hold you to rules, is that a cost she'll own?
- over-generation. The intuitionist doubts excluded middle without ever using excluded middle, so doesn't her reflexive test certify that practice too? Then what happens when two self-certifying practices disagree about whether a proof goes through — pluralism, or a preference for her own logic smuggled in as a discovery?
- whether a real tortoise is possible on her view at all: someone who accepts A, B and every conditional you write down and still never draws Z. Irrational, mistaken, or merely different? And does she have any way of criticising him that doesn't fall back into the demand she's declared malformed?

One thing I want you to be decisive about instead of handing me a neutral menu: tell me which two of these you think genuinely land, which ones she can swat without damage and why, then order the questions so the two that land come late, and finish with the one that forces her to say what it would look like for a rule to be warranted badly rather than just reflexively. No softballs, nothing that lets her simply restate the thesis.
