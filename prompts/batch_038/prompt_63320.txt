I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am currently reporting a piece on how American cities and states regulate political spending. Across two weeks of interviews, four words have come up more often than any others: "money is speech." I have now heard the phrase deployed by a constitutional litigator arguing for striking down donation caps, and separately by a political scientist arguing that regulating cash while leaving every other political resource untouched is arbitrary. Before I print it, I need to know what it can and cannot bear.

For this exercise, take the role of a philosopher of law who is also at home in First Amendment doctrine and in the political theory of free expression, and who is used to explaining technical distinctions to intelligent lay readers. Think carefully and do not rush to a verdict. Begin with a short brainstorming paragraph in which you map, in rough form, the full range of claims those four words could be doing — including at least one reading you believe nobody in this debate actually endorses but which is logically available and worth naming.

Here is my material.

Source A, a litigator who has argued campaign finance cases, summarized the settled position this way: limits on what a campaign may spend directly restrict how much political speech exists, while limits on what one person may give a candidate only indirectly affect it, and the state may not muffle some speakers in order to make others relatively louder. On that basis he told me a contribution cap is a cap on the donor's political voice, and that a donor-disclosure rule burdens the same right.

Source B, a political scientist, used the identical phrase to mean something nearly opposite. On her account, money is one of several interchangeable resources — cash, unpaid labor, celebrity, technical expertise, an institution's existing mailing list — by which a preference is converted into speech other people actually hear. Because the resources are substitutes, she argued, singling out cash for regulation is either arbitrary or an admission that cash is distinctive in some way her interlocutors never state. She was arguing for more regulation, not less.

Two concrete situations from my reporting that the analysis must be able to handle:

1. A draft ordinance before a city council would value every hour of unpaid campaign labor at a posted hourly wage and count that value against the same caps that apply to cash gifts. Source A called this indistinguishable from a cash cap. Source B called it proof that the slogan does no work, since the city is regulating a resource rather than a message.
2. In the closing fortnight before a ballot measure, a developer buys up essentially all remaining local broadcast, print, and digital advertising inventory and runs nothing whatsoever. He spends in order to ensure that nobody else can be heard.

For the piece I am also proposing a distinction of my own, and I want it stress-tested: loudness, meaning how much reach a given message acquires, versus audibility, meaning whether a message can be heard at all given competition for a scarce channel. Situation 2 seems to me to be about audibility and not loudness, though I cannot defend that intuition.

What I would like from you:

1. A numbered list of the distinct, precisely stated claims that "money is speech" could be making, ordered from the trivial to the radical. At a minimum, separate money as itself carrying a message from money as the instrument by which a message acquires reach, and tell me whether there is a third relation between them that I have missed.
2. For each claim, a verdict — platitude, substantive and defensible, substantive and contested, or false — with a sentence or two of reasoning. Do not give me citations; where you rely on a legal doctrine, simply describe what the doctrine says.
3. For each claim, which of my two sources it licenses: A's use, B's use, both, or neither. My suspicion is that most readings license neither, and I want to know whether that suspicion survives scrutiny.
4. A diagnosis of an equivocation I recorded but did not understand at the time. When I pressed Source A on the volunteer-labor ordinance, his first answer was that the city was restricting how much he could say; his second, offered moments later, was that the city was trying to make everyone equally audible, which he said the Constitution forbids. Identify the two readings involved and say whether any single reading could sustain both moves.
5. Run your two or three strongest readings against situation 2 specifically. I want to know whether any reading can distinguish spending that amplifies the spender's own message from spending that suppresses everyone else's, or whether the slogan is simply silent there. Say also whether my loudness and audibility distinction tracks anything real in your list, or whether it should be cut from the piece.
6. Close with the single most accurate sentence I could print in place of the slogan — one that a hostile expert could not fairly accuse of overstatement — and, separately, flag any claim on your list that proves self-undermining or collapses into a claim about something other than money, such as property, access to audiences, or equality.

No bibliography, no reading list, no footnotes: I am after the analysis, and I cannot verify references. Reason as long as you need before you commit to the menu, and prefer a smaller list of genuinely distinct claims to a longer list of near-duplicates.
