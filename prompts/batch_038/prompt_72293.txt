For roughly six years I have been a regular editor on the English Wikipedia, working mostly on articles that touch covert government programs and contested atrocity claims, and I am now drafting a chapter on the epistemology of conspiracy theories. Reading our own governing documents alongside the philosophical literature, I have become convinced that the site's content regime embodies a fairly specific — and never explicitly argued for — theory of when a belief is defective, so I want that theory reconstructed. Let me state the practice as precisely as I can, since the reconstruction should turn on the details. Three core content policies do the work: verifiability, which holds that article content is determined by published information rather than by editors' own beliefs, experiences, or unpublished analysis, and that anything likely to be challenged must carry an inline citation to a reliable source that directly supports it; no original research, which forbids novel analysis or synthesis of the published record; and neutral point of view, which requires that all significant views appearing in reliable sources be represented fairly and in proportion to their prominence, that the encyclopedia describe disputes rather than engage in them, and that editors avoid creating a false impression of parity between a near-universal view and a tiny minority one — the policy illustrates this last point with a Holocaust denial example. Layered on top is the fringe theories guideline, which defines a fringe idea as one that departs significantly from the prevailing or mainstream views in its field, names conspiracy theories as a paradigm case, treats scholarly opinion as generally the most authoritative marker of where the mainstream lies, requires that any statement about whether such a theory is true be supported by independent reliable sources, and instructs editors not to present a marginalized view alongside expert consensus as though the two were opposing but comparable positions. Reliability itself is then graded by institutional process: peer-reviewed and academic work is preferred for historical, medical, and scientific claims, and a source's standing rises with the number of people who were engaged in fact-checking, analyzing legal exposure, and scrutinizing the writing before publication.

Two features of this regime puzzle me, and I would like the reconstruction to take both seriously rather than explain them away. First, the same guideline carves out an exception for "alternative theoretical formulations from within the scientific community," naming continental drift before plate tectonics supplied a mechanism, the existence of Troy, the Norse colonization of the Americas, and the Big Bang as proposals initially resisted and later absorbed into the mainstream — but the carve-out is defined by where a proposal comes from and who is advancing it, not by the character of its evidence. Second, the evidential channels that actually expose real covert programs are precisely the ones our source hierarchy discounts: investigative journalism, participant memoir and testimony, leaked and later declassified documents, litigation records, reports of legislative inquiries, and material from organizations with an avowed point of view. I have argued on talk pages for material of exactly that kind and been told it fails as primary, self-interested, or improperly synthesized, while our articles on the Tuskegee syphilis study, the FBI's COINTELPRO, MKUltra, and Iran-Contra now sit there fully sourced and utterly unremarkable. The rules that would have blocked those claims when they were live are the rules that license the articles about them today, and nothing in the regime tells an editor which side of that asymmetry a given live claim falls on.

What I want is a reconstruction of the epistemology this practice presupposes, held to a demanding standard. Begin by identifying the view of justification, of testimony, and of the relation between consensus and truth that the regime needs in order to function at all: is it a species of social reliabilism, an acceptance of epistemic dependence and the division of cognitive labor in the manner of Hardwig, an implicitly Humean discounting of testimony by the antecedent improbability of what it reports, a credentialist account on which the defectiveness of a belief is a matter of the institutional standing of its sources, or nothing epistemic at all — merely the pragmatics of a crowdsourced reference work that must not become the "validating source" for otherwise insignificant claims? Then separate sharply what the practice strictly presupposes, in the sense that it could not be operated otherwise, from what it merely sits comfortably with. I am especially interested in whether the operative criterion for treating a belief as a conspiracy theory is content-based (secretive coordination, a particular explanatory structure) or instead uptake-based and provenance-based (whether credentialed institutions have taken the claim up, and whether its evidence arrives through vetted channels); if it is uptake-based, say what that commits the practice to about the possibility of a true conspiracy theory, and whether the retro-vindication asymmetry I described is a limitation the practice can acknowledge in its own terms or a contradiction it cannot. Say which rival views the regime quietly rules out — radical individualist evidentialism, the thought that a competent reader can weigh primary evidence for herself, any position on which institutional consensus is itself a thing needing justification — and mark the places where the practice is genuinely underdetermined, such that two or three incompatible theories would license exactly the same set of edits. Finally, tell me what would have to change on the ground if the uptake criterion were replaced by a content-based one: which policies would need rewriting, which familiar disputes would come out differently, and what the cost would be in claims wrongly promoted to significance. End your response with a single line in exactly this form — Verdict: LABEL — where LABEL is one of PRESUPPOSED, COMPATIBLE, or INCOHERENT, meaning respectively that the regime cannot be operated without the epistemology you reconstruct, that it merely fits that epistemology while remaining equally licensable by rivals, or that its parts presuppose views that cannot be held together; follow the label with one sentence naming the consideration that most drove your choice.
