I am presenting at a joint philosophy–law colloquium in six weeks: a paper arguing that Simone Weil's short wartime note on the abolition of political parties — written in London in 1943 and published only after her death — contains an argument that contemporary party theory has never actually answered. I should be clear about the room, because the argument draws very different fire depending on who is sitting in it. The audience will be roughly a dozen people: four deliberative democrats who think of partisanship as a virtue and of party competition as the engine of public justification; two comparative politics scholars who study party systems empirically and will care about incentives, nomination rules, and what actually happens in officially nonpartisan legislatures; one legal theorist who works on freedom of association and has already told me over email that any ban on party organization is a First Amendment non-starter; one historian of the French Third Republic and the Vichy period, who is allergic to being told that a 1943 French author was prescient; and two graduate students whose entire acquaintance with Weil comes from secondary literature on affliction and attention. Nobody in the room is a Weil specialist, and nobody is a decision theorist. Please build me the Q&A prep sheet: ten or eleven objections I should genuinely expect from *this* audience — not the ones a literary-Weil crowd would raise, and not the ones I would get from a room of partisans — ordered by how much damage each would do if I fumbled it, weighted by how likely it is to come up. For each: name the subgroup that will raise it, phrase it the way that subgroup would phrase it (the deliberative democrats will talk about internal pluralism and the cultivation of collective agency; the comparativists will talk about coordination failure and label substitution; the lawyer will talk about association; the historian will talk about 1943), give me a reply I can deliver in about two minutes standing at a lectern, and add a frank note on where that reply is softest.

So that the objections have something precise to bite on, here is the argument as I will state it. First, Weil's case is not a moralistic complaint about factional passion or careerism; it is structural and, I claim, epistemic. On her account a political party is identifiable by three features taken together: it is organized so as to bear collectively on the minds of its members; it runs on manufactured passion rather than on judgment; and its only determinate end is its own indefinite growth. My claim is that these three jointly entail that party-mediated judgment cannot reliably track what is true or what is just — not because members are bad people, but because the growth aim silently converts every question before them into a question about the organization's prospects, so that what a member is able to notice, entertain, and take seriously is fixed in advance by an institution whose criterion of success has nothing to do with correctness. Second, and this is my own coinage rather than Weil's, I distinguish interest capture from judgment capture: the familiar indictments of parties — donor influence, the revolving door, elite capture, logrolling — are about interests, and they are in principle addressable by transparency regimes and finance rules; what Weil is describing is a pathology one level up, in which the party determines not what the member wants but what the member can consider. Incentive reform cannot reach it, which is why I think the whole reformist literature talks past her. Third, I argue that the negative conclusion is separable from her positive program: I do not defend abolition, I defend what I call party-minimal republicanism — candidates standing without organizational labels, a prohibition on collective electoral propaganda, deliberation conducted in circles too small to acquire an institutional will of their own, and, most controversially, the retention of parties as research and drafting offices stripped of nomination and whip powers. Fourth, I claim the argument does not depend on her Platonism or her theology: the mechanism works on a thoroughly deflationary notion of correctness in factual and normative judgment, so a secular audience cannot dismiss it as mystical excess. I will also assert, as an existence proof, that officially nonpartisan legislatures and municipal systems show the design is not empty — I expect to be pushed hard there and I do not want to be bluffing.

Two things about format. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations, and please do not put words in Weil's mouth with quotation marks: I have the text and I will handle the exegesis, so paraphrase is what I need. And do not give me a general essay on the value of partisanship; the deliverable is a sheet I can hold. Beyond the ten or eleven objections, include: a short list of what I should concede in my opening five minutes so that three of the predictable objections never get asked at all; the single reply that has to land for the paper to survive the hour, and what I should say if it doesn't; the objection I am hoping nobody raises — the one that is hardest for me specifically because of the interest-capture/judgment-capture distinction — together with a candid account of why it is so damaging and the least bad thing I could say; a note on whether that distinction is vulnerable to the charge that it merely relabels something already in the literature on party discipline and preference endogenization, since the comparativists will make exactly that charge; and, for the three objections you rank highest, a thirty-second compressed version of the reply for the case where the chair cuts off discussion early.
