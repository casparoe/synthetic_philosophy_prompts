I write a science-and-ideas column — mostly brains, mostly consciousness — and I just got back from three days at a workshop where two camps sat on the same panel and talked clean through each other. My editor wants 2,500 words and honestly the most useful thing I could hand readers isn't my recap of the arguing, it's the document those two groups should have produced and didn't. So I want you to write it: the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between them, as though they'd shut themselves in a room for a weekend and come out with something they'd both put their names to.

The two sides. Side A are the overflow people: phenomenal consciousness is richer than anything that makes it into access, so there's more in the head at a given moment than can be reported, reasoned with, or handed to any other cognitive system. Their stock evidence is the whole partial-report lineage going back to Sperling — flash an array, mask it, cue one row, and people do far better on the cued row than they can do when asked for everything, while insisting the array looked like a lot more than the three or four items they can actually name. Side B are the global workspace people: conscious access just is the broadcast, what becomes globally available to report, memory, and reasoning. On their view an experience you can't report, can't use and can't think with either isn't an experience or is a way of talking that isn't buying anything, and above-chance guessing about unreported items is local non-broadcast processing doing the work — clever visual system, no phenomenology required. The access/phenomenal split itself is Block's, and both sides accept the vocabulary while disagreeing about whether the second term picks out anything that isn't the first.

Now the wrinkle, which is the part I actually care about, because it was the only thing at that workshop that got both sides to stop and stare at the same piece of paper. A grad student pitched a design nobody has run properly, and I'll call it the prepared-versus-surprise probe since no one agreed on a name.

Setup: each trial flashes a 12-item array briefly, then a pattern mask. On half the trials the subject was told in advance exactly what would be asked and about which location — after the mask, is the item at position 7 a letter or a digit — and on the other half both the question and the location are withheld and revealed only as a surprise after the mask. Questions always get answered with a two-button press and come in matched pairs so difficulty is held constant. Alongside the button press the subject gives a confidence rating and a plain yes/no about whether they had any visual experience of the probed location at all. The mask-to-question interval is varied in fine steps from 0 up to about a second.

I'm told the pilot came back like this. Prepared condition: roughly 78 percent accuracy on the probed item at the shortest intervals, confidence tracking accuracy the way you'd expect. Surprise condition: roughly 61 percent against a 50 percent floor, but confidence flat — not tracking accuracy — and on about 70 percent of those surprise trials the subject said they'd had no visual experience of that location whatsoever. So prepared beats surprise, and the surprise advantage arrives with no confidence and no claim of seeing anything.

Build the joint statement around that dataset rather than the tired old paradigms. Here's what I need in it:

1. A numbered section of claims both sides can genuinely sign. Include what they agree on about the partial-report tradition — what it does and doesn't establish — about what a mask actually buys you as a manipulation, and about what the ignition signature is supposed to be a signature of. Also include what they agree on about the pilot as data, before anyone interprets it. If a claim is only signable because it's been watered down to nothing, cut it; I don't want filler.

2. A disagreements table. Each row one disagreement, with a column for how Side A would state its own position and a column for how Side B would state its own, in wording each would accept as fair, plus a column marking whether the disagreement is substantive or verbal. Neither side gets to be a strawman in its own column — that's the whole point of the exercise.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions where settling them would actually move somebody. My hunches, which you're welcome to overrule, are that one is whether there's a bridging principle saying that reliable use of information implies access — in which case the surprise-condition advantage is just weak access by another name. Another is whether flat confidence plus a denial of seeing anything can be evidence about phenomenology at all, or only evidence about reporting. A third is whether that advantage is best read as a late readout from a rich residual trace or as the output of local processing that never needed a broadcast. Say which of these are real cruxes and which are word fights, and if you think some chunk of the whole dispute is verbal, name the chunk and say why. Don't be a coward about it.

4. For each crux, a concrete control, manipulation, or argument that both sides would accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance. I want at least one prediction per side that they'd be embarrassed to be wrong about, and for each crux I want each side's own stated condition for giving ground.

Format it as the numbered agreed-claims section, then the table, then cruxes with the predictions under each. No reference list and no bibliography — I'm not printing one, and I can't check it anyway. Don't put anything in quotation marks as though someone said it; this is a paraphrase-level document all the way through. Aim for maybe 1,400 words, and keep it readable by a smart non-specialist, because that's who my column is for. I want to be able to run this as a sidebar with both names attached, so it has to sound like something two people who still disagree could sign.
