I chair the research review committee of a mid-sized private foundation that funds roughly thirty grants a year out of about four hundred applications. Our procedure has been unchanged since the foundation was established. Each application is sent to nine reviewers drawn from a standing pool of about sixty; each reads it alone, over a six-week window, and submits a single accept-or-reject verdict with a short written justification; and we fund whatever receives a majority of accepts, with the committee rubber-stamping the tally. The founding director's rationale is printed on the inside cover of our reviewer handbook, and it amounts to the claim that any individual reviewer is frequently wrong — especially wrong about work that is unusual or hard to place — but that a majority of nine is very rarely wrong, because the individual errors pull in different directions and cancel. Two of my fellow committee members have taken to repeating a compressed version of this: more readers means more information, and more information cannot make a decision worse. Our board meets in October and I have to bring them a recommendation, so I would like to think this through properly rather than by instinct.

A trustee has now proposed raising each panel from nine reviewers to twenty-one, on the explicit grounds that whatever makes nine better than one makes twenty-one better than nine. I am hesitant, for four reasons I can articulate only loosely. First, our pool is not heterogeneous in the way the handbook's reasoning seems to assume: most of our reviewers trained in the same handful of doctoral programs, they meet at one annual conference, and they read much the same literature, so when I look back at last decade's scoring sheets I find that panels almost never divide five-to-four — they divide nine-to-zero, eight-to-one, or occasionally seven-to-two, as though a mistaken reading tends to be a shared mistaken reading rather than an idiosyncratic one. Second, our working assumption that the average reviewer is better than a coin flip rests on an exercise we ran five years ago, in which reviewer verdicts were scored against forty retrospectively labeled applications, judged to have been clear successes or clear failures; but those labels were themselves assigned by a majority vote of the same pool, and I am not sure what such an exercise can establish when the measuring stick is made of the thing being measured. Third, twenty-one readers per application is not affordable at our current reviewer honoraria, so the expansion would compress the window from six weeks to two, and my guess is that the reviewers who fall away under time pressure are the ones who read most carefully. Fourth, a colleague objects at a more basic level: she says the entire framing presumes there is a settled fact, independent of our decision, about whether a given project would have turned out well, and that in our field the funding decision is partly what makes that fact true. Some members have separately floated having panels confer by video call before voting, which I suspect is a different change than it appears to be, though I could not say why.

I would like a committed answer rather than a survey. Tell me what to recommend to the board — expand, hold at nine, shrink, or restructure the procedure in some way you think better — and defend it. In particular, say directly whether the trustee's inference from nine to twenty-one is valid, invalid, or valid only under conditions our foundation fails to meet, and give me the sentence or two I should actually say to him at the October meeting; say whether conferring before voting would help or hurt on your account; say what, if anything, can be done about the circularity in our estimate of reviewer accuracy, or whether that estimate should be abandoned as unrecoverable; and say whether my colleague's fourth objection is a real threat to the reasoning or a philosopher's worry with no operational consequences. Where your recommendation depends on a fact about our reviewers that you cannot have — the actual distribution of past splits, the true correlation between their errors — state the dependence explicitly and tell me what to measure before the meeting. If you think one of my four misgivings is a red herring, say so and explain why; I would rather be corrected than reassured.
