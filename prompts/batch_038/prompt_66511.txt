I'm a nurse on a mixed neuro/ICU floor at a hospital that does a lot of transplant work, and I'm also slogging through a part-time bioethics MA at night, mostly because the 3am arguments with the intensivist kept going nowhere. The thing I can't get straight: I have firm gut verdicts about who's dead and who isn't, and I'm pretty sure they don't come out of one principle. Maybe they can't. Cases, roughly in the order I've met versions of them.

1. Woman in her 40s, devastating bleed, no brainstem reflexes at all, fails the apnea test, and the vent is doing the breathing while her heart beats and she's warm and pink and her family keeps saying "look, she's right here." Dead. I say it out loud without trouble.

2. Same physiology, but she's about 22 weeks pregnant and they keep the body going, and it works — carries the pregnancy, clears a kidney infection, heals a pressure sore, handles her sodium. Dead, I still say. Except this one makes me feel like a fraud, because if a dog's body did all that we'd call it alive, and if you asked me to explain what "functioning as a single organism" means, that body is a really good exhibit for it.

3. Guy in a permanent vegetative state, three years, breathes on his own, has sleep-wake cycles, grimaces when you suction him. Alive. Obviously alive. Though if you asked me where he is, I've got nothing.

4. Locked-in patient, aware of everything, can move his eyes, on a vent. Alive — and here's the annoying part: from the foot of the bed, cases 1 and 4 look like the same thing. Both vent-dependent, both warm, both with beating hearts. I'm certain the verdicts differ and I can't state the difference in terms of anything you can see.

5. DCD donor. Vent withdrawn, flatline, we wait the five minutes our protocol says, we declare, we go upstairs. Dead at declaration, I think. But if that identical arrest happened in the corridor at minute five and we were running a code on him, I'd call him alive-and-dying, not dead. Same heart, same clock, opposite verdict, and the only difference is a decision we made about whether to try. That can't be right. Also I think it is right.

6. Anencephalic newborn — cries, breathes, heart going, essentially no cerebrum. Alive. Which is bad news for any story of mine that says losing the higher brain is what matters, and case 3 keeps dragging me toward exactly that story.

My own tentative principle, which I know is mushy: you're dead when your body has stopped operating as one organism and become a collection of organs being run by machines. Test it. I suspect it breaks on 2 and on 5, and I don't know how to patch it without patching it into saying nothing at all.

So — is there a principle that gets all six, or nearly all six? If not, which of my verdicts is the most expensive to keep and should go, and what does the surviving set look like? I'd also like your call on cases I havent listed: the barbiturate-coma patient with no reflexes while the drug's on board who'd wake up in three days; the early embryo with no brain whatsoever, which is alive, and which seems to make "no brain function" useless as a criterion on its own; a body whose brain is entirely destroyed but whose circulation is being fully handled by ECMO; and whether taking organs in case 5 violates the rule that you can only procure from someone already dead, or whether that rule was always resting on something looser than it looks. And tell me if the irreversibility/permanence thing — can't be reversed vs. won't be reversed — is a real distinction that does the work I need in case 5, or just a way of making death depend on hospital policy, which I find repulsive but might have to swallow. If your answer is that there are two deaths here, a biological one and a legal one, and practice just picks between them, say so plainly and say whether that's the truth or a cop-out. Please no reading list — I want your reconstruction of the principle, not who I should go read.
