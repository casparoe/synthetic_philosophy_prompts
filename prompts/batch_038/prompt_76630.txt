You're an expert in African philosophy of personhood who has also sat through enough standards-setting committee meetings to know how guidance actually gets written — not just someone who writes about the debate. Think carefully about this one and resist the urge to open with the standard summary of the ubuntu literature; start by brainstorming out loud for a few paragraphs about what the real cruxes are, and only then commit to a ranking.

Situation: I've been seconded for 18 months to a national bioethics council in Southern Africa, with a budget that covers roughly four people's time plus a run of workshops with clinicians, customary-law practitioners and community representatives. The council wants our work to show that a relational conception of personhood — the broad idea that a person is a person through other persons, and on the stronger versions that personhood is something achieved or graded through participation in communal life rather than a binary status you hold from conception to death — can actually bear on concrete policy, rather than sitting in the preamble as decoration.

Three candidate deliverables are on the table and we almost certainly can't do all three well:

1. Consent and assent where capacity fluctuates — advanced dementia, severe psychiatric illness, people in long-term residential care. A relational account seems to have something distinctive to say about proxy decision-making being continuous with the person rather than a replacement for them, and about personhood not evaporating the moment autonomous choice does. But the obvious failure mode is that it licenses other people to decide, which is precisely the abuse we'd be there to prevent.

2. Standing of the dead — posthumous interests, tissue retention, exhumation, burial disputes, the role of ancestors in decisions about bodies and land. This is where customary practice and statute collide hardest, and where a council's guidance either gets used or gets ignored.

3. Whether relational personhood gives us any purchase at all on synthetic agents — care robots, companion AI for the elderly. Does grounding personhood in relationships commit us to counting entities enmeshed in relationships with human persons, or is that a non sequitur?

Alongside those, here are the theoretical cruxes I keep circling, spelled out so you don't have to guess what I mean:

- Gradation versus binary status. If personhood comes in degrees and is partly conferred by the recognition of others, then the least recognised members of a community — the very old, the severely disabled, the socially isolated — end up with the least personhood, which are exactly the people the guidance is meant to protect. Is there a relational view that escapes this, or does the council have to accept a floor that recognition can't take you below, and if so what does the floor consist in, and isn't that just individualist capacity talk wearing a communitarian costume?
- Who counts as a party to the relationships that constitute personhood: only living human beings, or also the not-yet-born, the dead, non-human animals, land and ecosystems? The answer constrains deliverables 2 and 3 immedately.
- What happens under conflict. A view that grounds normativity in communal harmony or friendliness looks like it lacks a vocabulary for irreconcilable claims — a family wanting burial on ancestral land against a widow's statutory rights, say, or a community against a trial sponsor. Does the relational account need a theory of justice bolted onto it, or can rights be derived from the relationships themselves?
- Whether any of it is doing explanatory work. At least one influential critic has argued that ubuntu has been inflated into a catch-all slogan that can be made to endorse almost anything, which is exactly the failure mode a council would be publicly embarrassed by.

What I want:

(a) Which deliverable should get the bulk of the effort, and which should we cut or explicitly postpone? Rank all three and defend the order.
(b) Which of the theoretical cruxes is genuinely upstream of the rest — the one we cannot leave vague without the guidance turning incoherent — and which ones are we entitled to leave open and simply flag as unresolved?
(c) Tell me which cruxes are overrated: the places where philosophers will happily burn three years on a distinction that changes nothing a nurse or a magistrate does on a Tuesday.
(d) Be explicit about the criteria you're using and the trade-offs between them — downstream policy impact, tractability in 18 months by a mixed group that includes non-philosophers, how contested the theoretical ground is, embarrassment risk if we get it wrong, and whether the answer transfers to other domains. Then say what would change your ranking: what I might learn in the first two months of workshops that should push deliverable 2 to the top, or kill deliverable 3 outright.

No survey of the debate, and no reading list, bibliography or citations — I can't check anything and I already know the general shape of the literature. I want a defended agenda: what to work on, in what order, and why that order rather than another.
