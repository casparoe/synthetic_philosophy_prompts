I'm running a one-off 90-minute session for about 24 second-years on a team-taught course called "societal resilience" — a mix of history, anthropology, and a handful of environmental science students. Roughly half of them think collapse is a straightforward thing that happens to societies; the other half have picked up enough of the critical literature to think the word just describes a society changing in ways that inconvenience the people who kept the records. They argue past each other constantly and neither side has anything to say to the other. I want to design a session where they run into the actual problem themselves instead of hearing me describe it.

My own working framing, which they should not hear until the very end: a claim that a society collapsed is never just a report. It's a report relativized to four separate choices, and the disagreement between my two camps is almost entirely about the choices rather than about the facts. The choices are (1) which ledger you're counting — monumental construction, written output, political centralization, long-distance trade, population, average skeletal health, continuity of settlement, continuity of craft or language; (2) whose situation the ledger tracks — the palace, the literate elite, the farmers, the whole population averaged together; (3) which window you're looking through — a decade, a century, three centuries; and (4) what baseline you're calling the normal, since "decline" is only decline from some peak, and peaks are rarely good for everybody. Change any one of the four and the verdict flips.

Here's what I need from you:

Invent three fictional societies with invented names and invented dossiers — I specifically don't want the real cases, because two of my students have read enough about them to coast and argue from memory. Each dossier should be a short set of data cards covering several ledgers over a long stretch of time, with the ledgers spread across cards in a way I control.

Design the mechanic. My instinct is that teams get the same society but different subsets of the cards, and each team has to produce something concrete under scarcity — say a 25-word museum wall label plus a yes/no verdict — so the plenary is full of teams that looked at the same place and same years and came out with opposite verdicts and no idea why. Build that out properly, or replace it with something better if you think there's a sharper version. Add a scoring or betting scheme using tokens or slips, nothing that needs real money, and tell me what the scoring is actually supposed to be teaching them. Then a second round where a new card arrives that forces revision, and I'd like at least one of the three dossiers to be constructed so that it resists the easy reading — one case where the four choices don't rescue you and something genuinely awful happened, because otherwise the lesson they'll take home is that collapse is just a word, which is exactly the cheap conclusion I don't want.

Give me minute-by-minute timings, a materials list I can print on a Monday morning with no technology in the room, and the debrief, mapping specific moments from the rounds onto the four choices and onto where my two camps actually differ. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it with 24 people and how to handle it, and be frank about which feature of this idea a card game can't reproduce — my guess is something about the real cases' evidence being fragmentary and contested rather than neatly dealt out, but you tell me if that's the big one or if there's a worse gap I'm not seeing.

Please write the whole thing, including all the handout and card text, in American English.
