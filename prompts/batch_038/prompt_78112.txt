I'm a fourth-year PhD student who spends about half my time on mechanistic interpretability and half on philosophy of mind, and I've got a dispute in my lab that has been running for eight months with zero movement, so I want a diagnosis rather than another round of arguments.

Setup. Our group is writing a paper claiming that a certain coarse-grained "feature" — a direction in activation space that our sparse autoencoder picks out, roughly a flatter-the-user / avoid-disagreement direction — causes a specific downstream behavior in a model, and that this feature-level story is what lets us predict when the behavior shows up in new contexts. A senior colleague of mine insists this is loose talk: on his view the only genuine causes are the actual weights and activations and what they physically do to the next layer's numbers, and the feature is a summary we impose on that, not an extra thing that pushes anything. He's not saying our results are wrong. He agrees with every datapoint we have — the same activation patching numbers, the same ablations, the same generalization results. He just says none of that shows the feature is a cause, because you can't intervene on the feature without also intervening on the substrate: patching a direction nudges everything that's superposed on it and everything correlated with it, so it's never a clean, surgical intervention on the feature alone. By his standard the feature is a description of the weights doing the causing. My reply has been that if "can't be intervened on independently of the substrate" disqualifies something from being a cause, then individual neurons are disqualified too, and arguably only the fundamental physics survives, which would make the entire practice of interpretability — and frankly most of biology — unintelligible, which can't be right. He says that's a slippery slope and not an answer. Neither of us has budged, and we keep having the same conversation about once a month.

I keep thinking about Hofstadter's careenium thought experiment in Gödel, Escher, Bach — the substrate full of little simmballs bouncing around and colliding and forming new ones, where the question is whether the simmball-level collisions ever really do any pushing, or whether the careenium's own physics does all the pushing and the symbol story is just an irresistibly convenient way of talking about it. That seems to me to be exactly what my colleague and I are fighting about, with a transformer in place of the careenium.

Here's my working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and I want you to attack it if it's wrong: that this isn't a factual disagreement at all, and possibly not even a substantive one — that we're each using "cause" to mean different things (something like difference-making relative to a chosen set of variables versus something like production by the underlying process), or that we're answering two different questions under one heading, one about causal explanation and one about what exists, and that if we wrote down our variable sets explicitly we'd discover we agree on everything that matters. Or maybe I'm flattering myself and what's really going on is bedrock intuitions about levels of description that neither of us can argue for.

I know the general shape of the philosophy here already (the exclusion worry, multiple realizability, the interventionist accounts, the coarse-graining stuff that argues higher-level causation can come out stronger than the lower level) so please don't survey it, use it.
