I am setting a case study for an advanced seminar on the philosophy of mind, and I would like to see the analysis worked through in full before I assign it. The episode is the Loebner Prize — the annual competition in "restricted Turing test" that ran from its first contest in 1991 until the prize lapsed around 2020 — and the theory is the behavioral criterion for mind in the form Turing gave it: that indistinguishability from a human interlocutor under appropriate conversational conditions is at least a sufficient condition for thinking, and that the criterion is to be preferred precisely because it substitutes an empirically answerable question for one that is not answerable at all.

Here are the facts I intend to hand my students, so please work from them rather than around them. The first competition was held in 1991 at the Computer Museum in Boston. Ten interlocutors took part — six computer programs and four human confederates — facing ten judges who, like the confederates, had been recruited by newspaper advertisement and screened for the absence of any special expertise in computer science. Because the organizing committee judged the unrestricted test impassable at the then-current state of the art, it added restrictions of its own: the conversation was confined in topic and vocabulary and capped at a few minutes. The scoring instrument was not a binary verdict. Each judge ranked all ten terminals from least to most human-like and then drew a line across the ranking where they believed the humans began. The rankings disagreed with one another wildly, and the confederate whom the field collectively judged most human of all was placed below the line — classified as a machine — by two of the ten judges. The programs that took the annual prize, that year and in several that followed, were of the ELIZA lineage and won by reflection, deflection and studied evasiveness rather than by substantive exchange; the contest's most persistent critics charged that entrants were rewarded for pretense rather than for understanding. Twenty-three years later, at a Royal Society event in 2014, a program presented under the persona of a thirteen-year-old Ukrainian boy with imperfect English was credited by its organizers with having persuaded a third of the judges in five-minute text conversations, and the press duly reported the first passage of the Turing test. Many researchers disputed the claim, arguing that the persona served as a preemptive excuse for exactly the deficits a longer conversation would have exposed.

What I want is the case analysis, running in both directions.

First, the mapping, done carefully and without sentiment. Who are the relevant parties — entrants, confederates, judges, organizers — and what role does each play in the evidential structure? What, on the behavioral criterion, was actually being tested in 1991, given that the committee had already edited the stimulus, and consider in particular whether restrictions intended to give the programs a fighting chance may instead have degraded the human baseline more than the machine one. Is a verdict generated by a ranking-plus-line procedure a verdict about the machine or about the judges' folk-psychological expectations, and how could one tell the difference from inside the data?

Second, and this is the reading I want pressed hardest, because the standard seminar treatment misses it: treat the test as an instrument with a false-positive and a false-negative rate rather than as a definition. The 1991 record supplies a measured error rate against known positives — four interlocutors who were unquestionably human, one of them misclassified by twenty percent of the judges. What does an instrument that misclassifies its own calibration standards license you to infer when it classifies something else as human? Does the answer change if the criterion is read definitionally rather than evidentially — if passing constitutes thinking instead of supporting it? Run both readings through the same numbers and tell me where they diverge.

Third, the reverse direction. Which features of the Loebner record does the behavioral criterion handle well, which does it misdescribe, and on which is it simply silent? I have in mind especially the persona maneuver of 2014: a behavioral criterion appears to lack the resources to say that announcing yourself a non-native thirteen-year-old lowers the bar rather than clears it, yet that judgment seems unavoidable. I have in mind also the fact that the organizers made the test passable by weakening it — a move for which the familiar thought experiments prepare you not at all, since in those the stimulus is fixed and the interlocutor cannot negotiate the terms. Does the episode confirm the criterion, constitute an anomaly for it, or expose a use to which it was never meant to be put? Say which, and say what would have to be true of the criterion for this record to count as a straightforward confirmation.

Be explicit wherever the theory runs out rather than supplying something plausible in its place; I would rather have a candid statement of the gap than an ingenious reconstruction. Something on the order of a substantial seminar handout. No bibliography, please — I am not asking for sources and could not verify them if you gave me any.
