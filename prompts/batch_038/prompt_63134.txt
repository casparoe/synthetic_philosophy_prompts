I convene a taught MA seminar in formal epistemology at a UK university: fourteen to eighteen students, one ninety-minute session, week three. They have completed a term of propositional modal logic, so the K and T axioms, ◇ and □ and talk of accessible worlds are all comfortable notation for them; but nothing specifically epistemic has been covered, and nobody in the room has met the knowability paradox. My aim is that they reconstruct the argument themselves as something that happens to them during play, rather than watching me derive it on the board. I therefore want a runnable in-class game, not a lecture and not a worksheet.

The seed I have been turning over is an announcement market. Truths are distributed around the room in sealed form — some on cards, some as facts about the room's own arrangement that are discoverable but have not yet been discovered. Students score by standing up and announcing a truth, and the scoring is rigged so that a novel, as-yet-unannounced truth is worth far more than one already in circulation, which should build pressure towards claims of the form "such-and-such holds, and nobody here knows that it holds". The mechanic I most want built in is a verification rule: an announcement of a conjunction counts as two announcements, and each conjunct must be separately demonstrated to the class, so that the fragile claim blows up in front of them rather than merely being declared to blow up.

What I would like from you:

1. The complete game. Materials, all of it printable or assemblable in under an hour by one person; the exact wording of every card and instruction sheet; the round structure; the verification procedure; and a scoring scheme requiring no real money — tokens, tallies or something else, but tell me what the points are ultimately worth to the students and make it worth enough to matter to them.

2. A decision, with reasons, on whether the claimable propositions should be pre-printed by me, authored by the students during the session, or a mixture. I can see it both ways: authored claims risk the room wandering away from the argument altogether, while pre-printed ones risk the students guessing my intention from the wording alone.

3. A minute-by-minute plan for the ninety minutes, including how long the discovery phase may be allowed to run before it stalls.

4. A debrief mapping the game onto the argument step by step. I want this as an explicit two-column table, game event on the left, logical move on the right, covering factivity, distribution over conjunction, the construction of the crucial conjunction, and the collapse into the conclusion that every truth is known. Only at that point are they given the paradox's name. The debrief should then lay out the two broad lines of response — restricting the knowability principle so that it does not apply to truths whose being known would undermine their truth, and declining to restrict it and living with the verificationist conclusion — and say honestly which of the two my game has already biased them towards, and why.

5. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this. In particular: what happens if nobody ever picks up a fragile claim, so the central moment simply fails to occur? I need a forcing move that does not amount to my telling them the point. Also tell me how the design scales if only eight students turn up.

6. A frank statement of which feature of the argument the activity cannot reproduce and which I shall have to supply verbally. My own suspicion is that the game can only ever show these truths perishing when they are learned, whereas the argument needs the stronger and rather different claim that there is no accessible circumstance in which such a truth is both true and known — but tell me if you think the more serious gap lies elsewhere, for instance in the fact that fourteen students are a poor model of a single knower.

Two constraints. Nobody should hear the words knowability, Fitch or verificationism before the debrief; if your design makes those terms unavoidable, redesign it. And assume one student has already read about the paradox and will want to spoil it, so build in a role for that person which keeps them usefully occupied and quiet.

Please write everything in British English, including all text intended for printing, and please do not append a bibliography or reading list: I shall supply the literature myself.
