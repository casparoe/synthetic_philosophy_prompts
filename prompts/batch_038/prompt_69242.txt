I teach an upper-level metaphysics seminar and I'm putting together the final. I want one essay question on van Inwagen's special composition question that can't be answered by lining up the usual four options (nihilism, universalism, composition only when there's a life, and 'it's vague, so there's no fact of the matter'). The trick I want to build in: the question stipulates a candidate answer nobody in the literature has actually held, and the students have to interrogate it rather than slot it into the taxonomy.

Here's the view I want stipulated in the question — call it the Signalling View: some xs compose something just in case, over some finite stretch of time, each of the xs is both affected by and has an effect on each of the others, and no segment of those causal chains runs through anything that isn't one of the xs.

The question should force them to work out (a) whether this is a real restriction or whether it quietly collapses into universalism or into something mind-dependent, (b) whether the clause about chains not running through outsiders is a bug or actually the only thing keeping the view off of the vagueness objection, since it makes whether these xs compose depend on what else is in the room, and (c) what it says about a chair, a conversation between two people, and a scattered set of parts that only exchange signals once a millennium.

Give me the question with the view stated in it, a model answer around 650 words, and a marking scheme out of 20 that names the specific moves that separate an A from a B — including what a B answer will do with the chair case that an A answer will see through. Students get 45 minutes.
