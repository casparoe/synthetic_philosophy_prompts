I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and lately I keep getting dragged into higher-ed funding stories, so this one's been rattling around in my head. Bear with me, it's a bit long.

Here's the thing bugging me. Take the exact same event: a 19-year-old starts a bachelor's degree. In Germany (since the last state dropped tuition, around 2014) she pays nothing, though there's still a semester contribution for the student union and the transit pass. In Denmark she pays nothing and the state actually cuts her a monthly check for being a student. In England she's billed something like £9,000-something a year, but nobody hands over money — the state lends it to her, and she repays 9% of whatever she earns above a threshold until the balance is wiped after 30 or 40 years. In the US she borrows too, but a federal student loan basically cant be discharged in bankruptcy: you can walk away from a house, you can walk away from credit card debt, and you cannot hand back the degree, unless you can prove undue hardship, which courts make nearly impossible to do.

Same activity, three countries, three completely different legal-financial machines bolted onto it. What I want to know is what each machine presupposes. Not which one performs better — I can read an IFS report as well as anyone. I mean: what has to be true about a degree, about the person holding it, and about her relationship to everyone who didn't go, for each of these arrangements to be the sensible thing to do rather than a bizarre one?

Things I'd specifically like you to take on:

1. The English one is officially a *loan*, not a tax. But it's priced and collected like a graduate tax — income-contingent, no collateral to seize, written off on a schedule, and the government's own estimate is that a big share of the nominal total never comes back, which is why the national accountants eventually made them book the expected non-repayment as spending on the day the cash goes out instead of treating it as lending. What view of the graduate does insisting it's a loan presuppose, when the design contradicts most of what makes a loan a loan? Is the word doing conceptual work here or only political work?

2. Germany's version gets described as free, but it's funded out of general taxation, including from people who never set foot in a lecture hall and will earn less than the graduate her whole life — and the graduate "pays" later through a progressive income tax she'd owe anyway, made steeper by the fact that she now draws a graduate salary. Isn't that also an income-contingent charge on graduates, just pooled and rendered invisible? I keep coming back to the thought that its not about who pays but about whether the benefit of a degree is the kind of thing a person can own at all. Can you push on that? Is there a coherent notion of a degree as private property, and if the German arrangement denies it, what does it have to assume instead — that an educated populace is an asset the state produces for itself?

3. The American non-dischargeability rule looks to me like the one place where the private-ownership view is stated out loud and then has to be held up by force, because the collateral is your earning capacity, which is precisely the thing that can't be repossessed or sold. Is that the right read? What theory of the person does it need — something like you and your human capital being identical, so that a lien on the capital is a lien on you?

4. Where do these practices undercut themselves? Denmark pays people a stipend to study, which sounds like the state treating education as labor, yet the same Nordic countries are the ones most insistent that education is a right rather than a job. And Baden-Württemberg charges non-EU students tuition while charging Germans nothing — what does sorting students by passport presuppose about who the "we" is that the degree benefits, and is that consistent with the public-asset story, or does it quietly concede that this is an investment in particular named individuals after all?

What I want back: reconstruct the implicit theory of each arrangement as precisely as you can; then be honest about which parts the practice strictly requires versus which parts it merely fits comfortably with, since I suspect a lot of this is underdetermined and several incompatible theories could license the same fee schedule. Flag the places where the practice is philosophically silent and only looks like it's asserting something. And finally tell me what would have to change institutionally if a country decided to swap one of these theories for another — if England admitted it was running a graduate tax, or if Germany admitted it was funding individuals rather than a public good.

No bibliography, please, I'm not going to cite you and I couldn't check anything anyway. Just lay the argument out cleanly enough that I could rewrite it in my own words.
