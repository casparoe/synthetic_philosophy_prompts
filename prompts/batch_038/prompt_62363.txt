I am revising the second half of a novel, and two of my priests have begun to trouble me in the same way.

The first is trusted by his whole parish with confidences, and across two hundred pages he never once betrays one. The reason is his housekeeper: she burns the letters that might have tempted him and turns away at the door the visitors who might have asked him a dangerous question. She dies in Chapter Nine. Within a fortnight he has sold a secret to the police, and he is astonished by how little it cost him.

The second is trusted just as widely. He too never keeps a confidence that touches on state security, because his bishop has issued a standing order that any such confidence is to be reported at once: the moment the words are spoken in the confessional, his obligation of silence lapses, before he has any occasion to struggle with it. Outside that narrow class he is impeccable.

My readers disagree violently. Some say the first man was never trustworthy and that I have cheated them; some say the same of the second; a few insist the second is the only one of the two who has a character at all. One detail ought to complicate the quarrel further: the first grieves his housekeeper and would reinstate her screening tomorrow if he could, while the second, if his bishop rescinded the order, would report those same secrets on his own authority.

Which of these two, if either, must my novel treat as lacking the virtue rather than as merely prevented from showing it — and is there any fact about a person, over and above what he would and would not do when tested, that settles the answer? I would like advice I can use at the level of scenes and sentences, not a general defense of the draft. Please answer in American English.
