I facilitate a 75-minute workshop for about twenty adults (a community reading group, mostly non-specialists) on multipolar traps and races to the bottom, and I want an in-class game that makes the structure of the trap felt rather than described.

Some constraints on the design:

- It cannot be a relabelled iterated prisoner's dilemma or a tragedy-of-the-commons commons-grazing game. My group has met both before, and more to the point neither reproduces the feature I care about. In the standard prisoner's dilemma, defecting from the trap is individually costly, so the trap looks like a moral failing that better people would escape. I want participants to discover that the trap is an equilibrium: that after everyone has seen the collective outcome and regrets it, each player's own best move is still to keep doing exactly what produced it, and that unilateral exit is worse for the exiter than staying in. Nobody in the game should be stupid or vicious; every choice should be locally sensible.
- There should be an asymmetry the players can feel: concentrated, visible gains to the party that pushes hardest, against diffuse, delayed, partly invisible costs borne by people who are not seated at the table and cannot bargain.
- Two or three rounds in which the options themselves change would be welcome, so the group watches the floor drop out from under the cooperative alternative rather than merely failing to pick it.

Materials should be trivial — index cards, a whiteboard, tokens — and payoffs should be small integers readable at a glance, with no arithmetic required of me in real time. Points or tokens only, no real money. The rules are handed out or announced round by round; participants should not be told the name of the thing, or hear the words "coordination failure" or "race to the bottom," until the debrief.

Please deliver: the full rules and a round-by-round timeline with timings; the payoff structure and what a facilitator's score sheet should look like; a debrief that maps particular moments in the play onto particular parts of the concept, including the question participants will inevitably raise — why didn't you all just agree not to compete, and what is actually wrong with that agreement; a forecast of what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it; and a frank statement of which feature of a real multipolar trap the activity cannot reproduce and I will have to supply in talk.

Finally, tell me how confident you are that this design produces the intended discovery rather than an unrelated one, and single out the part of it you are least sure will work as written.
