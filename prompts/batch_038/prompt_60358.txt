I convene, within my department, a standing research network on Marx and technology, and this spring we are running an adversarial collaboration between two interpretive camps that have been talking past each other in print for the better part of two decades. The two convenors have agreed to sign a single joint statement. I have been asked to specify what it must contain, and I should like you to draft the statement itself — not an analysis of it, not a verdict on which camp is right, and not a dramatized exchange between them.

For reference, and with the proviso that my glosses are provisional and should be refined wherever refinement is needed:

Camp P, the productive-forces reading: Marx's historical theory gives explanatory primacy to the development of the productive forces; machinery is progressive in itself, and capitalism's indictment is that its relations of production come to fetter forces that automation could otherwise develop without limit; the emancipatory horizon Marx identifies is the expansion of disposable time made possible by automated production, and the notebooks of 1857–58 are him at his clearest about this.

Camp V, the value-form reading: Marx's object of critique is the value form and the reduction of concrete labor to value, not poverty or inefficiency considered as such; machinery is the specific instrument by which capital subsumes and disciplines living labor, and its development is shaped by the value relation rather than driving it from outside; emancipation is the abolition of value and of labor as the substance of wealth, not the maximization of output, and the same notebooks are Marx diagnosing the dissolution of capital's own measure rather than forecasting abundance.

Neither camp denies what the other insists upon most loudly: Camp P does not deny that Marx wrote as a critic of exploitation and of the degradation of work, and Camp V does not deny that Marx credited capitalism with a colossal development of society's productive powers. Their dispute is over which of these is explanatorily and normatively basic.

My own difficulty, which I want the statement to confront rather than smooth over, is this. Both camps accept that Marx held machinery under capitalism to raise productivity while degrading the position of the worker, and both accept that he thought the law of value would come under strain as automation proceeds. Yet one says the horizon is abundant disposable time and the other says it is the abolition of value and of labor as such, and both point to the same notebooks. If there is a real disagreement here beyond emphasis and rhetoric, the statement should locate the smallest divergence that generates the rest of it. If there is not, it should say so plainly — while remaining something both convenors can sign, which means it cannot simply declare the dispute empty.

The statement must work from two passages, both from the notebooks of 1857–58, in the section on fixed capital and machinery near the close of the chapter on capital that is customarily called the Fragment on Machines. They are given here in English translation:

"The exchange of living labour for objectified labour – i.e. the positing of social labour in the form of the contradiction of capital and wage labour – is the ultimate development of the value-relation and of production resting on value. Its presupposition is – and remains – the mass of direct labour time, the quantity of labour employed, as the determinant factor in the production of wealth. But to the degree that large industry develops, the creation of real wealth comes to depend less on labour time and on the amount of labour employed than on the power of the agencies set in motion during labour time […]"

"Thus, the specific mode of working here appears directly as becoming transferred from the worker to capital in the form of the machine, and his own labour capacity devalued thereby."

Please produce the following, in this order.

1. A preamble of no more than three sentences, in the first person plural, stating what the collaboration set out to do, what method it used, and what it does not claim to have settled.

2. An agreed-claims section: no fewer than twelve numbered claims that both camps can sign, each a single declarative sentence, each tagged [T] if it concerns what Marx's texts say or do, [C] if it is a conceptual or methodological claim about how to read him, or [E] if it concerns the economic or historical world. Diplomatic platitudes do not count. Every claim must be one that a partisan of either camp would find it at least mildly costly to have conceded, or that an informed third party would find non-obvious. At least three of the [T] claims must concern the two passages above.

3. A disagreements table with exactly these columns: # | Issue | Camp P formulation | Camp V formulation | Character | Crux. In Issue, state the disputed matter in vocabulary that begs no question against either side. In each formulation column, state that camp's position in words its own adherents would sign, without caricature and without borrowing the other camp's terminology to do the work. In Character, write exactly one of substantive, verbal, or mixed; if verbal or mixed, add a clause naming what dissolves, or what residue survives, once the terminology is fixed. In Crux, give the identifier of the entry in the JSON register below that would settle or most narrow the disagreement, or none if you judge no agreed test to bear on it. Where a formulation both would sign is one that neither would have thought worth writing down, move it to section 2 or discard it; do not pad the table with manufactured agreement.

4. A JSON object with the single key crux_register, whose value is an array of at least four objects, each with exactly these keys: id (C1, C2, and so on); crux (the sub-question, phrased so that a determinate answer would move at least one camp, and so that both camps accept the question as well posed); shared_test (the passage, argument, or contemporary case that both accept as bearing on the crux, together with a sentence on why each accepts it); kind (either textual, if the test is what a careful reading shows, or worldly, if the test is how things stand with automation today); prediction_P and prediction_V (what each camp records in advance that the test will show); falsifier_P and falsifier_V (what would count, by that camp's own lights, against that camp); and downstream (which numbered rows of the table in section 3 would collapse or narrow if this crux were resolved, and how). At least two entries must have kind worldly, and at least one of those must take as its shared_test the current wave of machine systems performing work formerly counted as skilled cognitive labor, including Marx's image a little later in the same passage of the worker stepping to one side of the automated process and relating to it as one who watches over and regulates it rather than as its chief actor. At least one prediction in the register must be about the world rather than about the texts.

5. A JSON object with the single key shared_glossary, keyed by these five terms: productive forces; abstract labor; real subsumption; general intellect; industrial reserve army. Each value is an object with three keys: signed_definition (a definition both camps can sign, in one or two sentences, or null if none exists); divergence (if signed_definition is null, the two competing definitions and the precise point at which they part company; otherwise, the residual difference in how each camp expects the term to be used in argument); and load_bearing (the row number in section 3 that turns on this term's usage).

6. A closing section of 200 to 300 words identifying the single textual or conceptual divergence from which the rest of the dispute can be generated, and explaining why you take it to be generative rather than merely characteristic. Then state which rows of the table you judge to have been about words all along, and what each camp was reaching for when it used the word.

Two constraints on sourcing. Do not name scholars, schools, or secondary works, do not give page numbers, and do not quote Marx beyond the two passages supplied; where you refer to his other writings, use familiar titles and locate passages by part or chapter only where you are confident of the location, and otherwise describe the passage. Note for orientation, and without quoting it, that Marx remarked in his 1847 polemic against Proudhon that different milling technology goes with different ruling classes, hand-mills with feudal lords and steam-mills with industrial capitalists; if you make use of that remark, treat it as a contested datum rather than as a settled proof for either camp.

The statement will be circulated to a British readership and issued by a United Kingdom press, so please write all of it — preamble, table cells, JSON values, glossary, and closing section — in British English, spelling and idiom alike. Aim for 1,600 to 2,200 words in total.
