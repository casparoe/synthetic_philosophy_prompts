I run activities at a 60-ish bed memory care unit and there's a policy mix here I can't get straight in my head, and everytime I've raised it I get told "it's just what works." Let me lay out what we actually do.

By the side door there's a bus shelter: bench, route sign, timetable. No bus has ever come there and it isn't on any route. It's there because a lot of our residents get a fixed idea that they have to go home, or get to the factory for their shift, and they'll push at the door and get aggitated; instead we walk them out to the shelter, sit with them, and after ten or fifteen minutes most of them are happy to come back inside. Same idea indoors: we have a reminiscence room kitted out like a kitchen from around 1960, and staff are trained not to correct people. If a resident asks where her husband is, and he died eleven years ago, you don't tell her — you ask about him, you let her talk, you go with what she's got.

Now the same company is completely different in other places. At admission every new resident gets a plain-language explanation of the diagnosis and the expected progression, and we're told not to soften it. Anything non-routine needs documented consent. And if someone gets hurt — a fall, a medication error — there's a rule that the family hears exactly what happened within the week, no smoothing over.

So what picture of what makes a life going well does the shelter and the not-correcting rule assume? Reconstruct it as precisely as you can without me needing any jargon, then tell me which rival pictures it rules out or just ignores — and specifically whether the assumption is that only the quality of felt experience matters, or something weaker than that. Then: is the clash with the diagnosis and injury disclosure rules a genuine inconsistency, or can one account license both, and if so what's doing the work that keeps them apart? My niece, who's doing philosophy at uni, said we'd basically built an experience machine out of a bus shelter and I couldn't tell whether she meant it as a compliment or an accusation.

Also tell me where the practice is underdetermined — where several quite different theories of wellbeing would all sign off on the shelter, so the shelter isn't evidence for any of them. And what would actually have to change if we decided the other side was right: the building, the training, the forms, the shift handover.

One last thing, because it's live for us. A resident was very upset about her dog last month and a carer told her the dog was fine, living at her sister's place. It calmed her right down. Families have started asking whether that's allowed now, as a general tool. Does whatever theory is behind the bus shelter license making stuff up like that, or does the shelter work because of some feature the invented dog doesn't have? I'd like a real answer on that one, not a shrug.
