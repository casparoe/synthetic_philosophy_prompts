So I'm running a 110-minute methods workshop for second-years — about 24 of them, mixed history and classics, they've done one intro course so they can say "primary source" and "bias" but they have basically no idea how texts actually get transmitted to us. They've read some Herodotus and Thucydides in translation and gotten the usual message that Thucydides was the rigorous one. I want an activity where the difficulty of source criticism happens to them, instead of me telling them it's difficult.

The shape I have in mind: hand them a dossier about one event in an invented ancient polity, make them answer questions about it under a scoring rule, then pull the rug out with the transmission history. Please build me the whole thing:

- the invented setting and the event (a palace coup, a famine, a temple robbery, a botched embassy — your pick, but set it somewhere made-up so nobody can shortcut with background knowledge, and make it specific enough that they care)
- eight sources, roughly 120 words each, one per printable sheet, written as excerpts and fragments rather than as tidy modern summaries
- six questions the groups have to answer, where some are answerable from the corpus, some only within a range, and at least two are permanently unanswerable — and don't label which is which anywhere they can see
- a confidence-weighted scoring scheme that doesn't need real money but makes overconfidence actually cost them, so that writing "the sources say he was assassinated" isn't free
- a two-round structure: round one they answer from the texts alone, then I hand out the provenance metadata — which copies survive, what century they are, where they were found, which text is a summary of a lost one, who's quoting whom — and they have to revise. The revision is the lesson.

The dossier needs to be rigged in specific ways, and I'd like you to tell me which piece of rigging is doing which pedagogical work:

1. one text that looks like an independent witness but is really an epitome of another, lost one, so a "two sources agree" is one source counted twice
2. two texts independent in content but sharing an odd phrase or a copyist's slip, so you can actually reconstruct a common ancestor
3. one author who is dead reliable about what he saw and useless about what he heard secondhand, with the crucial fact landing on the wrong side of that line
4. one fragment that survives only because a hostile writer quoted it in order to attack it
5. a silence that means something — an administrative archive that would have mentioned the event if it happened and doesn't, sitting next to an archive that just covers a different domain entirely, so they have to seperate "expected mention" from "no coverage"
6. a physical trace datable only to a window that straddles the event
7. one source with an obvious motive to invent the whole story that nevertheless lets slip a genuine administrative detail

Then a debrief that maps moments from the room onto the real methodological moves. I want them leaving able to explain why absence of evidence is sometimes evidence and sometimes nothing at all, and why you can't count witnesses before you know the transmission history. Also tell me: what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it (I'm assuming they'll blend everything into one smooth narrative, and that someone games the scoring by putting 50% on everything — how do I head that off), how the timing should run minute by minute, and an honest paragraph on what this activity just can't reproduce about real source criticism that I'll have to supply some other way.
