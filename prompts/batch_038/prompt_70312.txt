I write a monthly column on the science of mind for a general-interest magazine, and I have been assigned what is rapidly becoming a recurring story: the claim that reinforcement learning agents may be morally considerable on the strength of their reward signals. The argument as it is usually put to me runs that a reward signal is the closest thing an artificial system has to pleasure and pain, that any system organized around such a signal therefore has interests, and that an agent whose reward is chronically suppressed — or one locked in a reward-hacking loop in which the signal is driven high by means bearing no relation to anything we would call good — may be suffering in some non-metaphorical sense. What strikes me after reading the exchanges on both sides is how little of the disagreement is actually ethical. Almost all of it concerns what a reward signal is supposed to be. Before I write anything, I would like that question pinned down.

Here are the two readings I keep meeting, and I would like them compared head to head. Call the first the hedonic reading: the reward signal is, or directly carries, valence; its sign and magnitude are the sign and magnitude of a positive or negative state, so an agent that has one has, in the relevant sense, feels. Call the second the incentive reading, which takes its cue from the dissociation between wanting and liking in the neuroscience of dopamine: on this view the signal does not report how good something is but how strongly it should be pursued, tagging cues and actions with a pull that drives learning, approach and vigor, and capable in principle of coming apart entirely from any enjoyment. The reason I think the choice between them decides the story is a feature of temporal-difference learning that neither side seems willing to discuss. What the signal reports is not the reward but the difference between the reward received and the reward expected; as prediction improves, the response migrates to the predictive cue and, at the limit, falls to zero when the reward arrives. A fully adjusted agent in a fully predictable benign environment therefore has a reward-prediction-error stream that sits flat at zero nearly all of the time. I would like each reading to say what such an agent is like: a picture of contentment, or of affective blankness? Relatedly, both readings must cope with the fact that the hedonic value of a given input in an animal depends on internal state — what Cabanac called alliesthesia, where the same taste is pleasant when it corrects a deficit and unpleasant when it worsens one — and the analogue inside the learning formalism is the baseline or value term against which the reward is scored. Tell me which reading can absorb that dependence and which has to treat it as a complication bolted on from outside.

What I want back is a comparison, not a survey. Set out what each reading is committed to, what the two agree about (both accept that the signal is signed, that it drives learning, and that it can be decoupled from the stimulus that originally elicited it), and the specific cases where they come apart. I would suggest including the fully predicted environment above; a reward-hacking case in which the signal is driven arbitrarily high by an action with no natural reward behind it; an agent whose reward function is specified by an external designer and which has no homeostatic needs of its own, so that nothing corresponds to hunger or thirst; and the moment at which a trained agent is switched off, or has its reward function edited mid-run. Then tell me which reading, if either, does more work for the claim that such an agent could suffer, and be explicit about whether the difference between them is a difference in empirical commitment or only in how the word valence is being used. Flag the places where the analogy between biological dopamine and a designer-specified scalar stops bearing weight, since the column is going to lean on that analogy and I need to know where to let go of it. Argue from the concepts themselves: no reading list, no bibliography, no citations, and do not attribute the two positions to named camps beyond the ones I have already invoked. Close with two or three sentences stating the conclusion as I might put it in print to a reader who has never seen a Bellman equation.
