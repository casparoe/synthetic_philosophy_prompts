Last spring the board of the community foundation where I serve on the allocation committee wrote one sentence into our grantmaking policy: where two programs are comparable in cost per outcome, priority goes to the one serving people who are worse off. It has since become obvious that the sentence decides nothing. Each year we divide roughly fourteen million dollars between services for people at the very bottom — rough sleepers with no benefits and complex needs — and services for households that are poor but one shock from ruin, and several allocations now hinge entirely on how "worse off" gets spelled out.

Before we commission outside advice, I want a defended agenda rather than a survey. Rank exactly five open questions in the philosophy of priority and equality by how much their resolution would change where our money goes. Take the candidates from wherever the leverage actually is — whether wellbeing has a fixed zero point and unit we could write a weighting function against, whether priority should be applied before or after a program's risks are resolved, what the leveling-down objection really disposes of and whether our policy needs equality at all, whether to prioritize those badly off overall or badly off in one respect, what happens above a sufficiency threshold — and add ones I have not thought of if they rank higher. For each, say what turns on it in a portfolio like ours, and mark whether it is settled among people who work on it or genuinely contested; I would rather see the disagreement flagged than have one side handed to me as consensus.

Format: five numbered items of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion.
