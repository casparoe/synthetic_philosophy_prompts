For eleven years I worked as a registrar, and this spring our institution was folded into a national restitution process, which has left me responsible for articulating an ethical rationale for a position I have held mostly on instinct. The episode I keep returning to is the German–Nigerian settlement over the Benin Bronzes, and I want it analyzed through one lens in particular: the non-identity problem, and the person-affecting restriction that generates it.

The facts as I have assembled them, so that you need not hedge over them. The objects were taken by British forces who sacked Benin City in 1897; most reached continental Europe not by German military action but by purchase at auction in the years that followed, so the German holders bought rather than looted. In July 2022 Germany and Nigeria signed an agreement transferring ownership of the more than a thousand pieces in German collections to Nigeria, with most to remain in place for the time being as Nigerian loans; further objects were handed over at a state ceremony in Abuja that December. In the spring of 2023 it emerged that a Nigerian presidential order had vested ownership of all of them, transferred and still to be transferred, in the reigning Oba of Benin, on the ground that they were taken from the royal palace — a move that unsettled several German institutions and raised the question whether the objects had been returned to a state or to a dynasty. Meanwhile a group of African-descended Americans has argued that the Kingdom of Benin was itself a slave-raiding state whose brass came largely from that trade, and that the pieces therefore belong, if anywhere, to the descendants of those it enslaved. Note too that Nigeria, the recipient, came into being decades after 1897 and was neither the looter nor the looted.

Here is what I am after. First, the mapping: on a corrective-justice model of this settlement, who is the wrongdoer, who the victim, what precisely the wrong consists in, whether it was completed in 1897 or is still continuing, and who in 2022 could intelligibly receive a remedy. Second, the objection at full strength — that no one now living was made worse off by the expedition, because every living claimant owes their existence to the causal chain it set in motion, so there is no one to whom a correction could be owed. I want that taken seriously rather than waved away: does it bite here, where the objects are still in European stores, where the Oba's claim runs through a persisting office rather than through identifiable individuals, and where several of the justifications actually offered for the transfer were not framed in terms of harm at all? Third, run it back the other way. Does this episode expose the non-identity problem as an artifact of a framework designed for choices about whom to bring into existence, and misfiring when applied to a historical causal chain nobody chose? Or does it expose the restitution debate as having borrowed a vocabulary of harm it cannot cash — in which case, tell me which normative basis the practice is actually leaning on, and whether the rival claims of the Oba, the Nigerian state, and the descendants of Benin's slaves can all be accommodated by it. I would rather be told where the theory simply has nothing to say about this case than have it stretched to cover everything.
