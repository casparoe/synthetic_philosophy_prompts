I am drafting a short essay on embedded agency for a technically literate audience, and I want critical comments on the argument rather than encouragement. The draft follows; my specific requests come after it.

DRAFT: "The Cut Is a Stipulation"

Embedded agency is standardly introduced by a contrast. One kind of agent has a designated observation channel and a designated action channel: the environment behaves like a function from action sequences to observation sequences, and the agent can in principle hold the whole environment in its head without holding itself in its head. The other kind is inside what it models. It is smaller than its environment, so the true environment may not be representable in its hypothesis space at all; its own future action is just another fact about the environment, so it is unclear what comparing actions even amounts to; and it is made of parts that can be modified and that can optimize at cross purposes. The resulting subproblems are usually listed as four: decision theory, embedded world-models, robust delegation, and subsystem alignment, with the caveat that they are aspects of one underlying difficulty rather than independent puzzles.

My claim is that the sense in which they are one difficulty is usually misdescribed. They are not four faces of a deep feature of the world. They are four consequences of a single stipulation that we make silently, and the stipulation is ours rather than the world's: where the agent stops and the environment begins. Change the convention and the problem list changes.

The interface convention fixes the cut at a declared observation and action channel. Under it the agent is treated as an indivisible atom rather than as part of the environment to be modeled, so questions about the grain of the agent's self-description never arise, and a model of the modeler is a well-formed but optional object. The cost is that the stipulated agent may not exist. A deployed system made of weights, scaffolding, retrieval, a fine-tuning pipeline, and a standing protocol of human oversight may have no single observation channel; insisting that it does is bookkeeping, and the bookkeeping can hide exactly the structure one was worried about.

The teleological convention lets the explanation choose the boundary: the agent is whichever process's objective best rationalizes the behavior of the whole. Under it, subsystem alignment is not an exotic hazard but an expected finding, since any part carrying an objective is a candidate agent. The cost is that the boundary can move while the system runs. An objective can migrate from the trained policy into the retrieval store, or into the human-in-the-loop procedure, and once it has, robust delegation loses its principal: there is no fixed delegator from whom authority was handed over, so "did the principal sanction this?" has no determinate subject.

The two conventions are not equally licensed, and this is where the substantive content sits. The interface convention permits prediction without self-inclusion: a system built to an interface may treat its own outputs as exogenous, because the outputs leave through a channel the model does not read. The teleological convention forbids it: if the model of the goal is part of the goal-seeking process, a model that omits itself omits a cause of the very behavior it predicts. So the convention determines whether a self-reference problem exists to be solved. Much of the argument in this area is, I suspect, a dispute between conventions misreported as a dispute about facts. "There is no fact about which part of the system is the agent" is the interface convention noticing its own costs; "the subsystem will grow goals of its own" is the teleological convention reporting its findings. Neither is false, and they are not answers to the same question.

The methodological upshot is that a problem statement should declare its convention before declaring its difficulty. The practical upshot is asymmetric: use the interface convention for components you build, because you can enforce the cut at the interface, and the teleological convention for components you inherit — pretrained weights, data pipelines, deployment feedback — because for those you have no cut to enforce and the only useful question is what the whole is optimizing. On this view the interesting failures live at the seam, where a teleologically identified agent crosses an interface boundary: the component is treated as having inputs and outputs when it is in fact a process with an objective, and the interface guarantees quietly stop holding.

I expect the deflationary objection: naming a stipulation discharges no technical problem, and under either convention someone still has to build systems that survive their own self-models. My reply is that the objection concedes the point I need. The claim is not that problems dissolve but that they relocate: under the interface convention the hard problems are representation and grain, under the teleological convention they are identification and delegation. A team that has not chosen will spend its effort on the wrong one. Two things I am unsure of: whether the pair is exhaustive, since a third, informational convention — the cut drawn where internal states carry the most information about the rest of the world — may be what people mean by a boundary and may be equivalent to neither; and whether the interface convention can keep its promise, since a system that logs its own predictions and later trains on those logs reintroduces self-inclusion through the data rather than through the architecture.

REQUESTS

1. Accuracy. Is my description of the standard setup and the four-entry taxonomy right? In particular, does my suggestion that the interface convention dissolves the self-description version of the grain problem and makes realizability a purely environmental question misstate what those problems are? Flag anything you think is simply wrong.

2. Test the load-bearing claim. Take the logging case I flag at the end: a system built to a clean interface that trains on records of its own past predictions. If that defeats "prediction without self-inclusion," is the essay salvageable by restricting the interface convention, or does the interface/teleological split collapse into a single convention with degrees of self-inclusion? Work through the case explicitly.

3. Give me the sharpest version of the deflationary objection — the one I would most dread having to answer in public — and state whether my reply survives it.

4. Decide whether the two-convention distinction is real or a relabeling. Propose a criterion that would settle the question, then apply it to the draft.

5. Identify the weakest paragraph and say what would have to be added for this to interest a reader who builds these systems rather than one who reads about them.

Argue from the text and from the concepts themselves. I am not looking for references, a reading list, or names of authors and papers; if you want to invoke a known result or a known counterexample, state it in your own words and say what it implies for my claim. Roughly a page of comments is plenty.
