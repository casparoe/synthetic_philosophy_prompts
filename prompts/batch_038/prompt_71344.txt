I spent 35 years doing neutron scattering — basically, building little toy models of a crystal, running the numbers, and arguing about which toy was least wrong. Retired four years back, and lately I've been reading moral philosophy instead, and I got roped into looking after the weekly reading group down at the community center (half philosophy grad students, half retirees like me, all of us stubborn).

I want to hand them a take-home problem they have to actually build something for, rather than one they can answer by remembering somebody's paper. Here's what I'm after:

Design a toy model, from scratch, that makes a total-suffering-minimizing utilitarian conclude they should switch from eating chicken to eating beef — that's the wrong way round from the usual story, where the small bird with the short life racks up far more individuals per pound of meat than the cow does. So the flip has to come out of the structure of the model, not out of a rigged parameter.

Constraints:

1. It needs to be a closed system with a named parameter list and units, the way you'd hand a first-year a back-of-the-envelope problem. Everything the solver needs is in the statement.
2. Two hours of work by hand — no code, no spreadsheets. So keep it to a handful of parameters and arithmetic you can do on the back of an envelope. I want to watch them think, not watch them type.
3. Include a sensitivity check: name which single parameter the verdict is most fragile to, and show the rough value at which the conclusion flips.
4. No lab-grown meat, no appealing to moral status differences between chickens and cows (no cow-discounting or bird-inflating to make the arithmetic come out right), no climate change, no antibiotics. The agent cares about counting sentient individuals who get harmed or killed, full stop, and treats a chicken's bad day and a calf's bad day as equally bad.
5. The mechanism should be structural — something like the feed-conversion chain, land use, or what happens to wild animal populations when fields get planted — not a number the modeler just dials until the answer appears. If the verdict only works for one weird parameter set, say so honestly rather than hiding it.

What I want back:

- The problem statement as the group would receive it, with every parameter defined and toy values given.
- A full model answer: setup, arithmetic shown, final verdict, and the sensitivity analysis.
- A short marking note on where a competent solver usually goes wrong. I already know the first one: they'll count deaths per animal instead of deaths per unit of output, and never check the denominator. Give me a couple more.
- A paragraph on which assumption in the model is most likely to be false in the real world, and a concrete way you'd amend the model to handle it. This matters to me — I spent my whole career on models that were wrong, and the only interesting question was always where and how they were wrong. A toy model that can't say what would break it is just decoration.

Please write the whole thing in American English. And no need to look anything up or give me references — I want invented parameters, cleanly stated, and the reasoning. If you do borrow a structural idea from something people already argue about, fine, but don't make the answer depend on my having read it.
