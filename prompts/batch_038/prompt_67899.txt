**The question as set (25 marks, suggested length 700–800 words)**

Lewis's account of lawhood is offered as a way of retaining a Humean picture of the world while still giving laws the work we expect of them.

(a) State what, on Lewis's view, makes a true generalisation a law rather than an accident, and explain the role that perfectly natural properties play in the answer.
(b) Explain how the account is supposed to earn laws their connection to counterfactuals.
(c) Say in which direction determination runs between the mosaic of local matters of particular fact and the laws, and what that implies for the claim that laws explain the events falling under them.
(d) Set out what you take to be the most serious objection to the account, and say whether Lewis can meet it. Compare his position briefly with Armstrong's.

**The candidate's script**

(1) Lewis's position is Humean because of what it says the world contains: nothing but the mosaic of local matters of particular fact, the distribution of perfectly natural properties and relations across spacetime, together with whatever supervenes on that distribution. Humean supervenience is the thesis that everything else we speak of — lawhood, chance, causation, disposition — supervenes on it. Lewis holds this to be part of the very idea of a law rather than a substantive hypothesis about the world: a view on which two worlds could agree perfectly in all local matters of fact and yet differ in their laws would not be a rival account of lawhood but a misuse of the term. His account of lawhood is therefore the working out of an analytic constraint, not a discovery about how things happen to be.

(2) A deductive system is a set of sentences closed under consequence, and a true deductive system is one whose axioms are all true of the mosaic. Strength is how much of the mosaic's information the system's theorems carry; simplicity is how economically they carry it; the two compete, since a system can be made maximally strong by taking every truth as an axiom and maximally simple by taking almost none. The laws are the theorems of whichever true system wins that competition. The comparison presupposes a preferred vocabulary: systems are to be stated in terms of perfectly natural properties, because otherwise simplicity would depend on the language in which the axioms were written. Since the systems, not the generalisations, are the objects compared, lawhood is a global property; that is why a generalisation can be true, unrestricted and exceptionless and still fail to be a law, as with the claim that no gold sphere exceeds a mile in diameter.

(3) Laws earn their connection to counterfactuals through the analysis of counterfactuals rather than by being installed as governors. The truth of a counterfactual turns on a similarity ordering over worlds, and that ordering is fixed by match of law together with spatio-temporal fit, so the worlds closest to ours share our laws. A counterfactual is true when its consequent holds at the closest antecedent-worlds. Because the laws of a world are themselves fixed by its mosaic, the counterfactuals we accept are derived rather than posited; and accidental generalisations, which are not theorems of the best system, do not enter the ordering and so support no counterfactuals. This is precisely the result the Humean wants: modal work is done, but nothing modal is added to the world.

(4) Armstrong's alternative holds that a law is a relation of necessitation between universals, from which the corresponding regularity follows. So the two views agree about the world's patterns: on Armstrong's account the necessitation relation entails that all Fs are Gs, and on Lewis's account a law is a theorem of a true system and hence itself a truth about how things are distributed. The disagreement is not about the patterns but about whether anything exists over and above them — Lewis says no, Armstrong says a necessitation relation does. One clear advantage falls to Lewis: Armstrong's universals must be instantiated for the necessitation relation to hold, so his view cannot accommodate laws concerning quantities or kinds that never arise, whereas the best system can have theorems that outstrip the actual instances.

(5) There is a cost here that Lewis accepts. Which system is best depends on how simplicity is weighted against strength, and weighting depends on the kind of systematisation that beings like us can manage; simplicity is a virtue of theories for creatures with our cognitive economy. Lawhood is therefore partly a function of the interests of systematisers. Lewis does not conceal this: objectivity is recovered not from the world alone but from the rough convergence of inquirers on the same informative systems. Armstrong treats that consequence as a reductio, but the relativity is built into the account and is a mark of its candour.

(6) Determination runs from the mosaic to the laws: the laws are what they are because the mosaic is what it is, and there is no further fact. That does not cost Lewis the explanatory role of laws. What makes a generalisation explanatory is its place in the best system, and the theorems of the best system are what make the mosaic the lawlike pattern it is; to explain an event is to exhibit it as an instance of that pattern. The most serious objection, I think, is that this leaves the direction of explanation inverted: Maudlin's complaint is that laws are supposed to produce the mosaic, not to be read off it, and if a law is merely a summary of what happens, nothing answers the question why this pattern holds rather than another. A related difficulty is inductive. Because the best system is selected for its fit to the whole mosaic, including those parts of it that lie in our future, the laws depend on facts about which we can as yet have no evidence, and it is hard to see how the account can make sense of our ordinary justification for accepting them. Lewis cannot satisfy both demands at once: if the mosaic is prior, the laws are left with no explanatory work, and if the laws explain the mosaic, the priority that made the view Humean has been abandoned. And since the best system is chosen by reference to the very facts it is meant to systematise, the account is circular — lawhood is explained by appeal to a systematisation of what the laws already determine. That is a real cost, though one Armstrong shares, since his necessitation relation is equally unexplained.
