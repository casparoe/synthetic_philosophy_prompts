I sit on the dissemination review panel of a multi-institution research consortium: nothing produced inside the group goes out until we have cleared it. Our record so far runs as follows.

- A cheap route to enhancing a pathogen's transmissibility: withheld from publication, released only to three national biosafety authorities under confidentiality — over the objection of two members who argued that public availability would on balance accelerate defensive work.
- Evidence that the field's standard capability benchmark had been contaminated by training-data leakage, invalidating years of published comparisons: released in full the same day, no embargo, despite warnings that the resulting loss of confidence would set back a valuable research program.
- A fine-tuning recipe that strips refusal behaviour from released models: circulated to member labs immediately under a four-week standstill during which every lab patched its own weights, and never posted publicly.
- A proof that a widely deployed signature scheme is far weaker than assumed: published at once and unconditionally, even though we had no means of getting vendors to ship fixes quickly.
- An analysis suggesting that a mid-sized state could field a stratospheric aerosol program within a decade: sent only to negotiators from the five states best placed to build a regime around it; never published.

The case now before us: we have confirmed that a flaw in a ubiquitous open-source library lets a small, well-resourced team trigger cascading failures in infrastructure that most of our own member institutions depend on. A patch exists and can reach nearly all users within days of disclosure. Three unaffiliated teams have independently found the same flaw, and one of them says it will publish a full technical write-up in roughly three weeks whatever we decide. Our own median estimate is that publishing now, with the patch attached, is net-positive. But some 150 outside practitioners, several of whom have read our write-up, hold by a clear majority that a silent, maintainer-coordinated patch is preferable and that publishing the details does more harm than good.

Recommend the decision that best coheres with our own record. Which precedent is the closest analog, and how far does that analogy actually carry? Lay out the candidate principles our five verdicts are jointly consistent with, state what each of them implies for this case, and say plainly which principle we would be committing ourselves to whichever way we rule. Finally, describe one further case that would come out differently under the two principles we can least distinguish, so that we could settle which of them we have been applying all along.
