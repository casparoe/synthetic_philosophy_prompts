Retired four years ago after a career doing numerical simulation at a national lab — fluids and molecular dynamics, mostly, never a minute of philosophy in my life. Since then I've gone completely overboard on the supertask stuff: Thomson's lamp, the Zeno-style converging series, the Newtonian supertasks where infinitely many particles collide in a finite time and conservation somehow goes out the window, and the relativistic ones where an observer with finite proper time supposedly recieves the answer to an infinite computation (Malament-Hogarth spacetimes, I think they're called). I read slowly and I want to actually think, not just collect.

Here's the situation. I run a fortnightly reading group through our town's adult education outfit: eight of us, mostly retired engineers plus one physics grad student who does the actual reading and lets the rest of us do the talking. Twelve meetings a year, two hours each, and honestly we can only chew on one real question per pair of meetings. I owe the coordinator a topic list in about three weeks.

So — which open questions about supertasks deserve our year, in what order, and which should I just leave out? I care way more about the reasoning than the list. For anything you keep, tell me (a) how much else hangs on it, (b) whether eight people with calculus and physics but no set theory adn no formal semantics can actually make progress on it in two hours, and (c) what would demote it — what answer or discovery would mean we wasted the evenings.

Three hunches of my own, which you should feel free to tell me to abandon:

1. Every integrator I ever ran has a timestep floor and goes unstable near a singularity, so maybe the interesting question isn't whether the task finishes but what the state at the accumulation time is even supposed to mean when the dynamics stops being well behaved there.

2. Dissipation. Nobody ever says how much energy an infinitely-colliding system sheds; put in any friction at all and I suspect the whole family of paradoxes dies quietly. Worth two hours?

3. Whether the lamp was ever about lamps, or whether its really a disguised argument about the difference between a limit of states and a state at the limit.

And please flag anything where the honest answer is "that got settled years ago and the field moved on." I'd much rather be told a puzzle is dead than spend a Tuesday night on it.
