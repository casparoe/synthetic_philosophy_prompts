I am in the fourth year of a dissertation that sits between ancient philosophy and current epistemology, and I am drafting a chapter that asks how Aristotle would adjudicate a dispute that only took shape twenty-three centuries after his death: the argument between Ryle's anti-intellectualism about knowing how and the intellectualist reply that treats knowing how as a species of knowing that. I will be presenting a version of this at a work-in-progress seminar, so I need something that will survive hostile questions from both classicists and epistemologists. Please give me the argument rather than the apparatus: no bibliography, no list of secondary sources, no page references, and no attempts at quotation from either Aristotle or the modern debate. Where you render his views, paraphrase.

Let me stipulate one piece of machinery I want to hold fixed, since I think the contemporary dispute slides across it without noticing. Call the possession-conditions of a cognitive state whatever it takes to have the state at all, and its exercise-conditions whatever it takes for the state to be manifested in a particular performance. Intellectualists tend to argue from possession, from what a subject must be able to represent in order to have acquired a skill; anti-intellectualists tend to argue from exercise, from what need not be represented at the moment of fluent action. My working hypothesis is that Aristotle would find the resulting standoff uninteresting rather than unresolved, because his account of craft is dispositional through and through: the reason that distinguishes the craftsman is supposed to be in the state, not a contemplation staged in front of each act. I want that hypothesis tested, and if it fails, I want to be told so directly.

Here is the case I have constructed for the chapter. A master luthier has spent forty years arching violin bellies to a standard she can justify at length; she explains to apprentices why the thickness graduation must vary across the plate, what a tap-tone should disclose, and which anomalies in a piece of maple call for a different arch. After a left-hemisphere stroke she loses precisely that discursive access. She can no longer say why she does what she does, cannot complete the sentences she used to teach from, and cannot evaluate her apprentice's stated justifications. Her hands are unimpaired, and the instruments she now makes are, by the best available comparison, indistinguishable from her earlier ones. Her apprentice, meanwhile, has her notebooks. He can recite her principles fluently and correctly, and the instruments he produces from them are, on her own former assessment, second-rate.

Four questions I need answered in order.

First: is the post-stroke luthier still a craftsman on Aristotle's terms, or has she slid into the condition he contrasts with craft in the opening of the Metaphysics, that of the person of mere experience, who knows particulars rather than universals and who knows the fact without knowing the why? The difficulty I cannot resolve is that he says two things there that pull against each other: that for practical purposes experience is in no way inferior to art, and that the merely experienced often succeed better than those who have theory without experience; and yet that we take the craftsman to be wiser, because he grasps the cause. If her output is undiminished, what exactly has she lost? Is the loss one of ability, or only of standing in a hierarchy of wisdom? Can Aristotle have both judgments, or must one give?

Second: does the apprentice fit Aristotle's example of the person who has the universal but not the particular contained under it and therefore fails in practice? I suspect not, because the apprentice's failure is not a failure to notice particulars; he has plenty of particular practice and fails anyway. If that is right, Aristotle seems forced to say that what the apprentice has is not really the universal at all, that retaining propositions learned from another is not yet possessing them as a productive state. Is that a move his psychology of hexis can make, and what would it cost him?

Third, and most important for the chapter: does defining craft as a productive state involving true reason commit him to intellectualism in the modern sense, so that knowing how to build a violin just is standing in a knowledge relation to propositions? Or does his distinction between having a state and exercising it, together with his treatment of craft as capable of producing a bad result as well as a good one, let him deny the very premise Ryle attacks, that every intelligent performance is preceded by an inner act of rule-consultation? I would like a definite answer here, with the reasoning exposed, rather than a both-sides summary.

Fourth: a wrinkle that I expect Aristotle has no ready resources for. Suppose her loss is partial and asymmetric. She cannot state her principles, but she can reliably veto. Shown two candidate archings, or two of the apprentice's written justifications, she reliably rejects the worse, sometimes including ones she had herself endorsed in the notebooks, and she cannot say on what ground. What is this? A residue of craft, a highly trained experience, or something closer to a trained perceptual discrimination? And here is the question I most want pressed: given that on his account the difference between craft and its defective counterpart is that the one reasons truly and the other falsely, does her being right about the why, while unable to state it, show that her state still involves reason? Or is reliable rightness without the ability to give the reason exactly what he refuses to call knowledge? I suspect his verdict here will be uncomfortable for both sides of the modern debate, and I want to know whether that discomfort is real or an artifact of my framing.

Two cautions on method. Please mark, as you go, which steps are compelled by what Aristotle actually says and which are speculative reconstruction on my behalf; I would rather have a shorter argument with the speculative joints visible than a smooth one that quietly invents his commitments. And please tell me where the anachronism bites. His Greek has no complement construction corresponding to knows how to, and he sorts states of soul in a way that does not line up with a distinction between attitudes toward propositions and dispositions to perform. I am not interested in a relabelling exercise that simply identifies craft with knowledge-how; I want to know whether the relabelling distorts his taxonomy, distorts the modern debate, or both. If the honest conclusion is that Aristotle could not have posed this question at all, say so plainly and then describe the nearest question he could have asked about the luthier and her apprentice, and what he would have answered to it.

Roughly the length of a seminar paper section, organized under the four questions above, and ending with a short paragraph stating the verdict you would defend if challenged: does Aristotle come out as an intellectualist, an anti-intellectualist, or as someone for whom the distinction is drawn in the wrong place?
