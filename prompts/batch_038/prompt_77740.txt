I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and for the past two months I have been reporting a feature on the argument Gödel built on top of his rotating-universe solution of 1949 — the claim that, because the field equations admit a cosmology in which no global present is available and in which timelike paths loop back on themselves, an objective lapse of time cannot be part of the furniture of the world. The reporting has not converged. The relativist I interviewed considers the solution a beautiful curiosity that says nothing about the universe we inhabit; the philosopher of physics I interviewed considers it the most serious challenge to the reality of time that physics has produced, and thinks the curiosity response begs the question. My editor, meanwhile, wants the piece to open on the prospect of travelling back to meet your younger self, which is not quite the argument I want to make. Rather than let the disagreement sit in the copy as colour, I have written out the position I have actually absorbed and intend to defend, and I would like a consistency audit of it before I commit to it in print.

Here is the package, in the order I have drafted it:

1. Time passes — really passes, rather than merely being represented by a time coordinate — only if the world objectively divides into successive layers of the present, a global now that sweeps through the universe as a whole. A present that is merely local, or relative to an observer's state of motion, is not a present at all.

2. Special relativity already makes that division look optional, since which distant events are simultaneous depends on the frame. But Gödel's rotating universe finishes the job: it is an exact, singularity-free solution, homogeneous and filled with uniformly swirling dust, non-expanding, in which the geometry is arranged so that no decomposition into global layers of the present is available at all, and which contains closed timelike curves.

3. What does the real damage in that result is the closed timelike curves. The absence of a preferred slicing is the technical way of stating the point; the philosophical punch comes from the fact that an observer could in principle travel along such a curve and arrive at a moment in their own earlier life.

4. The force of the argument is modal. Because the laws of nature permit a world in which there is no objective lapse, objective lapse cannot be something the laws deliver; and what is not fixed by the laws is not an objective feature of reality. Hence time is unreal — not merely unreal in the rotating universe, but unreal, full stop.

5. Even so, I want to leave readers with the reassurance that nothing has changed for the world they actually live in. Our universe expands and is well described by a cosmological model with a cosmic time defined by the distribution of matter and radiation; within that model there is a global present. The rotating universe is therefore a mathematical curiosity rather than a rival cosmology, and Einstein's own objection — that Gödel's solution does not expand whereas the observed universe does — settles which world we are in.

6. I also want to insist that closed timelike curves are physically impossible: whatever quantum-field effects do near a would-be chronology horizon, nature protects itself, and no macroscopic object can return to its own past.

7. Finally, I want to say that none of this smuggles in contested metaphysics. The rotating universe is a theorem, not an interpretation, and any physicist who accepts general relativity is committed to it regardless of their views about becoming.

I would rather not tell you which of these I am most attached to, since I suspect that would steer your answer; treat them all as equally up for grabs. What I need is an audit, not a lecture on the philosophy of time. Go through the set and identify which pairs genuinely pull against one another and which merely look as though they do. Rank the real conflicts by severity rather than presenting them as a flat list, and for each say what would have to be given up, what the cheapest available revision is, and what that revision costs the argument — in particular, whether the headline conclusion in (4) survives it. Then tell me which single change would repair the most damage across the whole package. If one of my items is doing double duty, or if a distinction I am relying on turns out not to be available, say so explicitly and show why. I am not looking for references or a reading list; my fact-checker will handle attribution, and what I need from you is the reasoning laid out plainly enough that I could explain it to a reader with no physics.
