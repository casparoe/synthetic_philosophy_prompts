**Role and setup.** You are a philosopher who works on epistemic logic and the paradoxes of self-reference, and who is unusually fluent in the technical apparatus of probabilistic forecasting: proper scoring rules, calibration and sharpness, prequential evaluation, coherence and Dutch-book arguments, and the martingale property of Bayesian credences. Think the mapping through carefully before writing — work out where each step of the philosophical argument lands in my vocabulary, and where it refuses to land. Only the finished product needs to appear in your answer.

**Who I am.** I run model validation for a trading desk. I read and write probability forecasts every day, I have strong opinions about Brier versus log scores, I have argued at length about whether a model that is calibrated but not sharp is worth keeping, I know what a prequential evaluation is and why backtesting a live forecaster is not the same as scoring a static model, and I understand the sense in which coherent credences form a martingale. My formal epistemology background is one undergraduate course. I am fluent in my own vocabulary and I want the paradox brought to me in it, not explained to me around it.

**What I already know about the target.** Roughly: a teacher announces that there will be an examination on one of the five weekdays next week, and that the student will not know in advance which day it will be. The student reasons that it cannot be Friday, since by Thursday evening he would know; having ruled out Friday, he reasons it cannot be Thursday; and so on down to Monday, concluding that no such examination is possible. The teacher then gives the exam on, say, Wednesday, and the student is genuinely surprised. I know the familiar diagnoses by reputation — that the announcement is self-refuting, that it generates a true proposition the student cannot come to know, that the student's induction needs some margin-for-error principle to go through, that he conflates knowing that there will be an exam with knowing which day it will be on, that his reasoning assumes he can know that he knows. I have never been able to decide whether any of these is the answer or whether they are all circling the same thing.

**My working suspicion, which I want tested rather than flattered.** To me the student is simply doing bad probability theory: he treats "will be a surprise" as though it were a fixed, knowable property of the world rather than a property of his own forecast, and he treats knowledge as a state he can predict himself occupying. In my world, an event with nonzero assigned probability can happen without any contradiction arising, calibration is a property of a long sequence of forecasts and not of a single one, and the only thing I can say about my own future credences is what coherence constrains — my expectation of tomorrow's probability is today's probability. I suspect "surprise" is doing work in the philosophical argument that a one-shot probability forecast cannot do, and that once it is rendered as a threshold on a credence the paradox either dissolves or relocates. Tell me whether that is right, half right, or a way of changing the subject.

**The task, in three parts.** First, restate the paradox and the student's reasoning in my vocabulary as faithfully as you can: the announcement, the elimination of the last day, the induction backwards, and the moment of surprise. Render each of the philosophically loaded terms — knows, will not know in advance, is a surprise, could have deduced — into something I would actually write down, and where the honest rendering is "assigns probability 1 to," say so and say what breaks as a result, rather than sliding from knowledge to high probability without comment. Second, audit your own translation: identify every place where you had to distort the original claim to make it fit, and every place where you quietly imported an assumption of my framework and called it a translation. Coherence, scoring, and calibration come with their own commitments; do not present them as neutral. Third, give the residue report: which claims survive intact, which survive only in a weakened or altered form, and which have no expression at all in the language of forecasts and scores. Then tell me whose defect the residue is — whether my vocabulary is missing something real (a notion of knowledge that is not a credence, a first-person authority over one's own future states, something about the publicity of the announcement) or whether the residue shows that the original problem was resting on a term that never meant anything. Be willing to conclude that a piece of the paradox is a genuine formal problem about self-referential forecasts that my framework has real resources for, and be willing to conclude the opposite.

**Format.** Lead with a bottom line, then use exactly these sections in this order:
