I'm building the causal-attribution layer for an incident-review system used by a fleet of semi-autonomous warehouse robots — software that reads a timeline plus a list of standing duties and spits out the absences that caused the bad outcome ("the interlock was never re-armed", "nobody looked at the pallet sensor for eleven minutes"). Before I lock down what that layer is allowed to call a cause, I want the general spec for an account of causation by omission, so the ontology isn't just whatever the engineers half-remember from one philosophy elective.

Write the desiderata: what any adequate theory of omissive causation has to deliver. For each requirement give the case or consideration behind it — don't just name it, show me the case that forces it. I want the list to cover at least:

- that we seem to say true causal things about absences at all, and separately that we don't say them about just any absence (my neighbour's failure to water my plants isn't a cause of their dying, even though the dying depends on it in the usual way)
- redundant omissions: two people both under a duty to hit the stop button, neither does, and the machine ruins the batch
- double prevention and prevention-of-prevention, where the alleged cause is the absence of an absence
- omissions by artifacts with no intentions and arguably no duties — a sensor that fails to fire, a breaker that doesn't trip — and whether those get treated the same way or a different way, and why that would be principled rather than ad hoc
- whether the account needs a mind, a norm, or somebody's interests to select which of the endless non-events are the relevant ones, and if it does, whether that's a defect or just the honest truth about the topic
- consistency with whatever our best physics says about there being no negative events or absences out there in the world to serve as relata

Then sort the list for me. Which requirements are genuinely non-negotiable for a theory to be worth the name, and which are really just the pet constraints of one camp — transitivity, say, or the insistence that causation always run through a physical process, or the demand that the account be reductive and non-normative all the way down. And this is the part I care about most: is any subset of them jointly unsatisfiable? If demanding a few of these at once is impossible, say so explicitely, say which ones collide, and say which one I should be the first to drop given that I have to actually ship something that assigns responsibility. Finally, tell me which single requirement eliminates the most candidate theories — I want to know where the real action is.

Two house rules. First, I've coined a distinction for this project and I want you to either use it or tell me it's worthless: call an absence *duty-anchored* when some agent or artifact stood under an obligation, a design intent, or a normal function to bring the missing event about, and *free-floating* when nothing does (there being no second moon, nobody having poured arsenic in the coffee, the absence of a heart attack that was never coming). Does a theory that admits only duty-anchored absences as genuine causes survive the rest of your list, or does it break on one of the cases above? Second, close with a very short scorecard — counterfactual/difference-making accounts, process or conserved-quantity accounts, and the view that negative-causation talk is a useful fiction or a manner of speaking backed by positive facts — a few lines each against the list. No bibliography, no reading list, no page numbers; I'll chase down the sources myself if the scorecard is interesting.
