I am preparing an annotated commentary on the first section of Part II of Adam Smith's *Theory of Moral Sentiments* (1759; I am working from the text of the sixth edition, 1790) for a graduate reading group, and there is a paragraph whose argument I cannot pin down. Two excerpts, in the order they appear.

**Excerpt 1** — from the introduction to Part II, where Smith sets out the machinery the rest of the Part runs on:

> It has already been observed, that the sentiment or affection of the heart, from which any action proceeds, and upon which its whole virtue or vice depends, may be considered under two different aspects, or in two different relations: first, in relation to the cause or object which excites it; and, secondly, in relation to the end which it proposes, or to the effect which it tends to produce: that upon the suitableness or unsuitableness, upon the proportion or disproportion, which the affection seems to bear to the cause or object which excites it, depends the propriety or impropriety, the decency or ungracefulness of the consequent action; and that upon the beneficial or hurtful effects which the affection proposes or tends to produce, depends the merit or demerit, the good or ill desert of the action to which it gives occasion.

**Excerpt 2** — from Part II, Section I, Chapter I:

> But though dislike and hatred harden us against all sympathy, and sometimes dispose us even to rejoice at the distress of another, yet, if there is no resentment in the case, if neither we nor our friends have received any great personal provocation, these passions would not naturally lead us to wish to be instrumental in bringing it about. Though we could fear no punishment in consequence of our having had some hand in it, we would rather that it should happen by other means. To one under the dominion of violent hatred it would be agreeable, perhaps, to hear, that the person whom he abhorred and detested was killed by some accident. But if he had the least spark of justice, which, though this passion is not very favourable to virtue, he might still have, it would hurt him excessively to have been himself, even without design, the occasion of this misfortune. Much more would the very thought of voluntarily contributing to it shock him beyond all measure. He would reject with horror even the imagination of so execrable a design; and if he could imagine himself capable of such an enormity, he would begin to regard himself in the same odious light in which he had considered the person who was the object of his dislike. But it is quite otherwise with resentment: if the person who had done us some great injury, who had murdered our father or our brother, for example, should soon afterwards die of a fever, or even be brought to the scaffold upon account of some other crime, though it might sooth our hatred, it would not fully gratify our resentment. Resentment would prompt us to desire, not only that he should be punished, but that he should be punished by our means, and upon account of that particular injury which he had done to us.

What I need is help working through Excerpt 2 against Excerpt 1, at the level of individual clauses. Specifically:

1. Give me the logical architecture of the paragraph. There appear to be at least three distinct claims in play — a concession about hatred and dislike, a claim about being the innocent causal occasion of harm, and a claim about the object of resentment proper. Identify which sentences carry which, where the pivot at "But it is quite otherwise with resentment" lands, and what the paragraph is ultimately meant to establish about the difference between resentment and the other hostile passions. Then tell me what work the parenthetical "which, though this passion is not very favourable to virtue, he might still have" is doing: is it a rhetorical hedge, or does it restrict the class of agents the preceding claim applies to — and if it restricts it, does the restriction undermine the generality of the claim?

2. Smith uses three different locutions for causal involvement in this stretch of the chapter: "instrumental in promoting his happiness" (in the gratitude paragraph immediately above), "to wish to be instrumental in bringing it about," "the occasion of this misfortune," and then "punished by our means." Are these stylistic variation, or do they pick out different relations? In particular, is "by our means" narrower than "the occasion of"? If it is, does the paragraph still support the strong reading I am inclined to give it?

3. The sentence "Though we could fear no punishment in consequence of our having had some hand in it" deliberately strips away the sanction-based explanation of the painful sentiment. What is left inside Smith's own spectator theory to explain why it would hurt the man excessively to have been the designless occasion of the death? Sympathy with the sufferer seems unavailable, since the sufferer is the object of violent hatred; anticipation of the impartial spectator's disapproval seems to presuppose that the spectator disapproves, though the same paragraph implies there is no impropriety to disapprove of in the case; and self-command looks like the wrong category. Which resource is Smith using, or is he committed here to a sentiment that reduces to none of them?

4. State the tension with Excerpt 1 as sharply as you can, and then try to dissolve it. Excerpt 1 makes merit and demerit depend on the effects an affection "proposes or tends to produce," which is a dispositional formulation. Excerpt 2 attaches a distinctive pain to the effect that actually occurred, in a case where the agent proposed nothing. Either show how the tendency formulation absorbs the case — and say what, on that account, the actual occurrence adds to a tendency that was already present — or concede that Excerpt 2 is a counterexample to the account of merit and demerit announced pages earlier.

5. Test a colleague's reading, which I want either vindicated or dismantled. Her claim is that Smith is not making what a contemporary reader would call the claim that consequences alter how much blame is deserved. On her reading he is making a claim about ownership: once the agent is the occasion of the harm, the harm stands in a relation to him that it would not otherwise stand in, and the painful sentiment is a response to that relation rather than a misjudgment about desert — which means it is not an irregularity of sentiment requiring correction by general rules, but a perception of something real. Which words in the paragraph would have to carry that weight, and what resists it? Two things worry me: Smith locates the sentiment in a man under the dominion of violent hatred rather than in an ordinary or good agent, and he immediately assimilates the designless case to the voluntary one through the sentence about regarding himself "in the same odious light." On the second point, does that sentence apply to the designless case at all, or only to the imagined voluntary enormity? I have been reading it as continuous with the designless case, and I am no longer sure that is grammatically available.

6. Whose sentiment is being reported in "it would hurt him excessively"? Is Smith describing what such a man does feel, what the impartial spectator would judge he ought to feel, or does the sentence slide between the two? Since the whole normative apparatus of the book depends on keeping those apart, tell me what turns on the answer here rather than merely noting the ambiguity.

7. On the fever-and-scaffold case: does "punished by our means" commit Smith to the view that resentment demands that the resenting party be the executor of the punishment, or only that the punishment be responsive to the particular wrong in the right way? And if resentment is not satisfied by the offender's undergoing an equivalent harm from an unrelated source, then its object is a relation rather than a quantity of suffering. Does that give Smith a reason internal to this chapter to doubt that merit and demerit are a function of effects, and if so, is he in a position to use it, given Excerpt 1?

8. Finally, tell me where I have over-read rhetoric as doctrine. Smith frequently accumulates pieces of illustrative psychology without committing to their theoretical significance, and I suspect I am doing exactly that in at least one of the questions above. Say which of my puzzles survives that suspicion and which dissolves once the paragraph is read as description rather than as argument.

I am not after secondary literature, a reading list, or any bibliographic apparatus — I want the sentences themselves worked through. Where the paragraph cannot settle a question on its own, say so explicitly and identify which part of Smith's surrounding argument would have to be brought in to settle it.
