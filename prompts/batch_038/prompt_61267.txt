Our philosophy-of-language reading group has just finished the section of Davidson on radical interpretation, and for my closing contribution I want to test the machinery against a real case that nobody in the seminar has suggested: the decipherment of Linear B in 1952. Here are the facts I take to be uncontroversial, so the analysis needn't rest on anything you'd have to look up. A British architect, Michael Ventris, working essentially alone, concluded that the syllabic script found on clay tablets at Knossos and Pylos recorded an early form of Greek. He had no bilingual text and no living informant; what he had was a large corpus of unbaked clay inventories accidentally fired in palace burnings, consisting almost entirely of lists — place names, personal names, commodities, numerals, and stylized drawings of the objects being counted. For years he had favoured a different hypothesis, that the language was Etruscan, and only abandoned it late. Before him, Alice Kober, an American classicist who died in 1950, had deliberately refused to conjecture any language at all, and instead tabulated recurring groups of signs sharing an identical stem and differing only in the final syllable, plus a partial grid of sign values, thereby establishing that the underlying language was heavily inflected. Ventris then built a grid assigning syllabic values to signs, used a related Cypriot syllabary as a comparative aid, and announced a tentative decipherment in a BBC radio talk in July 1952; John Chadwick, a Cambridge philologist and wartime codebreaker, wrote to him sceptical and was converted within days. Weeks later, freshly excavated tablets from Pylos — most famously the tablet cataloguing ritual vessels, catalogued as PY Ta 641 — turned out to pair syllabic spellings with drawings of the very objects named, including a word Ventris's values rendered as a dual form meaning two tripods alongside a drawing of two three-legged cauldrons, and further vessels whose spellings predicted handle counts of two, three and four that the accompanying drawings confirmed. Even so, a sceptic (Arthur Beattie) pressed objections into the 1960s, and Linear A, the earlier Cretan script, remains undeciphered to this day.

What I want is a disciplined mapping before any verdict. Say precisely who or what occupies each of Davidson's roles here: who is the interpreter, who is the speaker, and what serves as the evidence base, given that the central datum for Davidson is which sentences a speaker holds true on which occasions, and this corpus affords no occasions, no prompting, no assent or dissent, and no shared perceptual situation. Then work through the points of contact and of failure. The Mycenaean scribes' drawings and numerals look like a substitute for ostension, but do they inherit the same ambiguities that ostension does, or is the handle-counting something stronger than pointing? Kober's purely structural, meaning-free stage seems to have no place in Davidson's picture, on which belief and meaning can only be assigned together — does her work show that substantial constraints on an interpretation can be had before any charity is exercised? And Ventris's assumption that the language was Greek: on a constitutive reading of the principle of charity, agreement is a precondition of there being an interpretation at all, so it cannot be a gamble; yet Ventris staked a decade on Etruscan, and the Greek hypothesis was a falsifiable bet that could easily have lost. For my own part I have been labelling these two readings constitutive charity and wager charity, and I want you to use the episode to decide whether Davidson can hold on to the constitutive version, or whether what he needs is a weaker, defeasible heuristic that competes with rivals on fit.

Finally, run the case back against the theory rather than merely applying it. Davidson's holism suggests that fixing meaning requires attributing a rich, mostly true pattern of belief; yet the decipherment delivered lexical and morphological readings while telling us almost nothing about what the Mycenaeans believed, beyond that they kept accounts. Is that an anomaly for holism, or does it show that the interpretive target in the Davidsonian sense was never really the tablets? Say what the episode does to Quinean indeterminacy — whether Beattie's residual dissent is the historical analogue of the indeterminacy thesis or merely of ordinary ignorance, and whether the eventual over-determination of the reading by the whole corpus settles anything about there being a fact of the matter. Say also whether the fact that the apparently alien script turned out to be a dialect of a language still spoken supports Davidson's rejection of wholly alternative conceptual schemes, or simply flatters it, given Linear A. And consider whether his later distinction between a prior theory and a passing theory fits the episode better than the 1973 material does, since the decipherment seems to have proceeded by constructing an ad hoc assignment on the fly and only later generalizing it. Where Davidson is silent — on dead languages, on non-conversational evidence, on interpretation of archives rather than interlocutors — say that he is silent rather than stretching the framework to speak. Please also state how confident you are in the main claims you make, and single out the one point in your analysis you are least sure of, whether that is a claim about Davidson's commitments or about a detail of the decipherment I have summarized.
