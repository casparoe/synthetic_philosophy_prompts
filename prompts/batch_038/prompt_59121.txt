I teach the last three weeks of an upper-level philosophy of language course — names, natural kinds, and the modal consequences — and those weeks are reliably where I lose the room. The students can recite that identity statements between rigid designators are necessary if true, and that this necessity is supposed to be knowable only a posteriori, but almost none of them has any idea which side of the resulting puzzle they are actually on, and they are too polite to find out in front of one another. So before the first seminar I want to hand them a self-scoring diagnostic in the manner of those magazine quizzes: ten items, each a concrete scenario with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps patterns of answers onto named positions. The point is to sort them by what they would say about cases, not by what they think they believe.

Hard constraints on the items. First, vocabulary: no item may contain the words "necessary," "necessity," "a priori," "a posteriori," "rigid," "essence," or "possible world." Counterfactual scenarios are not only permitted but required, but they must be framed as questions about discovery, surprise, retraction, and what would count as having been mistaken — what a competent speaker ought to say if the chemistry had come out differently, what someone who coined a word should retract upon learning a fact. Second, spread: I do not want ten items about water and the evening star. At minimum I want (a) one or two of the canonical theoretical identities; (b) one item on the mirror-image case, where a stipulation about a particular measuring rod seems to hand someone knowledge of a length without anyone measuring anything; (c) one item on a name introduced by description for something nobody had yet observed — the astronomer who predicts a planet from perturbations in another planet's orbit and proposes a name for it before anyone has seen it, that kind of case; (d) one item on a kind term that mineralogists eventually discover picks out two structurally distinct substances rather than one, as happened with jade, where the question is whether the community had been using an ambiguous word all along or learned something about the world; (e) one item on a psychological identification, pain and a certain neural process; and (f) one item on a pair of co-referential names with no theoretical reduction in the off at all, just a pen name and a birth name, so the instrument can detect whether a student is tracking the metaphysics or merely the difficulty of acquiring the information. Third, calibration: make two of the ten controls on which most positions should converge, and tell me in the key what a divergent answer to a control indicates. Every option must be one a novice could pick without having read anything, phrased so that knowing the literature does not let you spot which leaf your answer feeds.

The key is where the real work is, and I do not want it to be name-matching. Score each answer as a coordinate on two or three dimensions that you specify — for instance, whether the person treats the felt gap between the two names or terms as tracking a genuine way things could have been; whether they explain that gap by what the words mean, by a second kind of content, or by limits on what anyone can know; and whether they think the epistemic categories in which the puzzle is stated are themselves in good order — and then locate the positions in the resulting space. These leaves must appear: the package on which the necessity is real, the epistemic intuition is explained away by the way reference was originally fixed, and nothing has gone wrong with our modal thinking; strong two-dimensionalism, on which there is a genuine sense in which the chemistry could have come out otherwise and a second intension is needed to capture it; a neo-Fregean or sense-based account that keeps the necessity and explains the cognitive gap without positing two intensions; a deflationary line on which the necessity is just a consequence of what our words mean and the puzzle is philosophically inert; a view that gives up the necessity of identity altogether; and a view on which the a priori/a posteriori distinction is too crude an instrument to bear the weight the puzzle puts on it. Give each leaf its standard name if it has one and mark it unnamed if nobody defends it, then tell me which cells of the space are empty, with a line on whether each is empty because it is incoherent, because nobody would want to live there, or because nobody has thought to. Flag honestly any pair of leaves that ten items cannot separate — I suspect two of them come apart only on questions about what is genuinely conceivable, and I would rather know that than have you paper over it. Finally, for each leaf write a two- or three-sentence readout in plain language that a student could stand to read about themselves, including which single item most nearly gave them away and what they should say in seminar on Tuesday. No bibliography, no citations, no reading list: the instrument has to stand on its own, and they have not been assigned anything else. Keep each item to roughly sixty words so the whole thing takes fifteen minutes.
