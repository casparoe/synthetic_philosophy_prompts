Right, so I run a little thinking group — back room of a community hall, every other Sunday, two hours, usually about twelve of us turn up. Nobody's done any studying. I'm a live captioner for a touring theatre company, so I sit on a laptop typing the subtitles that go up on a screen at the side of the stage; the rest of the group is a midwife, a scaffolder, two retired teachers, a couple of lads from the pub and my sister. I do all my reading on my own, youtube plus whatever the library's got, so I'm well aware half of what I think is probably wrong.

Here's the thing I'm stuck on. There's a writer who says that when your brain edits your experience there's two ways it could be doing it, and he gave them jokey political names after two dictators — Orwellian, where you have the experience first and something quietly rewrites the record of it afterwards; and Stalinesque, where it holds everything back until it knows how the scene is going to end, so there was never a first draft and nothing ever needs rewriting. He reckons you couldn't tell those apart from the inside even in principle. He uses that old illusion where two dots of different colours flash up one after the other a fraction of a second apart and you see a single dot travel across and change colour halfway, when nothing actually changed colour until the second dot came on.

I want to build an activity for the group out of that, using the captioning kit I can lug along: two laptops, a projector, coloured card, phone stopwatches, paper, envelopes. Cheap and bodgeable, no proper lab timings. Assume the illusion probably won't fire — I've tried phone apps and the timings are rubbish — so it has to still land in a room where nobody sees anything odd at all.

The bit I actually want is this: they get put through both kinds of editing without being told that's what's happening, and afterwards they end up genuinely unable to say which one they went through, even though the paperwork on the table shows plainly that the two rounds worked differently. Don't hand them the two labels until the very end.

So: roles and rules, what each person is doing and when, a timeline for the two hours, materials list, and a debrief that points back at specific moments and says what each one was doing. Also tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. I had a rough go on my flatmate and it collapsed instantly — she just said well obviously I knew it was going to change colour, I was guessing, and that was that. How do I stop the whole evening turning into everyone saying they were guessing? And be straight with me about which bit of the idea a room of amateurs with a projector just can't reproduce and I'm going to have to explain myself. No reading lists or references please, I can't keep track of them anyway.
