So, slightly weird ask. I'm the designated "public member" (read: token non-historian) on the curatorial advisory panel for the Meridian Museum of Science & Industry, and we're redeveloping the second-floor history gallery, currently called "How We Got Here." As built, it's three dated period rooms in a row — "The Middle Ages, c. 500–1400," "The Renaissance, 1400–1600," "The Scientific Revolution, 1600–1700" — with objects slotted into whichever room they were supposed to belong to. The redevelopment runs on one lead gift, the donor's late husband was an aerospace engineer, and the gift is earmarked to "the Scientific Revolution room," which is also where the naming plaque goes. We vote in three weeks, the panel is 3-2-1 (three keep-the-spine, two scrap-the-labels, me), and two of the historians have stopped replying to each other's emails.

Here's what's actually on the table. (a) Keep the three period rooms and their wall dates, because visitors need scaffolding and because our state's 9th-grade standards name the "Scientific Revolution" explicitly — roughly 60% of our school bookings come through those standards, and the education department has already written pre-visit worksheets keyed to the three room titles. (b) Our junior curator's proposal: no period labels anywhere in the gallery, just five thematic clusters (working titles something like "Seeing at a Distance," "Keeping Accounts of Nature," "Who Gets to Know"), with dates appearing only on individual object labels, on the grounds that printing a period name in 4-inch letters on a wall is an endorsement rather than a description. (c) A compromise: keep the names but push the boundaries way earlier, add a room on instruments and translation, and put a short "this category is contested" panel beside each doorway. (d) The one I can't stop thinking about, which came out of a design workshop and has never been costed: digital signage at each threshold letting visitors toggle among three periodizations — progress-driven, long-durée continuity, and global/connected — so that the classification itself becomes the exhibit. For context, our historical consultant's report is on the record saying the whole tripartite scheme is a retrofit: Italian humanists in the 1300s branding the intervening centuries "dark" partly to flatter themselves, a 17th-century German textbook writer making ancient/medieval/modern the standard grid, and then the awkward fact that the book which made "Scientific Revolution" a household period label (Butterfield, 1949) was written by a man whose earlier and more famous work was a demolition of exactly that kind of retrospective march-of-progress storytelling. Seperately, our visitor-studies pilot found that people in the undated mockup literally couldn't tell whether they were moving forward or backward in time, one of them asked a docent whether the Renaissance happened in China, and the docent corps — about 40 retirees who have memorized the existing script — is informally threatening to walk if the rooms get renamed.

What I want: draft the memo I'll circulate to the chair before the vote, roughly 900–1100 words, that lands somewhere specific. No "all four options have real merits" salad — if a hedge is genuinely the right answer, argue for it *as* a hedge, name which parts of which options and why, and be willing to spend the donor or the docents if that's what the reasons imply. It should (i) recommend one option, or a fourth we haven't thought of; (ii) give the panel a reusable test for when a contested historical category is fit to be printed on a wall — something a future curator could apply to "the Enlightenment" or "the Age of Discovery" in 2031 without relitigating this from scratch; (iii) handle the donor-naming problem concretely, including draft wording for whatever ends up on the plaque or the wall; and (iv) engage the strongest argument against your own recommendation, not a strawman of it. On (iv): my prior is that (b) is right in principle and catastrophic in practice, and I'd genuinely like the memo to tell me whether that's motivated reasoning, because I've read enough rationalist blogging to know how reliably I can generate respectable-sounding justifications for whichever conclusion happens to be administratively convenient. This also feels to me like the same argument we have about whether intelligence is one thing or whether a recession is a real event, and I suspect that analogy is pushing me toward treating all the labels as obviously fake, which is a failure mode rather than an insight — feel free to say so. Your prose to me can stay casual, but the memo itself needs to read like a document a board could act on Thursday. I'm not a historian — I do forecasting at a logistics company — so when you lean on a piece of historiography, say what it actually establishes rather than name-dropping it.
