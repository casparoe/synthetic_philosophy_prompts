I am setting the final examination for an upper-level seminar in value theory: seventy-five minutes, closed book, and this question carries 25 of the paper's marks. I want one essay question on the wrong kind of reasons problem that cannot be answered by reproducing the familiar taxonomy of object-given versus subject-given reasons. To rule that out, the question should hand students a criterion they have never encountered and require them to break it.

The criterion is this. Call it the Inert Attitude Test: a consideration is a right-kind reason to adopt an attitude just in case it would still be a reason to adopt that attitude in a world exactly like ours except that having the attitude makes no difference to anything whatever.

The case the question should be built around is one I have made up for the paper. A museum's trustees will forfeit a bequest that would keep the museum open unless they come to admire the donor's collection of second-rate landscapes. One trustee, looking long at a particular canvas, finds that its handling of late light is genuinely unusual, though she is unsure whether that is the same as its being admirable, and she suspects she is looking hard only because of the bequest.

The question should ask students to do three things: to say what the test gets right and what it gets wrong, in this case and in the stock cases of a threat that pays you to admire something unworthy and a payment for believing what your evidence contradicts; to explain what "makes no difference" has to be read as excluding, since the test does not say whether the consequences to be stripped out include the very facts on which an attitude's fittingness depends; and to judge whether the test can be repaired or must be abandoned in favour of the rival criterion on which a consideration is a right-kind reason just in case it bears on the question the attitude answers.

Please write the question, a model answer of about nine hundred words, and a marking scheme out of 25. I expect a strong answer to locate at least one wrong-kind reason that survives inertness precisely because it involves no payoff at all — I have in mind the sort that arises from a promise or an obligation of honour — but keep that out of the question itself: it belongs in the marking scheme, with the marks each required move carries and a line on how candidates typically mishandle it. Finally, in the model answer, distinguish clearly between what is settled among philosophers who work on this material and what remains genuinely contested, and see to it that neither the answer nor the marking scheme presents a contested position as the consensus or penalises a candidate for defending the other side of a live dispute.
