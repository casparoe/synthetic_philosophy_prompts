A policy essay I've been asked to comment on defends Germany's postwar settlement with the following analogy: political guilt, it says, is a debt inherited along with the estate. The state is the debtor, the victims or their successors are the creditors, later generations stand in the position of heirs who take the assets and the liabilities together without having contracted them personally, and the German programme of Wiedergutmachung — literally, making good again — plays the role of repayment. On that basis the essay concludes that a sufficiently large and well-targeted payment can discharge the obligation, after which the matter is closed and the personal faultlessness of the payers is beside the point. Please audit the analogy rather than the history: set out the correspondence explicitly (who or what plays creditor, principal, interest, repayment schedule, and settled account), identify which features the source quietly imports that the target lacks, and say which of those disanalogies actually threaten the conclusion and which are harmless. Note that Jaspers tied political guilt specifically to liability and its consequences, including reparation and loss of political power, while treating criminal guilt as personal and judicially determinable; consider which of those two registers the debt analogy is really tracking, and what happens to the essay's closure claim if it is tracking the wrong one. Then propose the most defensible alternative analogy for national responsibility after atrocity, if there is one, and state what it costs. Finally, tell me which single consideration, if it turned out otherwise, would make you reverse your verdict about whether the essay's conclusion survives, and where in your own reasoning you are most likely to have gone wrong.
