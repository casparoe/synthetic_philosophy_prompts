I am a junior, and for my literature seminar I have to write a critical essay that puts a familiar work into conversation with a philosophical problem of my own choosing. My teacher has forbidden outside sources entirely, so everything must come from the book and from argument; I am not looking for references, only for a reading I can test. I have chosen Dickens's *A Christmas Carol* together with the dilemma usually called torture versus dust specks, which asks whether one ought to prefer torturing a single person for fifty years or inflicting on each of an astronomically large number of people a momentary, trivially irritating speck of dust in the eye. The pairing is not obvious, and the first thing I want from you is an honest judgment about whether it is real or whether I am forcing it.

Here is the intuition I cannot yet articulate properly. The novella appears to hold two incompatible accounts of how small harms combine. Marley arrives dragging a chain that he explains he forged for himself link by link, which suggests that moral weight genuinely accumulates out of acts each of which is negligible on its own; the scene in the final stave, in which Scrooge's stripped belongings are itemized and sold by weight for a few shillings, makes a similar point in the register of arithmetic. And yet when the question becomes one of trading lives, the book refuses arithmetic altogether. Scrooge's suggestion to the charity collectors that the poor would do better to die and so reduce the surplus population is treated as the deepest indictment of him, and the Ghost of Christmas Present turns that same sentence back on Scrooge over Tiny Tim's expected death as though repeating it were sufficient to refute it. Meanwhile the two wretched children the ghost brings out from beneath his robe, Ignorance and Want, seem to be the diffuse misery of an entire city given a single face: a total rendered as two persons rather than as a number.

So I want to know which of these two pictures the novella is actually committed to. Is Dickens an anti-aggregationist, holding that certain harms cannot be outweighed by any quantity of lesser harms distributed across many people, or is he instead something closer to a sentimentalist about totals, someone who thinks the sum is real but believes it can only move us once it has been made visible in a particular child? Please argue for one reading against the other by working through scenes rather than impressions, and attend to who in the story is doing any calculating and what happens to them.

I also want you to be candid where the comparison breaks. As I understand it, the dust specks case concerns spreading one harm thinly across many different people in order to avoid a concentrated harm to one, whereas Marley's chain concerns a single agent accumulating many small acts against many victims over a lifetime, and I suspect those are structurally different problems that an essay ought not blur. If that difference is fatal to the analogy, say so plainly and explain what survives it. Then tell me what the novella contributes to the debate about aggregation that the thought experiment, stated as a paragraph, cannot: I have a hunch it has something to do with the fact that Scrooge is shown totals repeatedly and remains unmoved until one of them is given a name, but I do not know whether that hunch is an insight or a confusion about what aggregation even is.
