I cover logic, computation, and the philosophy of religion for a science-and-ideas magazine, and two springs ago I spent a week at a workshop on computational metaphysics where a presenter did something I have not been able to shake. On a projector, in front of a room of logicians and theologians, she ran a proof assistant through Gödel's ontological argument: an axiomatization of what it is for a property to be positive, the definition of a God-like being as one that has every positive property, the claim that whatever is positive is positive in every possible world, a definition of essence, the further claim that necessary existence is itself a positive property, and then the conclusion that a God-like being exists in every world. The machine produced a green checkmark in seconds. What stayed with me, though, was not the verification but two remarks she made almost in passing. The first was that a well-known objection holds the axioms prove far too much: on the natural reading they collapse contingency altogether, so that every truth turns out to be a necessary truth and nothing anywhere could have been otherwise — which arguably makes the exercise self-defeating, since a demonstration that eliminates the very distinction between what is and what merely might have been does not so much establish a discovery as dissolve the space in which discoveries live. The second was that years later a higher-order automated prover found the axiom set inconsistent, meaning that in principle anything whatsoever follows from it.

I want to commission a piece of fiction that lives inside the first of those two facts, and I am hoping you can write it. I would like a short story of roughly three thousand words — anything between twenty-five hundred and four thousand is fine — that dramatizes what it is like to accept a necessitarian conclusion rather than argue for or against it. Please do not set it in a philosophy department, and do not put Gödel on the page; I am not interested in the biography of the argument, only in its cost. A setting I would suggest, though you may substitute a better one: an outfit whose entire product is the subjunctive mood. A consultancy that sells counterfactual reports to the bereaved and to insurers — clients pay to be told what would have happened had the flight been delayed, had the surgeon gone the other way, had the bolt not been left loose. Make the protagonist someone inside that business who has become persuaded by the axiomatization, and let the story show how regret, apology, blame, premiums, hope, and ordinary deliberation deform once a person genuinely holds that there are no true alternatives to what has happened. I would rather see the change in behavior than hear about it.

Three constraints, since I will be editing this for a general readership that has never opened a modal logic text. First, the technical content has to be load-bearing, not decorative: it should be evident from the scenes that you understand where the work is actually being done — in the treatment of positive properties as holding across every world, together with the definition of God-likeness and the essence-and-necessary-existence machinery — and not in a general fog about infinity and perfection. Second, include at least one scene in which a character tries to say something sincerely contingent in ordinary language and cannot manage it, or is corrected by someone who can; that failure should be felt as loss, not as cleverness. Third, do not let the story escape through a loophole. The later discovery of inconsistency may be used as a turn if you can make it earn its place, but nobody may find a typo and send everyone home relieved, and the ending should land on an image, an action, or a piece of work being done — not on a summary of what the argument means. Keep explicit exposition about possible worlds to a sentence at most, avoid dialogue that functions as a lecture, do not attribute any line of text to a real publication, and give the story a title.
