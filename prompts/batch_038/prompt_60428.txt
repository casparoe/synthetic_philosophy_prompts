I have just come off four hours of argument with a friend about Taurek's paper on whether the numbers should count, and it ended, as such arguments do, precisely where it began. He holds the aggregative line: five deaths are worse than one, and anyone who denies it has mistaken a grammatical scruple for a moral insight. I find myself on the other side — that there is no being who loses five times over, that a death is a loss to the one who dies it and to those bound up with him, and that what strangers in such a case are owed is an equal chance rather than a sum. Neither of us moved, but the shape of the disagreement stayed with me, and I should like to see it fixed in a demanding form.

Write me a sestina — thirty-nine lines, six sestets and the three-line envoi, the end-words rotating in the traditional pattern — in the voice of the person holding the single dose: enough for five, all of it for one. He means to toss a coin, and he knows exactly how that looks from the shore.

The form has to carry the argument, not merely frame it. Specifically:

1. Choose six end-words that amount to the argument's whole furniture — the dose, the coin, the chance, the word *worse*, and something standing for the one and something for the five are all candidates, though the selection is yours. What is not negotiable is the arithmetic. Across the first thirty-six lines the same loss must return as the same loss, in a new place each time, and must never total. Avoid, grammatically as well as thematically, any construction that would let five of them be summed into a single larger fact: no plurals that behave like quantities, no *more*, no *all of them* in the sense the shore would mean.

2. The envoi is the only place the form permits all six end-words to sound in one breath, so treat it as the betrayal it is. The coin is in the air; the speaker who has declined to count for thirty-six lines is caught counting. It should read as a lapse — a confession, or a collapse into my friend's idiom — and the reader ought to feel the form doing it to him rather than the poet choosing it.

3. Give my friend his best case somewhere in the middle stanzas, and give it in its strongest form: that equal concern for each person is exactly what makes numbers matter; that separate complaints need no single sufferer in order to weigh against one another; that a coin offered to a man nearly certain to lose is a courtesy and not a respect. The rotation should do work here — let the objection arrive wearing the speaker's own end-words, so that it sounds like his own thought turned against him.

4. On accuracy: a moral philosopher reading the poem should be able to identify the position without the poem ever naming it. Taurek is not to be mentioned, no term of art may appear as a term of art, and if a line would need a gloss, it does not belong in the poem. The denial that the many's deaths are worse in any impersonal sense, the appeal to what each death is to the person who dies it, and the lottery as an expression of equal concern rather than an evasion of the question — all three should be recoverable by someone who knows them and merely felt by someone who does not.

5. On tone: formal, contemporary, unsentimental. No elegiac uplift at the close, and the coin is not to be redeemed as a species of heroism. I want it to end in the same discomfort it opens in.

A roughly five-stress line is my preference, so that the returns are audible; no rhyme is required beyond the end-word repetitions themselves.
