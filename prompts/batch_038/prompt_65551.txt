In three weeks I am to give the closing craft lecture at a festival devoted to the novel, and an expanded version will run in a literary quarterly. The working title is The Kind Liar: On Working Upon a Reader. For two decades I have defended a position about the ethics of my trade — usually at parties, always with more confidence than rigor — and I want to know whether it survives being written down in full. My suspicion is that it does not. My further suspicion is that I will not be able to see where it fails on my own, since I have never had to hold all of it in view at once.

What I hold is this.

1. Narrative fiction manipulates. It rations information, fixes the order of disclosure, and administers attachment and withdrawal on a schedule of its own choosing, in order to bring the reader to beliefs and feelings they would not otherwise have arrived at. I do not treat this as a regrettable feature to be minimized; I treat it as the art.

2. When manipulation is wrong, what makes it wrong is that it reaches a person by routes that bypass or subvert their capacity to weigh reasons, instead of addressing that capacity.

3. In my case the objection is answered by consent. Whoever opens a novel has agreed to be worked upon, and that agreement is the difference between my practice and the propagandist's, the salesman's, and the gaslighter's.

4. The consent, however, cannot be informed in any sense that does useful work. What a novel does to a reader must go unremarked while it is happening; a device that declares itself has already failed. The reader therefore agrees to being moved, not to any particular moving, and I could not tell them in advance what I intend to do to them, since telling them is precisely how I would undo it.

5. My third novel spends four hundred pages inside the perceptions of a man who is cruel, until the reader finds that they are, in spite of themselves, on his side. A number of readers wrote to me afterwards to say that they were disturbed to discover their sympathies had moved, and that they did not approve of where they had moved to. I believe the book did those readers a service. I would not alter it, and I would not have warned them.

6. I hold myself to one flat prohibition. I will not, in the narrative voice speaking as itself, assert anything I take to be false in order to carry an argument for a position. My characters may lie without limit; the voice that speaks for the book may not lie at all.

I want an audit rather than a defense. Which of these genuinely pull against one another, and which merely look as though they do? For the real conflicts, rank them by severity — I have no use for a report in which every pairing is described as somewhat strained — and say what would have to be surrendered in each case. Then, for the worst of them, tell me the least expensive revision that would let me keep the remainder intact, and what that revision would cost me in practice: what I could no longer do in a book, not merely what I could no longer say in a lecture.

Two cautions. I do not know which of the six I would abandon if forced, so please do not read my ordering or my emphasis as a hint about that. And I would rather be told plainly that one of my apparent conflicts is only apparent than have it listed alongside the real ones for the sake of completeness.
