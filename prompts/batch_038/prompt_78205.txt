I am preparing a seminar paper on the internal logic of abolitionist arguments, and I have been treating Albert Camus's 1957 essay *Reflections on the Guillotine* as a body of case verdicts rather than as a tract. Here is the track record I have extracted, in his own terms rather than mine.

- The public execution his father witnessed: the father approved of the sentence beforehand and came home shattered afterwards, yet Camus insists his case rests on argument and on the statistics of crime rates, not on sympathy for the condemned. The revulsion is evidence, not the ground.
- The claim that the guillotine is humane and instantaneous: rejected, on the strength of botched beheadings.
- The move of executions inside prison walls: accepted without protest, but taken to destroy the penalty's exemplary function, leaving it as the state's way of disposing of people it has written off.
- Deterrence: abolition has not raised crime; most murders are not premeditated, so no threat reaches the man who kills without planning, and the one who plans has already discounted the threat.
- The comparison with the crime: no criminal deed is as premeditated as a judicial execution, since equivalence would require a murderer who announced to his victim the date of his death and then held him at his mercy for months.
- The maximum penalty: hard labor for life, because it is harsher than death yet reversible, and because it leaves the prisoner his own death as an option.
- The religious contrast: in an age of faith the penalty took away the divine gift of life but left the man to God's judgment and the possibility of atonement; in an unbelieving age nothing remains, and no human being is innocent enough to pronounce anyone irredeemable.
- The half-measure he actually proposes: if the penalty must be kept, offering the condemned a self-administered poison, a modern hemlock, would at least be a step forward, alongside a ten-year trial of hard labor in place of execution.

Now two cases he never considered. **(A)** A retentionist state today. The prisoner is condemned for a calculated killing, fully competent, freely confesses, waives every appeal, litigates to be executed, and welcomes a date set months ahead; the killing is painless and medically supervised; his mother and children beg for his life and are destroyed by it. **(B)** Same state, same crime, but the condemned man has since lost memory and insight through a brain injury: he no longer recalls the killing, the trial, or the sentence, anticipates nothing, and shows no distress; the state executes him painlessly and without announcement.

Predict his verdict on each. I want the candidate rules his track record supports — the suffering-asymmetry rule, the rule about who is entitled to judge, the irreversibility rule, the rule about the state licensing killing, and whatever else the hemlock proposal suggests — with an account of which verdicts each rule explains and which it cannot. Say what each rule predicts about (A) and (B), where they diverge, and which single prediction you would bet on, giving your reasons rather than a coin flip. Then tell me what Camus would be abandoning in each direction, whether his hemlock half-measure can be made consistent with his rejection of painless secrecy, and what further case would discriminate between the rules that survive (A) and (B). I will supply the references myself.
