I am grading the timed essays for my upper-level seminar on the philosophy of technology this weekend, and one of the two set texts for the unit was Langdon Winner's 1980 essay "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" (we paired it with selections from Feenberg's critical theory of technology, and with a short historiographical piece on mid-century New York infrastructure). The essay question I set was deliberately two-part, because I wanted to see whether students could separate Winner's account of what makes an artifact political from the older debate about the direction of causation between technology and society:

> **(a)** Winner distinguishes two ways in which artifacts can have politics. State the distinction and illustrate it with two of his own examples, explaining what does the illustrating in each case. **(b)** Winner's title is a question, and his answer is often filed under the heading of technological determinism. Is the central claim of the essay best understood as a version of technological determinism, as a refutation of it, or as an attempt to displace that debate altogether? Defend your reading by reference to the argument of the essay rather than to its reception.

Students had fifty minutes, were permitted the essay itself but no notes, and were told that a strong answer would take a position on part (b) rather than merely survey the options. The submission below is from a student who has attended regularly and whose earlier work in the unit was solid but unremarkable. I would like a grade and, more importantly, a defensible justification for it that I can hand back to the student.

**Student submission:**

> The question in the title is doing real work. Winner is not announcing that artifacts are political actors in the way that parties or legislatures are, and he is not answering a question anyone had previously thought worth asking in those terms. He is trying to pry apart two questions that get collapsed whenever the phrase technological determinism comes up: what counts as political, and who or what is doing the determining. My claim is that the essay's contribution belongs almost entirely to the first question, and that this is why part (b) is a bit of a trap.
>
> Start with what Winner means by politics. He is not using the word in the sense of partisan controversy or public dispute. He is using it in a much older sense, the sense of arrangements of power and authority, of who is permitted to do what and on whose behalf. That is why a bridge can be political on his account without ever having been debated by anyone. It is also why he insists that the two familiar positions in the controversy over technology and society, namely that technology is an autonomous force that drags social life along behind it, and that technology is nothing but a mirror of social forces, both miss the point. Both assume the question is about direction of causation. Winner thinks the prior question is whether there are politics in the technical arrangements at all.
>
> He distinguishes two ways in which artifacts can be political. In the first, a technical problem arises within a community, and the only available way of solving it requires that the community's relations of authority be reorganized. His illustration is industrial pollution control: the means of dealing with the problem cannot be installed without someone acquiring new powers over someone else. The politics here is not something the machine expresses; it is something the solution requires. In the same family he places the choice among sources of energy, arguing that some of them, solar and wind among them, are compatible with dispersed, small-scale, locally controlled arrangements, while others, nuclear power most conspicuously, are not merely compatible with but seem to demand centralized, hierarchical, expertise-guarded institutions. In the second way, the politics is not required by the problem but chosen by the designer: a technical feature is built in for the sake of a political effect. His famous case is the low overpasses on Robert Moses's Long Island parkways, built, on Winner's telling, too low for public buses to pass beneath, which kept poor and Black New Yorkers away from Jones Beach while leaving the parkway open to private automobiles.
>
> Both cases are political in the same sense, and that is what makes the distinction worth drawing. But notice that only the second case involves anyone's intentions, and only the first involves anything like determination. This is why I say the essay is not really a determinist thesis at all. Determinism says that the development of technology fixes the shape of social and political life whether anyone wills it or not. Winner says something much weaker and much more interesting: that once you look closely at particular technical arrangements, you find power and authority in them, and you find that in some cases those arrangements could have been otherwise. If a technology could have been otherwise, then it was not determined, and if it was not determined, determinism is simply false. So Winner is not in the determinist camp, and he is not in the camp that reduces everything to social forces either. He is in a third place, which later writers have called technical politics: the view that the design of artifacts is itself a site of political struggle, and that changing the artifact is a way of changing the politics.
>
> One reservation. The Moses anecdote has not aged well. Historians of New York infrastructure have cast doubt on whether the overpasses were built low for the reasons Winner gives, and Winner himself, when the essay was reissued, allowed that the story is not as clean as the 1980 telling implies. That is a genuine problem for the example, though I do not think it is fatal to the essay, because the first category of cases does not depend on anyone's intentions and so cannot be touched by evidence about what Moses wanted.
