I'm two weeks into a seminar on knowledge and I've been keeping my own list of gut verdicts, because the assigned examples are all bank cases and airport cases and I wanted to see what happens with situations I actually recognize. Play the role of a working epistemologist here, think carefully, brainstorm out loud at the top if that helps you, and then give me the structured version. Here's what I've got.

Nurses: two nurses, identical training, identical double-check, identically legible order. One is drawing up saline for a flush, the other a drug where ten times the dose is fatal. The first one knows the dose is right; the second doesn't, and she needs to check a third time. Route: my wife has driven the same 400-mile route to our daughter's hospital a dozen times, checked traffic that morning, and literally everything rides on her arriving by two. I still say she knows the way. Café: I'm picking between two patios purely for the view, nothing at all turns on it, and I have a wierd hazy memory that the left one has tables outside — except I also vaguely remember hearing they changed owners. I'd say I don't know. Bridge: a footbridge I've crossed daily for six years. I know it holds me; I don't know it holds the pickup truck. Same belief, same evidence, different thing I'm relying on it for. Timetable: my friend is catching the 6:05 and I looked it up, he didn't. Nothing rides on it for me, everything rides on it for him. I say I know it, but somehow I feel I shouldn't turn around and tell him "you know it's 6:05." Hands: my entire career depends on it, I'm under oath, and I still say I know I have two hands — stakes don't seem to touch that one at all. Turnoff: my brother's wedding, and if I misremember the exit he's late to his own ceremony. I've been there once and I remember it clearly. I say I know, and if I got it wrong I'd have been unlucky rather than careless.

My tentative principle is something like: you know p only if p is good enough to rely on for the thing you're actually about to do, and only if you've ruled out the ways it could be false that you'd be blamed for ignoring. A classmate's diagnosis is harsher — she says half the time I'm not tracking knowledge at all, I'm tracking whether acting would be reasonable, and that where my verdicts genuinely shift it's because I'm silently sliding between two senses of "knows," since the word tracks what's salient in the conversation rather than anything about the subject. So: can you do the reflective-equilibrium thing for me? Lay out a menu of candidate principles instead of picking one and running with it, and state each precisely enough that I can tell whether it's actually different from the others — what parameter is doing the work, stakes, the specific action taken, whose stakes, salient doubts, probability of error, blameworthiness? For each candidate, say which of my verdicts each clause exists to save and which verdicts it breaks. Then tell me which one you'd actually sign up for and which of my intuitions it forces me to abandon. Also run it forward onto cases I haven't listed: (a) a colleague tells me our meeting moved to Thursday, I have no reason to doubt her and the stakes are trivial, but theres some small chance she misspoke; (b) a juror who is convinced beyond any doubt he could articulate, being asked to send someone to prison for twenty years; (c) me trusting my memory of where I left my keys when I'm already running late. No bibliography and no references please, I don't want a reading list — I want principles I can argue with. And be blunt about which of my intuitions are just bad.
