A friend and I argued for most of last night about whether the harshness objection to luck egalitarianism has ever been properly answered — the charge that a view making entitlements turn on responsibility cannot look with equanimity on the imprudent person who now needs help and gets none. My position is that the objection has no real answer: every candidate reply either spends the core claim (that shares should track choices rather than circumstances) or leaves a residue of the very harshness it was meant to dissolve. My friend thinks I am attacking a straw man. I should like you to adjudicate with numbers rather than rhetoric.

Score each reply below from 0 to 1 on two dimensions — fidelity (how much of the luck/choice distinction survives intact) and relief (how much of the harshness it genuinely removes, in the case of the uninsured motorcyclist who chose badly and now needs care):

1. The hypothetical insurance market: the imprudent receive the pay-out on a policy everyone would have taken out, not a handout.
2. The scope reply: the objection attacks a political programme nobody defends; the doctrine concerns the justification of shares, not a licence to let people suffer.
3. The degrees reply: responsibility is never binary, so a merely choice-sensitive view scales assistance down without ever reaching zero.
4. The institutional reply: the harm lies in the means test, not the principle — design transfers so no claimant must make a shameful revelation about their own imprudence.
5. The floor reply: a decent minimum is guaranteed irrespective of responsibility, and luck-sensitivity operates only above it.
6. The reframing reply: abandon responsibility-tracking as the point of equality and ask instead which distribution could reasonably be rejected.
7. The bullet-biting reply: the intuition behind the objection is paternalism about second-guessing people's choices, and respecting them as choosers entails accepting some bad outcomes.

Then propose a weighting between fidelity and relief, justify it rather than assume it, and give me the resulting ranking. State explicitly whether you take the trade-off I posit to be genuinely binding: if any reply earns above 0.8 on both dimensions, explain what it is doing that the others cannot, and concede the argument on my friend's behalf. Finish by naming the one reply I should concede and the one I should press hardest. Please write in British English.
