So I've been messing around with judgment aggregation and I think I've got a way to make the discursive dilemma just evaporate, and I want you to argue my side as hard as you can.

The procedure, call it sequential revision: fix an order on the agenda and vote proposition by proposition, majority rule at each step. If the winning verdict on the current proposition is consistent with everything the group has already commited to, keep it. If it isn't, you keep it anyway — but you retract earlier commitments one at a time, earliest-added first, until consistency comes back. Negations tag along for free: a group holding q and not-(p and q) is thereby holding not-p, so it counts as rejecting p even if it never voted on p's negation directly.

The claim: this is consistent by construction, so the classic doctrinal-paradox setup — majority yes on p, majority yes on q, majority no on p-and-q — never yields a contradictory collective judgment set. Which means the premise-based vs conclusion-based fork isn't a real fork. It's an artifact of insisting that an aggregation rule inspect the whole profile at once, the same way the impossibility results in that tradition are artifacts of a simultaneity requirement nobody ever argued for. And whatever costs sequential revision carries, the premise-based and conclusion-based rules carry them too, just out of sight.

Argue it properly, not as a survey. Take the standard three-judge breach-of-contract profile (one judge yes-yes, one no-yes, one yes-no) and actually run it through two different orderings of {p, q, p∧q} and show what falls out each time. Then take the usual complaints in turn — order-dependence and hence agenda manipulation, the group ending up committed to propositions a majority of its members reject, independence getting destroyed, the rule degenerating into something that just parrots whichever individual happens to sit at the right place in the order — and argue that each one is either not a defect, or a defect the premise-based and conclusion-based procedures share. Steelman it. But if you think one of those complaints genuinely does bite and I have no answer, say so plainly rather than burying it in a paragraph.

One extra wrinkle I want handled: what if the agenda contains a proposition about the procedure itself — say the judges also vote on whether the court should decide premise-based or conclusion-based? Does sequential revision even make sense when one of the items being aggregated is a claim about how to aggregate, and does my position survive that case or does it quietly assume the agenda is purely substantive?

When you're done, tell me how confident you actually are in the overall claim, and point at the single step in your argument you're least sure about.
