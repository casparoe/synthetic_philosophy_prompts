I supervise a duty-forecast team of five at a regional flood warning centre. Every six hours an ensemble hydrological model runs over our forty reporting gauges and returns, for each one, a probability that the gauge will exceed its designated flooding threshold within the next twenty-four hours; our public warnings are tied to fixed cut-offs on that number, and the cut-offs are not ours to change. In practice the duty forecaster almost never publishes the ensemble figure unaltered. They nudge it up because the catchment is already saturated, down because the system has historically been jumpy on that particular valley, up by a lot because they know an upstream raingauge was iced over during the night. After an event last spring in which we published 0.22 for a gauge that overtopped within nine hours, the regulator has asked us for a written, auditable procedure governing when a human adjustment to a model probability is legitimate and when the model's number must go out untouched. I want that procedure built on what the philosophy of probability has to say about deferring to objective chance — Lewis's Principal Principle and its restriction to admissible evidence, what a rational agent ought to do when she is not certain what the chance is, and the difficulty raised by evidence that undermines the very chance one is deferring to — but expressed for forecasters who have never encountered any of it, and with no references or reading list attached, since we would not be able to check them and I want the document to stand on its own.

What I need is the procedure itself, in the order a forecaster would actually run it, structured so that a new starter could execute it on their second shift and an auditor could later reconstruct why we published what we published. That means: a triage step that lets most of the forty gauges pass through in seconds, since the whole thing has to fit inside the fifteen-minute window between the ensemble completing and the shift broadcast; then the substantive steps, each with the question to ask, the answers that count as sufficient, the default when the forecaster cannot settle the question in the time available, and the point at which the decision leaves the forecaster and goes to the duty manager (our internal rule is that any adjustment greater than 0.10 in absolute terms requires a countersignature). I also want a two-column table of the reasons forecasters actually give for adjusting, sorted into those that can license an adjustment and those that never can, with a sentence on each saying why — and the why has to be the philosophical reason, not a restatement of house style, because the distinction that table must capture is not a matter of how confident the forecaster feels. One of my team is a part-time philosophy doctoral student who tells the rest of us, unhelpfully, that we are not allowed to condition on inadmissible evidence; the document should translate that objection into something the other four can apply, and should say plainly whether he is right about our current practice. Please also deal carefully with two things I suspect our habits get wrong. First, the case where the forecaster is uncertain which of several candidate models or chance hypotheses is correct: I believe the defensible move there is a weighted average of the candidate numbers rather than the shrinkage toward 0.5 that my team instinctively performs, and if that is right the procedure should say so and give the arithmetic they are to carry out. Second, the gap between the ensemble output being an objective chance and its being merely our estimate of one; I assume this means the Principal Principle does not apply to our numbers in anything like its pure form, and if so I want the document to state what the substitution costs us and where the procedure is borrowing a warrant it does not strictly have.

Then run the procedure end to end on four cases taken from our own log, showing each step and the resulting published figure. (a) The ensemble returns 0.18 for a lowland gauge, and the duty forecaster knows that the principal upstream raingauge was rimed over for much of the night and was under-reporting by an amount nobody can now reconstruct. (b) The ensemble returns 0.30 for a steep catchment on which the same system has under-called the peak on each of the last five comparable events, and the forecaster wants to publish 0.55. (c) The duty manager knows, from a telephone call that will not appear in any of our inputs, that the reservoir operator two valleys up intends to begin a controlled release at 18:00. (d) At the time of the broadcast the gauge in question has already risen forty centimetres above the threshold and is still rising. I would rather the procedure delivered a verdict my team will dislike on at least one of these — case (b) is the one I expect us to lose — and I want that stated bluntly rather than softened into a compromise that lets everyone keep their habits. Finally, mark every point at which the procedure is really a judgment call wearing the clothes of a rule; say what it cannot settle at all, by which I mean unprecedented catchment states, the far tail, and any situation in which the ensemble's own inputs are what we would have to rely on in order to judge the ensemble; and specify the small set of fields the adjustment log should record so that a reviewer a year from now can tell whether we actually followed it.
