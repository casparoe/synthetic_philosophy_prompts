I take the minutes for a reading group in cognitive science at my university, and last week two members — Claire, a fourth-year philosophy student, and Yusuf, a second-year in the psychology program — argued for the better part of an hour about the side-effect asymmetry in intentionality judgments (the chairman vignette pair, in which roughly 82 percent of participants say the chairman harmed the environment intentionally, against roughly 23 percent who say the same when the side effect is beneficial). I have reconstructed the exchange below as faithfully as I can. Neither of them asked me to referee, and I would rather not suggest which of them I found more convincing; I would simply like the argument audited.

1. Claire: The asymmetry is the most robust result in this literature, and it shows that the folk concept of intentional action is not value-neutral. A moral assessment of the side effect changes whether the concept applies, so moral considerations are doing constitutive work inside the concept rather than biasing a prior, purely descriptive judgment.

2. Yusuf: That is a leap from a pattern of judgments to a claim about conceptual content. The more economical story is pragmatic: in the harm vignette the chairman declares that he does not care about the damage, and participants read that as more than indifference — as something closer to endorsement — so they answer yes to a question about what he was really up to. Alter the question format, or make the conversational stakes explicit, and the asymmetry shrinks. If the effect lives in how people answer, it tells us nothing about what the concept is.

3. Claire: If the asymmetry survives the removal of the pragmatics, you have not explained it away, you have relocated it. The folk concept turns out to be sensitive to precisely these blame-relevant cues, and a concept whose application is toggled by cues of that kind is a concept with blame built into it. The asymmetry also appears in children young enough to lack the conversational machinery you are invoking, and in forced-choice formats, which rules out implicature as the mechanism.

4. Yusuf: Those replications are uneven and the effect sizes swing considerably across samples, so I would not lean on them. But grant them: they still do not get you to intentional action in particular. The same harm-benefit asymmetry shows up when people are asked whether the chairman was in favor of the effect, whether he decided to bring it about, whether he did it freely, and even whether he caused it. That is a general halo of blame spilling onto every agential predicate — a finding about evaluation, not about the content of one concept.

5. Claire: Generality argues for my position, not against it: if moral evaluation sits upstream of a whole family of agential concepts, then the non-neutrality is systematic rather than local. And here is a test that discriminates. Our lab has just finished a preregistered 2 × 2, n = 412: side effect harming versus benefiting, crossed with the agent's stated attitude, indifferent versus conflicted — in the conflicted version he says the outcome troubles him but that the profits justify it. Intentionality ascriptions came out at 79 percent for harm/indifferent, 76 percent for harm/conflicted, 18 percent for benefit/indifferent, and 41 percent for benefit/conflicted, while blame ratings in the harm conditions fell from 6.1 to 4.2 on a seven-point scale. If blame were driving the yeses in the harm case, cutting blame by nearly two points should have cut the intentionality rate; it moved three points. Blame is not the mechanism, and the halo account is refuted.

6. Yusuf: The harm cells are sitting at the ceiling, so they cannot display a fall — that is a scale artifact, not a finding. And the conflicted wording is not a clean manipulation of the perceiver's blame: it changes the agent's own conative profile, which is something the concept of intentional action is supposed to track in the first place. Meanwhile the benefit condition, which had room to move, moved enormously — 18 to 41 — exactly what you would expect if these are graded, evaluation-sensitive judgments being forced through a binary question.

7. Claire: If ceiling effects rescue your account every time the numbers go against it, your account is not testable with these instruments. Nor have you answered the deeper point: the two vignettes differ in moral valence, and once the agent's psychology is held fixed, it is the valence that predicts the judgment. I piloted a variant in which the side effect is a harm everyone would agree is deserved — the program wipes out an illegal dumping operation run by a rival firm that has been poisoning the town's water. Intentionality ascriptions fell to 34 percent, n = 48, close to the benefit-condition level. Valence, not harm, is doing the work.

8. Yusuf: That is an unpreregistered pilot with 48 online participants in which you changed not only the valence but what the agent is doing and what the side effect consists of; participants may not even classify that as a side effect. And notice the shape of this whole debate: when the asymmetry is robust you call it proof that the concept is moralized, and when a manipulation dampens it you say the effect has merely been relocated. I cannot identify any observation that would count against your thesis.
