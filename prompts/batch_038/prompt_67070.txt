So my sister works in traffic engineering for a mid-sized city and over the holidays she and her husband (a plaintiff-side personal injury lawyer, which I think explains a lot) got into a genuinely heated argument at the dinner table about a case that is basically her job right now. I was just sitting there with a plate on my lap. Their city switched on an adaptive signal-control system about fourteen months ago — cameras and in-pavement loop detectors feed a central optimizer that retimes the lights continuously to keep vehicles moving on the arterials. The important detail, and this is what they kept circling: it isn't a fixed program that was tuned once and frozen. The vendor's system keeps re-learning from field data after go-live, so what it was doing in month fourteen is not identical to anything my sister's team reviewed and signed off on in month zero. Over the year it quietly learned to favor the heavy corridors, which meant a couple of low-density neighborhoods on the edge of town got systematically starved of green time. Nobody chose that, there's no line of code that says "penalize the quiet neighborhoods." Then in the spring an ambulance leaving one of those neighborhoods hit five reds in a row on a corridor that used to be green-waved for emergency vehicles, lost something like six minutes, and the patient died. Family is suing, the city is trying to work out whether anyone actually did something wrong.

Her husband says there's a real responsibility gap here and that it's disqualifying. His version: for blame to be fair you need someone who could have done otherwise and who had some grip on the outcome; here every individual actor did a locally reasonable thing — the engineers built and documented a system that met the spec, the vendor shipped what was contracted, the city council approved a safety-improving upgrade on the strength of a review that was of the system as reviewed, and the ambulance crew drove correctly. The fatal behavior was produced by the interaction of a learned policy with a road network, months later, in a configuration nobody had in front of them, and the aggregate risk that was foreseeable ("some people will lose time at these lights") is not the same thing as foreseeing that this man would die. So either you scapegoat some junior engineer for a thing that wasn't his to prevent, or you accept the gap — and if you accept the gap, he thinks the honest conclusion is that this kind of self-modifying deployment shouldn't be happening at all, because a system that can kill without anyone being aptly blamed is exactly the thing the autonomous-weapons people were worried about, just with a much duller chassis.

My sister says the gap is fake, or at least that it's a magic trick performed with the word "control." Her version: the city knew at procurement that the system would keep changing in the field, and it deliberately chose a design where the deployed artifact diverges from the reviewed artifact — choosing to put a learner on a public road without a monitoring and override layer is not a neutral omission, it's a decision, and decisions to create unreviewable risk are the kind of thing you can be blamed for. When her husband said that proves too much — on that standard every statistical tool makes its deployers answerable for every outlier harm, so the concept stops discriminating — she said no, it proves exactly enough: the discriminating feature isn't foreseeing the particular death, it's that they knew the mechanism by which such deaths arise, had cheap options for constraining it (freezing the policy, keeping the emergency-preemption logic hard-coded outside the learner, an audit trail on drift), and didn't take them. He said fine, but then whose name goes on the indictment, and she said the corporation's — the city and the vendor jointly are a decision-making body with procedures, budgets, and the power to have acted otherwise, and bodies like that can bear responsibility in a way that doesn't reduce to any one employee. He said that's just a fine with philosophy taped onto it: a cost of doing business, no resentment, no stigma, nobody actually blamed, so the gap survives anyway. She said so what — maybe what the family is owed isn't a villain but a repairable obligation: compensation, redesign, a duty to audit drift, which don't require anyone to be culpable in his narrow sense. And he said she was sliding between "responsible" and "on the hook," which she denied.

I couldn't referee it and I still don't know where I land — I find myself agreeing with whichever of them spoke last. Can you go through their exchange move by move and tell me who's right at each step, not just who wins overall? I mean: rate or judge each of the distinct moves (his control-and-foreseeability condition, his specific-vs-aggregate foreseeability point, the "proves too much" objection, her procurement-choice reply, her "knowing the mechanism" reformulation, the group-agent move, his "it's just a fine" reply, her forward-looking-obligations retreat, and his closing accusation of equivocation), say which ones actually land and which ones don't, and say what if anything both of them are missing. Then give me an overall verdict on whether there's a real responsibility gap in this case. Flag anywhere one of them is quietly shifting between senses of responsibility — blame, causal attribution, legal liability, duty to repair — because I suspect that's where a lot of the heat is coming from.
