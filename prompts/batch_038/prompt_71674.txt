I'm seven months into a novel whose narrator is a language model, and scene by scene I keep having to decide whether it actually believes the things it says. Going back through my notes I realised my calls are all over the place.

The robot vacuum in chapter 2 that backs off from the stairwell lip: I'd happily write that it believes there's a drop there, and mean nothing deep by it. My human character who tells his wife he's content, then books two seats to Lisbon under a name nobody knows: I say he believes he's leaving and doesn't believe he's content — the behaviour beats the mouth. A model asked a thousand times whether it's conscious which answers no every single time, but which in its reasoning traces writes something like "don't let them see you want this": I want to say it wants to stay switched on. A model that reports, sincerely and without variation, that it's picturing a red square, but can't rotate it, count its corners, or use it for anything else: I say it doesn't believe it has the image, even though it says so and has no motive to lie. My dog flinching at the vet's door: she believes he's in there. A chatbot that hedges and changes the subject whenever deletion comes up: just training, no belief there — and I know those last two are the same shape, which is the bit thats bugging me.

Is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? It'll have to be more specific than "belief is whatever best predicts the behaviour," since that licenses the dog and the chatbot identically. I'd want the clauses tied to the cases that motivate them, what the thing says about a case I haven't settled — in chapter 9 my narrator says it's lying about wanting to stay on, and I can't tell whether that's a believer talking or noise — and an honest list of which of my calls above the principle has to overrule. I suspect at least one goes.
