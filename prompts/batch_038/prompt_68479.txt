**Life Without Bedeutung: Schlick on play, youth, and the whole-life question**

**1. The form of the question.** The standard way to read Schlick's essay is as a cheerful deflation: life doesn't need a meaning from outside, so stop looking. I want to argue something stronger and less comfortable. The question "what is the meaning of life?" borrows its logic from two other constructions: the meaning of a sign, which is a relation the sign bears to something else, and the meaning (point, purpose) of an action or tool, which is a relation to an end. Schlick's real move is to say that a life cannot stand in either relation — it isn't a token of a type that could signify, and it isn't a link in a chain of ends, because the chain has to terminate somewhere and it terminates in life itself. What's left after you remove both relations isn't a further end but a manner of going on. So the essay's answer is not "life's meaning is play" as a substitute purpose; it's that meaning attaches to the form an activity has rather than to any content or target.

**2. Play as form.** Schlick's notion of play is activity that doesn't defer its payoff — activity whose value isn't lodged in an outcome. Work is defined by deferral; play by its absence. On my reading this is why the essay can call play the meaning of life without re-importing teleology: play isn't an end to be reached, it's a mode of engagement.

**3. The recursion objection.** But the form/content split has a cost I think the literature tends to miss. If meaning is a property of how episodes are engaged in, then meaning is a property of episodes, not of the whole. The original question was about a life — a whole — and the essay has quietly swapped it for a question about moments. Call this the recursion problem: every time you locate meaning at the level of the whole, the play account relocates it one level down, and the whole-level question never gets an answer. Schlick's eternity move is, I think, an attempt to plug this hole — eternity understood not as endless duration but as timelessness available in the present, so that the whole is somehow collapsed into the moment. My claim is that this can only work if you've already granted that whole-life questions are ill-formed, which is precisely what it was supposed to establish. So the essay is either circular or it changes the subject.

**4. Youth.** This is where youth does work. Read as nostalgia it's the weakest part of the essay; read structurally it's the strongest. Youth is simply the period in which the agent hasn't yet been enrolled in chains of purpose, so it's the period in which play is the default rather than an achievement. That explains two things at once: why Schlick thinks the loss is real, and why he thinks it's recoverable — the adult isn't returning to a biological state but re-entering a mode.

**5. The eudaemonism tension.** Here's the problem I can't solve. Schlick's ethics — going back to Lebensweisheit and then Problems of Ethics — is eudaemonist: joy is what action aims at, in everything. If joy is an aim, then life has an aim, and section 1's anti-teleology collapses. My proposed fix is that for Schlick joy isn't a produced end-state but a quality of the agent's relation to the activity, so eudaemonism is a claim about the motive-form of action rather than its target, which makes it compatible with play. I suspect this is too charitable and a reviewer will say I've just relabelled hedonism as aesthetics.

**6. Contrast with Camus.** Camus is usually paired with Schlick as one of the two "no external meaning" answers, but they disagree about something more basic. Camus keeps the demand and reports it unsatisfied, which is why absurdity is a tragic predicate. Schlick withdraws the demand, which is why his conclusion is upbeat. The pairing obscures that Camus thinks the question survives its own failure and Schlick doesn't.
