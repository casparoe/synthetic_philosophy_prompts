I'm a third-year doing a joint degree in philosophy and ecology, and this term I'm in an environmental ethics seminar where a big chunk of the syllabus is wild animal suffering. Last week we got onto a pretty grim claim: that when a wetland gets drained or a woodlot gets levelled for a housing estate, total suffering might actually go *down*, since you've deleted a few hundred thousand animals whose lives were mostly parasitism, starvation, and being eaten alive as juveniles before they ever got to be adults. I tried to push that line (badly) and another student shut it down with "yeah but you can't mess with the balance of nature," the prof sort of nodded, and we moved on to something else. I left feeling like a big concession had just been made that shouldn't have been, but I couldn't say what, and now I want to write my seminar paper on exactly that moment.

The annoying thing is I don't think the phrase means one thing. Sometimes it sounds like a claim about population dynamics — things self-regulate, you pull one thread and everything else wobbles. Sometimes it sounds like a claim about function, that every species is doing a job so the system needs all of them. Sometimes it's about disturbance and recovery, sometimes it's flatly normative — don't intervene, nature's arrangements are presumptively better than anything we'd design — and sometimes I suspect it's just a dressed-up way of saying "you're being hubristic about what you can predict." Can you lay out the distinct precise claims that slogan could be making, sorted from the ones that are true pretty much by defininition or that just restate something we already knew, through the ones that are genuine empirical hypotheses ecology could test, to the normative ones, to the ones that are probably just false? For each reading I want to know what exactly is supposed to be balanced or stable — population sizes, species composition, biomass, nutrient flows, total welfare — over what timescale and under what kind of perturbation, and then whether it does any real work in the habitat-destruction argument: would it show that clearing the fen produces more suffering than leaving it alone, or only that we can't tell which? I'm aware ecologists mostly ditched the tidy equilibrium picture a while ago and that "stability" got split into several technical notions — resistance, resilience, persistence, something else I've forgotten — but I have no idea how those map onto what a philosopher in a seminar is actually asserting when they say the slogan.

Also worth knowing: the same people say it in totally different situations. My flatmate says it about feeding the ducks in the park and about a beaver dam that flooded a lane near my parents' place, and there it seems to mean something closer to "hands off, it'll sort itself out," whereas in the seminar it was doing work about whether destroying habitat is net bad for the animals in it. I'd like the menu to be able to account for both uses and to flag which reading is being reached for in each case. And if some of the candidate meanings turn out to be doing purely rhetorical work — no content anyone could disagree with, just a way of ending the conversation — say so bluntly, because I think that's what happened to me last week and I'd like to be able to name it.
