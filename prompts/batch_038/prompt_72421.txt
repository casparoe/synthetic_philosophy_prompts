You are a political philosopher who has taught a graduate seminar on the concept of liberty for many years, and who is especially good at the reverse-engineering task: given a person's judgments about particular cases, reconstructing the decision procedure they are actually running — including when they insist it is a single procedure and you suspect it is not. Please think carefully and work through this systematically rather than answering from first impressions.

**The situation.** For two years a colleague of mine has been writing a book about freedom, and I have kept a private log of her verdicts, drawn from circulated drafts, seminar discussion, and correspondence. She is emphatic that she works with one idea of freedom, not a cluster of ideas, and that the same idea accounts for every entry below. Her term is always "unfree," and she resists being told she means different things by it in different places. Note that she always relativizes: she says someone is unfree *with respect to* some specified option, never unfree simpliciter.

The log, in my paraphrase:

- **E1.** An elected council prohibits amplified music in a public plaza after 11 p.m. following residents' complaints. Buskers petitioned, were heard at two public hearings, and lost; they have since organized a ballot initiative and several have stood for the council. *Verdict:* the buskers are not unfree with respect to playing after 11 p.m. She adds that they are of course prevented from doing it — prevention and unfreedom come apart here.
- **E2.** A month-to-month tenant whom the landlord may turn out at any time, for any reason, on thirty days' notice. No statute constrains his discretion; no cause is required; her only recourse on receiving notice is to delay it by not leaving. He has renewed automatically for nine years, likes her, and has said he would never turn her out. *Verdict:* unfree with respect to staying, notwithstanding that she has never been interfered with and probably never will be. What matters, she says, is that whether she stays is, at bottom, up to him.
- **E3.** A migrant worker whose permit is renewed annually by an official with complete uncoded discretion: no published criteria, no reasons given, no appeal. He has renewed every application for eleven years and seems well disposed. *Verdict:* unfree; the renewal record is beside the point.
- **E4.** Public housing in which building management controls the heat, but a housing code fixes minimum temperatures, an inspectorate must be triggered by any tenant's complaint, complaints must be answered within a fixed period, and the code has twice been amended after tenant associations lobbied. *Verdict:* not unfree with respect to indoor temperature. She allows that the position would be precarious in a different way if the inspectorate's funding were itself discretionary.
- **E5.** A woman who has stopped walking through a park after dark because of repeated assaults there. Nothing forbids her entry, no one is presently threatening her, and she may report any assault afterward. *Verdict:* unfree with respect to walking there. A report filed after the fact is not a say over whether the risk exists, and she is unfree on the nights when nothing happens too.
- **E6.** A scholarship program funded by a progressive income tax, enabling recipients who could not otherwise attend university to attend. *Verdict:* the recipients are freer with respect to attending than before, and the taxpayers are not made unfree by the tax in the sense that matters, since the tax is laid down by rules they can contest and change. She concedes the second half is the part of the view she has least to say about.
- **E7.** An adult recovering from a compulsive checking ritual that once consumed four hours a day. During the worst period nobody restrained him, no law or person blocked his leaving the house, and he could have walked out at any moment. *Verdict:* he was unfree during that period. She acknowledges this is the entry her critics pounce on, and says any theory forced to call him free has lost the plot — but she has not explained to my satisfaction how the same idea covers both E7 and E4.

**The new case, on which she has agreed to rule in her final chapter.** A court has appointed a professional guardian for M, who acquired a cognitive disability in a collision at twenty-nine and is now thirty-four. He manages her money and consents to her treatment. Statute binds him: he must give effect to M's expressed wishes unless doing so would seriously harm her; he is licensed and bonded; he files an annual accounting that a public office audits; and M may petition the court to review any particular decision, which the court must hear within thirty days. She has petitioned twice and won once, reversing his choice of group home. She reports being content and does not want the arrangement changed. Two features trouble me. First, she cannot end the guardianship herself: termination requires a judicial finding of capacity, and although she may petition for that finding, the assessment it triggers is arranged through the guardian's own office; his appointment has no term and no renewal, so nothing about his position depends on her satisfaction. Second, staff at her day program report that over three years she has stopped requesting things she used to request — a trip to the coast, an allowance she would control — and a former client of the same guardian, discharged two years ago, says she spent years inside the arrangement not wanting what she wants now.

**What I want from you.**

1. Begin with brainstorming. Enumerate at least five candidate decision procedures that could generate her verdicts — including at least one that is not a familiar member of the standard triad of freedom as non-interference, freedom as non-domination, and freedom as effective self-direction or self-mastery. State each as a rule someone could actually apply to an unfamiliar case: conditions to be checked, not a slogan or a school label.
2. Test every candidate against all seven entries. Present this as a table or an equally systematic comparison, marking where each delivers her verdict, where it strains, and where it fails outright. Identify which entries do most of the filtering work, and say whether any single entry is compatible with only one candidate.
3. Narrow to the survivors and predict her verdict on M. If they converge, say what she will write. If they diverge, commit to a single best guess with your reasoning and a rough confidence — I do not want a survey of possibilities dressed as an answer. State the verdict in her own option-relative form: unfree with respect to what, exactly?
4. Predict the one-line gloss she will attach to the verdict, and identify which prior entry she is most likely to invoke as the closest analog. My own hunch is that E4, E2, and E7 each have a claim; defend your pick against the strongest alternative.
5. Take seriously the possibility that she splits the verdict — free in one respect, unfree in another. If you think she will, say which half she treats as freedom proper and which she demotes to some other concept, and what that demotion costs her claim to be working with a single idea.
6. Design the one further case I should put to her that would most sharply separate the two leading candidates, and state in advance what each predicts about it. The predictions must genuinely conflict; a case on which both rules agree is worthless to me.
7. If, after all this, you conclude that no single rule is consistent with the whole log, say so plainly and specify the minimal revision to one entry that would restore consistency.

Reason from the material I have supplied. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations, and do not build your answer on attributing positions to particular authors; if naming a tradition helps you flag where a rule comes from, do it in passing, but nothing should turn on an attribution being right. Use the tripartite vocabulary as shorthand where it helps, and tell me where you think those labels would mislead. Show your enumeration and your entry-by-entry check in the open before you reach the prediction, and take as much space as the analysis requires — but none that it does not.
