Last term my reading group spent a session on choice without preference — the Buridan's ass problem — and afterward two of its members, Mara and Julian, argued about a real case involving a friend of ours I'll call N. N. had ended up holding two job offers he could not rank: same salary, same teaching load, same city, same department size. He listed every consideration he could think of and the two columns came out even. He asked both institutions for more time, agonized for five weeks, and by the time he was ready to answer, both offers had been withdrawn. He tells the story as one of paralysis; Mara and Julian tell it as a case study.

Their exchange ran as follows.

1. **Mara:** N. was irrational, not merely unlucky. Between two options he judged exactly equally good, any selection is arbitrary, and an arbitrary selection is not a reasoned one. The only course that preserved his rationality was to keep deliberating or to decline to choose.

2. **Julian:** That standard condemns itself here. Declining to choose was itself one of his options, and it was dominated: it guaranteed the worst outcome (no job at all), while either of the other two guaranteed a good one. His error was not that he chose arbitrarily; it was that he chose five weeks late.

3. **Mara:** You have restated my point. The consideration you cite — do not take the dominated option — is a reason to choose, not a reason to choose *this one*. The asymmetry you found sits between choosing and waiting; between the two offers there was still nothing. An action can be required and yet be performed for no reason, and "you ought to pick one" does not make "I picked A because…" true.

4. **Julian:** That division will not hold. His menu was A, B, and wait; wait is dominated; so the rationally eligible set is {A, B}, and taking any member of the eligible set is what choosing rationally amounts to. If instead you say every choice from a tied set is irrational, then rationality is impossible whenever two options tie at the top — and ties at the top are not exotic, they are ordinary: two identical products on the shelf, two equally clear routes home. Your view indicts daily life wholesale.

5. **Mara:** Then notice what you have surrendered. On your account, "I had no reason to prefer it" is a complete and rational explanation of a choice — which is to say, no explanation at all. It also severs an inference everyone relies on: we routinely learn what people prefer from what they pick. If choice can come unhooked from preference in cases like N.'s, that inference fails exactly where it looked most secure.

6. **Julian:** It is not severed, because it was never drawn from single choices. Revealed-preference reasoning works with choice sets rather than choice events: an agent counts as indifferent between A and B when the set he would accept out of {A, B} is {A, B}. Watching N. take A once is consistent with indifference and consistent with preference, and the framework already concedes that one observation cannot separate them. Nothing has come unhooked; you have only noticed that a single data point is a single data point.

7. **Mara:** For an agent with one life and one choice, that concession swallows the case. N. chose once; there is no second draw in which his chosen set could be revealed, so indifference does no work here beyond dignifying delay. And I doubt the tie was ever a tie in goodness. If the offers had really been equally good, adding $500 to one would have had to make it strictly better; ask N. and he will tell you it would not have settled anything, because his difficulty was that he could not rank them at all, not that they scored the same. If they were neither better nor worse nor equally good but simply not rankable, then the assumption your eligible sets depend on — that any two options can be compared — fails, and your dominance argument has no framework to stand in.

8. **Julian:** Dominance does not need A and B to be comparable. It needs only that each of them beats wait, and those are two separate pairwise comparisons, either of which can hold even if A and B cannot be weighed against each other. And your $500 test proves too much: it would show that no two real options are ever exactly equally good, which leaves your own notion of a tie without a single instance.

They both turned to me at the end and I could not decide, which is why I am asking you. Please open with a one-sentence verdict on which of the two is more right overall. Then take the eight moves in order and rate each — clearly right, defensible, questionable, or clearly mistaken — with one or two sentences saying why it earns that rating; where a move is partly right, say which part survives and which does not. Close with no more than three sentences on what both of them overlooked. Keep the entire response under 700 words.
