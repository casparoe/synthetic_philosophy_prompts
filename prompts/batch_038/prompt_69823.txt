ok so I've just been landed with a prosecution for something I honestly didn't know was an offence — I run a tiny food business out of my kitchen and apparently there's a registration requirement buried in some regulation that nobody mentioned to me when I started. My solicitor gives me the old line: getting a fact wrong can be a defence, getting the law wrong basically never is. And that's what's got me stuck at 2am, not the case so much as the philosophy underneath it.

What picture of knowledge does that rule presuppose? It looks like it treats me as though, once the rules are published somewhere, I know everything that follows from them — every implication, every instrument my activity happens to fall under — which is precisely the omniscience everyone agrees no actual knower has. But the same legal system will refuse to enforce a rule too vague for an ordinary person to work out what it requires, and it excuses people all the time for misjudging plain physical facts. So does it really presuppose we're logically omniscient about the law, or only that we carry a standing duty to find out — and if it's the second, what's doing the work in the cases where finding out was genuinely impossible?

And contract law binds me to terms I never read, which looks like the same move in a different building. Reconstruct whatever theory of knowledge and of blame the whole package needs, separate what it's strictly committed to from what it merely happens to fit nicely, say whether the parts are consistent with each other, and tell me what would have to change in practice if we took seriously that someone can know all the premises and still miss the conclusion. British English please, and no reading list — I want the reasoning, not the references.
