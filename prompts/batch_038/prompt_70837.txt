It's about sphexishness — the classic digger wasp example, the one everybody repeats. Wasp paralyzes a cricket, drags it to the burrow entrance, goes inside to inspect the nest before hauling the prey in. If you nudge the cricket a few inches while she's in there, she comes out, finds it, drags it back to the entrance... and then restarts the whole inspect-the-nest routine instead of just pulling it in. Repeat forever. That's supposed to be the picture of behavior that looks intelligent and is really a canned subroutine.

His argument, written out as premises:

1. Call this the interruption test: an agent is genuinely non-sphexish only if there is no intervention on its environment, mid-task, that makes it re-run a step it has already completed rather than adjust to the change. The wasp fails it. That's what makes the example worth telling.
2. Every agent is a physical system, so what it does at time t is a function of its state and its inputs — i.e. the output of some fixed procedure, whether anyone can write the procedure down or not. Human brains included.
3. The standard reply — humans learn, re-plan, form intentions, that's the difference — just adds more procedure. A learning rule is a fixed procedure for changing procedures. A planner is a fixed procedure for generating plans. So it doesn't escape (2), it just hides the code behind more code.
4. Same for self-modification. An agent that rewrites its own policy is running a fixed policy-rewriting policy. Stack as many levels as you want; every level is still a subroutine, so (1) and (2) apply at every level.
5. Therefore every agent that runs on physics is sphexish in the sense that's supposed to matter. There's no difference in kind between me and the wasp, only a difference in how many layers of procedure sit between the interruption and the response.
6. My own version: I can build something that looks exactly like the wasp but "learns" — a webhook handler that gives up after five retries and pages a human. By (3) and (4) that handler is no less sphexish than the wasp, which I'll happily grant. But then by the same route I'm no less sphexish than the handler, and the handler was my example of a mere mechanism.
7. But if nothing is non-sphexish, "sphexishness" isn't tracking a distinction in the world, it's just a fancy way of saying "made of matter." So the wasp example can't be doing the work people think: either the wasp is as flexible as I am and the famous experiment proves nothing, or the concept is empty and all the free-will and AI talk built on top of it goes down with it.
