I'm about two-thirds through a novel whose narrator is a decision theorist, and she's loosely based on my actual sister, who does this stuff for a living — so I've been sending my sister the cases the character chews on in each chapter, one at a time, mostly to stop myself writing her into a contradiction I can't fix later.

Her verdicts so far, in the order I asked. Newcomb: take only the big box. The XOR blackmail letter: don't pay. Counterfactual mugging, coin already landed and it came up tails, so they want $100 from you that would have got you $10,000 had it come up heads: pay. Parfit's hitchhiker, driver's already dropped you in town: pay up, and don't pretend you were planning to all along. The smoking lesion guy: smoke. A one-shot prisoner's dilemma against a stranger you'll never see again and know nothing about, except that they're probably a normal person reasoning roughly the way you do: cooperate. And when I asked whether she'd ever sign off on different answers for "what you'd commit to in advance" versus "what you'd do once you're standing there," she said no, never, those two have to come out the same or you've got a broken agent.

Now the last chapter, which I've allready half-written. My character gets a letter from a foundation that runs the mugging scheme at industrial scale: they flip a coin, they form their prediction by simulating her beforehand, and had it come up heads they'd have handed a large sum to some stranger picked at random. It came up tails, so they want $1,000 from her. She'll never meet or hear about that stranger, the foundation keeps no list of who paid and who didn't and never tells anyone, nothing about her reputation is in play anywhere, and I've written her as self-interested without being a monster. Pay or refuse — which way does my sister come down?

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, a couple of sentences apiece, nothing before or after the list: (1) the rules that fit every verdict above, named so I can tell them apart; (2) what each of those rules says about the foundation letter; (3) your best guess at her ruling and why you'd bet on it; (4) the one further case I could send her that would settle which rule she's really running; (5) if it's genuinely close, a way to write the scene so the novel holds up whichever answer she gives.
