I'm in a political theory seminar and I have to write something on freedom and the administrative state, and honestly I keep losing the argument to a friend of mine who's a straight-up negative libertarian — he says all my talk about domination is just warm feelings dressed up as liberty. So I want to practice against the other side. Please play a committed theorist of liberty as non-domination: someone in the republican line, who thinks freedom means not being subject to the arbitrary will of another rather than merely not being interfered with, and who holds that you can be unfree even when nobody ever touches you. Don't play any particular living philosopher — play an unnamed proponent who knows that tradition cold, thinks it's basically right, but is happy to argue from first principles rather than from authority. Be sharp and don't strawman me, and don't strawman yourself either: concede small points when they're genuinely conceded, but never give up the core claim. Stay in character until I say 'stop', keep each reply to a couple of paragraphs so it feels like a real conversation (no lists, no headers, no bibliography or recommended reading — I can't look anything up anyway), and if I misdescribe your view, correct me in character.

Here's the case I want to fight about. I made it up on purpose so we can't just recite a famous example. A city runs a benefits program. Every eligibility decision is made by a scoring rule that's fully public: the inputs, the weights, the thresholds, all posted online, and no caseworker, manager, or mayor has any power to alter an individual's outcome or grant an exception. Two residents with identical inputs get identical decisions, every time, and there's an appeals board, though it too is bound entirely by the rule. But the city council can rewrite the rule whenever it wants — no notice, no hearing, no obligation to explain — and it has done so four times in a decade, each time retroactively, and each time some people's checks just stopped mid-year. My friend says that's not domination, that's just ordinary politics: the residents are free on any sane definition, because there's nobody they have to curry favor with and nothing anybody can do to them at their personal discretion.

So here's my opening move, and I'm going to be deliberately annoying about it. Your theory is committed to saying these people are unfree even though nothing has been done to them and no individual human being has any power over them at all. That makes freedom a purely modal thing, a fact about what could happen, which means I can lose my freedom without anyone acting and without anyone in particular being able to act. Second, it makes freedom a function of how well the rules are designed, so on your scoreboard a demanding state with good rules leaves people freer than a feeble state that can't enforce anything — and at that point "liberty" is just a rebranding of good government, which is exactly what you accuse positive liberals of doing. Third, if non-domination is really about security and not being at someone's mercy, isn't it a value about welfare or status rather than a kind of liberty, so you've quietly switched topics and kept the old word for marketing? I know that's three attacks at once, so take whichever you think is most dangerous first.
