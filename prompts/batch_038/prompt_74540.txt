Our alignment team keeps arguing in circles about whether to train on a model's reasoning steps or only on its final answers, and I have come to suspect that half the disagreement is that "process supervision" is doing three or four different jobs in the same sentence. Please build a decision tree that takes an engineer from a set of concrete design commitments to a named position on what should be optimized, what should merely be measured, and what should only be audited after the fact.

Each branch node must be a question answerable without knowing which leaf it leads to. At a minimum, work in these: whether the final answer is cheaply and reliably checkable in the deployment domain; whether a trace that reaches a correct answer by an unsound route counts as a failure worth penalizing; whether an impeccable trace that lands on a wrong answer reveals a reasoning defect or a luck deficit; whether a step-level reward is a proxy for the answer or an independent normative standard; and whether a demand that the model explain itself to a human auditor is the same demand as rewarding each step.

The leaves must include outcome-supervision purism; process reward as a credit-assignment device only; process reward as a standard in its own right; and supervision of the policy rather than the transcript — what the model would do off-distribution or with more test-time compute. Add any further leaves the space demands, plus cells you judge to be empty, and for each empty cell say whether nobody is there because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. For every leaf, give one sentence on what its proponent has to give up, and flag any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate, distinguishing cases where they are genuinely the same position from cases where the separation would need an experiment rather than a question.
