So in my history-of-economic-thought seminar we just spent two weeks on Berkeley's Querist, which was genuinely not what I expected from the guy who thinks tables don't exist. The paper assignment is to take one of his money claims and run it against a real historical episode, and I've picked the Continental currency — partly because I suspect it's about the worst news available for him and I want to find out whether he survives it.

Facts I'll take as given, so you don't have to guess at any of it: Congress started emitting bills of credit in 1775, denominated in dollars and nominally payable in specie someday, but there was no redemption mechanism and under the Articles Congress had no power to tax anyone, so nothing was ever going to soak the notes back up. Emissions ran through 1779, the states piled their own paper on top, the British counterfeited it out of New York, and by early 1780 it had collapsed far enough that Congress recalled the old currency at 40 to 1 and tried a new issue backed by the states, which also died. Hence "not worth a Continental." What finally steadied things was Robert Morris, French loans in actual coin, and the Bank of North America in 1781-82.

Here's the analysis I want. Start from three specific claims: that money is "so far useful, as it stirreth up industry"; the query about "whether the true idea of money, as such, be not altogether that of a ticket or counter"; and the one asking whether, the denominations being kept even if the bullion were gone, things might still be rated, bought and sold and a circulation of commerce maintained. Take them one at a time against what actually happened, and tell me which ones the episode supports and which ones it breaks.

The thing I can't resolve on my own is that Berkeley looks like he's got two different mechanisms and I can't tell which one is load-bearing. One is a proportion story — the quantity of money has to be calibrated to the circulation and industry it's carrying, and over-issue is what killed the Continental. The other is closer to a ticket-has-to-be-honored story — the note was worthless because no authority would ever take it back, since there was no tax demand behind it. Does the Querist actually commit him to either? Can you show me where the two come apart on this case, i.e. where they'd predict different things about 1779?

Then the harder question: is this even a counterexample? Berkeley keeps writing about what a "well-govern'd State" ought to do, and Congress wasn't one, so maybe the collapse just shows what happens when you ignore him rather than that he's wrong. I want you to push on whether that move is a legitimate scope restriction or a way of making the theory unfalsifiable, and whether the Querist is doing prediction at all versus exhortation. Also worth considering the opposite worry — his own psychology seems to predict the death spiral exactly (money stirs industry only while people will it to, and once the prevailing will turns the note stops moving anything), which would make him the best explainer in the room.

Whether the 1781-82 recovery — a little paper, some confidence, a bank — vindicates his national bank pitch or is just metallism wearing his vocabulary, and whether Hume or Smith would explain the depreciation better than he does. Be explicit about where his theory goes silent: counterfeiting, legal tender laws, the New England price-fixing attempts.
