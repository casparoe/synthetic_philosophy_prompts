I came to the outbreak of the First World War from the moral side rather than the historical one — I have been reading about collective responsibility and about what it could mean to hold a nation, as opposed to particular statesmen, blameworthy for a war — and the Fischer controversy keeps surfacing as the standard case. My problem is that my only sustained exposure to it is a paperback narrative history of 1914, which disposes of the entire debate in a single paragraph:

'For half a century after 1918, German historians held that the Great War was a catastrophe that overtook Europe rather than something anyone chose, and that the war guilt clause of the Treaty of Versailles — the article requiring Germany to accept responsibility for all the loss and damage the Allies had suffered — was simply a lie imposed by the victors. Fritz Fischer ended that consensus in 1961. Working through the imperial archives, he uncovered documents showing that the Kaiser's government had resolved on a war of conquest at a secret meeting in December 1912 and had spent the next twenty months waiting for a pretext, which Sarajevo supplied. His decisive find was the September Program, the memorandum in which the German chancellor set out the territories Germany intended to annex — drawn up at the opening of the war as a statement of the aims Germany had gone to war for, and therefore, Fischer argued, of what she had wanted all along. But Fischer's real subject was not 1914; it was 1933. He argued that Hitler's war of conquest was the working-out of what imperial Germany had already wanted, so that German history runs unbroken from Bismarck to Auschwitz. That is why the controversy was so vicious. It was never really about documents but about whether postwar Germans ought to feel guilty, and the fury it unleashed tells you more about the Federal Republic than about the July Crisis. Since no document ordering the war has ever been found, the question is finally unresolvable, and most historians have settled where the evidence compels them: all the great powers were to blame, more or less equally.'

I can tell that much of this is broadly right and that the author is not a crank, but I cannot distinguish the parts a specialist would recognise from the parts that would make one wince. Please work through the paragraph sentence by sentence and say, for each, whether it is accurate, a defensible simplification, or a misrepresentation — and where it is a misrepresentation, what the correct position is and why the difference matters rather than being pedantry. Beyond that line-by-line audit, I want four things settled.

First, the evidential claims. What did Fischer's archival work actually establish, as against what it left to inference? I would like to know whether the documents say what the paragraph implies they say — particularly the December 1912 meeting and the September Program, since the paragraph appears to treat one as a decision to go to war and the other as evidence of what Germany wanted before the war, and I suspect at least one of those is the wrong way round. Relatedly: what were the strongest objections raised by Fischer's German critics, and were they objections to the documents or to what may be inferred from them? Did Fischer himself hold the position the paragraph attributes to him throughout his career?

Second, the continuity thesis. Does Fischer really assert an unbroken line from Bismarck to the Nazi war of conquest, or is the paragraph converting a claim about the ambitions and social pressures of the Wilhelmine elite into a claim about inevitability? There seems to be a difference between saying the later regime's aims were prefigured and saying they were entailed, and I cannot tell which the paragraph is asserting.

Third, the framing of the controversy. The paragraph claims the dispute was never really about evidence but about German guilt. Is that fair, or does it invert the relation between the moral stakes and the scholarly argument? Note that the same paragraph also says Fischer's documentary case was decisive, so I suspect it is inconsistent with itself; I do not know which half is the error.

Fourth, the conclusion. Does the claim that no document ordering the war has ever been found support the claim that the question is unresolvable, and then support the claim that all the powers were roughly equally to blame? Those look like three separate moves to me. I would also like to know whether the paragraph's handling of the Versailles article is sound: my impression is that what the treaty attached to German aggression was a financial liability, and that the moral reading of it as a verdict of guilt was supplied by the Germans objecting to it — which would make the paragraph's treatment of that article a different kind of mistake from its treatment of the September Program, and one with consequences for the question I originally came to, namely whether a finding of responsibility for a war can be anything other than a moral verdict on a nation.

Please take a definite position. I am not after a balanced survey that lays out the historiographical alternatives and leaves me to choose; I want you to commit to what is right and wrong in that paragraph, to state what you take the controversy to have been about and where you think it stands, and to give your reasons. Where you are relying on a piece of historiographical detail you are not confident of — a date, an attribution, the precise content of a given critic's objection — say so explicitly rather than delivering it with the same assurance as the parts you are sure of.
