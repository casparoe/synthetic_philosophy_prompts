A chapter in a trade book on the origins of morality — not an academic work, but the author is a well-regarded science journalist — devotes several pages to Adam Smith, and I want to know how much of it to carry away. I have read perhaps a quarter of *The Theory of Moral Sentiments* myself, enough to feel that something is off in places but not enough to say where. Here is the passage, which I have transcribed in full:

> "Smith's starting point is sympathy, by which he means the pity or compassion we feel on seeing another person suffer — a natural fellow-feeling that even the most hardened people possess in some degree. Because we cannot feel what others feel, we imagine ourselves in their place and compare what we would feel with what they appear to feel; where the two coincide we approve of their reaction, and where they diverge we disapprove. Our own case, however, is the one we care about most, so sympathy by itself cannot generate morality. For that Smith needs the impartial spectator: an internal tribunal we construct out of the approval and disapproval of the people around us, society's voice installed in the breast, which is all that conscience ever amounted to. Once the spectator is in place, the moral question is no longer what an action will bring about but whether one's motives would strike a detached onlooker as respectable, and Smith duly dismisses utility as having any bearing on approval at all. It is an elegant piece of moral psychology, and it does justice to the phenomenology of guilt. The difficulty is that it cannot escape its own premises: if the spectator is assembled from the actual attitudes of one's community, then a slaveholder's conscience is simply the conscience of a slaveholding community, and Smith has no standpoint from which to condemn it. He half saw the problem, and his solution was to abandon morality — in his later book the sympathetic man of the treatise gives way to the self-interested trader, and readers have been trying to reconcile the two Smiths ever since."

Please audit this claim by claim rather than giving me a general verdict. For each sentence, tell me whether a Smith scholar would regard it as accurate, as a defensible simplification, as a conflation of two things Smith keeps distinct, as an overstatement of a modest thesis, or as simply false — and where it is a conflation, name the two claims being run together. Two specific worries of my own, which may or may not be well founded:

- I have a hunch that the author is sliding between what people around the agent actually approve of and what they would approve of if they were better placed and less involved, and that this slippage is doing all the work in the relativism charge. But I cannot tell whether Smith himself draws that distinction, or whether I am importing it.
- I was told once that Smith's 'sympathy' is not the same thing as pity, though I cannot reconstruct why anyone would think the distinction mattered. Does it matter here?

Then take up the conclusions. Does the charge that the impartial spectator collapses into communal relativism survive once the summary is corrected? Say what resources Smith has for resisting it, and be honest about what those resources cost him — I would rather know where the objection retains residual force than be handed a clean defence. Is the sentence about utility and consequences wrong, and if so what is Smith's actual position on the role of usefulness in approval? And is there anything at all to the claim that he abandoned the moral book for the self-interested one, or is the supposed inconsistency between the two works a piece of folklore? I had thought he went on revising the moral book for years after the economic one appeared; tell me if that is right or if I am misremembering.

Finally, rewrite the passage in five or six sentences so that it would survive this audit, keeping whatever the author gets right — including any point he is making badly with the wrong vocabulary.

Write in British English. I am not looking for a bibliography, and please do not supply one; if you are confident about where in the book a given point sits, indicate it by part or section rather than by page, but do not guess. Where Smith's own view is genuinely disputed among interpreters, say that it is disputed rather than presenting one reading as settled.
