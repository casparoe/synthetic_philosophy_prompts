So — slightly unusual question, and I'd rather get it from someone who's actually worked through the chance-and-credence literature than from a textbook summary of it.

Our department just got a small pot of money that has to be spent over the next 18 months: enough for one two-day workshop and one grad RA at half time, and I'm the person who has to name the theme in the proposal, which is due at the end of next month. It has to be the chance/credence interface — basically everything downstream of Lewis's Principal Principle — and it has to be one framing question, not a vague banner. Two constraints on what's realistic for us. First, we're strong in formal epistemology and comfortable with probability theory plus a bit of modal logic, but nobody here does philosophy of physics seriously, so a theme that only pays off if you understand statistical mechanics or the Everett stuff is a theme that produces a room full of people nodding politely. Second, I want the workshop to generate real disagreement — a question where someone smart could leave on day two having changed their mind — not a round of status reports.

Here's the shortlist I've been circling, in my own words, which I should warn you are probably sloppy in places:

1. Admissability. Whether there's any non-circular, non-pragmatic account of which evidence is admissible at all, or whether we should concede that the term has ended up meaning whatever makes the principle come out true — in which case the live question is whether the principle is analytic or a substantive norm about rational credence.
2. Undermining, i.e. the thing Lewis called the big bad bug. Are undermining futures a genuine cost for best-systems accounts of chance, or was Lewis overreacting? And if they're genuine, is the right response Hall's New Principle, one of the Vranas-style reconciliations of the old and new principles, or giving up on a general chance-credence constraint for Humeans altogether?
3. The new bug. My understanding — which may just be wrong — is that once you move to the New Principle, chance hypotheses themselves stop being governed by it, so you can't use the chances to reason about what the chances are. If that's right, how bad is it in practice?
4. Whether the Principal Principle can be derived from something more basic, decision-theoretic or accuracy-based, instead of being a primitive norm.
5. Imprecise chance: can chance itself be a range or a set of functions, should deference generalize to that case, and have the imprecise-probability people actually answered the arguments that rational credence has to stay sharp?
6. Deterministic chance — whether there can be chances in a deterministic world, and what the principle is even supposed to say when it's handed one.
7. Everett and branch weights. My instinct is that this is out of scope for us, but maybe that's exactly where 1 through 3 stop being technical tidying and start mattering.

What I want: rank these for my purposes — the workshop theme plus one year of an RA's time — and defend the ranking rather than just assert it. Be explicit about your criteria and how you're trading them off: how much of the rest of the list depends on settling this one, whether resolving it would change what anyone does next, how saturated the ground already is (I do not want to fund the nineteenth paper on the same objection), and whether it's tractable with the toolkit we actually have. Then commit: tell me which single one to build the workshop around, which to cut or demote to a side session, and which of these are secretly the same question wearing different clothes. Also flag every place I've got the dialectic wrong in the list above — that's genuinely part of what I'm after, so don't be polite about it. And say what would move the ranking over the next year and a half: what result, or what sort of argument, would promote something you've ranked low or sink something you've ranked high.

Two last things. My own bias is toward 2, mostly because I wrote my dissertation on best-systems accounts and I suspect that's familiarity doing the work rather than judgement — push back if you think it is. And I'd much rather you take a stand than give me a balanced tour: if you think one of these is a pseudo-problem, or that the whole debate turns on a distinction nobody has bothered to draw, say so and argue for it. No references or reading lists, by the way — I can pull those myself, and I want the reasoning, not the bibliography.
