I am drafting a conference paper that pairs Sophocles' Oedipus Rex with the decision theorist's fable of Death in Damascus: the man who meets Death in Damascus, is told that Death is surprised to see him there because they have an appointment in Aleppo the next day, and who hires the fastest conveyance available to reach Aleppo, where Death is waiting. In the decision-theoretic literature the story is used to make a distinctly non-fatalist point — because Death's whereabouts are causally independent of the flight, evidential decision theory's dread of the Aleppo ticket is misplaced and causal decision theory's indifference is correct — and the fatalist reading of the tale is treated as a confusion to be dissolved.

My hypothesis is that this use of the fable depends on a stipulation that the story itself does not contain, and that the older Baghdad version brings out why. There, the master asks Death afterwards about the threatening gesture and learns it was only surprise, since the appointment had already been made in Samarra. What is fixed in that telling is the death, not the route to it: the borrowed horse is precisely what delivers the servant to the place where the appointment is kept. Sophocles, I want to argue, exhibits the same structure on a larger scale, and the play is therefore not the fatalist parable it is usually taken to be.

To get the argument going I have been working with a threefold distinction among the ways an announcement of the future can stand to the future it announces. Call them the report, the trigger, and the trap. In the report, the announcement is a true description of a future fixed independently of anyone's reaction to it, and acting on it is causally inert. In the trigger, the announcement causes the outcome it describes by causing its hearers to behave in ways that bring it about. In the trap, the announcement is true, but not under the reading its hearer adopts, and his confidence in that reading is what puts him where the other reading is satisfied — the structure of the prophecies Macbeth treats as guarantees of invulnerability.

Please read Oedipus Rex through this apparatus, taking both the play and the decision-theoretic puzzle seriously rather than using one as decoration for the other. I would like you to address, in particular:

- Which of the three structures each of the play's oracular utterances has — the one given to Laius, the one given to the young Oedipus at Delphi, and the one Creon brings back at the opening, which is not a prediction at all but an assignment. Whether the play's reputation for fatalism rests on prophecies that are already completed and offstage, while the onstage action is a procedural in which choices are load-bearing.
- Whether the flight from Corinth and the exposure of the infant on the mountainside are the same kind of act. One is taken to escape a prediction, the other to prevent one; do they belong to the same structure, or does the play distinguish them?
- Jocasta's consolatory argument, which reasons from the reported manner of Laius's death to the worthlessness of oracles. Is this a case of the trap, and does her inference fail for a distinctively evidential reason — namely that it rests on a report she has no way of testing?
- Oedipus's refusal to abandon the inquiry once Jocasta begs him to. He appears to treat the truth as already settled and his investigation as unable to change it, which is a fatalist posture held by a man whose act is about to destroy his wife and himself. Is this a Damascus-style choice inverted — the act treated as causally irrelevant when it is the most causally potent thing in the play — and does it turn on a preference for knowing over for good news?
- Whether the closing reckoning the chorus offers about counting no man happy until the end of his life endorses the fatalism the scenes have just undermined.

Then tell me where the reading strains and what I should cut. Two obvious pressure points: Apollo is an agent with intentions, whereas Death in the fable is not, and the decision-theoretic puzzle is about a predictor rather than a schemer; and someone will object that the report and the trigger are not really two structures, since a trigger is only a report whose content includes the hearers' reaction to it. Say whether you think my threefold distinction is doing genuine work or is a redescription, and whether the Damascus case needs a fourth category of its own — the case in which the outcome is fixed but the route to it is not. I would also like you to end by saying what the play contributes to the debate over fatalism that the formal treatment cannot: what it is like to be the agent inside a trigger structure, where every exercise of prudence feels like the working of fate.

Do not quote the play — I am working from my own translation and do not want mismatches — and I do not need a bibliography or a survey of who has said what about fate in Sophocles. I want the reading argued out from the scenes and the choices. Something in the range of fifteen hundred to two thousand words would be right.
