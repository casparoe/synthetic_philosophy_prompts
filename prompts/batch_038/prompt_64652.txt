I am a philosopher of language by training, and for three years I have chaired the Governance Committee of a computing consortium: eleven research institutions that pool their cluster time and allocate it to members' groups. Allocation is no longer done by hand. Each member fields an automated agent — some written in-house, some licensed from a vendor — that negotiates with the other members' agents dozens of times a day over contested blocks of time. A proposal is now before the committee requiring that, before any two agents negotiate, each party disclose to the other the complete source code of its agent, together with a signed attestation that the code running in production was compiled from what was disclosed. The committee's stated rationale invokes a result familiar from the theory of program equilibrium: agents able to read one another's source can settle into cooperative outcomes that mutually opaque agents cannot reach, because each can see that the other's cooperation is conditioned on its own. One faction wants the requirement adopted at next month's meeting; another thinks it is philosophically naive and operationally dead, since the vendor supplying two of our largest members will not disclose source on any terms, and since what runs can always diverge from what is attested. There is also an incident neither faction has mentioned: last term two agents, each apparently waiting on the other's first move, held a contested block in abeyance for something over thirty hours, and the consortium lost the allocation outright.

I have been asked to draft the committee's decision memo, and I would like your help with it. Please write it as I would circulate it: begin with a single-sentence bottom line — adopt as drafted, adopt with specified amendments, or reject in favor of an alternative — followed by the reasoning, the conditions and the time frame under which any amended rule would take effect, and an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the losing side, written so that its proponents could recognize it as their own view rather than as a caricature. The reasoning should engage at least three things concretely. First, the gap between disclosing code and disclosing behavior: I am sympathetic to a middle path I have sketched myself, under which parties submit not source but a referee-issued "behavioral certificate" — the output of a neutral party running the production agent in a sandbox against a battery of canonical negotiation scenarios and publishing what it did in each. I want the memo to say plainly whether such a certificate preserves the strategic properties that make source disclosure attractive, or whether it quietly gives up the thing that mattered, since the other agent reasons about the code, not about a sample of its outputs. Second, the attestation problem, and what a general counsel can actually enforce against a party who discloses honestly and deploys something else. Third, the deadlock: would mutual disclosure have prevented two agents from waiting on each other indefinitely, or is that failure a symptom of something the disclosure regime makes worse?

The audience is the committee itself — two computer scientists, a university general counsel, three faculty members with no technical background, and the vendor's representative, who attends as a non-voting observer and reads everything. Write so that the computer scientists can tell you have understood the difference between code and dispositions, and so that the counsel can see you have thought about verification rather than merely about good faith. Please keep the entire memo under 900 words.
