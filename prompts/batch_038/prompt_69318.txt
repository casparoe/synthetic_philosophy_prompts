I head the reliability function at a company that operates two certification regimes side by side, and they appear to rest on opposite intuitions about when a system is still the same system.

**First regime.** We maintain a legacy pricing engine that no one fully understands. Before authorizing any rewrite, we run the old engine over several years of archived production transactions and save every output to disk, byte for byte. The rewritten engine must reproduce those files exactly. If it does, we declare the rewrite equivalent and ship it, however thoroughly its algorithms, data structures, and control flow differ from the original. If it diverges on even a single archived transaction — including cases where everyone agrees the old output was wrong and the new one is right — we revert, and the rewrite is recorded as having changed the system's behavior.

**Second regime.** Our machine-learning risk models are periodically compressed to run on cheaper hardware. Every individual prediction from the compressed model differs slightly from the original's, yet we ship it as the same model provided aggregate performance metrics stay inside a fixed tolerance band. By contrast, a model retrained from scratch on a newer data vintage, with identical architecture and indistinguishable metrics, counts as a different model and must undergo full re-certification. Our change-management policy draws the line the same way: any change to weights, tokenizer, or training data triggers re-evaluation, while any change to the inference stack — kernels, batching, numerical precision — does not, so long as the metrics hold.

I would like a reconstruction of the implicit philosophy here, not a discussion of testing methodology.

1. State the criterion of mechanism-identity each regime presupposes. In particular, what does the first regime treat as constitutive of the mechanism — its input–output extension over a finite sample, its internal causal structure, the intentions of its authors, or something else — and what does the second treat as constitutive that the first disregards entirely?
2. Is there a single underlying criterion that renders both regimes coherent? If you think so, state it precisely enough that it predicts our verdicts in all three cases above, including the revert-the-improvement case and the tolerance-band case. If no such criterion exists, identify exactly which commitment they cannot share.
3. Distinguish what each regime strictly presupposes from what merely sits comfortably with it. Does exact reproduction across the archive really commit us to an extensional account of identity, or could it be defended as a conservative proxy under uncertainty about the true input distribution? Do the tolerance bands commit us to identity being a matter of degree, or only to a chosen grain of description?
4. Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined — which rival accounts of sameness of mechanism would license these very rules? And consider a deflationary possibility: that neither regime presupposes any metaphysics at all, that the phrase "same system" is doing purely pragmatic work in allocating burden of proof and liability. If you take that line, say exactly what work the phrase is doing and why the regimes differ in how they use it.
5. Suppose we adopted the opposite view — that two systems are the same mechanism if and only if they share the relevant internal causal structure, with observable outputs counting only as evidence about it. What would have to change in our testing apparatus, our change-management triggers, and our treatment of the improvement that currently forces a revert?
