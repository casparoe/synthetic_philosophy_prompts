Ok so I run a very small podcast (about 300 listeners, mostly coworkers) and next month we're doing a bit where a guest historian plays Johannes Kepler in character and I interview him — but not about the laws of planetary motion, everyone does that. I want the whole segment to be about his astrology. I'm a software engineer, I build forecasting models for a logistics company, and I have zero philosophy or history training, so please write these so a guy like me can read them out loud without tripping over the terminology.

Here's what I gathered from a wikipedia rabbit hole, and I'm defintely fuzzy on parts of it, so your first job is to correct me:

- he made his living partly from annual calendars and prognostications, and cast nativities for patrons including Wallenstein, whose household he later joined
- he wanted to reform astrology rather than drop it: keep the claims he thought defensible — that the configurations of the planets influence weather, and something like a person's temperament or character — and cut the claims about specific events (who wins a battle, who you'll marry, when you'll die)
- his explanation was geometric/harmonic: the soul, and something like a formative faculty in the earth itself, reacts instinctively to the angular relationships between planets, roughly the way music moves you without your deciding to be moved
- he argued against people who wanted to throw astrology out entirely, on the grounds that you shouldn't blame a whole practice for what its worst practitioners do
- he spent years defending his mother in a witchcraft trial, which sits weirdly next to all of the above

Act as a historian of early-modern science who also does a lot of recorded interviews, and think carefully before drafting. I don't want the generic "isn't it ironic that a great scientist believed this" questions — I want ones that press on specific claims, where he can't just say that's how people thought at the time.

Please use exactly these sections, in this order:

1. **Correct me first** (~120 words): fix or flag anything in my bullets that's wrong, oversimplified, or that I'd get laughed at for saying on air. Don't lecture, just tell me what to stop believing.
2. **Where the tension actually is** (~150 words): your brainstorm. Two or three internal pressure points in his position — places where his own commitments pull against each other — that the questions should be built on.
3. **Warm-up** (2 questions): short, non-hostile, but already pointed at one of those pressure points. No "what drew you to astronomy" filler.
4. **The hard middle** (5 questions): for each, give me the question in a sentence or two I can read verbatim, then **what he'll say** (his most likely answer or deflection, in a sentence or two), then **the follow-up** that makes that answer uncomfortable rather than just repeating the question louder.
5. **Closing question** (1): the one I save for the end, ideally something where either answer costs him something.
6. **Cut list** (~80 words): which three of the nine to drop if we only have 25 minutes, and why those.

Across the questions, please make sure these come up: the mechanism (why should geometry move a soul — is that a testable hypothesis or a nice story); his track record and how he scores it, which is my favorite angle because I want to sound like someone who does this for a living — what counts as a confirmed prediction to him, does he keep a ledger, and how does he answer the objection that a vague forecast is right often enough by accident; the money question, and how much of the astrology is just patronage; his mother's trial and whether celestial influence leaves room for anybody being responsible for anything; and one where I offer him a hypothetical modern dataset (decades of weather records and birth dates and outcomes) and ask what he'd do with it.

Tone note: don't let me be a smug presentist. The interesting version of this is that he's running a research program with a mechanism, a restricted domain, and a reform agenda — so the questions should treat him as someone who could be wrong in specific, arguable ways, not as a sucker. Occassionally I'd like a question that concedes something to him.

Aim for about 900–1100 words total.
