I TA an upper-level Kant course and next week I'm running the review session for the final, which is one long case. I want to predict what the prof will say without telling 60 students something she'd flatly contradict, and prepping I've realized her rulings this term fit more than one account of what the universal law test is even supposed to be doing.

Here's the record:

- "I'll borrow money by promising to repay it, knowing I won't" — impermissible, contradiction in conception, perfect duty. The textbook one.
- "I'll cut to the front of the food truck line because I'm late" — impermissible, perfect. Someone objected that they'd be fine in a world where everyone cut lines, they'd just eat later, and she said that misses it: your maxim is to be served ahead of people who waited, and that's precisely what can't hold up when everyone has it.
- "I'll keep drinking the office coffee without ever putting money in the jar" — impermissible, perfect.
- "I'll never help anyone with anything, ever" — impermissible, but contradiction in the will, imperfect duty. She stressed that turning down one particular request on one particular day is fine; it's the maxim that's condemned.
- "I'll sell my apartment now while prices are at their peak" — permissible. A student said if everyone sold at once prices would crash and she'd get nothing, and she called that a composition fallacy rather than a Kantian objection — nobody's exempting themselves from anything there, everyone in the market is doing the same kind of thing.
- "I'll learn to code instead of the violin" — permissible, even though if everyone made that choice there'd be no violinists.

Two other things worth knowing. When a student tried to rescue the false promise by re-describing the maxim as "say whatever gets me the money," she wasnt having it — too thin to be a maxim at all. But in the food truck case she re-described the maxim herself, unprompted. So she has some constraint on which descriptions are admissible and I cant tell what it is. And when someone said the test can't be about what would happen if everybody did it, since conceiving something and willing it aren't the same operation, she said that was the right question and then moved on.

The final's case is already posted. A well-known epidemiologist gets an email from a reporter asking her to comment on a claim about a vaccine that's circulating online. She declines. Her reasons: she's published three papers saying the opposite, the quote will get garbled anyway, and the afternoon is better spent finishing her actual work. The exam states the maxim as "I'll decline to speak publicly on matters in my expertise, since I've already contributed what I know and my time is better spent elsewhere."

So: predict her verdict, and if it's impermissible, which species — contradiction in conception or in the will, perfect or imperfect duty. Then the part I actually need. Lay out the candidate rules that are consistent with all six precedents (I can see at least a practice-preservation one, a making-an-exception-of-myself one, and one about what a rational being has to will whether or not this particular agent gives a damn), say what each delivers on the epidemiologist, and tell me which you'd bet on and why. Which precedent is the closest analog — and is the composition-fallacy move she made on the apartment available here too, given she's already published? Finally, give me one further case I could put to the class that would best separate whatever rules survive, with each rule's prediction about it: something where they genuinely come apart, not another one they all rule the same way.
