I'm a fourth-year PhD student in behaviour genetics and on Friday I have to give the speech at my supervisor's retirement dinner. Two senior people were asked first and both wriggled out of it, so its landed on me. About seventy people in a college hall: roughly half the room are geneticists, statisticians and epidemiologists who know the technical material far better than I do, including two who've spent their careers arguing that the twin method is a mess and would enjoy nothing more than catching me out; the other half are family — her husband, her three kids, a couple of grandchildren, a retired GP, two of her old schoolteachers. And at table four, awkwardly, is the science journalist who wrote the headline.

The headline. Marion's group published a big twin study of anxiety symptoms in adolescents and got a heritability estimate of around 0.6 for that cohort. It went out in a national paper under something like "Anxiety is in the genes: why your child's worries are hard-wired", and the last paragraph said this meant school programmes and therapy were a waste of public money, because you can't argue with DNA. She spent the next twelve years writing letters to editors, giving public lectures in libraries, and keeping a jam jar with two packets of seeds in it on her desk — every new student had to explain what that example does and doesn't show before she'd let them near a dataset. I'm not a philosopher, so I don't have a good vocabulary for what I actually want here, which is a celebration that doesn't use the department head's word "impact" in the department head's sense, because impact culture is precisely what produced the headline.

So: about six minutes, call it 750–900 words. Constraints, sorry, but they matter more than the jokes:

1. Nothing in it may be something Marion would consider false. Concretely: no "X per cent of your anxiety is genetic", no suggestion that a high heritability estimate makes a trait hard to change, nothing that slides from differences within the population she studied to differences between populations, and no "genes and environment interact" as a get-out-of-jail slogan. If a number is going to appear in the speech it has to arrive wearing the population and the range of environments it was measured in, because that was her whole habit — she wrote heritability estimates with the cohort in the subscript on birthday cards, on leaving cards, once on a referee report, and she had a standing offer to buy a pint for anyone who could say "70 per cent genetic" and then name the population and the environments. In thirty years she bought one pint, and the person who won it was a plumber.
2. One genuinely funny heritability joke that both halves of the room can enjoy. Not a pun about twins. The geneticists should laugh at how precise it is and the grandchildren should laugh as well.
3. There has to be a passage about the headline, and the journalist is sitting in the room. The correction has to be made out loud and clearly rather than winked at, but she mustn't be made into a villain or a punchline, and nobody at table four should want to slip out early. Ideally she ends up being part of the reason the work mattered.
4. The non-scientists should leave with something true and moving, not just a corrected error. At most two technical terms in the whole speech, and each one has to pay for itself with a single clause of explanation.
5. The department's present is a glass paperweight with the two seed packets set in resin. You can use that as a closing beat if you can do it without it being twee.

After the speech, could you add five or six lines flagging any sentence where you thought you'd got close to saying something untrue, and what the safer wording would have been? British English throughout, please.
