I am a second-year doctoral student in philosophy working on explanation in the historical sciences, and this term I have been convening a Thursday reading group on the philosophy of history whose theme has been the opposition between cyclical and directional accounts of the historical process: Polybius on the succession of constitutions, Ibn Khaldun on the generation and consumption of group solidarity within a ruling house, Spengler on the morphology of cultures, Toynbee on challenge and response. After eight weeks the group has split and nobody has moved, which is why I am writing to you for a diagnosis rather than continuing to argue. Let me state the two positions as their holders actually state them, since I want the diagnosis to engage them and not replace them with a doxography. On one side, whom I will call the mechanists: the cyclical tradition is not a mood but a family of causal models. Polybius explains how each constitution passes into its corrupt successor by reference to the character of the generation that inherits an arrangement it did not build; Ibn Khaldun explains the dynastic arc by a resource that scarcity produces and prosperity spends, so that a house consumes the solidarity that founded it within roughly three generations; Spengler offers a claim with at least the grammar of an empirical one, namely that high cultures live out lifespans of comparable length. On the other side, the accumulationists: they concede local oscillation but hold that it rides on a ratchet, because technical and organizational knowledge, once externalized in records and artifacts, does not decay when the polity that produced it does; a recurrence of pattern is not a recurrence of state, since the substrate is different each time; and what the mechanists call a morphology is a biological metaphor promoted to the status of a law. One of our members, a visiting fellow in economic history, adds that the accumulationists have an asymmetry in their favor that nobody has answered: the mechanist must produce a mechanism for every cycle, whereas the accumulationist can describe any collapse as a local interruption and still count the record as confirming the trend.

The form of the stall is what puzzles me. Every case one side produces is absorbed by a redescription from the other. The mechanists say the ratchet itself needs an explanation and that nobody has supplied one for why complexity should accumulate rather than reset; the accumulationists reply that the mechanists' stages are individuated only after the fact, so that any trajectory can be narrated as a cycle once you know where it ended. Neither side can say what evidence would count against it, and yet neither admits to being unfalsifiable, and I do not think either is simply confused. My own hypothesis, which I hold loosely and would like you to test rather than flatter, is that the dispute is not about the shape of history at all but about the unit over which shape is being measured. The mechanists quantify over bounded units — a polis, a dynasty, a culture — whose boundaries the theorist draws; the accumulationists quantify over a single unit, the species or its technical tradition, whose boundaries are inherited from the surviving record. If whether you see periodicity or a trend depends heavily on the aggregation window you fixed before looking at the data, then the two camps may be answering different questions under one heading, and the impasse is a symptom rather than a difficulty.

What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who is right and not a survey of the four authors, all of whom I have read. Please sort through the candidate accounts of this disagreement and argue for one of them or for a disjunction: that it is verbal, turning on what "decline," "the same stage," or a "cycle" is supposed to mean; that it concerns standards of evidence, in particular whether a dynasty counts as an observation or as a sample of size one; that it concerns which entities count as data at all, as my unit hypothesis suggests; that a hidden empirical question is doing the work unacknowledged, such as whether recorded knowledge really is non-decaying across institutional collapse; that it rests on bedrock intuitions about whether the past is commensurable with the present that neither side can argue for; or that the question is ill-posed rather than merely hard. Then tell me what would count as progress given the diagnosis you reach: a distinction to be drawn, a reformulation that would make the two positions genuinely contraries, a way of fixing the unit of analysis independently of the theory being tested, a formal result about when cycles and trends are jointly detectable in a noisy series, or an argument that no progress should be expected and why. Finally, I would be grateful if you pointed out where my own hypothesis is likely to be self-serving, since proposing a dissolution is a cheap way to look even-handed while conceding nothing, and I intend to present something like this paper at the end of term.
