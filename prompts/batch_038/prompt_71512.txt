In the public repositories of most countries, the governing rule of arrangement and description is provenance: records are grouped and described according to the body that created and accumulated them — a ministry, a court, a hospital, a plantation office, a mission station — and the top level of description must identify that body. Below it, files are supposed to stand in the order the creating office kept them in, not in any subject order of a later researcher's devising. A person who appears inside the files only as the object of documentation — the petitioner, the applicant, the patient, the accused, the enslaved person entered on an inventory, the villager named in a district officer's report — is not, on this system, a creator of anything. At most that person becomes an index term, an added entry, a name in a field that may or may not have been filled in, depending on how much time the cataloguer had.

The same profession has a distinctive account of why the resulting records are trustworthy. A record, on the standard view, is not testimony offered to posterity; it is a by-product of business transacted, made by the party transacting it for its own purposes and never intended to persuade a future reader. Its authority is supposed to come from exactly that: it is unselfconscious, it was not composed for us, and it is therefore harder to lie with than a memoir or a chronicle. Value is graded to match. A file is valuable first as evidence of what the creating office did, and only second as information about the people who passed through it.

A repository I am acquainted with is weighing a change. For series consisting wholly of transactions with outside parties — petitions and their endorsements, applications and case files, interrogations, intake forms, correspondence with persons who never held the pen — it proposes to list the documented party as a co-creator alongside the office, and to permit, in those series only, an arrangement by documented person rather than by the office's own filing order. The stated motive is the obvious one: for many such persons these files are the only surviving trace that they existed at all, and the present system files that trace under the name of the party that produced the paper.

I want to know what the existing arrangement presupposes, and what the proposal would break. Which account of testimony and of authorship does provenance-based description make sense only under — what has to be true about who is speaking in a record, and about why records are credible, for it to be unremarkable that the documented are not creators? Which rival accounts does the practice quietly rule out, and does it rule them out on grounds its practitioners would be willing to state aloud? Is the standard account coherent even in its own terms: can a profession hold both that a record's credibility derives from its being the unselfconscious by-product of one party's business, and that the very same record is the surviving trace of a transaction with a second party who had no hand in its making? And is the by-product doctrine in fact neutral between the powerful and the powerless, or does it already encode an answer to the question of whose purposes constitute a record?

Then take the proposal seriously rather than dismissing it as sentiment. If the documented become co-creators, what else has to give? Original order is a principle about the arrangement someone imposed while records were in active use, and the documented party never had custody and so never imposed any order at all — is arranging by documented person therefore a violation of provenance, or the acknowledgment that there were two provenances in the file from the beginning, only one of which had a registry? What becomes of appraisal, where the destruction of a routine case file is currently justified by weighing its value to researchers studying the office's functions: does co-creator status give the documented a claim against destruction, or equally a claim in favour of it — a right to have the file destroyed, redacted, or corrected rather than preserved and made searchable? And what happens to the citation conventions by which a historian refers to a document, if a document can have two creators of different kinds, one of whom may be a person the record was written against?

Finally, mark where the practice is underdetermined. Some features of the present system look like consequences of a theory of evidence but may be nothing more than administrative constraint: the cost of re-arranging large series, the impossibility of naming every person in a ledger, the fact that a single creator field is what makes retrieval tractable at all. Distinguish what provenance-based description strictly presupposes from what it merely fits comfortably with, and say which parts of the practice a repository could retain if it adopted instead the view that records are joint traces of transactions rather than emanations of a single creating body.
