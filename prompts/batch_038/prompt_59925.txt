I've been reading argument blogs for the better part of two decades, and one habit I've never shaken is treating a settled institutional arrangement as evidence about the beliefs of the people who built it. The arrangement I want examined is the current spread of legal regimes governing adult sex work, because the four on offer look to me like four different answers to a question that is rarely stated aloud. Sweden, since 1999, criminalises the purchase of sexual services while leaving the seller unprosecuted; Norway followed and then pushed its purchase ban extraterritorially, so that a Norwegian buying sex abroad can be charged at home. The Netherlands for decades ran a formal policy of toleration — conduct that remained unlawful but was simply not prosecuted — before lifting the general ban on brothels around the turn of the millennium and substituting municipal licensing. New Zealand's Prostitution Reform Act 2003 decriminalised the trade and folded it into ordinary employment and occupational health-and-safety law, giving workers an explicit right to refuse a client, while its section 19 confines the right to work to citizens and permanent residents and makes any temporary visa holder liable to deportation for doing it, and its local councils retain bylaw powers over where brothels may sit and what they may advertise. What I want is a reconstruction of the implicit normative theory behind each: what has to be true about consent, about who the moral patient of the transaction is, and about the expressive function of law, for each regime to be the thing its architects chose rather than something else?

Two hypotheses of my own should be tested rather than assumed, because I suspect both are partly wrong. First, the purchase-only ban is structurally an incitement offence: it punishes the party who occasions another's conduct while exempting the party who performs it, which normally happens only where the performer is a child, is defrauded, or is otherwise denied full agency — so either the regime is committed to a claim of structural coercion that impairs the seller's consent, or it is committed to something else entirely, and I cannot see what. If it is the coercion claim, why does the same legislature not apply that claim to agricultural or care work performed under comparably desperate conditions? Second, I think "liberalisation" is a bad axis. Non-prosecution of still-unlawful conduct, decriminalisation, licensing, and positive certification are four distinct expressive postures, and the New Zealand statute appears to want a fifth: its own purpose clause says the Act decriminalises prostitution "while not endorsing or morally sanctioning prostitution or its use", which reads to me as an attempt to authorise an activity while disclaiming the authorisation. Please tell me whether that is coherent, and if not, which half of it the rest of the Act actually implements — because the occupational-health-and-safety framing presupposes that this is work the state helps organise, whereas the exclusion of migrant workers and the zoning bylaws presuppose either that it is a land-use nuisance to be contained or that its supply should be rationed by immigration policy. Distinguish carefully throughout between what a regime strictly presupposes, what merely fits it well, and where the practice is philosophically underdetermined so that several rival theories would license the very same statutes.

Finally, for each regime, say what would have to change in concrete administrative practice — policing, licensing, tribunal jurisdiction, border screening, council bylaws — if the presupposed theory were replaced by its most serious rival. I am not asking you to take a side on whether sex work should be legal, and I would rather you did not spend words on the empirical disputes about trafficking flows or street-based displacement, except where an empirical claim is doing hidden normative work in a regime's self-description and you can point that out. End with an explicit statement of what would change your mind: which argument or piece of institutional evidence would lead you to a different reconstruction of any one regime's commitments, and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to have gone wrong — my strong prior is that the incitement analogy is doing more rhetorical work than it can bear. Please write the whole response in British English.
