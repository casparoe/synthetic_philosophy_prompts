I run a professional development day for people who work in municipal emergency management — about 22 of them, a mix of dispatchers, planners, two lawyers, and a few folks from the mayor's office. I get a two-hour block after lunch, tables of five or six, no computers, and I can hand out paper. They're sharp and they've all sat through the same ethics-of-triage slideshow a dozen times, so I want them doing something rather than listening.

Target: the problem of dirty hands. I want them to end up personally committed to a decision they think was right and would make again, and then to discover that no tidy description of it — least evil, necessary, the numbers were on our side — quite fits, and that the option of keeping their own hands clean was only ever available because somebody else was willing to get dirty. Don't say the phrase or name any philosopher until the very end. I'll do the theory myself in the next session, so no reading list, no citations, nothing in the debrief that depends on them already knowing the literature.

Here's the mechanic I want you to build it around, because I think it's what'll make it land with this crowd instead of with philosophy majors. Two rounds, and the group doesn't know the second one is coming. There's a shared resource — call it the town's standing willingness to comply with whatever the emergency operations centre asks — that gets spent whenever they use a measure that's deceptive, coercive, or breaks a promise, and gets partly replenished by measures that are honest and costly to the agency. Round one is a levee-and-evacuation scenario with a real choice between a truthful measure that saves fewer people and a manipulative one that saves more but burns compliance. Round two hits them with a different emergency (heat wave, contamination, whatever you think works best) where compliance is exactly what they need and it's already spent. Tune the numbers so the group that lied in round one has more survivors on paper and less capacity in round two, and the group that didnt has the reverse — so neither can claim to have simply won. Give me the actual ledger in a table: what each measure costs, what it saves, how big the pot is at the start, and where it stands at the end of each branch.

Two structural things on top of that:

- Every member of the group has to sign the round-one decision sheet, or else there has to be some visible way to decline — and declining should turn out to have a price, so that recusing isn't a free way to stay clean. Make the cost concrete and make sure they can't work out what it is in advance.
- A closing card, one per person, handed back before any debrief starts: "was what you did wrong? yes / no / it was wrong but it's what I should have done." I'll read the tally out loud. Predict what distribution I'll get and tell me how to use it.

Then the deliverables, in this order, with these rough lengths:

1. **Brief for participants** — what they're told at the start, no more than 150 words, and it must not tip them off to round two.
2. **Materials** — a short list of what I print or write on the board.
3. **Round one** — the scenario, the menu of measures with compliance costs, the time limit, and the rule that forces them to actually decide rather than deliberate forever. ~350 words plus the table.
4. **Round two** — same shape, ~250 words.
5. **The reveal and the card** — how and when I show them the two-round comparison and collect the cards. ~150 words.
6. **Debrief** — a run of questions in the order I should ask them, each tagged with the moment in the game it hooks onto, so the mapping from what they did to the idea is explicit. ~300 words.
7. **What will go wrong the first time** — the predictable failures and what I do about each one. Include something about the participant who's been through a real disaster and starts talking about it. ~200 words.
8. **What the game can't do** — name the feature of the view it structurally can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply in talk, and say why the design can't reach it. ~150 words.

Whole thing should be runnable straight off the page. Keep the town and the disasters invented — no real places, no real events.
