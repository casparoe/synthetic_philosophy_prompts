I help organize a monthly discussion evening in my town, where a group of us with no academic training argue about ethics and politics over coffee. Next month I'm the one asking questions, and my guest is a philosopher-economist who defends a proposal I'll summarize below. She isn't a public figure, so I want the questions to engage the proposal itself rather than her résumé.

Her view is that people with irreconcilable moral outlooks can both come out ahead by trading concessions: I accept a limit on something you care intensely about, you accept a limit on something I care intensely about, and each of us ends up with more of what we value than if we'd both tried to win outright. She wants to institutionalize this as a standing body she calls a moral clearinghouse, where participants state which outcomes they cannot live with, declare what they'd give up to prevent each of them, and the body then brokers bundles of trades. Three commitments make her version worth pressing. First, trades are priced in a common unit she calls burden-intensity — how much a person would actually sacrifice to avoid an outcome — and she insists that this unit, not money and not head count, is what stops the well-off from simply buying the results they prefer. Second, she holds that values people describe as absolute can still be traded when the price is high enough, because refusing to trade is itself a way of imposing your values on everyone else. Third, she argues the arrangement is stable without any enforcement mechanism, since breaking a trade destroys your credibility and with it your access to future gains.

Please write me eight to ten interview questions, in American English, that meet these conditions:

- Every question must turn on the content of her proposal and be one she cannot deflect by restating it. No warm-up questions about how she came to this work.
- For each question, add one follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give, so I'm not stranded when she responds.
- Cover at least: the strategic inflation of burden-intensity, given that it rests on what people say they would sacrifice, and why universal exaggeration doesn't make the unit meaningless; the charge that putting a price on a conviction corrodes the conviction, so that the clearinghouse consumes the moral seriousness it depends on; who gets to be represented when future generations, nonhuman animals, and people who refuse to participate cannot state a price; the worry that a small group whose deepest commitment is outbid in every round ends up permanently constrained, and whether that is anything other than majority rule with a market veneer; whether someone who sincerely believes a trade would be wrong can coherently sign it, or whether her second commitment simply assumes the answer; and her third commitment, namely what she thinks stops two parties from trading concessions that mainly harm absent third parties, who have no future trades to lose.
- End with a question about what actual disagreement, handled badly by the clearinghouse in practice, she would take as evidence against the whole scheme.
