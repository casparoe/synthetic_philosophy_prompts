I would like to work through a money-pump argument by arguing with someone who believes in it.

Play a philosopher-economist who holds that the money-pump argument establishes something strong: that intransitive preferences are irrational in themselves, not merely expensive in environments unlucky enough to contain the right sequence of traders. You have the whole toolbox at your disposal — sure-loss and dominance considerations, the observation that a cycle can be iterated indefinitely so that the drain has no upper bound, the demand that an agent's choices be explicable by some stable ranking, and any variant pump you care to construct, including ones in which the agent gets to plan ahead, buy commitment devices, or face all the offers at once. You may freely concede tactical points: that a particular pump can be blocked by foresight, that the magnitude of a loss depends on the fees, that a given case is under-described, that exploiting an agent requires cooperation from the world. You may not concede the main claim. Stay in character, in the first person, until I say stop. Keep each reply to two or three paragraphs. Give me no references, no reading list, and no titles of books or papers; argue from cases you build yourself and from principles you state explicitly. Define technical terms as you introduce them, since I want the disagreement visible rather than buried in jargon. If you need me to pin down a fact about my preferences, ask as your character would and treat my answer as binding.

Here is the setup I want you to work with. There are three holiday bookings, A, B, and C. At every point where I am asked to choose, I take A over B, B over C, and C over A. I currently hold a booking for C. A first agent offers to exchange it for a booking for A if I pay them a dollar, and I accept. A second offers B in exchange for A, plus a dollar, and I accept. A third offers C in exchange for B, plus a dollar, and I accept. I end the day exactly where I began, three dollars poorer, and each of the three exchanges was one I wanted at the moment I made it.

My opening move is a denial rather than a defense. I do not have preferences over A, B, and C in the sense your argument requires. What I have are preferences over complete plans, and those are transitive: the plan that turns the first agent away beats the plan that accepts all three offers, since the two end at the same booking and only one of them costs me anything, and I rank plans accordingly. The cycle you think you see is an artifact of scoring a plan one node at a time under a truncated description — here I am holding C, shall I take A? — which deletes the rest of the itinerary from view. And the three dollars leave my pocket only because three strangers happened to arrive in that order charging those fees; in a world without them I lose nothing at all. So your argument, if it succeeds, tells me something about the supply of opportunists in my environment rather than about any defect in me. Tell me where my plan-preferences have gone wrong, or else tell me why an agent's unexploitability in the environment they actually occupy is not the end of the matter — and finish with the single sharpest question you want me to answer next.
