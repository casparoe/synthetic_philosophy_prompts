Two weeks ago I hosted a dinner at which a friend of mine — a structural engineer, sharp, not a professional contrarian — asked what I was teaching this term and, on hearing "Greek political thought," said that keeping Aristotle on the syllabus is how departments launder hierarchy. His argument was not the crude one: he said that the claim that some human beings are natural slaves is not a local error Aristotle happens to make, but the load-bearing wall of the whole system, since the household is supposed to be built out of natural ruler-and-ruled pairs the way the city is built out of households, and that seventy-five minutes spent reconstructing it is seventy-five minutes of respectability lent to it. I did not answer him well, partly because I think he is half right. The problem is that in week five I have to answer something like him in the room. I am the teaching assistant for a first-year history of political thought course, and I get the students for one seventy-five-minute section on Politics Book I. They will have read Book I in translation as homework and nothing else: no Nicomachean Ethics, no Plato, no prior philosophy. Perhaps a third of them will have arrived with my friend's verdict pre-formed from a podcast; the rest will be waiting to be told that the argument is simply false and that they may now move on.

What I want from you is not the argument — I can recite it — but a plan for the hour. Set out the order the pieces have to go in and what depends on what, and be explicit about the cost of each dependency: how much teleology I can realistically install before the students start fidgeting, and whether the normative sense of "by nature" (what a thing is for, what it is at its best) has to be in place before I say a word about slaves, or whether I can introduce it afterwards as the thing that makes the earlier sentences legible. Tell me where the tool-and-property material belongs relative to the claim about the deliberative faculty — the definition of the natural slave as one who can apprehend reason in another without having any of his own, and the thought-experiment about instruments that would work at a command, like the fabled tripods that went of their own accord to the assembly of the gods — and where the household's two models of rule (soul over body, and reason over appetite, mapped onto despotic rule and political rule) should be cashed in. Then give me the single example the hour should be built around, and tell me what it must have and must lack. My own candidate is the relation Aristotle himself reaches for at the end of the book, between a parent and a child whose deliberative faculty exists but is not yet developed; I want you to say whether that carries the argument, or whether it does more damage than good — either by suggesting that the natural slave is a permanent child, which is closer to what Aristotle means than anyone in the room will be comfortable with, or by letting the students dismiss everything on the grounds that children grow up. Say too whether the modern guardianship case, where a court appoints someone to decide for an adult judged unable to decide for himself, is usable in this room or whether I should keep it out entirely.

Map the misconceptions for me, and pin each one to the point in the sequence where it has to be headed off rather than argued down later. I expect at least these: that "by nature" means "on average" or "innately"; that the thesis is a falsifiable empirical claim about intelligence, already refuted, so there is nothing left to discuss; that it is proto-racism, and its mirror image, that because Greek slavery was not racial the whole thing is less objectionable than it sounds; that Aristotle is merely describing Athenian practice, so there is no argument to assess; and the one my friend would press, that the stretch near the end of Book I where Aristotle takes up whether slavery rests on law and force rather than nature, and grants that enslaving the wrong people is unjust, is proof of ideology rather than a genuine difficulty. Tell me how the sequence should absorb that last one, since I also want the students to see the pressure the text puts on itself: that the master's science turns out to be trivial and delegable to a hired overseer, which undercuts the claim that the master's superior practical wisdom is what benefits the slave; and that Aristotle allows friendship with a slave insofar as the slave is a human being, which sits badly with the slave's being an article of property. Decide for me whether those two belong inside the main arc as leverage on the thesis or at the end as a coda, and tell me what to cut and say so out loud — I assume the Greek terminology, the historiography of ancient slavery, and the whole secondary debate go, but if you would instead drop one of the passages I have just listed, say which and why. Close with one question I can put to the room in the last three minutes that separates a student who has grasped the structure of the argument from one who has merely learned to disapprove of it articulately.

Be decisive: give me one plan I could actually run, not a menu of options with pros and cons attached. But then step back and tell me where it is most likely to fail — which sequencing decision you would reverse if you watched the hour go wrong, and which of the two readings of the force-and-convention passage the whole order is betting on, that it is a concession which hollows out the theory or a completion of it, and what feature of the text would move you to the other one. If you think my friend is right and that Book I cannot honestly be taught as an argument at all, say so plainly, and tell me what I should do with the seventy-five minutes instead.
