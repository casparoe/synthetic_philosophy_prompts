For most of a year, three of us — none of us academic philosophers, though one teaches high-school mathematics — have been arguing about Moore's proof of an external world: the one where he holds up first one hand and then the other, offers those as his premises, and concludes that at least two external things exist, and so that there is a world outside his mind. Two camps have hardened, and we have come to suspect that we have been talking past each other rather than against each other. The first camp says the proof is cogent: its premises are known far better than any principle a skeptic could bring against them, and the only thing wrong with the argument is that a skeptic declines to count the knowledge it already displays. The second camp says it is transparently question-begging: the conclusion is packed into the first premise, since nobody could know that here is a hand without already having settled that they are not a brain in a vat, so the argument can never be the route by which that knowledge is acquired.

Draft the joint statement this collaboration ought to produce, in wording both camps could put their names to. I want a numbered list of the claims both accept — including claims about what the argument's validity requires and about what each premise actually asserts; the surviving disagreement stated three times, once neutrally, once in the first camp's preferred vocabulary, and once in the second camp's, with each side's version written so that side would sign it; then a table of cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one of us — each with the argument or case both camps agree would bear on it, and both camps' predictions recorded in advance. Close with a short section naming any part of the dispute that turns out to have been merely verbal, and saying why.

As a check on the drafting, run the same machinery over a variant neither camp has yet addressed: in place of hands, the premise is a memory. I seem to remember eating breakfast in this kitchen this morning — the kitchen, the meal, and my being awake for it all included in the seeming; therefore the past reaches back further than five minutes. One camp expects this proof to fail in a way the hand proof does not; the other expects it to fail or to succeed for precisely the same reason. Say what each camp's diagnosis of the original argument predicts here, and whether either diagnosis is embarrassed by its own prediction.

Finally, tell me where you are least confident of your own handiwork: which crux you may have framed so that it quietly favors one camp, and what consideration or argument would make you say that one side is simply right rather than merely defensible.
