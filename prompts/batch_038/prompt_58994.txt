ok so I want to run a real episode through the Ulysses-contract machinery and see whether the machinery survives it. I've read Elster and the time-inconsistency literature and I've argued about precommitment on forums for years, and I think the euro's first fifteen years is the cleanest large-scale case we have, but I've never seen anyone in my circles actually do the mapping carefully instead of just saying "see, rules beat discretion."

The case. Maastricht: you create a single currency and deliberately refuse to build a fiscal union to go with it. To keep that from being a disaster, the designers lash themselves down in two ways — a set of budget rules with the famous 3% deficit and 60% debt ceilings, and a treaty clause saying nobody bails anybody out, i.e. neither the union nor the member states are liable for each other's debts. Plus an independent central bank with price stability as its mandate, which is the other classic mast. Then 2009–10 happens: Greece can't borrow, the accounting turns out to be worse then advertised, and the whole thing starts to unwind. Over a couple of years you get rescue funds that didn't exist in the treaty, a permanent mechanism that needed a small treaty amendment to make room for it, central-bank bond buying the treaty was not obviously written to permit, and eventually the head of the central bank standing up in London in summer 2012 pledging to do whatever it takes. A few years after that the German constitutional court takes a swing at the bond-buying program and says, roughly, if I'm remembering it right, that the ECB has a short window to justify the program as proportionate or else the Bundesbank has to stop participating. Don't fuss over exact dates and figures — I care about the structure, not the trivia. And no bibliography, no reading list, no "for further see"; I know the lit, I want the analysis.

What I want, in order:

1. The mapping, done honestly. Who is Odysseus, who is the crew, what exactly is the mast? The awkward bit: the people who wrote the no-bailout clause were binding future governments that never signed anything, so is this even a Ulysses contract, or is it one generation tying a different one — which seems like a different problem entirely. Then pin down what the temptation was supposed to be (fiscal free-riding, I assume) versus what the temptation turned out to be (bank panic, spreads, a liquidity problem wearing a solvency costume). My claim is the contract was tied against the wrong siren. Tell me whether that survives scrutiny or whether you can show the actual siren was the anticipated one after all.

2. Did the rope snap, or did it stretch? This is where I think people fumble. Nobody formally repealed the no-bailout clause. What you get instead is a run of reinterpretations: loans aren't the assumption of debt, a stability mechanism is fine if you amend a paragraph, secondary-market purchases aren't monetary financing, and so on. So what does a precommitment theory owe us here — is there a real difference between a contract that gets broken and a contract that gets reinterpreted so that no breach is ever recorded? If the theory can't draw that line, say so plainly rather than papering over it.

3. My own coinage, which I want attacked rather than flattered. I've taken to calling this mast maintenance. The standard picture treats binding yourself as a one-shot act at t0: you tie the knot, then the knot does the work. For institutions that looks wrong to me. A mast is something that has to be continuously re-lashed by somebody, and the people doing the lashing in year twenty are not the people who chose the mast in year ninety-two, their incentives have drifted, and the lashing work is itself discretionary. On this view what makes precommitment decay isn't weakness of will at the moment of temptation — it's the quiet accumulation of small interpretive decisions by whoever happens to be holding the rope. So: does this buy explanatory power the one-shot model lacks, or is it just Elster's enforcement problem (the crew has to be trusted not to untie you) with a nicer name on it? If it's a rebrand, say that. If it does work, name the specific feature of the euro case that the one-shot model gets wrong and maintenance gets right.

4. Where the theory is silent. I expect it can't handle everything, but I want the silences named rather than filled with confident noise. Two I'd push on: (a) who gets to interpret the contract, and whether a constitutional court is best read as a crewman with plugged ears or as a second Odysseus with sirens of its own; (b) whether precommitting against an emergency you can't specify in advance is coherent at all, given that any force-majeure clause you write down immediately becomes the thing everyone aims at — which feels like a Goodhart problem wearing a precommitment costume, and I don't know how to hold both thoughts at once.

5. Verdict. Does the episode confirm the rules-versus-discretion case, kill it, or reveal that the debate was never really about cases like this? And if you were redesigning the mast in 1992 with what we now know, give me one concrete structural change — one, argued for, not five wishy-washy ones.

And if you think my framing is bad, say so. If the euro is the wrong case for Ulysses reasoning and there's a better real one, name it in two sentences with the reason, then do the analysis on the euro anyway.
