So I'm not an academic and I don't have any philosophy training — I restore old tower clocks and player pianos for a living, which I guess is how I ended up down this hole. Over the past year I've been reading about functionalism and multiple realizability: roughly, that what makes a mental state the state it is is the job it does rather than the material doing it, so the same pain could in principle be running on neurons or silicon or beer cans and pulleys, and it'd still be that pain because it's playing the same role.

The thing that's been bugging me is an objection I half-remember from somewhere and can't relocate: that a functional definition is either so loose it admits things that clearly aren't in any pain at all — a whole country organized so that its citizens collectively mimic one head — or you tighten it up until it excludes creatures that plausibly do feel things, and there doesn't seem to be a non-arbitary place to stop. Too permissive on one side, too chauvinistic on the other. I might be mangling it badly.

I don't want an essay about that though. I want a story, because I think a story can hold both sides at once in a way the argument can't.

Here's the setup I keep turning over. Change the furniture however you like, but keep the bones:

A town has a clock tower, and the bell is what actually runs the place — when the market opens, when the mill starts, when to eat, when to bring the water in, when the school lets out. A keeper maintains it. Over the course of the story the thing gets replaced, in stages, by something that does the same job out of completely different stuff. Maybe: the brass gearing gives out and she rebuilds it in wood because that's what's available. Then the mechanism can't be saved at all and she keeps the time herself, walking a round and striking the bells from a schedule she holds in her head and on paper. Then she can't do it any more and an apprentice does, and then the apprentice stops doing it — and the town keeps going anyway, because now everyone starts when the light hits a particular stone on the bridge, and the school lets out when the swallows go, and the whole place runs on habit and cues with nothing being struck at all.

Now bring in an outsider with a reason to care that isn't philosophical: a surveyor, an appraiser, somebody from a railway company writing down what the town owns and what it's worth. He has a line in his ledger for the clock, and at every stage he has to ask whether the entry still refers to anything. And the keeper, every single time, says of course it's still the clock, nothing's changed, it's the same job getting done — and she isn't confused or sentimental, she means it.

The important part: don't make him a pedant who loses. Give him a real reason at each stage. Each replacement should actually fail at something the old one did, or do something extra the old one didn't, and the town should have to bend around that a bit — a wedding that starts late, a shift that runs long, somebody's whole routine wrecked, some new thing that only became possible once the bell stopped. The reader should feel the town changing while everyone insists it hasn't.

Write it as a proper short story, roughly 1,800 to 2,500 words, with scenes and dialogue, not a fable with the lesson stapled on the end. No philosophy vocabulary anywhere — nobody says "function," "realize," "substrate," or any of it; the ideas should be entirely in what happens. And please don't resolve it with a trick: no reveal that the outsider is God or an angel, no dream frame, no "the keeper was dead all along," no dying-child ending. I also don't want either character turned into a mouthpiece who wins the argument.

One more thing before you write: if you think my framing of that objection is confused, or that a story genuinely can't do the job I'm asking of it and the honest answer is that I'd get a worse story or a better essay, just say so plainly up front instead of politely handing me something that doesn't work. I'd rather be told.

After the story, add a clearly separated note of a few paragraphs on where you took liberties: where the town's situation isn't actually like the philosophical case and the analogy strains, which reading of what functionalism commits you to you leaned on (there's more than one and I don't pretend to know which is right), and whether the story quietly comes down on one of the two horns — too permissive or too chauvinistic — and if that was deliberate or just where it went.

Last line of everything, on its own: Summary: followed by one sentence saying what the story's answer to "which one is the clock" turns out to be, or stating that it refuses to answer.
