I am preparing a short statement of my own position to circulate in advance of a two-day workshop on aggregation and the ethics of rescue, and before I send it out I would like a genuine consistency audit rather than a tour of the debate. The participants will include committed contractualists, a couple of Taurek sympathisers, and at least one person who thinks the entire framing of the problem is mistaken, so I would rather discover the soft spots in my package myself than have someone locate them for me at the second session. Please treat the following as a set and tell me which items pull against one another, how badly, and what would have to be surrendered or weakened to make the remainder cohere. I have deliberately not indicated where I suspect the pressure lies, and I would ask you not to assume in advance that any particular item is the expendable one.

Here is the package. (1) In rescue cases of Taurek's shape, where I can save either one stranger or five strangers and the claims on both sides are equally strong, I should save the five; the coin flip strikes me not as a requirement of respect for each person but as a way of evading the fact that each of the five would otherwise be treated as though their claim counted for nothing at all. (2) My justification for (1) is complaints-first and strictly pairwise: I compare the strongest complaint one party has against the strongest complaint another party has, and I never add complaints together. I accordingly reject any appeal to impersonal totals, sums, or the goodness of states of affairs taken as wholes. (3) I do not believe there is any class of harms too trivial to enter moral deliberation in its own right, and I reject sharp cutoffs between harms that can be outweighed by numbers and harms that cannot. (4) I nevertheless hold that no number of very mild harms — headaches, say, or the inconvenience of a missed train — can outweigh one person's being tortured or killed. (5) I am a reductionist about personal identity: identity over time is not what fundamentally matters, and the boundaries between persons are considerably less deep than common sense and much of moral theory assume. (6) I explain the intrapersonal case by appeal to the unity of a single life: it is unproblematic for me to accept a sharp pain now in exchange for avoiding a worse one next week, and I use the uncontroversial character of that trade-off to mark off interpersonal aggregation as the thing that stands in need of special justification. (7) Finally, I think I may save my own daughter rather than five strangers without needing to justify that by appeal to the greater good, while also thinking that a public health authority allocating a fixed budget under crisis conditions should aggregate in a thoroughly impersonal, quality-adjusted-life-year fashion.

What I want back is an audit, not a lecture. Order the tensions you find by severity and, for each, identify precisely which two commitments generate it, state the cheapest repair, and say what that repair costs me elsewhere in the set — in particular, whether fixing one problem opens another. Be equally clear about the pairs that only look problematic: I expect at least one of my commitments to survive scrutiny that you might initially be tempted to flag, and I would rather be told why it dissolves than have it lumped in with the genuine difficulties. Where a tension is real but merely contested rather than fatal, say so and explain what the opposing side would have to show to dissolve it. If a single revision would resolve more than one problem at once, tell me which revision that is and sketch, in a few sentences, what the resulting view looks like and which of my intuitions it abandons. Please do not send me a reading list, bibliography, or citations; I know the literature well enough, and what I need is an assessment of my own position. A thousand words or so will be ample, and I would prefer density to coverage.
