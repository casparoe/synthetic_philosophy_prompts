So, context first, sorry. I write novels. This is my third, and my narrator is a woman who, since her husband died, can't rank anything anymore — everything she notices arrives with the same urgency, the sound of a kettle, a stranger's limp, a word on a shop sign. I keep telling myself that's a real cognitive thing and not just my prose showing off. The way I've been describing it to my editor, badly, is that she has the frame problem: the AI one, where the system can deduce plenty but can't work out which of the deducable things matter.

I've been rereading Klara and the Sun for how Ishiguro handles a mind that has to guess at relevance, and I want a proper read of it through the frame problem — from someone who knows that literature, not someone who tells me it's a book about love and technology.

Some things I want taken seriously:

- Which frame problem does Klara actually have? The narrow technical one — frame axioms, the qualification problem, ramification, that sort of thing — or the philosophical one about relevance and holism that came after it, the version where the robots keep getting blown up while they're still working out what's worth attending to. She has no database of axioms to protect, so I suspect the fit is the second one, but I'd like the difference argued rather than assumed.
- Her solar religion as a relevance heuristic. My hunch, which may be stupid: the Sun isn't a delusion bolted onto the story for pathos, it's her solution — one ordering principle that collapses an unbounded world into a handful of things that matter, and that's why she can act at all. Is that defensible, or am I forcing it?
- Does love do the same job? Klara's devotion to Josie seems to supply exactly the ranking function nobody could ever specify. If that's right the novel is making a claim about relevance that no nonmonotonic logic makes.
- The scenes I care about: the beggar and his dog she takes for dead and then sees revived; the barn pilgrimage and the bargain; the sabotage of the Cootings Machine, where she spends part of what she carries in her own head and loses something of herself to do it; the mother asking her to imitate Josie and the body being built behind the daughter's back; and the yard at the end, where the Manager tells her only as much as she can hold. That last one — is the Manager doing a benevolent narrowing of someone else's frame, relevance-management as a form of care? I don't think the literature has a name for it and I want to steal it.
- Where the reading breaks. Be blunt: Klara isn't a symbol cruncher from the sixties, Ishiguro never says how she works, and maybe what she suffers from is underdetermination plus confabulation, not the frame problem at all. If my whole framing is an anachronism I'd rather hear it now than build a book on it. Also: does this lens turn Klara into a buggy expert system, and is that a cost the novel resists?
- And the humans. The mother gambling on a second lifting after the first one killed a daughter, the father who's been replaced, the boy who wasn't lifted. Are the people worse at relevance than the AF is?

End by telling me what the novel adds to the argument that the argument can't make on its own. No reading list please, I won't read it, and don't try to quote the book back at me — my copy is in a box in storage.
