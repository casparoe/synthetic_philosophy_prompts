The argument below is my own, and I have used it for years as a diagnostic exercise in the ethics-of-war unit of my graduate seminar. I have come to distrust it: every premise strikes me as true, the conclusion is one I cannot hold, and the generalizations it licenses are worse than the conclusion. I am after a diagnosis, not a survey.

Let S be a state that maintains a nuclear deterrent, A its principal adversary, R the retaliatory strike that S's doctrine prescribes against A's cities and industrial base, and C the circumstance in which A has already launched a first strike that has destroyed S's capacity to resist, so that R is the only act left to S.

(1) Commitment Constraint. An agent may adopt a policy committing it to perform φ in circumstance C only if it may perform φ, were C to obtain.
(2) Credibility. A deterrent deters only if S is committed to R in C in the sense required by (1), and only if A knows this. It is not enough that S would, in C, have decisive reasons to fire: in C S would also have decisive reasons to stand down, and A can see that. Deterrence therefore requires that S have removed its own opportunity for prudent reconsideration.
(3) Pointlessness. In C the incoming warheads cannot be intercepted, the cities cannot be saved, and the dead cannot be restored; A has already secured its objective, and S has nothing left with which to bargain and nothing further to defend. So no consequence of performing R in C improves on the consequences of refraining in C.
(4) Cost. R in C kills several million people who are attacking no one and who, by hypothesis, gain nothing from being killed.
(5) Hence R would be impermissible in C. (From 3, 4.)
(6) Hence S may not adopt the deterrent policy. (From 1, 2, 5.)
(7) Netting. A state that forgoes a credible deterrent while its adversaries retain theirs invites the coercion, conquest, and conventional wars that deterrence prevents, and the deaths those wars produce are not fewer than the deaths that the occasional failure of deterrence produces.
(8) Hence S is required to adopt the deterrent policy. (From 7.)
(9) So (6) and (8) contradict one another, and since each premise seems true, at least one is false.

Three things about this worry me.

First, the argument does not stop at nuclear weapons. If (1) is right, it condemns any policy of retaining a capability whose use would be forbidden in precisely the contingency that justifies retaining it; and generalized from policies to intentions, it seems to condemn promising outright, since a promise commits me to φ in circumstances where φ will have become pointless and burdensome. Either that is a reductio of (1) or it is a conclusion I must learn to live with, and I do not know which.

Second, a colleague suggests that (3) is not a truth about retaliation as such but a truth about a particular strategic configuration: that it silently presupposes mature, survivable second-strike postures on both sides, and that under other arrangements — a state whose forces are vulnerable and whose doctrine escalates early, or a strike ordered while a conventional invasion is still in progress — retaliation in the relevant circumstance would still avert worse outcomes. I cannot tell whether (3) is thereby doing covert doctrinal work or whether this is a red herring.

Third, and most seriously, whatever exit I find must be one I can consistently deny to non-state actors. A kidnapper who credibly commits to killing his hostages unless his demands are met, and who has destroyed his own capacity to recant, is running the same structure; a threat that is credible because its author cannot change his mind is exactly the device whose construction we condemn most firmly.

What I would like from you:

1. Is the argument valid as stated? If some step fails to follow, identify it; and wherever a phrase admits of more than one reading, state which reading each premise requires and whether any single reading makes all of them true together.
2. If it is valid, which premise should go? Rank the candidates — (1), (2), (3), (7) — by what their rejection costs, and for each say what must be accepted in exchange. I want prices, not a catalogue of the standard replies.
3. Explain why the premise you reject looked true in the first place: what weaker principle in its neighborhood survives, and why the weaker principle does not yield (6).
4. Take a position on the promising analogy and on the kidnapper. If your diagnosis licenses an agent to commit itself to what it would be forbidden to do, say whether that license is confined to states acting in self-defense — and if it is confined, say what does the confining and why the confinement is not ad hoc.
5. My own sympathies: I find (5) much harder to surrender than (8), and I suspect that pushes me toward rejecting (1), though I may be wrong about that as well. Tell me what surrendering (1) costs, and specifically whether that cost can be paid without giving up the constraints on targeting noncombatants that got me to (5).

Please do not send me the history of this debate, a list of positions, or references. Name the step, explain why it was tempting, and price the alternatives.
