My seminar partner and I are stuck on iterated dominance and I want a counterexample I can put on the board. His position: in a two-player 3×3 game, if no pure strategy is strictly dominated by another pure strategy, then iterated dominance can't get any traction at all, so the game can't be dominance-solvable. And he adds that whenever a game does solve down to a single cell, its because one of the players had a dominant strategy to begin with. I want one matrix that kills both claims at once.

Specs, and please treat them as hard constraints rather than aspirations:

- Two players, three pure strategies each, integer payoffs.
- At the very start of the game, exactly one strategy in the whole matrix is strictly dominated: one of player 2's. And that one is not strictly dominated by any pure strategy of player 2 — only by a mixture of the other two.
- No ties: all nine of player 1's payoffs distinct, same for player 2, so no dominance claim has to lean on an equality.
- No dominant strategy for either player at the outset.
- Once that first elimination is made, iterated strict dominance runs all the way to a single surviving cell in three or four more steps, and at least one of those later steps is plain pure-beats-pure. I want the mixture to be essential at the start but not to be the whole story.
- The surviving cell is the game's unique Nash equilibrium, and a strict one.
- Weak dominance is off the table entirely — every step has to be strict.
- Give the strategies plain-English names and a one-line setting (two food trucks picking locations, two councils picking a budget, whatever), but the story can't do any of the work: the numbers have to stand on their own.

What would count as cheating, since this is meant to actually refute him: asserting the eliminations instead of showing them; a game where the answer is visible by inspection; a game that only solves if you're allowed weak dominance; or the payoff numbers drifting between the matrix and the walk-through.

Then show the work. After the matrices, go round by round. Also confirm that player 1 has nothing strictly dominated at the outset, and I mean nothing dominated by a mixture either: check each of player 1's three strategies against all mixtures of the other two, not just against the pure ones, and do the same for player 2's remaining two.
