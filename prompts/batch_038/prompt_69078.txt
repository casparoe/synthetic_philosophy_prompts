Write a deadpan internal memorandum, roughly 700 words, from the chief financial officer of a national physics laboratory to its director, recommending that the lab cancel a billion-dollar flagship experiment on the grounds that a visiting philosopher has already settled the question a priori. Invent the philosopher, the experiment, and the rival theories; name no real people and cite nothing. Use conventional bureaucratic headings — Purpose, Background, Fiscal Impact, Anticipated Objections, Recommended Actions — and never step outside the memo to explain the argument or wink at it.

The humor has to come from the reasoning being correct rather than from jokes about philosophers. The odds in favor of the theory that predicts vastly more observers should be stated as one concrete figure and then used consistently everywhere else in the document. The Anticipated Objections section should include a second memo from the laboratory's other retained consultant, who reasons from a random sample of observers that the lab is probably near the end of its history and therefore also advises against spending — leaving the CFO unable to arbitrate between two advisory documents that both claim overwhelming odds. Include a short annex showing the same method applied to smaller decisions: a disputed headcount, a pending safety audit, whether to survey the cafeteria.

Somewhere the memo should surface the real fight over who belongs in the observer tally — aliens, uploaded minds, Boltzmann brains, future generations, the philosopher's own graduate students — and it should let the philosopher give his best reply to the charge of presumption, roughly that refusing to treat your own existence as evidence is arbitrary and that the objection only looks damning if you have already assumed the rival principle. He should not be a strawman. A specialist in anthropic reasoning should recognize every move as a genuine one; a reader who has never encountered the debate should still find the memo funny.
