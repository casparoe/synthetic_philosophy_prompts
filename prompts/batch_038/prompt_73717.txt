I got seated on a national time-scale subpanel about six weeks ago and I'm the one who has to draft the rationale section of the minutes, which means I have to guess where the chair lands before she actually says it. Context, because it matters: after the Anthropocene epoch proposal got voted down in 2024, our remit was narrowed to something that sounds like a technicality and has turned into the most political room I've ever been in. We aren't declaring an epoch. We're picking one reference point — a section plus a marker, a golden spike basically — that our official chart and the schools materials will point at when somebody asks when humans became a geological force. So someone has to decide when it started, and it turns out that's us, and half the people who think it should be somebody else are sitting on the panel.

Her record since I joined, as I've logged it:

- Endorsed a varved lake core where you can count annual layers down to a single year and where a fallout radionuclide spike sits exactly on the first appearance of plastic fragments. She called it the only candidate she'd seen that a stranger could re-find.
- Rejected the same signal in an Antarctic ice core, which has better resolution, on the grounds that ice isn't a section. When one of the historians pointed out that the Holocene's own golden spike is in a Greenland ice core, she didn't blink — said that was a different problem with a different history behind it.
- Rejected a peat bog with a gorgeous fly-ash rise because the radiocarbon control was ±60 years.
- Rejected a marine core where the marker is definitely there but smeared across about 40 cm by slow sedimentation and burrowing.
- Rejected a proposal to fix the point at a modelled threshold crossing — a particular CO2 value being passed — with no physical layer anywhere.
- Rejected a proposal to fix it from an archival date (a documented first-of-something in paper records) even though the sediment at that site happens to preserve a matching layer. Her line was that we can't have the archive of paper picking the archive of mud.
- Endorsed, in a different remit for an older boundary, a volcanic ash bed whose date comes from the ash itself and which is traceable across three continents.
- Rejected a candidate built on technofossils — concrete, a certain class of plastics — because it won't still be readable in ten million years.

Rules I think fit all of that: (a) the marker has to sit in a durable, continuously deposited, re-visitable sedimentary section; (b) it has to be globally synchronous rather than regional; (c) it has to be readable from Earth material alone, with no dependence on documents, models, or human intentions; (d) it has to stay legible over deep time. And maybe (e), which I can't tell is a rule or just a description of what the others add up to: she's guarding jurisdiction, so the point has to be one that only stratigraphers can adjudicate.

Thursday's case. A core from an anoxic coastal basin. Fast deposition, a couple of milimetres a year, no bioturbation at all, so the marker is a knife-edge you can count down to the year. It's lithifying — that interval will be hard rock in a million years, unlike nearly everything else we've looked at. And it correlates: matching layers turned up in two other basins on other continents inside what looks like the same year or two, so the synchrony objection doesn't bite. The problem is what the spike *is*. It's a very sharp excursion in one trace element and its isotopes, and there are two live explanations — a particular industrial process, or a natural hydrothermal pulse in the basin itself. Nobody has settled it and there's a decent chance it stays unsettled for a decade. Sitting on top of that, there's a petition in from a historians' association asking us to adopt their preferred date instead, on the argument that the start of a human epoch shouldn't be chosen by a body whose members are all stratigraphers, and the chair has to rule on whether that's even admissable.

What I want from you:

1. Where she lands on the basin core — endorse, reject, or defer — and what I should write in the minutes as the reasoning, in her voice rather than mine.
2. Which of her past rulings is the closest analog, and exactly where the analogy breaks down.
3. Which of (a) through (e) predicts what here, and which ones genuinely diverge. I think at least two do, but I can't tell whether I'm manufacturing the divergence so my memo looks clever.
4. If the precedents really do underdetermine it, what principle we'd be adopting by each choice — including the ruling on the petition, which I suspect is the actual question and the core is a proxy for it.
5. And the one further case I could bring her that would separate the rules for me. Something concrete, not a thought experiment she'd swat down.
