I write about higher-education finance, and one comparison does most of the argumentative work in the dispute between the Continental and Nordic model — no tuition for domestic students, provision paid for out of general taxation, in Denmark a monthly grant on top — and the Anglo-American model of high published fees met by loans, in Britain with repayment contingent on earnings above a threshold. The comparison is that a student loan is a mortgage. The borrower takes on debt to acquire an appreciating asset; the asset is her own enhanced earning capacity; the loan is secured against it; the schedule is simply amortization; and income-contingent repayment is a cleverer mortgage, because instalments flex with the value of the collateral. From this it is inferred that a fee-and-loan system does not burden students but finances an investment they could not otherwise make, that charging nothing is the arrangement in need of justification, and that free tuition is a transfer from taxpayers who never attended to graduates who did and who will out-earn them.

A colleague has raised a complication I cannot resolve. Her claim is that the comparison fails in an interesting direction: once repayment is a fixed percentage of earnings for a fixed term, with the residual balance cancelled at the end, the instrument is not debt in any ordinary sense but a minority equity stake in a person — and the whole debate is mis-framed, because we have been arguing about the price of a loan when we should be arguing about whether a state may hold shares in its citizens.

Please audit the mortgage comparison rather than wave it away. Work through the correspondence term by term — principal, collateral, appraisal, the interest rate as a price for risk, default and foreclosure, discharge in bankruptcy, the secondary market in the paper, the lender's capacity to diversify, and the buyer's information about what she is buying — and for each term give a verdict on whether the target case has a genuine counterpart or merely shares a name. Then separate the disanalogies that bear on the conclusions above from those that are merely picturesque. Say what the comparison quietly assumes that nobody in the debate argues for; I suspect at least one thing about who sets the price of the asset and who advises the buyer, and another about what happens to the value of the asset when everyone buys it. Then tell me whether the three conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying them all along — noting that the transfer conclusion in particular may not need the analogy at all, and saying so if that is right.

Finally, assess my colleague. What would a shareholding framing map onto what, where does it strain, and does adopting it change any policy conclusion that the mortgage framing had already delivered? If neither instrument fits the case, say so plainly and propose the one that does, together with the commitments it would force on both sides of the tuition debate.
