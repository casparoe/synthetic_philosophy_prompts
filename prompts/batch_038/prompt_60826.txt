I have no training in economics or in mathematics beyond secondary school, and I want to be plain about that, because what follows is something I worked out for myself rather than learned. For twenty-three years I ran a small building-supply yard, and a recurring part of the job was settling with someone else how to divide the cost of a thing we both needed and both benefited from, in different degrees, with different alternatives available to each of us if we failed to agree. Over the years I converged on one rule. I have come to think it is not merely workable but correct — that it is the fair way to divide — and I want to know what would have to be true for me to be wrong about that.

The rule. For each party, establish two figures: what they are left with if the deal collapses, and the most they could conceivably get out of the project if the cost of it fell entirely on the other side. Those two figures give each party a range. Then choose the split that leaves both parties the same fraction of the way along their own range — the same fraction, not the same amount.

An actual case, in thousands. A shared driveway serving two properties, needing proper repaving at a cost of 45. It is worth 80 to me, because delivery lorries use it daily, and 30 to my neighbour, who crosses it on foot. If we cannot agree, I gravel my own half, which I count as 40 to me net; my neighbour makes do, which I would count as 10 for them. So my range runs from 40 to 80 and theirs from 10 to 30. Doing the job jointly yields 65 of value net of cost, against 50 if we both fall back, so there are 15 to divide; the rule puts each of us a quarter of the way along our own range. I finish at 50 and pay 30 of the cost, my neighbour finishes at 15 and pays 15.

Compare the obvious rivals on the same figures. Splitting the 15 equally gives me 47.5 and my neighbour 17.5: equal money, but they will then have covered 37 per cent of the distance from their fallback to their ceiling while I have covered under 19, and the only reason is that their ceiling is lower. I cannot see why a modest appetite should earn a proportionally larger reward. Splitting the 45 in proportion to the benefit each of us receives gives 32.7 and 12.3, and I do not know what principle that answers to. Splitting the cost down the middle leaves my neighbour at 7.5, below their fallback, so there is no deal at all.

What I am asking for is a map of this rule's exposure, not a defence of it. If it turns out to have a name in some literature, one sentence on that will be enough; I am not after references.

- What does the rule quietly assume? I can guess at candidates myself — that the two sides agree on what is feasible, that a ceiling is well defined at all, that ranges are the right thing to normalise by — but I want to know which of these could fail in the world rather than only in a logician's example.
- Are there cases where its verdict is not merely surprising but plainly unacceptable to a fair-minded person? My own standing worry is that the rule pays whoever can present the widest range. Suppose I could persuade my neighbour that the driveway was worth 200 to me. Or suppose some third arrangement became available that would have made it worth that much, though neither of us would ever choose it. Either way my share grows and nothing about the actual work has changed. Is that fatal, is it manageable, or is my way of computing the ceiling already mistaken — should the ceiling count only arrangements the other side would actually accept?
- Which objections would a defender of the rule concede as biting, and which would they wave off, and on what grounds? Please rank what you give me from fatal, through serious but repairable, to merely awkward.
- Does it survive three or more parties, projects whose value is not a fixed pie, and parties who disagree about the figures themselves? If each of these needs a patch, tell me whether the patches are principled, or whether what I have is a two-person, fixed-pie habit dressed up as a general account of fair division.
- Finally, and most honestly: can I be wrong about this at all? A rule of this kind is not an empirical claim, and I notice that almost any counterexample can be absorbed by redefining what counts as a fallback, or as feasible, or as the ceiling. Tell me whether that leaves my position unfalsifiable; whether that is a defect in a proposal about fairness or only in a proposal about the world; and what I should treat as a genuine strike against the rule — in how people actually settle these things when left to themselves, or in my own past settlements, several of which I suspect were unfair in ways I could not see at the time.
