Three years ago I retired from a comparative physiology lab — forty years of work on how fish kidneys handle salt, which is not a sentence that gets you invited to parties — and to keep my head from going soft I've started reading philosophy, pretty much at random. It turns out I also can't leave biology committees alone, so I'm now the "retired member" of the curation policy committee for a database consortium that annotates gene function in a group of fungi (fermentation strains plus their wild relatives). Eleven of us, mostly by video call, and we vote next Thursday on a case that has been eating the committee alive for two months. My daughter says this is a midlife crisis arriving about fifteen years late.

Here's the case. A grad student characterized a paralog — call it locus 2714 — and in a clean in-vitro assay it turns over a substrate we'll call S about as fast as anything else in that family. In the living organism, though, S basically isn't there: it doesn't occur in the tissues where 2714 is expressed, and knockouts show no growth difference under anything anyone has managed to grow. What 2714 does measurably do in vivo is bind a divalent metal and buffer it, and that buffering makes a real difference to the cell's metal-handling capacity. The phylogenetics says the ancestral copy was doing the S reaction in relatives that still have the substrate, and that this lineage lost the substrate afterwards. So: annotate the S activity, annotate the metal buffering, annotate both, annotate neither? Half the commitee says a database describes mechanisms and not history, and anyway we have no evidence code for "was selected for," so the S activity has to go. The other half says if we start annotating whatever a protein happens to do inside a cell we'll drown in hundreds of incidental activities, and that the S chemistry is what makes 2714 a member of that family in the first place. There's a related headache: some wild isolates carry a 2714 with the catalytic site wrecked and the metal buffering intact, and one curator wants to flag those as loss-of-function, which only makes sense under one of the two readings. And a partner database on the other side of the Atlantic follows the opposite convention, while our interoperability agreement says our annotations should translate into theirs without loss — which may just not be possible here.

I've been reading about how philosophers split "function" into the historical kind — what a trait was selected for — and the current-contribution kind — what it actually does now as part of a system's capacities — and it looks to me like our fight is that same fight wearing a lab coat, except nobody on the committee has heard of it and I'd rather not lecture them. So: draft the decision memo I'll circulate before Thursday. Give me a bottom line — a rule the committee can adopt, plus the specific ruling on 2714 and on those wrecked-catalytic-site isolates — and then the criteria a curator at the bench could actually apply to the next case without ringing me. Write it for people who are smart, tired, and allergic to jargon; if the philosophy is doing work, let it show up in the criteria rather than as named schools of thought. Take the losing half seriously enough that they'd recognize their own best argument in the memo and still be able to answer it. And at the end, tell me what would change your mind: which consideration, if it turned out true about this locus or about how the database gets used downstream, should flip your recommendation, and where you think your reasoning is most likely to break.
