I'm on the science-and-ideas desk of a general-interest magazine and I'm drafting a long feature about the philosophy and neuroscience of pleasure. Before my own line goes into print, I want a consistency audit of the position I've informally committed to in conversation with sources — not a defence of it, just a check on whether it hangs together.

Here is the package:

(1) Well-being hedonism: nothing is intrinsically good or bad for a person except pleasure and pain.

(2) Pains are phenomenologically unified — there is something common to what it is like for them to hurt — but pleasures are not. Cold water on a hot day, a proof finally coming out right, and a friend's good news share no single felt quality, and "pleasure" is a disjunctive, family-resemblance term.

(3) The affective-neuroscientific result that wanting and liking come apart — that dopamine-driven incentive salience is not pleasure — is something I want to deploy as support for hedonism against desire-satisfaction theories of well-being.

(4) Two lives containing the same total quantity of pleasure are equally good for the people living them, other things equal; and yet I also want to say that an hour of absorbed conversation counts for more in a life than an hour of equally intense snacking, and that this is a fact about the pleasure rather than about anything else.

(5) It is perfectly consistent with (1) that we should often pursue things that are not themselves pleasant — demanding work, hard training, difficult friendships — precisely because they reliably produce pleasure.

(6) The experience-machine intuition does not refute hedonism; it is status-quo bias dressed up as insight.

Which of these actually pull against each other, and which only look as if they do? Please rank the tensions by severity rather than treating them as equally bad, and for each one name the cheapest revision and what that revision would cost the rest of the package. For the worst one, say explicitly which commitment has to go and what is lost with it — including whether anything in (3) or (6) survives the loss. I'd also like you to mark clearly any tension that dissolves on inspection, since I suspect at least one of these does and I don't want to pay for it in print. No general primer on hedonism; I want the audit, specific enough that I can tell whether the feature can be built on this foundation.
