I am co-facilitating a two-day citizens' assembly for twenty-four randomly selected residents of a mid-sized city: a retired bus driver, two secondary-school civics teachers, a nurse, several people who have never voted in anything, and one person who canvasses for a party every election. The assembly's charge is a bundle of franchise reforms — whether to lower the voting age to sixteen, whether to make turnout compulsory, whether to extend the vote to long-resident non-citizens, whether to restore the vote to people serving sentences, and whether to give parents or guardians an additional fractional vote on behalf of their minor children. On the first morning I want to administer a short diagnostic that sorts participants by what they actually believe about the franchise, and I want to hand them the identical instrument on the second afternoon to see whether two days of argument moved them, and along which axis. Only the first administration matters for your purposes, but it constrains the design: the thing must be completable in about twelve minutes by people who have never read political theory, so nine items, each a concrete scenario with exactly three responses, none of which is visibly the serious or the caring answer. The only two labels in circulation among my participants are democracy and epistocracy, and the instrument must work without either.

The scenarios should be jurisdiction-free — I will tell participants that nothing about our actual electoral law is at issue — and I want them modelled on the following nine, which you should sharpen, order, and word yourself. A fifteen-year-old who would pass a civics test and his eighty-year-old grandmother who would fail it, and what follows if you gate one but not the other. A nurse who has lived in the city for twenty-two years, pays every tax, is subject to every law, and cannot naturalize because her country of origin refuses release and ours refuses dual nationality. A system in which turnout is compulsory but the fine is discharged by depositing any ballot at all, including a blank one or one marked none of these. A convicted fraudster losing the vote for five years while an unconvicted billionaire may buy advertising without limit. The fractional parent-vote proposal, under which a household with three children casts extra weight that lapses as each child comes of age. A second chamber filled by lottery and holding a real veto rather than an advisory one. A citizen who emigrated thirty years ago, has never returned and pays nothing here, but still receives a ballot. An adult who admits to choosing names at random and asks why his ballot should count as much as yours. A person who abstains deliberately and says the abstention is the message. No item may ask about a principle in the abstract — nothing of the form "should the vote be a right or a duty?" — because the whole point is to sort people by what they say about cases rather than by what they call themselves.

Here is the angle that should organize everything. I do not want a list of positions recited back to me; I want the instrument built out of three axes that I will stipulate, with the positions then located in the space those axes generate. The axes are: whether having a vote is a claim the individual holds against the polity or an office the polity confers and may condition; whether the constituency is fixed by who is affected by decisions, by who is subjected to its coercion, or by durable membership over time; and whether a ballot's contribution to legitimacy is aggregative — it adds weight to an outcome — or expressive — it matters that this particular person cast it. Each of the nine items should be scored so that an answer pattern yields coordinates on all three axes rather than a single left-to-right placement, and I want the complete scoring key: which response maps to which pole of which axis, and why that response and not another is diagnostic of that pole. Then give me the map of the resulting space — twenty-seven cells, though you may collapse any cell that no coherent answer pattern can actually reach — and for each occupied cell a descriptive name for the position living there, adding a standard label in parentheses only if you are confident the label exists and is normally used the way you are using it; flag any label you are unsure of rather than borrowing authority for it. For the unoccupied cells, identify them and diagnose each in a sentence: is nobody there because the combination is incoherent, because it is politically unattractive, or because it is merely unexplored and possibly worth a defender? My own suspicion, which I want you to test and report honestly on rather than humour, is that treating the vote as an office rather than a claim already forecloses competence-gating, in which case the first axis is not independent of the epistocratic answers to the competence cases and at least one of my nine items is doing no work — if that is right, tell me which item to cut and what to put in its place. Finally, name any pair of leaves your instrument cannot genuinely separate; if two views differ only over something no scenario in my list touches, say so and describe the scenario that would separate them.

Close with a short paragraph stating how confident you are that the three axes are independent and that your cell-map is correct, which single point in the whole instrument you are least sure of, and whether that residual uncertainty concerns the political philosophy or your prediction of how real people will read the scenarios. No bibliography, no reading list, and no attribution of any position to a named author — the deliverable is the instrument and the map.
