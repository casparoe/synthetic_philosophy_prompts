Forty-odd years in renal physiology, retired two springs ago, and now I spend my afternoons reading political ethics instead of napping, which my wife says is progress. Currently stuck on corruption — what makes a gift a bribe rather than a courtesy. My complaint with the literature is that everybody argues from anecdotes, and anecdotes have a dozen variables moving at once. In my line of work you change one thing and hold the rest fixed or you've learned nothing. So build me a controlled case.

What it's for: I want to know whether all this talk about abuse of office and captured roles is doing real work beyond the plain old exchange requirement, or whether it's the same requirement wearing a nicer hat. That's the standard for whether you've cheated.

Specs. Realistic modern setting — a licensing office, a public hospital, a school district buying something. No cartoon villains, no politicians on yachts. The official is competent, sincere about their own motives, and the decision they eventually make is genuinely defensible on the merits. One paragraph per case. Label the four accounts up front so we're not sliding around: the exchange account (corruption needs a benefit traded for a misuse of the office), the private-gain account (abuse of entrusted power for private benefit, deal or no deal), the role-integrity account (the wrong is letting the office's own purposes be captured by a personal relationship, gain not required), and the conventionalist account (what counts depends on the norms actually in force in that setting).

Case A: a person with business before the official gives them something of real but not enormous value, no request attached, no expectation of return on either side, nothing said and nothing implied. Exchange account says not corruption; private-gain says corruption; role-integrity says corruption and worse than the private-gain account admits. Amount, secrecy, identity of the giver, the decision and its defensibility stay absolutely fixed across every version.

Case B: same facts, except the gift sits inside a local custom that is simply recieved as part of how that office works, and that even reform-minded locals don't treat as improper. Conventionalist now says not corrupt. The part I actually care about: the two not-corrupt verdicts in A and B must not be the same no — different accounts, different reasons. Then give me the minimal edit to B that flips the conventionalist to corrupt while the exchange account still says not corrupt. If you can't do it, say so and tell me what that shows.

Then the writeup: which feature of the case is doing the work — the expectation of return, the secrecy, the value, the fact that some third party got passed over, or the norms of the office itself. Run the ablations and report which account moves on each: make the gift public, make it trivial, make it reciprocated in fact years later. Then give me the best attack the exchange-account defender can make on my design, and say whether I should just loose that argument. Finish with whether any of this survives when you run the identical facts in a setting where the norms are supposed to be strict — is the cultural frame load-bearing or decoration?

And don't smuggle the verdicts in through the back door. If your case has a hidden deal or a wink in it, own up to that, because a scenario that stipulates what each account says instead of generating it from the account is worthless to me.
