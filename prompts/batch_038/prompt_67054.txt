I'm a novelist and I've got a piece I want to try out on you before I burn a week on it myself. Somewhere around 1500–2000 words. Start with the story itself — no preface, no "here's what I was going for," no note at the end unpacking the theme. A title at the top is fine if you want one, but nothing else around the fiction.

The seed is a real clinical thing: asymbolia for pain. With certain insular lesions — and, less cleanly, after some cingulotomies — people keep the sensory-discriminative half of nociception perfectly intact and lose the affective half. They'll tell you where it hurts, how sharp, how intense, what position makes it worse, and then say it doesn't bother them. Not bravado, not dissociation in the trauma sense. You can prick them, burn them, hold them in a stress position, and they won't withdraw and won't ask you to stop.

Now the part I invented, so there's nothing to look up: in my book's world this is elective. There's a licensed procedure — a targeted lesion, marketed under some gentle name like the Kindness or the Anodyne — and a whole labor class built on top of it. People get it done so they can hold jobs that would otherwise be unendurable: reactor decontamination, burn-unit nursing, saturation diving, livestock handling, certain kinds of rescue. Their union calls them cool hands. They're well paid, they pass medicals, they consent on camera, their families think it's the smartest thing they ever did, and by every metric anyone has bothered to measure they are the most satisfied workers in the economy.

My protagonist has been a cool hand for eleven years. I want the story where that stops being obviously fine.

Constraints, because this is exactly where my own drafts keep dying:

1. Nobody gets to articulate the philosophy. No character says "so is it even pain if you don't mind it," nobody delivers a little seminar on whether the badness of pain lives in the sensation or in the disliking of the sensation. The reader should be able to reconstruct that whole argument from what happens and what people do. If a line of dialogue sounds like a thesis, cut it.

2. Treat the protective function of pain as plot machinery, not as a talking point. These people aren't immune to injury, they're immune to the alarm. Somebody's body is being ruined quietly and the only evidence is a logbook entry, a changed gait, a smell in a locker room. I want at least one concrete scene where the absence of suffering is precisely what causes the harm — not a scene where it heroically lets someone tough it out.

3. The sales pitch has to actually work. The best scene in this story would be a skeptic being convinced, calmly and with specifics, by a cool hand that they'd be better off lesioned too. Don't strawman it. Whatever horror is in here has to survive the strongest version of the case for it.

4. End on an action, not a realization. Something physical and small, ideally involving hands.

Tone: workmanlike, close third, present tense. I want the flatness of an occupational procedural rather than literary shimmer. (My agent hates the term cool hands. I'm keeping it.) Keep the prose clean — I'll add my own mess later.
