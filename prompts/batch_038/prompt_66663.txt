Our reading group cooked up an argument that's supposed to show the whole indicative/counterfactual distinction is fake, and I can't tell wether it's clever or broken. Here it is:

(1) Both kinds of conditional get evaluated by the same procedure — the Ramsey test: add the antecedent to your stock of beliefs, make whatever minimal adjustments you need to keep the whole set coherent, and see whether the consequent comes out.

(2) The only difference between "if A, B" and "if A had been the case, B would have been" is where you're starting from. In the second case you already believe not-A, so adding A forces you to give something up; in the first you usually don't know whether A, so you just add it and conditionalize.

(3) Which beliefs get given up is never fixed by the sentence itself. There's no rule in English saying: when you suppose the shooter didn't do it, drop these beliefs and keep those. Any candidate rule either reduces to "give up what you'd least mind giving up," which is a fact about the believer, or it's a similarity ordering over worlds that has to be handed to the semantics from outside, by context.

(4) So the difference between the two constructions is a difference in the state of the person doing the evaluating, not in what the conditional says.

(5) Take the case everyone reaches for. We all agree Oswald shot Kennedy. The indicative — if Oswald didn't shoot him, someone else did — sounds flat out true. The counterfactual — if Oswald hadn't shot him, someone else would have — sounds false, barring a conspiracy. Same people, same event, same stuff in the antecedent slot and the consequent slot. On (4) that's just two runs of one test from different starting credences, exactly like one person asserting that it's raining while someone indoors says it might be raining.

(6) Therefore English has one conditional. The mood difference is grammar, not meaning, and there's no such thing as subjunctive semantics for anybody to write a theory of.

My problem is that if this works it works way too well. Run it on comparatives: 'tall' needs a comparison class from context and no rule in the language fixes it, so by (3)-(4) there's no semantics for 'tall' either, just tall-in-the-speaker. Same for indexicals. And (6) seems to predict we could never straightforwardly disagree about a counterfactual — when two clinicians argue about whether the patient would have survived the operation, on (6) their just reporting their own belief-revision habits, which can't contradict each other.

I want to keep (1); the Ramsey test is the thing I find most illuminating here. I'd happily lose (6). What I can't see is which of (3), (4), (5) is actually doing the damage, since each one looked fine to me when I first read it.

Lead with your one-sentence verdict on the step that breaks — just the verdict, no preamble, no summary of my argument back at me. Then explain why that step looked innocent going in and what precisely it slides from. Then price the repairs: if I keep (3) and reject (4), what do I owe; if I keep (4) and reject (5), what do I owe; and is there a reading on which the argument is actually valid and I should just pick a premise to abandon. Finally, tell me whether my comparatives and disagreement objections really have the bite I think they do, or whether I'm overreading them.
