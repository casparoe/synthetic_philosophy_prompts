I sit on the board of trustees of a small residential college, and in March we must dispose of a matter that has become, to my surprise, the most contentious item on the agenda.

Since 1934 — though nobody on this campus knew that until three weeks ago — our October Founders' Day has closed with the Cresset Procession: first-years carry unlit brass cressets across the quadrangle at dusk to the chapel, the president lights one from a standing flame and gives it to a first-year, and the light is passed until every senior in the building holds a lit lamp. The legend recited over it holds that the flame has burned unbroken since the winter of 1847, when the founder's wife kept a lamp in her window for students walking back from town after curfew during a cholera season. Our archivist, commissioned to write a new history of the college, has established that the ceremony was written by a student dramatic society for a pageant; that the brass cressets were ordered from a trade catalogue; that the continuity claim first appears in a development-office pamphlet of 1951, the year a brass plate bearing the legend was fixed inside the chapel; and that the founder's wife died in 1844. He has also found something the rest of us keep forgetting to weigh: in the winter of 1847 students really did carry lamps back from town, and the pageant's author borrowed the image from that practice.

A student paper has now summarized his report, so the findings are public and the option of silence is gone. The committee is split three ways. One trustee wants the procession abolished outright, on the ground that the college should not stage an assertion it knows to be false. The alumni association wants everything preserved exactly as it is and the report kept internal. The president proposes keeping the ceremony, dropping the legend from the spoken script without explanation, and leaving the chapel plate alone. Underneath these tactics there is a disagreement I cannot resolve: some of us have been invoking Hobsbawm's distinction between an invented tradition, which claims invariance and continuity with a suitable historic past, and mere custom, which is changeable and makes no such claim; others answer with Anderson's insistence that any community larger than a face-to-face village is imagined, and that the interesting question about one is not whether it is false but how it is imagined. We have been reciting these slogans at each other for a month and they have decided nothing.

Please draft the written opinion I will circulate before the March meeting. It must give a recommendation the board can vote on, item by item — the procession, the spoken legend, the 1951 plate, the continuity sentence still standing on our website and in the viewbook, the orientation script — with a time frame attached to each, and it must say what we do about the first-years who tell us the procession is the moment they came to feel they belonged. I want the reasoning to work through the distinctions above rather than to cite them, and I want an honest statement of the strongest consideration on the side you recommend against, put in terms its own holders would recognize. Three constraints: no literature review and no bibliography; do not resolve anything by proposing a further committee or a study; and if your recommendation is to rewrite the script, say what the new script should assert and why that is not merely a second invention replacing the first.
