Bit of background, since it matters for what I'm asking. I'm 71, retired three years ago after about 35 in an environmental tox lab — dose-response work, trace analysis, method development, detection limits, that sort of thing. Philosophy is a retirement hobby that has got a bit out of hand. Lately I've been reading around the "cluelessness" problem — Lenman's paper, and the more recent stuff building on it — and I've talked myself into a view I don't think is quite anybody else's, and now some bright spark has invited me to present it to our city's EA meetup plus the grad students who tag along. 25 minutes, then open floor. I have never done philosophy in front of philosophers and I'm a little worried.

The view, roughly. The cluelessness literature frames it as: we can't know the long-run consequences of our acts, so either you throw your hands up, or you go longtermist and lean on expected value anyway. Both moves skip a step that risk analysts take for granted. In my old job you first seperated variability from uncertainty, and then you stated a limit of quantitation — the level below which your instrument cannot tell signal from noise and you are simply not permitted to report a number, only "not detected." My claim is that most far-future consequence claims sit below the limit of quantitation rather than merely being noisy: they're non-assessable, and publishing a point estimate for them is a methodological violation, not a brave philosophical stance. And non-assessable doesn't mean do nothing — you apply a default uncertainty factor, the way you'd divide by ten for interspecies extrapolation, and you act on the robust near-term signal you actually have. So cluelessness doesn't refute consequentialism, it condemns the spreadsheet, and the fix is a reporting standard plus defaults rather than a change of theory.

The room: two people who work at longtermist-ish orgs and are fully committed to the far future dominating; one population-ethics postdoc, friendly but exacting; three or four software types who maintain their own cost-effectiveness spreadsheets and are suspicious of anything that can't be a cell formula; one hostile bloke who thinks the whole enterprise is a cult and comes every month; a couple of normals.
