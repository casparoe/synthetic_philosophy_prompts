I have been asked to sit on the jury of a short-fiction prize — one of those regional awards that carries a modest sum and an unreasonable amount of local feeling. Three jurors, four hundred stories, one month. My colleague the critic proposes a rubric: point of view handled without slippage, images that labour rather than decorate, an ending earned rather than merely arrived at, and six other criteria besides, each scored one to five, the entries ranked by total. The third juror, a poet, will have none of it; she says she knows within a page and a half whether a story is alive, and that scoring craft is a reliable way to reward the diligent and bury the gifted.

I see the force of both and trust neither. The rubric will favour the flawless and the hollow — I have read dozens of stories that satisfy every such criterion and leave no trace whatever. But the poet's method is unfalsifiable, unarguable, and, if the prize means to survive a complaint from the runner-up's camp, indefensible in public. There is also the arithmetic: four hundred stories scored on nine criteria means reading each of them three times over, which none of us has the year to do, whereas her instinct costs nothing but attention. And there is a competence problem I have not confessed to them. I write novels, long and slow and in the third person; two of the strongest stories in last year's pile were in forms I cannot assess from the inside — one entirely in dialogue, one in a compressed second person — and had I been scoring craft, I should have marked them down.

Finally, a matter that disqualifies me from designing anything. Eleven years ago I won a prize of exactly this kind by writing a story I knew the judges would like; I had read their criticism and built the thing to specification, and I did not enjoy writing it and have not been able to think about it since. If the rubric is published, as the critic wants, the writing programmes will have it within a week and next year's pile will be four hundred competent imitations of the rubric. If nothing is published, instinct rules, and instinct can be flattered, lobbied, and dressed.

Tell me where each of my colleagues is right and where each is missing something, what procedure three exhausted readers could actually carry out, how much of it ought to be said aloud — and whether my own disqualification is genuine or merely convenient.
