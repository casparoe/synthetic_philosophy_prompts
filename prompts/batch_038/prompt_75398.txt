Last week in a reading group on the distribution of primes, an objection to the Hardy–Littlewood derivation of the twin-prime constant was dismissed with the line "just pretend the primes are random." The objector had argued that nothing licenses treating "n is prime" and "n + 2 is prime" as independent events, since both are governed by congruence conditions; the reply was that the singular-series correction factor is exactly where those conditions get absorbed, and that once they are, no further correlation is to be expected, so independence is the proper default.

I would like to know what that slogan can actually be claiming. Please set out the distinct, precise theses "the primes are random" might express, ranging from the near-trivial to the metaphysical, and say of each whether it is true, false, or merely fruitful, and whether it is by itself strong enough to license the computation of the twin-prime constant. Then identify the reading our speaker actually needed and assess whether his appeal to the congruence corrections rescues it or merely relocates the difficulty — bearing in mind Maier's theorem on primes in short intervals and the parity obstruction in sieve theory. Finally, if any of the readings amounts to a presumption of independence, state it as sharply as you can: what would count as a reason to expect dependence, who carries the burden of proof, and what the presumption licenses you to conclude when it is not rebutted.
