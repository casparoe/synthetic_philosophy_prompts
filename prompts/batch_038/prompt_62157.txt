So I'm on a small committee at a state nursing board and we're rewriting the rules for the practical competency exam — the one where a candidate has to do a mock shift handover on a simulated patient and catch the three errors we've planted in the chart. Right now the rulebook just says "no electronic devices," which everybody reads as banning phones and smartwatches while quietly permitting a candidate's own beat-up paper card of drip rates that lives in their scrub pocket, and permitting a stethoscope, and obviously permitting whatever is inside thier head. One of my colleagues wants to scrap the whole thing and replace it with what she calls a cognitive integration standard: if a tool is so bound up with how the candidate actually thinks that yanking it away would measurably degrade the performance, it counts as part of their competence and comes into the room with them; if it's just a lookup they could take or leave, it stays outside. The rest of us suspect that's a distinction without a difference but nobody on the committee can articulate why, and she's been throwing Otto and his notebook at us for three meetings running.

Can you score each of the following from 0 to 10 on "how much is this genuinely part of the candidate's mind rather than a crutch," where 10 means removing it is like removing a chunk of their memory and 0 means it's no more a part of them than a library book on the shelf? For each: the number, one sentence of justification, and then all twelve sorted into a tier list. (a) the hand-written pocket card, updated by crossing things out in pen; (b) a stethoscope; (c) their own smartwatch, face down on the table, never consulted; (d) a board-provided locked-down tablet with a searchable drug reference and no calculator; (e) the same tablet but with a dosage calculator that just outputs the answer; (f) the laminated resuscitation algorithm chart that's already bolted to the wall of every real crash room; (g) a second nurse standing in the corner whom the candidate is allowed to ask "does that look right to you," exactly as they would on a real shift; (h) an AI scribe that listens to the handover and drafts the note, which the candidate then reads back and edits; (i) a memory palace the candidate built in nursing school that they cannot explain to anybody; (j) a four-function calculator; (k) live-captioning glasses worn by a candidate who's hard of hearing; (l) a mnemonic tattooed on the inside of the forearm, visible under short sleeves.

Then I want the diagnosis, not just the scores. Which criterion were you actually leaning on when the numbers came out — trust and automatic endorsement of what the tool tells you, constant availability, the cost of losing it, whether the candidate can articulate what it's doing, or something else — and name the single item on the list that most threatens to collapse the whole integrated-versus-lookup distinction, explaining why. Redo the ordering quickly under two seperate tests, pure trust-and-automatic-endorsement versus pure constant-availability, and tell me which pairs of items swap places, because that's where my colleague's standard is going to fall apart in front of the board. Also: if we found out a candidate hadn't opened their pocket card once in three years, does its score move, and does that answer depend on anything philosophically interesting? Finally give me one number, a fraction between 0 and 1 of the twelve items you'd actually let into the exam room under the integration standard, plus a sentence on whether you'd want that standard written into a legal rule at all.
