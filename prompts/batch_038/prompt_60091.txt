I am a postdoc teaching one seventy-five-minute seminar in a second-year interdisciplinary course on the history of the cognitive sciences. The session is on von Neumann's posthumous 1958 volume The Computer and the Brain, built from the Silliman Lectures he was invited to give at Yale in 1956 but died before delivering. The students have just done Turing's 1950 paper and Putnam's early functionalism, and they will arrive convinced of a slogan: the inventor of the stored-program architecture looked at the brain and declared it a computer. My problem is that the book does not say that, and in several places it says something close to the reverse. I do not want you to explain von Neumann's views to me; I want a plan for how I should explain them, in order, to twenty students who can follow an argument but have no neuroscience and no information theory.

Here are the pieces I intend to work with, so you can arrange them rather than guess at them: the all-or-none character of the nerve impulse, which makes the nervous system look digital at first pass; his insistence that individual neural components are imprecise where machine components are made precise; the contrast he draws between natural automata, which are massively parallel and shallow in the number of serial steps between input and output, and artificial automata, which are serial and deep; his treatment of that depth as the resource by which a machine buys precision and reliability through repeated, checkable stages; the arithmetic by which he bounds total lifetime sensory intake at roughly 1.4 x 10^11 bits per second across some 10^10 neurons, multiplied by about 2 x 10^9 seconds for a sixty-year life, giving something near 2.8 x 10^20 bits; the unease between that figure and anything the neuron count alone could house, which pushes him toward candidate substrates such as shifting synaptic thresholds, the changing distribution of conducting axons, and genetic memory; and finally his conjecture that the message system of the nervous system is statistical rather than arithmetical in character, with the further suggestion that its underlying logic may not share the axioms of formal logic, since a small perturbation of the premises there produces only a small change in the conclusion.

Plan the session for me under the following headings, in this order:

The Dependency Structure — about five hundred words. Give me the ordered sequence with rough minute allocations, and for each step say explicitly what it depends on and what would collapse if I introduced it out of order. I particularly want to know whether the parallel-versus-serial contrast has to be established before the statistical-language conjecture can mean anything to them, or whether the reverse order is defensible.

The Carrying Example — about three hundred words. Name the single case or calculation that should carry the whole session and say what it must have and what it must lack to do that work. I am tempted to build the spine out of his own memory arithmetic, done live on the board; tell me whether that functions as the carrying example or whether it is better placed as a late payoff, and if it fails, what should replace it.

The Misconception Map — about four hundred words, as a table with three columns: the misconception, the point in my sequence where it is most likely to form, and the specific claim I should make at that point to head it off. Cover at minimum the ones I expect: that von Neumann endorsed the brain-as-computer slogan; that the 2.8 x 10^20 figure is a measurement of memory capacity rather than a bound on lifetime input; that statistical language means probabilistic inference over hypotheses in anything like the contemporary sense; that he was anticipating connectionism or deep learning; and that precision and reliability are the same property.

What I Will Not Explain — about two hundred words. Tell me which of his other work I should explicitly set aside and announce as set aside: his probabilistic logics and error-correcting constructions, automata theory and self-reproduction, the Shannon framework, and whatever else would swallow the session if I let it in.

The Check — about one hundred and fifty words. Give me one question I can put to the room in the final five minutes that would distinguish students who grasped why he refused the slogan from students who simply learned that he refused it, together with what a passing answer and a failing answer would each sound like.

Keep the whole plan to roughly sixteen hundred words, and do not recommend any reading.
