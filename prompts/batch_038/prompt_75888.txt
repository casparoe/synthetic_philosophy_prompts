My roommate's deep into this stuff and he keeps reaching for the same image when he defends tranquilism — the view that the only thing that really matters is the absence of craving and suffering, never the presence of pleasure. Desire is an itch, he says, and satisfying it is scratching: the relief feels good only because the itch was bad, so the itch is doing all the work and the scratch is worth nothing on its own. He leans on it twice over, first to say that chasing pleasures is just self-inflicted itching, and then to say that a calm mind with nothing left to want is the best state available rather than a shrunken one. When I pushed on the obvious counter — if all the value sits in the itch going away, then a heavy sedative that deletes your capacity to itch should score exactly the same as decades of practice — he sort of stalled.

Can you audit the analogy for me? I want the mapping spelled out explicitly: what plays the itch, the scratch, the relief, the skin, the person who can't stop scratching. Then the places it breaks, sorted into the disanalogies that actually threaten his arguement and the ones that are cosmetic. What's being quietly imported — I suspect the sign of the baseline is the whole game, i.e. whether the itch-free state counts as positive or merely zero — and can the analogy do any work at all in separating the sedated patient from the tranquil sage? If it can't, is there a better analogy that can, or is that difference one no analogy will carry? American English, please.
