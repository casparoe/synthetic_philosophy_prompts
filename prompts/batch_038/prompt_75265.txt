Got into a fight in a comment thread last week about ad-blocking and I keep replaying it because I think I lost on a technicality. Someone posted a piece arguing that running an ad-blocker on a site you actually read is straightforwardly wrong, and the load-bearing move in the whole thing was 'what if everybody did that?' — if everyone blocks, the ad revenue collapses, the site dies, and then there's nothing left to read, so your free-riding is what kills the thing you love. I mumbled something about how my one blocked banner ad doesnt change any advertiser's or publisher's behavior at the margin, and got ratio'd for missing the point.

Here's my problem: 'what if everybody did that' is doing a bunch of completely different jobs inside that one sentence and I can't tell which job the argument needed. It might mean the act-consequentialist thing (if everyone did X the outcome would be bad — which then has to survive the fact that everyone won't). It might mean the Kantian thing, where the maxim is supposed to be self-defeating when universalized because the practice only works while some people abstain, like promising or queueing. It might mean a fairness/consistency point that says nothing about consequences at all, just that you're not allowed to make an exception of yourself absent a relevant difference. It might mean a rule-consequentialist thing about which code everyone should internalize and teach their kids. And it might mean the threshold/coordination thing where each individual act is genuinely harmless but the aggregate is terrible, harmless-torturer style, which is I think the structure ad revenue actually has. Could you lay these out properly as a menu rather than picking one and running with it: for each reading, state the precise principle it commits you to, then say whether it's trivially true, substantive but defensible, or just false — and separately whether it's a principle a utilitarian can accept, since I don't think my opponent was a Kantian at all, he just reached for the slogan because it sounded decisive.

Two extra things I'd really like handled. First, the maxim-description problem: half my pushback was that the counterfactual changes depending on how you carve the act — everybody blocking ads, vs everybody blocking ads on small independent blogs they read daily without paying, vs everybody blocking ads on giant aggregator sites. Say which level of description each reading picks out and why, and whether any of the readings can dodge the charge that they let you trivially universalize your way to whatever conclusion you wanted. Second, tell me which readings survive the reply that universalization counterfactuals are hostage to the empirical probability of everyone actually doing X — i.e. whether the slogan can support a conclusion about *my* act in a world where 40% of people already block. End with a bottom line on whether the 'what if everybody did that' move earned its keep in that thread, or whether my margin-reply was actually sufficient against the version he was using.
