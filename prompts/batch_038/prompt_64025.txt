I'm a staff engineer at a mid-sized payments company, and I sit as one of five members on our Platform Investment Review Committee — two engineers, one from finance, one from legal, one from product. Our charter is short and was written by people who expected tidy numbers. Clause (a) says the Committee shall record a recommendation to proceed only where the modeled expected net value of the proposal over the evaluation horizon exceeds $1.2M; clause (b) says only where no modeled scenario entails a net loss to the operating entity exceeding the Program Reserve, currently $40M. The charter says nothing at all about what to do if clause (a) has no answer, and that is exactly the situation we are in. The proposal on Thursday's agenda is a three-year program to expose our ledger internals as a public API with a revenue share on third-party volume. Our modeling group, which is one very good analyst and a spreadsheet, produced the following scenario table rather than the usual three-case high/base/low: for n = 1, 2, 3, ..., scenario n occurs with probability 2^(−n), and its net value is (−1)^(n+1) × $2M × 2^n / n. So a 50% chance of +$4M, 25% chance of −$4M, 12.5% chance of +$5.3M, 6.25% chance of −$8M, then +$12.8M, −$21.3M, +$36.6M, −$51.2M, and so on without end — the analyst defends the unbounded horizon on the grounds that the program has no planned sunset and the compounding platform effects genuinely do not stop. The justification for the exact 1/n attenuation factor is thin, and I have said so on the record.

Every scenario therefore contributes ±$2M/n to the weighted total, and if you add those contributions in index order you get $2M × ln 2 ≈ $1.386M, which clears clause (a). That is where things fall apart. The contributions are an alternating harmonic series: the gains-half and the losses-half each diverge on their own, so the $1.386M exists only as a limit that depends on the order in which you add the terms. I spent part of a weekend confirming this rather than taking anyone's word for it. Twenty lines of NumPy let me re-pair the same probabilities with the same payoffs in a different order and land on −$5M, or +$100M, or anything else I asked for. A Monte Carlo run does not rescue it either: at ten million trials the running average was still lurching by hundreds of thousands of dollars between seeds and never settled anywhere near $1.386M, because roughly one trial in ten million draws an n in the low twenties and single-handedly moves the mean. There is also a capped version. Legal points out that clause (b) is satisfied because the operating entity's exposure is structurally and contractually limited to roughly the reserve, so the honest model truncates every payoff at ±$40M; the analyst re-ran it that way and got ≈$1.41M, and ≈$1.37M at a ±$10M cap, and confirms the figure only falls below the $1.2M threshold if you cap below about $3.6M, which nobody will defend as our real exposure. Other facts I can vouch for: a gain in two-thirds of scenarios, a loss in one-third, a median outcome of +$4M, and about a 0.5% chance of a scenario whose uncapped loss would exceed the reserve. The committee is split. Finance says expected value is a modeling convention like any other, every defensible regularization lands near $1.4M, and we should stop pretending a mathematician's objection is a business problem. Our quant says the opposite: uncapped there is no expectation at all, so clause (a) has no truth value, and a recorded finding that expected net value exceeds $1.2M would be a finding about nothing. Our two non-technical members have said they cannot tell which of them is being ridiculous. My manager thinks I am gold-plating a decision that should take ten minutes, and I want to arrive Thursday with something better than that.

Please draft the memo I will circulate to the committee in advance of the meeting, roughly 900 to 1,100 words. It has to be a document the committee can act on, so: bottom line up front, followed by the exact motion sentence our chair could read aloud, and then the reasoning. The reasoning needs to do four things. First, explain the mathematical difficulty in prose that the finance, legal, and product members can follow using only the numbers I have given you — no measure theory, no notation beyond what is above, and no appeal to authority. Second, take a position on the crux, which I think is this: the probabilities and the payoffs are both fixed by the physical description of the program, so re-pairing them seems to describe a different program rather than the same one, and yet the standard expectation of a random variable is supposed to be a property of the distribution rather than of the order in which someone happened to list the states. Say whether clause (a) is satisfied, unsatisfied, or inapplicable on this record, and say what reading of the clause you are relying on; then give at least one competing reading and explain why you reject it. Third, make the strongest case against your own recommendation in the voice of the member who holds it — for finance that includes the robustness of the capped figures across every plausible cap, the two-thirds gain probability and the +$4M median, and the worry that a committee which refuses to act on indeterminate expectations will refuse to act on most interesting ones; for the quant that includes the precedent risk of recording a finding we cannot support and the fact that the simulation cannot validate the number — and then answer it rather than merely acknowledging it. Fourth, if your recommendation is anything other than a flat rejection, specify conditions the committee can monitor and enforce, and give one sentence of proposed charter language covering indeterminate expectations for future proposals, since an amendment motion is already on file. Ground everything in the case as I have described it and do not invent facts about our company, our market, or our finances. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind — I cannot check them and would rather you spend the words on the argument. If the situation has a standard name in the decision-theory literature, one passing mention is fine, but the memo should not turn into a survey of it.
