**The exam paper (relevant front matter and question):**

> For this paper the following usages are fixed. "Presentism" means the thesis that only present things and events exist. "Eternalism" means the thesis that past, present, and future things and events equally exist. "The A-theory" and "the B-theory" are views about what is needed to make ordinary temporal claims true: the A-theory holds that irreducibly tensed facts, or the passage of time, are required; the B-theory holds that they are not. The growing-block view and the moving-spotlight view are both versions of the A-theory that are not presentist.
>
> **Q3.** Set out the argument from the relativity of simultaneity against presentism, usually credited to Rietdijk and Putnam. Then evaluate two responses available to the presentist: (i) the postulation of a privileged but empirically undetectable foliation of spacetime; (ii) the rejection of the transitivity of the *is real with* relation. Say which of the two is more promising, and why. Finally, say what the argument, if sound, shows about the growing-block view and about the moving-spotlight view.

**The student's answer (paragraphs numbered for reference):**

[1] Special relativity tells us that simultaneity is not absolute. Two inertial observers moving relative to one another divide spacetime into "here, now" and "elsewhere, otherwise" differently, and neither division is the correct one. Given any two events at different places, there are frames of reference in which they are simultaneous, frames in which the first is earlier, and frames in which the second is earlier. Which distant events are happening *now* is therefore not a fact about the world but an artefact of one's state of motion.

[2] The argument exploits this. Suppose an observer O is at rest on Earth and an observer O′ is passing at high relative velocity. Let *e* be an event in the Andromeda galaxy which, in O's frame, lies in O's future. In O′'s frame, *e* is simultaneous with O′'s present moment; and since what is simultaneous with the present just is present, *e* is present relative to O′. But O′'s frame is no less legitimate than O's — there is no experiment that could show otherwise — so *e* is really present, hence real. Yet *e* was future relative to O. So a future event is real, and the presentist, who says only present things are real, is refuted.

[3] The premise that does the real work is that *is real with* is transitive, or, as it is sometimes put, that reality must be an equivalence relation. It is transitivity that lets us chain from O's now to O′'s now to the distant event; without it, each observer's present is real only relative to that observer and nothing follows. I think this makes response (ii) the more promising of the two. It concedes nothing to the physics: it denies only a metaphysical principle the argument smuggled in. Its cost is that the presentist owes an account of why reality should fail to be transitive, and cannot simply assert it.

[4] Response (i) is much worse off. To say that there is a privileged frame is to say that the relativity of simultaneity concerns only what observers can measure, not what is really simultaneous. But this is ruled out by experiment: no experiment has ever detected a preferred frame, and the Michelson–Morley result shows that there is none to detect. A foliation that no possible observation could reveal is not a hypothesis at all.

[5] If the argument is sound, then, it refutes presentism, and with it the A-theory, since the A-theory is just the view that only the present is real. The growing-block theorist, by contrast, is untouched: she already grants that past events exist, so the argument's conclusion is half of what she believes, and relativity poses her no threat. The moving-spotlight theorist is in trouble, because she holds that all times exist but adds an objective present that sweeps along them, and once simultaneity is relative there is no way to single out a unique sweep. The upshot is that we should accept the B-theory — the view on which past, present, and future equally exist — as the only account of time compatible with physics. It is worth noting, though, that the argument is often said to establish determinism, which is a misnomer: its conclusion concerns what exists, not what the laws compel.
