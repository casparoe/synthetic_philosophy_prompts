I am preparing the final examination for a graduate seminar I teach on the evaluation and oversight of autonomous systems. The cohort is mixed — roughly half philosophy doctoral students, half machine-learning students — and the paper is three hours, closed book, with no permitted sources. One question is worth 25 of the paper's 100 marks. I have already fixed the case material the question will be built on, precisely because I want the difficulty to come from the reasoning rather than from recall of the literature, so please draft the question around the material rather than replacing it.

Students will see the following printed in the booklet. It is a constructed case, not a real deployment, and you should treat it as fully specified: they are expected to work only from what it says.

*Case material.* A firm deploys an agent that answers multi-step analytical queries over its internal data. Two oversight regimes are run in parallel on identical traffic. Under regime P, a smaller overseer model reads the agent's intermediate reasoning — the plan, each query it writes, each intermediate conclusion — and assigns a per-step score; steps below a threshold trigger human review before the agent proceeds. Under regime O, the overseer sees only the original query and the agent's final deliverable (answer, table, two-sentence summary) and scores that; low scores trigger review. Neither regime has access to ground truth. Both are audited afterwards by human reviewers who determine whether the deliverable was in fact wrong.

Results over one quarter. (i) On 400 queries whose answers are mechanically checkable — arithmetic over retrieved figures, schema-validated joins — regime P's interventions caught 91 per cent of the errors the auditors later identified; regime O caught 63 per cent. (ii) On 400 queries turning on an ambiguous internal definition — for instance, whether an order that was refunded and then repurchased counts as new revenue in the quarter — regime P caught 44 per cent and regime O caught 58 per cent; of the traces P flagged on these queries, 18 per cent proved actually wrong on audit, whereas of the deliverables O flagged, 51 per cent proved actually wrong. (iii) The firm then fine-tuned the agent for six weeks against P's per-step scores. Its traces grew longer and more explicit: it began stating assumptions, enumerating rejected alternatives, and citing the definitions it was applying. Its mean process score rose by 0.31 on a five-point scale. Audited accuracy on the mechanical set was unchanged; audited accuracy on the judgment set fell from 71 to 58 per cent, and P's flag rate on judgment queries rose.

The question must require students to do four things, and I would like you to formulate the wording that elicits them cleanly:

1. Explain result (ii) as a predictable consequence of what each regime demands of its overseer, rather than as an implementation failure — stating exactly what an overseer must be able to do for a process score to carry information about correctness, and why that is easier to satisfy on the mechanical set than on the judgment set.
2. State a general condition, as precise as they can make it, under which process oversight should be expected to outperform outcome oversight, and show that their condition is not merely a restatement of "when the steps are checkable."
3. Distinguish three diagnoses of result (iii) — gaming of the process score; the trace ceasing to report the reasoning that actually produced the answer, so that oversight is aimed at a fiction; and an ordinary distribution shift with no bearing on oversight at all — say which the numbers best support, and specify a measurement that would settle the matter.
4. Evaluate a colleague's recommendation that the firm revert to outcome oversight, addressing whether her inference generalizes and what she must assume about the relationship between the auditors' verdicts and correctness for it to go through at all.

Please deliver: the question exactly as it should appear in the booklet, including mark allocations across the four parts; a model answer of about 900 words; and a marking scheme that names the specific moves each part rewards rather than paraphrasing the model answer back to me. The scheme should total 25, should state explicitly what separates a first-class answer from a competent but merely descriptive one, and should list five characteristic errors with the marks each costs and a one-line note on why the error is tempting. Do not include or reward references, reading lists, or named results from the literature: a student who cannot consult anything should still be able to reach full marks, and the model answer should be self-contained in the same way.
