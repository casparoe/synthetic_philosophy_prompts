Last month I accepted a research position in Baden-Württemberg, and my first payslip arrived with a line I had not anticipated: a church tax, withheld by the state tax office and remitted to a church I have never attended and, so far as I know, have no connection to whatsoever. The payroll office explained that the deduction follows from how I was registered on arrival, and that the only way to stop it is a formal declaration of exit before the local court, after which the church will treat me as having left its communion. Before deciding what to do, I wanted to understand what kind of arrangement I was actually living under, so I read a long comparative essay in a general-interest magazine — written by a well-regarded political commentator, in a publication I usually trust — that promised to sort the Western democracies into a small number of models. It said, in effect:

"Every Western democracy runs one of three systems: establishment, as in England; separation, as in France and the United States; or cooperation, as in Germany. In England the church is established, which is to say the sovereign is its head and freely appoints its bishops, its senior bishops sit and vote in the upper house of the legislature, and every English person is a member of that church by default unless they take positive steps to leave. France went the other way in 1905: since then the Republic has recognized, salaried, and subsidized no religion, which is why religious life there is confined to the private sphere, why conspicuous religious symbols are banned in public, and why no public money reaches any religious body. The United States has a wall of separation forbidding any public aid to religious schooling; the phrase comes from a Jefferson letter rather than from the Constitution, but the Supreme Court has enforced it steadily for the better part of a century. Germany is the confusing middle case: it has no state church, yet the state collects a religious tax and hands the proceeds to the two large churches, so Germany is an establishment in all but name, and the difference between a state that funds churches and a state that has a church is bookkeeping. What the comparison shows is that neutrality is binary — either the state stays out of religion or it does not — and every system that tries to straddle the line collapses into one side or the other under pressure. The only coherent model is therefore the French one; the American model is the French model with a slogan."

That typology is the one I had already absorbed from twenty years of American civics, and reading it made my deduction feel like a state subsidy to a church I never joined. Three things do not fit, though, and I cannot tell whether the misfit is in the essay or in my reading of my own payslip. First, nothing on that payslip resembles general revenue being transferred to a church; it looks like my own money, taken because I am registered as a member, at a percentage that my colleagues say differs by state, with the tax office apparently retaining a collection fee. Second, a French colleague of mine grew up near Strasbourg and describes religious instruction in her public school as entirely ordinary and state-organized, which the essay's account of France leaves no room for. Third, I follow American constitutional news closely enough to remember that over the past decade the Supreme Court has repeatedly required states to include religious schools in generally available funding programs, which is not what I would call a wall.

Could you audit the passage for me claim by claim? For each sentence, tell me what a specialist in comparative church-state relations would accept as a fair simplification, what would make them wince, and what is simply false. I particularly want to know whether the essay's central inference — that a state which collects a religious tax on behalf of churches thereby has an established church, and that the only difference is bookkeeping — rests on a real distinction or on a conflation of two different things, and what the German arrangement actually amounts to, so that I can judge whether my deduction is a subsidy I am being compelled to supply. Then tell me whether the closing argument survives the corrections: does the three-model typology hold up once the details are put back in, and is "neutrality is binary" a defensible claim or does it collapse several distinct questions into one? Please also flag which of the essay's errors would embarrass a scholar working in the field and which are ordinary journalistic compression.

I am not asking for sources or a reading list — I have no library access at the moment and would rather have your judgment on the claims themselves. But I do want you to be explicit about your confidence: separate what is settled and uncontroversial from what is genuinely disputed among people who work on this material, including where American doctrine is still in flux and where the characterization of French secularism is itself a live disagreement rather than a mere misdescription. If any of the specifics I have relayed touch on matters you are not sure of, say so instead of smoothing over the gap, and do not present a contested normative view — for instance about which model is really the neutral one — as though it were the professional consensus.
