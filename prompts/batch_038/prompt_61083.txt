I write about physics and philosophy for a general-interest science mag — long read up front, with a Q&A box running next to it. Next month I'm interviewing a mathematical physicist who defends what she calls a maximally psi-epistemic account of the quantum state, with retrocausality doing a lot of the work. Roughly: the wavefunction isn't a thing out there in the world, it tracks an agent's information about an underlying physical state, and that underlying state is partly fixed by which measurements get performed later on. She's actually published the model, so its a serious toy theory and not just vibes, and she's a very polished interviewee who has done this dance before. She has two go-to moves I want to stop her getting away with. One is retreating to "the psi-ontic/psi-epistemic thing is just a useful classification, don't take it too seriously," which dissolves my question instead of answering it. The other is saying the PBR theorem assumes what its trying to prove, and then moving right along.

Give me eight to ten questions I can ask on tape, in an order that builds, each with the follow-up I should fire for the answer she's most likely to give — not the answer she'd ideally give. I want coverage of: what her lambda actually is, and why calling the state "information about it" isn't just a hidden variable wearing a hat; whether she can keep preparation independence or has to surrender it, and what that does to her story about two separate labs; the Bell squeeze — nonlocality or backwards-in-time influence, she shouldn't get to duck both; where she breaks with the QBists, who also say the state is epistemic but think the whole ontological-models framework she's working inside is a mistake; whether her model is testable or is just rival bookkeeping that reproduces the same predictions forever; and a closing question about what observation would make her abandon the view.

Two constraints. Readers are bright but do no maths, so every question has to be askable in plain words — and for each one, tell me whether a general reader can follow it or whether it's really for the physicists in the audience. Also flag the traps honestly: which of these questions is only there to get me a quotable line, and where she could give an answer that's technically correct but would mislead a lay reader, so I know what to listen for.
