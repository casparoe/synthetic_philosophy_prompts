My mother retires at the end of term and there's a dinner for her on the 14th — about sixty people in the function room above a pub, her old colleagues and the students she supervised (a lot of them game theorists), plus my sister and me, my uncle Ray, and a load of neighbours who only know her as the woman who wins every dispute about watering rotations on the allotment. She has asked twice that nobody make speeches about her work, and she says she's had forty years of people explaining her own ideas back to her badly. The trouble is, the idea is the only thing about her job I actually understand, and its genuinely beautiful, and I don't think the room splits down the middle the way she's imagining. So I want to disobey her, but disobey precisely. I teach maths to fourteen-year-olds, so I've got the concept and not the vocabulary, which is sort of the point. She spent her career on the Kalai–Smorodinsky bargaining solution — not one of the two who came up with it, just the person who wouldn't let it stay a footnote. My dad, who died in March, was a Nash man, an applied economist, and their marriage was more or less the running argument between the two solution concepts: he thought the right deal is the one that maximises the product of what each of you gains over walking away, and she thought that's just a licence for one person's dream to crowd out the other's, and that fairness means each of you ending up with the same fraction of the best you could possibly have hoped for, measured up from wherever you'd land if you walked out instead. That's the bit I want in the speech, in language Ray can follow — no axioms, no Pareto, no utility functions, no equations, no chalkboard jokes. But it has to be correct, and precise enough that the economists at the top tables can hear that I've got the geometry, including the part where you draw the line from the walk-away point to each person's best case and then push along it as far as the two of you can jointly get, and including her favourite deflation of the whole thing: the two solutions give the same answer whenever the two people are exactly alike, which she said was the only reason anybody tolerated hers.

Two landmines. Her oldest sparring partner is coming, the one who spent thirty years telling her that her solution lets you win by putting an option on the table that nobody ever takes, because it changes what your best case looks like even though it's never chosen. I want him in the toast warmly, and I want it obvious that I'm not conceding the point on her behalf, but also obvious that I know exactly what he was objecting to and why it isn't stupid — it can't read as politeness about something I don't follow. And my sister still thinks our parents' marriage was a negotiation in the bad sense and that we were raised as bargaining positions; I'm not going to argue with her at a retirement dinner, but the speech shouldn't paper over her either.

The ending has to deal with March. The whole framework assumes there's some agreement both people would rather have than no agreement at all, and I want to say something true about what happens to a model like that when one side of it dies, without being sentimental or clever about it. Five or six minutes, ends with an actual toast and glasses going up. Don't make her a saint, and don't make my dad the punchline.
