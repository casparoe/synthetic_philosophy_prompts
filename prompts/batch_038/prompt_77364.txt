A foundation is funding a four-person, four-year conceptual research group — no new modeling, no new data — whose remit is to determine what survives of Popper's case against historicism now that there exist quantitative models of long-run societal dynamics: agent-based simulations of agrarian state collapse, statistical early-warning schemes fitted to centuries of demographic, fiscal, and conflict time series, and work of that kind. The deliverable is supposed to be arguments and conceptual clarifications that both modelers and philosophers can use.

Popper's case is not one argument but several: that a complete description of the whole of a society is impossible, so its future cannot be known; that history is a unique process, and what its study yields are trends, whose continuation is not guaranteed, rather than laws; that the growth of knowledge influences the course of history decisively and cannot itself be predicted in advance; and that historicist doctrines are so holistically framed and so readily adjusted that they escape refutation altogether.

Which two or three open questions should this group treat as its core agenda, and in what order? Defend the ordering rather than surveying the field: for each, say which of Popper's arguments it bears on, how much other work in the philosophy of the historical sciences turns on its answer, whether it can be advanced by argument and conceptual analysis within four years or would require new formal machinery, and what a settled answer would let working modelers do differently. Then identify which of the four arguments is the most overrated target — where a resolution would be philosophically tidy but practically inert — and say what result or development over the next few years would make you re-rank the list.
