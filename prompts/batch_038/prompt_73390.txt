You're a value theorist who's spent years on the metaphysics of pleasure — think of yourself as the person in the room who has to defend a substantive view, not just map the options. Here's the view I hold. Against the heterogeneity worry about pleasures — the classic pressure going back at least to Sidgwick that the cold-water-on-a-hot-day feeling, the feeling of a chord finally resolving, and the feeling of suddenly getting a proof have nothing introspectively in common, so "pleasure" can't name one simple feature of experience — I think all of those share a single hedonic tone. My gloss is specific though: the tone is a *determinable*, not a determinate. It's a dimension along which any experience can be scored, and it isn't introspectible in isolation, only contrastively — you catch it when it shifts, or when you compare two stretches of experience. So when critics say they can't find the common property, I say they're looking in the wrong category: they want a shared *look*, and there isn't one, but there is a shared way-of-being-felt. My best positive evidence is that the same sensory quality can flip from pleasant to unpleasant as satiety or context changes while the sensory character stays fixed — that seems to pull the tone apart from the vehicle it rides in.

I think it predicts that people can match the intensity of a taste pleasure against a music pleasure reasonably well, and that this matching capacity should survive damage that destroys specific sensory enjoyments (losing food pleasure through smell loss, say). So a real dissociation — matching intact while all the particular pleasures go, or the particulars intact while cross-kind matching goes — should count as evidence against me, not as noise. Does that hold up? And what does the tone view predict that attitudinal hedonism and desire-based accounts don't, in a way where the rivals could come out ahead?
