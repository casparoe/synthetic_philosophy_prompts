You are a historian of late-Victorian philosophy with specialist knowledge of Henry Sidgwick and of the early decades of the Society for Psychical Research. Before writing anything, spend a moment brainstorming privately: list the Sidgwick arguments you intend to rely on, and for each question below note at least two defensible answers and what would decide between them. Then write.

Set-up. Sidgwick was the SPR's first president, and the committee he chaired produced the Census of Hallucinations (1894): a mass questionnaire putting roughly 17,000 people the question whether they had ever had a vivid sensory impression of a person who was in fact absent, and then asking whether the number of such impressions coinciding with that person's death exceeded what chance alone would predict. He also wrote, in the 1870s controversy over the ethics of belief, about how much assent we owe a proposition given the evidence for it; and in *The Methods of Ethics* he left the dualism of practical reason unresolved, in part for want of any assurance that the universe is morally governed.

Now suppose Sidgwick were shown this field as it stands today: more than a century of continued investigation, culminating in preregistered, high-powered, multi-laboratory replications of precognition and "presentiment" experiments that return null results; a much-publicized precognition study published in a mainstream psychology journal in 2011 that several independent teams failed to reproduce; and a statistical apparatus — preregistration, power analysis, p-curve, bias detection, meta-analytic sensitivity to selective reporting — of which he had no inkling. Suppose further that a modern statistician tells him the Census was not merely underpowered but structurally confounded, since it could not exclude selective recall, retrospective reinterpretation, or ordinary family knowledge of a relative's illness.

Answer these:

1. How would Sidgwick absorb the reclassification of his own flagship study from positive evidence into invalid design? Separate the response that costs him nothing in his own evidential principles from the response that would force him to give something up.
2. Would he count the accumulated nulls as evidence against telepathy and against survival, or as a continued absence of evidence? His census was engineered to detect an excess over a chance baseline — say what that design implies about the force of a well-designed null, and whether he could consistently resist that implication in this case.
3. Which of the new tools would he adopt as continuous with his own method and which would he distrust? And would his impatience with a program that has failed for a century and a half be an evidentialist scruple or a utilitarian calculation about the cost of investigators' time?
4. If the survival hypothesis is now closed to him, what becomes of the dualism of practical reason — a permissible hope, an admitted tragedy, or some third position you can reconstruct out of his own commitments?

Constraints: no bibliographies, reading lists, or citations, and do not put words in Sidgwick's mouth with quotation marks unless you are certain of the exact wording — paraphrase instead. Flag each substantive claim as (a) grounded in something Sidgwick actually argued, (b) an extrapolation his framework licenses, or (c) your own speculation. Aim for 800–1000 words.
