I'm TA-ing an intro philosophy-of-math course and for the last session I want to hand out a poem about the axiom of choice that actually does some work instead of just name-dropping it. It's a two-part thing, so bear with the setup.

Write a diptych: two poems, 12 lines each, same voice, same rough meter, titled only I and II.

I is set in the ordinary universe, where the axiom is granted. The speaker is carrying out the Vitali construction, though you don't have to use that name: sort the real numbers into piles so that two numbers land in the same pile exactly when they differ by a fraction, then take one number out of every pile and gather the picks into a single set. I want the poem to feel those two halves separately — the sorting is harmless, describable, you can point at it; the taking is the part where there's no rule and nothing to say about how you picked, only that you did. Get the old socks-and-shoes contrast in there somewhere (Russell's bit: from infinitely many pairs of shoes you can just take the left one, from infinitely many pairs of socks you cant give any instruction at all). The piles here are socks. And the closing movement of I should be the payoff: the set you built has no length. Not a strange length — no length, measure just breaks on it.

II is set in a world where that construction cannot be carried out at all. Solovay showed in 1970, assuming a large enough cardinal, that there are models of set theory where dependent choice holds and every set of reals is Lebesgue measurable; in a world like that the speaker of I would go looking for the set and it wouldn't be there. This is the part I care most about and it's where most attempts go wrong: II is NOT a world where choosing is impossible. The speaker can still take one thing, then another, then another, can still follow a recipe countably far, can still build sequences. What's missing is only the single undescibable grab-one-from-each. So II should feel lossless in every local respect and hollow in exactly one global one.

Formally, and this is the constraint that carries the whole argument: II has to be built out of I's lines. Take I, strike exactly one line — the one where the picking happens — let everything below it move up to close the gap, and then write one new final line that could not have appeared in I. Twelve lines again. II still has to work as a complete poem, not as a puzzle with the answer torn out; it should read as though it always ended that way.

Two prohibitions: don't mention Banach-Tarski, and don't reach for "paradox" or "mind-bending." The strangeness has to come from the construction, not from adjectives. Someone who's never heard of the axiom of choice should get two good poems out of this; someone who has should be able to put a finger on the exact line where the axiom did its work.

After the poems, add two sentences telling me which line you struck and why that one.
