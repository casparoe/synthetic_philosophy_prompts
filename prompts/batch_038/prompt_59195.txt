Here's the distinction I think does all the work, and I want you to actually use it and sharpen it, not just restate it back at me prettier. Say a historical process is contingent at level 1 if the exact sequence of events could have been different — different coin flips, different order in which firms adopt, whatever. Say it's contingent at level 2 if which *outcome* you end up in could have been different, but the set of possible outcomes and what each one is like are fixed and known — you know there are two basins, you know their shape and their payoff, you just can't say in advance which one you'll fall into. And level 3 is when the set of outcomes itself, or the payoff structure that carves up the space into basins, could have been different. My claim: every famous path-dependence argument I can find only ever gets you to level 2. And level 2 contingency is completely compatible with a deterministic, lawlike, genuinely predictive theory, because the theory can still hand you the number of basins, their boundaries, and the probability distribution over which one you land in as a function of where you start. Which is literally everything a social scientist would want from a general theory. So "history matters" in the path-dependence sense isn't doing any of the anti-general-theory work that people load onto it.
