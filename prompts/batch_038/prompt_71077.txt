I have just taken over as convenor of the history advisory panel at a mid-sized trade publisher. Our terms of reference say that the panel exists to protect the credibility of the list without declining manuscripts merely because their conclusions are unfashionable; in practice we vet contested submissions and return one of three verdicts — publish, publish subject to conditions, or decline. The minutes record verdicts but no rule, and I would like to understand the standard we have actually been applying before I chair my first meeting.

You are, for this exercise, a philosopher of science with specialist interests in the demarcation problem, the epistemology of expert consensus, and the ethics of editorial gatekeeping, and you have sat on advisory boards of this kind. Please think the matter through in stages rather than answering directly, and show your reasoning at each stage, not only your conclusions. I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or a reading list — only reasoning about this record.

The eight cases from the last four years:

1. *Declined.* A geologist, working with a populariser, argued from weathering on a monument's enclosure wall that the monument predates by several millennia the dynasty universally credited with building it, and that a sophisticated pre-dynastic culture must therefore have existed. The panel noted that everything turned on a single line of physical evidence read against a densely stratified and independently dated archaeological context, and that the manuscript met the excavators' objections only by saying that they assumed what was in question.

2. *Declined.* A manuscript reading monumental iconography in four mutually unconnected cultures as depictions of visitors from elsewhere, and treating the absence of any corroborating artefact as itself evidence that the establishment had suppressed it.

3. *Declined.* A multi-volume work by a mathematician applying statistical tests to dynastic name-lists and to re-dated astronomical records, concluding that the accepted ancient history of the Mediterranean is a much later fabrication, duplicated from medieval events, and that the standard timeline was fixed by a handful of powerful institutions which specialists serve and cannot criticise.

4. *Published.* A prehistorian argued that a famous literary account of a sunken island kingdom is a distorted recollection of a real Late Bronze Age eruption and the collapse of the civilisation it damaged, and proposed a specific mechanism — a factor-of-ten error in the transmission of the numbers — for how the account became so inflated. The eruption itself is securely dated, and no accepted chronology moved.

5. *Published subject to conditions.* A manuscript arguing that a national museum had for a century attributed a celebrated find to the wrong excavation, and that the field's neglect of the discrepancy was self-interested. The author reproduced archival documents the museum could check. The panel accepted on condition that the charge of bad faith be restated as a charge of neglect, and that a museum specialist be invited to append a reply.

6. *Declined.* An amateur's re-reading of a well-excavated site, arguing that its main phase is eight centuries older than the excavators concluded, on the basis of published section drawings, an unpublished field diary, and stylistic comparison with neighbouring material. The manuscript cited the professional literature accurately, conceded every counter-argument it could not answer, framed its conclusion throughout as conjecture, and proposed no observation that could settle the question.

7. *Published.* A philologist argued that a single corrupted numeral in one recension of a chronicle had propagated into the standard date for a dynasty's foundation. She engaged directly with the astronomical record that appeared to contradict her, showed that all surviving copies of that record post-date the corruption she posited, and identified a class of dated legal documents from a named archive whose recovery would decide the matter.

8. *Published subject to conditions.* A manuscript arguing that a structure universally described as a cult building was in fact a storehouse and administrative archive. The panel accepted once the author agreed to present the case as a reinterpretation of the building's use, leaving its date and its builders untouched.

The case before us next month. An epigrapher with an unimpeachable record in her field argues that the anchor date for an entire regional chronology — the accession on which the synchronisms with three neighbouring chronologies are hung — is roughly 120 years too early, because a numeral was corrupted in one recension and every later chronicle inherits the error. She states the two findings that would refute her. She reproduces her strongest critics' arguments at length and credits them by name. She offers to let a rival specialist write a closing chapter against her. And she specifies a test: dated contracts and grain receipts in three temple archives should show a break exactly where her re-dating places a period of disruption.

Two features complicate it. First, the astronomical observation traditionally used to confirm the accepted date survives only in copies made after the corruption she posits, and she treats it as an interpolation by the same scribes; beyond the lateness of the copies she has no manuscript evidence that any such interpolation occurred. Second, her revision does not stay local. Moving the accession moves the pottery cross-dates and the treaty synchronisms on which the chronologies of three neighbouring states are themselves anchored. In her final chapter she remarks that the accepted date is load-bearing for a thousand textbooks and that nobody's career is served by questioning it.

What I want from you:

(a) Begin by brainstorming. List at least six candidate decision rules that could account for all eight verdicts, each stated precisely enough that some precedent would falsify it if it did not fit. Include rules you think are wrong — I want the space mapped, not filtered.

(b) Test each rule against the record. For each, say which precedent, if any, strains or breaks it, and what minimal repair would save it; flag any repair that looks like it was written to fit one case. Also tell me which of the eight precedents are doing real work and which are consistent with almost anything.

(c) For every rule that survives (b), state the verdict it implies for the new manuscript, including conditions if the rule implies them. Treat the closing-chapter remark about textbooks and careers as a datum the conspiracy-premise considerations must handle, not as rhetorical decoration.

(d) Give me your single best prediction of what the panel will decide, with a rough confidence, and explain why you prefer that rule to the runner-up.

(e) Identify the closest analog among the eight and the disanalogy that, in your view, actually decides the case.

(f) Design one further manuscript — described as concretely as I have described these — that would most sharply separate your two leading rules, and say what verdict each rule predicts for it. This is the one I most want, because I can put it to the panel at the same meeting.

(g) If you conclude that the record genuinely underdetermines the new case, say which principle the panel would be committing itself to by publishing and which by declining, and which commitment you would advise for a panel operating under the remit quoted above.
