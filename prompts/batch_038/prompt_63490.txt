I'm a second-year PhD student and I have to hand my supervisor a proposal next Tuesday for a chapter-sized project — call it 8,000 words, written over one semester — in a philosophy-of-economics sequence. The topic I'm circling is the common prior assumption, and my worry is that the arguments for and against it never actually connect, because the phrase is doing at least three seperate jobs and people pick whichever job suits them mid-argument. As I have it: (1) the analyst's prior over the players' types, which is really just a bookkeeping device for the analyst's own ignorance about which situation is being modelled, so that "different priors" would mean different information rather than different beliefs; (2) a structural coherence property of a type space — the requirement that a whole profile of mutual beliefs be generated from one shared starting measure by conditioning — which is the thing you can actually prove or fail to prove about a given model, and which some model-builders deliberately arrange not to hold; (3) a normative claim about what ideally rational agents would converge on if they'd been given the same evidence, which is the claim that Aumann-style agreement results seem to get pressed into service for. My hunch is that the "you can't test priors, they aren't observable" objections bite on (1), the "but real people keep disagreeing even after they've talked" objections bite on (3), and the technical results about spaces that admit no common prior are about (2) — and almost nobody ever says which one they mean, so the debate looks intractable when it's really just under-specified.
