Bit of an odd request for this list, but I was supposed to be marking essays this weekend and instead I've been stewing on something that came up in my seminar on Thursday. I co-run a reading group with the cognitive neuroscience lot here, and every single term we hit the same wall around week five, when someone drags in Hofstadter's careenium-and-simmballs picture — roughly, the brain as a seething substrate of neurons (the careenium, in his coinage) in which symbols are the patterns that bounce around and, importantly, bounce off one another, so that a symbol can be what makes other symbols move. Half the room loves this because it looks like it hands mental content genuine causal oomph; the other half, led by one of our more pugnacious neuroscientists, mutters some version of 'the symbols are nothing over and above the firing, so nothing at the symbol level ever pushed anything around in the history of anything' and considers the topic closed. He said it again on Thursday, at a first-year who'd argued that my believing the kettle is on explains my getting up in a way the neural narrative simply doesn't, and the poor kid had nothing to say back.

I've told her she's entitled to push back, but I'd rather hand her something better than vibes. What I want is a menu — not an essay defending one line — of the different precise claims that 'nothing over and above' could be standing for. Off the top of my head I can see at least an identity claim, a supervenience claim, a realisation claim, something about mereological composition, and something that isn't metaphysical at all but about whether the higher-level description could be scrapped without explanatory loss; there are probably others, and I suspect a couple of my candidates collapse into each other once you state them carefully rather than gesture at them. For each reading I'd like: a reasonably explicit statement of what it claims; whether it's a platitude nobody disputes, substantive but defensible, or just false; and — the bit I actually need — whether it licenses the move from 'symbols are nothing over and above firing' to 'so symbol-level talk has no causal work to do'. My hunch is that only one of the readings gets him that conclusion and that it's the least plausible of the lot, and that he's sliding between an innocent metaphysical reading and a strong causal one, but I want the slide identified joint by joint rather than merely alleged, because he isn't stupid and he will not be fobbed off with 'you're equivocating'.

Two more things, then I'll stop. First, the first-year's argument actually turned on a distinction I hadn't thought about and I'm not sure it's coherent: she said that some symbol-level facts are the sort that could have been realised by quite different neural goings-on, whereas others — her belief about the kettle, in her head, on that particular Thursday afternoon — are as a matter of fact bolted to exactly one neural pattern, and she thought the second kind might escape whatever trouble the first kind is in. Tell me whether the menu of readings should be indexed to cases like that, i.e. whether 'nothing over and above' could plausibly be true-for-one-sort-of-symbol-fact and false-for-another, or whether that's just a confusion dressed up as a subtlety. Second, say which reading you think people in this kind of argument usually have in mind, and whether there's any respectable route at all from the metaphysical readings to the causal one — the worry being that if the neurons already fully account for the effect on the occasion in question, the pattern-level fact is left with nothing to do; I want that worry spelled out and then assessed, not merely named. Please write the whole thing in British English, it's going straight onto the reading pack, so 'realise', 'behaviour', and so on, and keep the notation light enough that a first-year can follow it without a glossary.
