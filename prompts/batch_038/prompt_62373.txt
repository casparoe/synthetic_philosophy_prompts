I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I am three weeks from filing a long feature on suffering-focused ethics — the family of views holding that the relief of pain and the production of pleasure do not sit on a single scale. The most technically interesting member of the family is sometimes called lexical threshold negative utilitarianism, and the formulation I have been working with says: there is some level of lifetime suffering, call it the line, such that no distribution of well-being across any number of other people, however vast, outweighs one person's being at or above the line; below the line, ordinary trade-offs resume, and any amount of well-being can outweigh any amount of suffering. I spent a weekend at a population-ethics workshop trying to pin down whether the line can be drawn anywhere at all, and afterward I sketched an argument of my own to test it. The argument has gotten away from me. It now appears to establish not that the line is in the wrong place but that no line can exist, and then — in its last step — that no normative theory of any subject can contain a lexical cutoff. That second result is plainly too strong, and I cannot tell whether the argument is invalid or valid and expensive. Since my piece turns on the answer, I would rather settle it now than discover it in galleys.

Here it is, numbered so you can point at a single step.

1. Suppose a line t exists with both properties: (a) some distribution of well-being among others outweighs any lifetime suffering below t, and (b) no distribution of well-being among others outweighs any lifetime suffering at or above t.
2. Lifetime suffering comes in grades along a continuum, and for any grade there is another so close to it that no one — not the person living it, not the most sensitive observer one can describe — could detect the difference even in principle.
3. Let s⁻ be a grade just below t and s⁺ a grade just above it. By (2), they differ undetectably.
4. If some distribution of well-being outweighs s⁻, then some distribution outweighs s⁺; for the smallest distribution that tips the balance against s⁻ would have to fail against s⁺ only if the difference between them made a difference to the balance, and a difference no one could detect cannot make a difference to anything.
5. By (1a), some distribution outweighs s⁻.
6. By (4), some distribution outweighs s⁺ — contradicting (1b).
7. Nothing in steps 2 through 6 constrained where t sat, so no candidate line survives: there is no grade of suffering immune to being outweighed.
8. Accordingly either every grade is outweighable or none is, and since a dentist's drill is outweighable by enough well-being elsewhere, every grade is: pleasure and pain share one scale with no lexical breaks anywhere on it.
9. Steps 2 through 7 never mentioned welfare. Substitute any dimension along which a theory posits a cutoff — the priority some theories of justice give basic liberties over distributive advantages, the boundary between persons and non-persons, the division between compensable and uncompensable harm — and the reasoning runs unchanged. No theory can contain a lexical threshold anywhere.

One philosopher at the workshop told me, without elaborating, that I had merely dressed a sorites in welfare-economics clothing, which has not helped. Step 9 is what actually frightens me, because I know respectable theories that contain lexical priorities and I do not believe anyone has refuted them with a maneuver this cheap; yet I also cannot locate a premise that is specific to suffering, which is what step 9 needs. Step 4 still feels obviously right to me, and I would like to keep it if I can. Step 8 is where I stopped being able to audit my own work, since I no longer know whether the alternatives I listed there are the only ones on offer. What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a defense of lexical views in general and not a tour of the positions. Name the single step you would reject and explain why it looked innocent when I wrote it down — I want to feel the pull it exerts, not merely be told it fails. Then price the rejection: what does the theorist who gives up that step owe us, separately in the welfare case and in the step 9 cases, where I suspect the price differs. Finally, tell me whether a repaired version survives. If you patch the argument so it no longer proves the absurd general claim, is anything left that bears on my real question — whether the relief of suffering and the production of well-being can ever be traded against one another on a common scale, or whether that trade is a category mistake? And if your verdict is instead that the argument is valid and a premise simply has to go, rank the candidate premises by cost and tell me which goes cheapest.
