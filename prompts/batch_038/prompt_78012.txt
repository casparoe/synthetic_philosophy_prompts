I have been four years at work on a novel whose second half turns on a memoirist being found out, and to keep the character's remorse from arriving as cheaply as my agent says it currently does, I have been reading in the philosophy of personal identity — specifically Dennett's proposal that the self is a center of narrative gravity, an abstraction posited by a life's story in something of the way a physicist posits the center of gravity of an irregular body: nothing you could touch, yet indispensable and, for purposes of explanation and prediction, entirely real. My difficulty is that the analogy appears to license precisely the conduct my readers are meant to find monstrous. If a self is constituted by the narrative rather than by the facts the narrative reports, then the fabulist has not failed to be a self; he has assembled one from different material, and the objection to him becomes a question of genre rather than of metaphysics.

Rather than argue the point with you in the abstract, I want to work through the case that put it in my head: James Frey's *A Million Little Pieces*, published in 2003 and sold as a memoir, taken up by Oprah's Book Club in the autumn of 2005, and then, in early January 2006, dismantled by a six-week investigation at The Smoking Gun showing that the arrest record, the jail time, and much of the criminal history on which the book's authority rested were inventions. Frey's first reply was that the book remained essentially true in substance even where particulars had been altered; later that month, on the same programme, he conceded the fabrications, the host told him she felt deceived, and the publisher's response included offering refunds to purchasers and, in due course, re-presenting the identical text as fiction.

Please give me exactly five numbered points, one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion, no survey of personal-identity theory, and no retelling of the scandal, which I know as well as you do:

1. The mapping. Which item here is the narrative, which is the self, and what plays the part of the calculation that yields the center of gravity — the text, the marketing category, the readers' uptake, or all three simultaneously?
2. Whether the theory can account for the audience's sense of betrayal, or whether betrayal is an anomaly the analogy cannot absorb: if there was never a factual self to misrepresent, who was wronged, and by what?
3. The re-shelving. The sentences did not change when the book became fiction, so say whether, on this view, a center of gravity moved at all, and if it moved, what did the moving.
4. Where the analysis falls silent or misdescribes. I especially want your verdict on a term I keep using in my notes without confidence — narrative overdraft, a self posited by a story that spends against a factual credit line it does not possess. Can the theory cash it out, or does the notion dissolve and leave only a breach of contract?
5. What the episode does to the theory: confirmation, anomaly, or evidence that it was never addressed to cases in which narrator, author, and commodity are the same person — and if the last, what the case reveals about what the analogy had been doing all along.
