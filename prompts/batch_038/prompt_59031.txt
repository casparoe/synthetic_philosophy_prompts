You're going to play the role of a philosopher of language who has also spent a few years inside a real trust & safety org — someone who knows both the technical arguments and how policy documents actually get written and enforced. Context: I'm a backend engineer at a mid-size social app (~40m users). I have no philosophy training at all, so explain things in plain language, but don't dumb down the reasoning. I got roped onto our "policy language working group" because I own the enforcement pipeline that parses these rules and turns them into routing decisions. There's twelve of us, we meet thursdays, and I've been asked to circulate a short written recommendation before the next one. Here's the situation.

Our policy lead — I'll call her Priya — wants to rewrite the entire community guidelines so that no rule contains a single evaluative word. Her argument: words like "degrading," "harassing," "manipulative" aren't describing anything out there in the world, they're just us saying "boo" in a tone of voice that impersonates a finding of fact. Since nobody can settle what's actually degrading, we shouldn't smuggle our disapproval into the doc as if it were a discovery. So every criterion becomes a "rater-checkable descriptor" — e.g. "post contains a term from list S," "post names a private figure," "post asserts a checkable claim about a named person without a source" — and we only keep a descriptor if our pilot hits inter-rater agreement (kappa) above 0.8. Legal likes it too: a guideline that never says anything is bad is a guideline that can't be read back to us as an admission of a standard we failed to meet.

For maybe 60% of the rules this works fine. Where it falls apart is that almost none of our interesting rules are single descriptors. They're all logic built on top of descriptors. Our current rule 4.2 is roughly: if a post mocks someone for a characteristic they had no hand in choosing, and the target is a private figure, escalate to human review. Try stating that with descriptors only and you either end up with an enumerated list of forty concrete cases (which is exactly the slur-list problem — it always misses the next one, and raters fall apart the moment something's off-list), or you keep a phrase like "mocks for a characteristic they had no hand in," which is just the evaluative thing wearing a trenchcoat.

Second thing that bugs me and I can't articulate it: my pipeline is conditionals and disjunctions all the way down. If "degrading" isn't stating anything and is only expressing our attitude, what is "if degrading AND target is private figure, then escalate" even saying? It feels like you can't put a boo inside an if-clause. Same with negation — we have rules that say something is NOT covered, and I don't know what the descriptor-only version of "this does not count as demeaning" would be. And weirdly, raters agree with each other on the atomic cases and disagree precisely on the compound ones, which is definately the opposite of what Priya's scheme predicts.
