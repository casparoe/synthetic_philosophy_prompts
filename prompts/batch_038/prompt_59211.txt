You are a philosopher of cognitive science with working knowledge of both the linguistics of language acquisition and contemporary machine learning, and you have taught contested technical material to smart, skeptical, non-philosopher audiences before. Please think carefully before answering, and treat what follows as a design problem rather than an exposition problem: I do not want the argument explained to me, and I do not want an essay on nativism versus empiricism. I want a teaching plan.

Context. I run an internal seminar series at an industrial machine learning lab, and in three weeks I have seventy-five minutes on the argument from the poverty of the stimulus. Whiteboard only — no slides. The room will hold roughly twenty-five people who build, evaluate, and ship large models; essentially none of them have taken a linguistics course, though several have undergraduate-level exposure to philosophy of mind and all of them are fluent in the vocabulary of priors, inductive bias, sample complexity, and generalization. They will arrive having read the standard takes to the effect that a model acquiring grammar from raw text settles the matter, and most of them hold some loose version of the view that the debate is finished and the empiricists won.

Here is the complication that should shape your whole plan: I am not a nativist. I do not accept the argument's conclusion. But my aim for the session is not to win an argument either. The target outcome is that they leave able to state the argument precisely, locate its actual pressure points rather than the ones that merely sound good, and explain why pointing at a large language model is not by itself a refutation — while also being able to say what such a model does bear on. Teaching an argument you reject, to an audience that has already rejected it for different and worse reasons, is the hard part; assume that if I let the session drift into a debate, everyone simply entrenches.

Please give me the following.

First, a short diagnostic pass. What does this specific audience already possess that I can lean on, what does that prior knowledge make easy to get across, and — more important — what does it make predictably hard, because their existing framework supplies a tempting but wrong slot for each piece of the argument? Do not write this as a survey; write it as a list of assets and liabilities with the liabilities tied to particular steps of the argument.

Second, the order of ideas. Lay out the sequence in which the components must be introduced, with approximate minutes and what each step depends on. I specifically want your judgment on: whether to establish the positive-evidence-only point before or after the underdetermination point; whether the degeneracy and variability of child-directed input earns its own step or is a footnote; where, if anywhere, a Gold-style identification-in-the-limit result belongs, and what it costs me to include it; and whether the whole session should be framed from the start by the language-model comparison or whether that comparison must be held back until the argument stands on its own. Defend the ordering, not just list it.

Third, the carrying example. I was inclined to build everything on the yes–no question case: a declarative whose subject contains a relative clause with its own copula, and the fact that a competent speaker fronts the main-clause copula rather than the first one encountered in the linear string. Tell me whether that is the right vehicle, what the example must have in order to do the work and what it must lack, how long I can afford to spend on it, and what I lose if I use it. Then tell me what the alternative vehicles cost — an anaphora case where a pronoun may or may not take an antecedent depending on structural position, a raising or passive alternation where a linear-order rule predicts the wrong sentence — and pick one for me, explaining the choice.

Fourth, the misconception map. I already expect two, and I want to know exactly where in the sequence to head each off rather than rebut it at the end: the curve-fitting move, on which the audience concludes that since no child ever heard the relevant construction the case is settled; and the priors move, on which the audience concludes that since every learner has inductive bias the argument is empty and unfalsifiable. Add the ones I have not anticipated — I suspect something about treating universal grammar as a grammar written into the genome, something about equating the absence of teaching with the absence of data, and something about scale being treated as automatically dispositive. For each, give the point of maximum vulnerability in my sequence and one or two sentences I can say there that defuse it preemptively.

Fifth, the check. One question I can put to the room in the last five minutes that discriminates between having understood the argument and having merely formed an opinion about it. Say what a good answer contains and what a bad-but-plausible answer sounds like.

Sixth, what I should refuse to cover and announce that I am refusing: the internal machinery of parameter setting, the Minimalist Program, the evolutionary question, and whatever else would eat the clock. For each, give me the sentence that closes the door without sounding evasive.

Do not give me readings, references, or a bibliography — I am not distributing anything, and the plan has to stand on its own in the room. Keep it concrete: minutes, board layout where it matters, and the actual sentences I would say at the joints.
