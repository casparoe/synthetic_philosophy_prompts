Take the ELIZA episode of 1966 and run it through Dennett's counterfeit people framework. Specifically the Weizenbaum bit: his secretary — who knew perfectly well it was a program he'd built, and had watched him test it — asked him to leave the room so she could talk to it privately. Weizenbaum was rattled enough by that to build a chunk of his mid-70s book around the claim that very short exposure to a simple program can induce powerful delusional thinking in quite normal people. I've read the counterfeit-people stuff and the *From Bacteria to Bach and Back* version of it, and I know the usual gloss on this case — ELIZA effect, intentional stance, moving on. What I want is the careful mapping, and then an honest account of what the theory can't touch.

Here's the wrinkle. For Dennett a counterfeit person is worth worrying about because of the competence: comprehension-less competence, harvested from the accumulated products of human comprehension, and that's what makes the surface behavior person-like and what licenses the attribution of an inner life. ELIZA had almost none of that. It was keyword spotting plus a Rogerian therapist script, and Weizenbaum picked the therapist role precisely so he wouldn't have to give it a knowledge base. So it looks like a cheap counterfeit — one that buys the personhood illusion without buying the competence — and I don't think his account has a slot for that category. Work it through: does the framework have to say there was no counterfeit person in that room, just an over-eager stance-taker, and is that a cost or a feature? Where does the intentional-stance story actually explain the secretary, given that the stance is supposed to get adopted because it pays off predictively, and here she knew there was nothing to predict and engaged anyway? Does a belief-as-disposition view swallow this too easily, to the point where being taken in and not being taken in stop being different states?

Then the other direction, which is the part I care most about: what does 1966 do to the theory as applied to chatbots now. If the illusion can be manufactured by turn-taking, first-person grammar and a deferential register alone, is competence the right risk variable, or is Dennett grading systems on the wrong scale entirely — in which case does "counterfeit person" stop doing critical work, since it would apply just as well to a 200-line script as to a frontier model? Related: the DOCTOR script is arguably a counterfeit of a role that was already engineered to be content-free, a fake of a fake, and I can't tell whether that has any place in his picture or whether it dissolves the whole category.

Tell me which features of the episode the theory explains, which it merely redescribes, and where it goes quiet. And if some other account does better — Weizenbaum's own moral reading, or a Turkle-style relational one where the interesting thing is what the encounter does to the human rather than what the machine is — say so plainly instead of reconciling everything into a synthesis. Take the facts as I've given them; I'm not interested in litigating the anecdote, and definately not in whether she was really his secretary or whether the story got polished in the retelling.
