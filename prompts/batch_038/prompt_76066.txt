Every year I teach a seminar on prediction and long-run claims, and every year it collapses into the same fight. This year I've decided to make the two loudest people in it actually produce something, and I need a model of the document I want from them.

Setup. One side is a systems modeler in the tradition of the 1972 world model — stock-and-flow, feedback loops, the baseline scenario everyone calls the standard run, overshoot-and-decline rather than a cliff. Her position: the structure of that model has been corroborated by four decades of data on population, industrial output, resource extraction and persistent pollution, and the reason the turn hasn't arrived yet is that the turn is supposed to arrive later. Being early is not the same as being wrong. Other side is a resource economist of the cornucopian persuasion. His position: any model without prices and substitution in it is missing the one mechanism that has empirically kept scarcity from translating into collapse; real commodity prices fell across most of the last forty years, which is evidence against depletion-driven decline; and "right but early" is what people say when a theory has stopped being testable.

They have been talking past each other for four years, and I think I finally see why. The same datum does double duty. Falling metal prices are, for him, proof that substitution works; for her they are exactly what her model's endogenous technology feedback produces, so the observation doesn't discriminate between them at all. And the one case they'll both nominally accept as a real test — the Simon–Ehrlich wager, five metals, real terms, 1980 to 1990, Ehrlich lost on all five — has been re-litigated by a study that re-ran every possible ten-year window across the twentieth century and found the economist would have won maybe 38 percent of them, with 1980–1990 landing among the worst windows for the other side. So now they're fighting about windows, which is progress of a sort.

What I need from you is the joint statement of an adversarial collaboration between these two: not a transcript, not a verdict on who's right, and not a diagnosis of why they're stuck. A constructive document both of them could put their names to, with the residual disagreement made exact. Write it.

Contents, in this order:

1. Numbered agreed claims. Non-vacuous ones only — if a claim is one that any two sane people would sign, cut it. Include at minimum: what the model actually does (and specifically what "collapse" means inside it, since that's a word they use differently), what the historical record up to now does and doesn't show, what the wager settled and what it didn't, and whether "the model predicted X by year Y" is even the right logical form for these claims.

2. A disagreements table with exactly these columns: `id` | `dispute` (one neutral sentence) | `modeler_wording` (a sentence stating her view that she would sign) | `economist_wording` (a sentence stating his view that he would sign) | `kind` (genuine / verbal / mixed; if verbal or mixed, name the word doing the damage and propose a neutral replacement both would accept) | `crux` (which crux this row turns on) | `shared_test` (the observation, case, or argument both accept as bearing on it) | `modeler_prediction` | `economist_prediction` | `resolution_horizon` (the year by which we'd know).

3. The part I care most about, one item per side. I've been pushing a rule I'll call the precursor requirement: if a mechanism is supposed to produce a visible turn decades out, something should already be moving now — a measurable precursor you can name and check. For her, give me the precursor: the specific observable already going the way her mechanism implies (something in the neighborhood of falling energy or capital return per unit of extraction effort, or a rising share of output spent just holding the line), plus how much of it we should already have seen if she's right. For him, give me the mirror image — not a precursor but a kill condition: the specific observation over a specific window that would make him say the price-and-substitution mechanism has limits and the modelers were onto something. Both have to be written so that failing to find them would mean something.

4. A classification of each side's defensive moves. Take the actual moves they make — appealing to a different window, adding a feedback loop, re-reading the same data, saying the horizon was misunderstood, saying the basket was badly chosen — and sort each into (a) a new auxiliary hypothesis with independently testable content, (b) a parameter or structural adjustment internal to the model, or (c) a redescription that saves the claim without exposing it to anything. Be even-handed. Both sides have type-(c) moves and I want them named.

5. Where the fight is only about words. Tell me whether "collapse," "falsified," and "right but early" mean the same thing to each of them, and separate the genuinely empirical residue from the definitional mess. If you end up concluding that almost nothing survives as a real disagreement, say so and say why — that's an acceptable answer, just not a comfortable one.
