I need your help planning — not writing — a ninety-minute training evening for volunteers, and I should explain at the outset why the subject has ceased to be academic for me.

Twelve years before her diagnosis, my mother executed a directive covering the state she is now in. It is unremarkable in its main provisions, but it ends with a clause she was evidently proud of, which anticipated the objection her present condition would raise: if she should later seem content and ask to be kept alive, that request was to carry no weight, because it would come from someone who no longer held her values. She is now that someone. She is contented, she asks for breakfast, she is frightened of nothing in particular, and she cannot tell you why any of this matters. My siblings and I have stopped agreeing about whether the clause settles the question or merely performs it.

I volunteer with a hospice service, and next month I run the ethics evening for the incoming cohort: roughly twenty people, none with any philosophy, several of them recently bereaved, all of whom will arrive with a private version of this already in progress. I have chosen the concept deliberately. Not the legality of anything, and not the general question of when treatment may be withdrawn, but the narrower puzzle underneath both: who is speaking when a directive is read aloud, whether the authority of a decision survives the decision-maker, and what an advance disavowal of one's own future dissent could possibly add to the document it appears in.

What I want from you is the architecture of the session:

- The order in which the components must be introduced, and what each one depends on. My suspicion is that the room cannot perceive that there is a puzzle at all until something prior is in place; I want to know what that prior thing is, and specifically what will collapse if I bring out my mother's final clause before it.
- The single case that should carry the evening — mine, an invented one, or a third I have not considered — together with what that case must contain and what it must conspicuously lack in order to do the work.
- The misreadings I should expect, in the order they will surface, and the point in the sequence at which each needs to be headed off rather than argued down. I can predict two myself: that the whole issue is about memory, and that the contented present self wins by default because she is the one still in the room. I expect there is a third and more damaging one peculiar to a hospice audience, and I would like you to name it before I encounter it.
- One question I can put to the room near the end whose answers will distinguish genuine uptake from a group that has simply been moved.
- What I should refuse to teach at this level, and how to decline it without appearing to dodge.

One constraint I ask you to treat as load-bearing rather than as colour. I am not a disinterested instructor: this is my mother's document and my family's dispute. Tell me where in the sequence that fact should be disclosed, how much of it the evening can bear before the room stops thinking and starts looking after me, and what I should do at the moment — it will come — when somebody says the thing my brother says. Please do not offer readings or references; nobody there will read anything, and I would rather you did not pretend otherwise.
