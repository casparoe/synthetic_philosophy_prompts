I'm co-running a one-day public humanities workshop next month, working title "Judging the Dead," and the room is going to be a wierd mix: about 25 people, half of them local-history volunteers and county archivists, a handful of philosophy grad students, and a few who mostly come for the free lunch. In our planning calls the same fight keeps breaking out, and I've come to think it's actually three different fights in a trenchcoat. Some people mean a conceptual question: can you even describe what a seventeenth-century estate owner did to his dependents without importing categories nobody in his world possessed? Others mean a normative one: even granting that you can describe it, are you entitled to blame the man, given that he did what everyone around him did and what the best thinking of his own tradition endorsed? And a third group means something purely practical: whatever the verdict, does the hospital keep his name over the door, does the statue stay, do we still assign the text. I want an instrument that separates those, because at the moment everyone answers a different question and concludes the others are stupid.

So please write me a sorting quiz — ten questions, each one a concrete case with two or three answer options — plus a decision-tree rendering of the same material and a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves. Every question has to be answerable by someone who has never heard any of the labels; nothing may be a label in disguise (no "do you think presentism is wrong?" and no question whose wording tips you off which camp an answer belongs to). The cases have to carry the sorting weight, so at minimum include: an ancient slaveholder who never registers a qualm anywhere in the surviving record; a mid-nineteenth-century surgeon whose non-consensual experiments were already being denounced by a vocal minority of his own colleagues; a 1950s psychiatrist prescribing what we now call conversion therapy, in line with the mainstream of his profession; a medieval inquisitor who is sincere, careful, and scrupulous about the procedural rules internal to his own tradition; one case where the ignorance looks self-made — the actor read the abolitionist pamphlet, wrote a marginal note dismissing it, and carried on; and one symmetry case in which the participant has to say what would make it fair for our own great-great-grandchildren to condemn us for something we currently cannot see at all. I'd also like at least one question that forces a choice between what the historian is permitted to believe and what she's permitted to publish, since a lot of people happily judge in the study and refuse to put it in the book, and one that pins down whose standards are doing the work — the described community's or the describer's.

The leaves I want to end up with, roughly: the no-discount view, where the historian applies the morality she actually lives by and says so; the understand-don't-blame contextualist; the split view (judgment legitimate, but quarantined out of historical description) and its mirror image if you think that's a real position — verdicts on the page, no moralizing voice behind them; the culpable-ignorance view, where the discount only covers ignorance that wasn't self-made, so it excuses the conformist but not the man with the marginal note; the two-tier view where we may judge but not enact, so the condemnation stands and the building keeps its name; the symmetry view, on which any rule you endorse has to be one you'd accept having applied to you from the future; and the deflationary case-by-case view that "the past" isn't a single thing and there's no general answer to give. For each leaf, one sentence on its most awkward commitment — the thing whoever lands there is going to have to swallow. Then the bit I care about most: lay out the full grid of answers to the three questions I described at the top, tell me which cells are empty and why, distinguishing cells that are incoherent from cells that are merely unattractive from ones that are just unexplored and somebody should write the paper on them. And flag the pairs of leaves your tree can't really separate — I already suspect "he didn't know better so he isn't to blame" and "blame is anachronistic so don't" will come out identical on a tick-box questionnaire, and if that's true I'd much rather you said so than dressed it up. No citations or reading list, I'll add my own pointers afterwards, but if you attach a person's name to a leaf, describe the view in enough detail that someone who's never heard of them still gets sorted correctly. Two sides of A4 if you can manage it, questions first and key after, so nobody can see the scoring while they're answering.
