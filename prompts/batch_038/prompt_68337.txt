I am a novelist at work on a book whose central character is a proof theorist in her late sixties whom a machine-learning laboratory has invited to have her reasoning "profiled" against a model of it. A long scene turns on her delivering, from a lectern, a compressed version of the Gödelian anti-mechanist argument; before I let a real logician in her audience take it apart, I need to know precisely where it is vulnerable.

Please act as a professional philosopher with expertise in mathematical logic and the philosophy of mind, and in particular in the Lucas–Penrose line of argument and the standard replies to it. Think carefully, and begin by sketching in a few lines the range of readings the speech could bear before you settle on any; then work only from what I supply here together with your own understanding, and do not offer references, sources, or a reading list.

Here is the passage as it now stands in my draft. It is my own prose, not a quotation from any published work, and you should not try to trace it to one:

> Consider any machine you please: a device whose output is fixed by a specification, which specification is a formal system — call it M. If M is consistent, then there is a sentence of arithmetic, G(M), that M cannot produce as a theorem; and yet I, inspecting M, am able to recognize G(M) as true. The arithmetical sentences I recognize are therefore not the arithmetical sentences M recognizes, and my powers exceed its. But M was arbitrary, chosen by you and not by me; so no machine whatever captures what I do in judging. You will reply that I too am consistent with some system, and perhaps I am — but then you must produce that system, and once produced it falls to the same stroke, while I step outside it and see the truth of the sentence it cannot prove. The gap lies not in what is provable but in what is recognizable, and recognition is no rule.

Against that passage, please do the following:

1. Reconstruct the speech as an explicit list of premises and conclusion, and mark every place where the character silently relies on something she has not said — especially anything turning on the difference between mere consistency, ω-consistency, and soundness, and anything turning on what she is entitled to know about herself as against what she knows about the machine.
2. State what her claim to "recognize" G(M) as true really requires of her, and whether that same requirement could not equally be met by whoever builds and inspects M — that is, whether the speech establishes a difference in kind or only a difference in who happens to be doing the inspecting.
3. Give me the two or three objections a competent member of that audience could raise which the speech as written cannot answer, and for each a revision of no more than two sentences that would let her answer it or concede it with dignity. At least one of those revisions must cost her something a novelist would rather keep, and you should say plainly what the cost is and what she loses as a character by paying it.
4. Tell me what the speech commits her to in the rest of the book: whether she must treat human reasoning as idealized — unbounded in time, free of slips — whether she can also hold that she is a physical system, and which of her other convictions become unstable once she has said this aloud.

Where the prose admits two readings, evaluate both rather than choosing one silently. Be rigorous and concrete; I care more about accuracy than about sympathy for my character.
