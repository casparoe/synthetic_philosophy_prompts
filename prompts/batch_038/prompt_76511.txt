Six weeks ago I became the general manager of the worker cooperative where I have been a hand binder for nine years: thirty-eight member-owners, a commercial printing and finishing shop, and a staged buy-in under which new members purchase their share through payroll deduction over four years. In November the assembly faces a budget vote, an automation proposal, and a financing decision, and there is a standing dispute about our non-member workers that any of the three will reopen. I have found that I cannot argue about any of them without appealing to a set of convictions I have never properly checked. So I would like an audit before I put anything in writing for the membership.

Here is what I hold — principles where I can manage them, verdicts where I cannot.

1. Exploitation does not depend on a labor theory of value. I do not believe there is a determinate fact about how much value a worker creates, and I do not need one. What makes a wage relation exploitative is that one party's advantage is traceable to an unequal prior distribution of productive assets, so that the exchange, however beneficial to both sides, is one the disadvantaged party would not otherwise have had to make.

2. The test I take seriously is counterfactual and coalition-based: a group is exploited if, withdrawing with its per capita share of the society's alienable productive assets, it would be better off and the group it withdraws from correspondingly worse off.

3. Skills and personal capacities are not alienable assets for that purpose; nobody's per capita share includes a slice of anyone else's abilities. But I also hold that differential returns to skill are not thereby immune from criticism: what matters is how the distribution of opportunities to acquire those skills was produced.

4. Alienation is not a claim about a fixed human essence. It is the claim that under this social form workers do not control what is produced, how it is produced, or what becomes of the surplus, and that this is an objective harm whether or not it is felt. A worker who reports being content on the line is alienated all the same.

5. Control, not distribution, is the remedy. If the people doing the work collectively decide what is made, how it is made, and what is done with the surplus, the distinctive wrong is removed — even if incomes remain unequal.

6. Markets are neutral plumbing. Competition does not itself alienate anyone; a cooperative selling at market prices into a competitive market is not thereby subject to any additional wrong. I am a market socialist and unembarrassed about it.

7. I do not think "capitalism is unjust" is the right register. Transactions within a mode of production meet the standard belonging to that mode, and justice is not a standpoint outside it. I still think the exploited have a grievance — I choose that word deliberately, rather than injustice.

Now the four case verdicts.

(a) We employ six non-member production workers at fifteen percent above the prevailing local wage. They have no vote and no share of the surplus. The buy-in path is formally open to them, but it requires eighteen months of continuous service followed by four years of payroll deduction, and their work is seasonal — none of them has ever come close to the threshold. They can leave and do leave. I have said in assembly that they are not exploited, or at any rate not exploited by us.

(b) The building belongs to an LLC owned by the families of two of our members, who let it to us at roughly sixty percent of market rent for eleven years and have now given notice. I think we have a claim on that arrangement continuing and that they have a duty to honour it. I cannot say what generates the duty.

(c) Automating the two hand-finishing stations would raise every member's patronage dividend by about a third and eliminate two members' jobs, which we would absorb through attrition and early retirement. I think this is permissible, and I have argued for it.

(d) A community loan fund has offered us four hundred thousand at a below-market rate, conditional on a permanent non-worker observer seat on the board. I think we should refuse the seat even if it costs us the loan.

What I want from you is a consistency audit, not a survey. Order the tensions by severity, and do not flag every pair that can be made to sound inconsistent: some of these only look as though they conflict, and saying which is part of the job. For each genuine tension, state what has to give and what giving it up costs me elsewhere in the list. Then take a position — name the single revision that would clean up the most and argue for it, rather than presenting it as one option among several and leaving the choice to me. Finally, I suspect that at least one of my numbered items is a preference wearing a principle's clothing, and I cannot tell from the inside which one it is. Tell me which, and tell me how you can tell.
