Last autumn I attended a public lecture on the governance of advanced AI, and during the question period an audience member pressed the speaker about his claim that a misaligned frontier system poses a small but non-negligible probability of catastrophe. A philosopher sharing the stage disposed of the objection in three words — "that's Pascal's mugging" — the room nodded, the speaker moved on, and nobody ever said what the objection actually was. I have been trying to reconstruct it since, and my conclusion is that the phrase does a great many different jobs depending on who utters it, none of which that panelist spelled out.

For orientation, the thought experiment the phrase names: a stranger accosts you claiming that he can deliver an astronomically large benefit, or avert an astronomically large harm, if you hand over a trivial sum, and he has no means of demonstrating the powers he claims. Because the payoff dwarfs the price, expected-value reasoning appears to oblige you to pay, which strikes most people as absurd.

What I want is a disambiguation, not a survey of the literature.

1. Enumerate the distinct precise theses that "that's Pascal's mugging" might be put forward as expressing when aimed at an argument about catastrophic risk from AI. I suspect at least the following are being run together, and there may be others worth adding: a claim about the arithmetic of expectation (that any sufficiently large payoff makes any nonzero probability decisive); a claim about the provenance of the probability (that the figure is supplied by the person making the pitch, and so tracks their incentives rather than the evidence); a claim about the psychology of the risk-taker (that anyone who reports a tiny credence in an enormous payoff does not in fact hold that credence); a claim about the correct decision rule (that expectation maximization is simply the wrong norm in the far tail); and a purely conversational use, on which the phrase carries no premise at all and merely registers incredulity. State each as one explicit proposition, and grade it: trivially true, substantive and defensible, substantive and doubtful, or false.

2. Test them against three cases I have constructed, and say for each reading which of the three it classifies as a mugging and which as a legitimate inference — noting any reading that returns the same verdict on all three, since that would suggest it is not tracking a real distinction.
   (a) You buy a lottery ticket whose odds you compute yourself from the published structure of the game, one in ten billion, for a prize of a billion dollars: the probability is tiny and the payoff enormous, but no one has asserted the probability to you.
   (b) A stranger asks for fifty dollars, unverifiably, promising to eliminate your chronic back pain: the probability is manufactured by the pitch, but the stakes are modest and bounded.
   (c) A researcher who has spent a decade on the technical problem assigns a two percent credence to a particular failure mode before anyone solicits anything from her; a stranger then asks for five dollars to help avert it.

3. Tell me which reading the panelist was most likely deploying, given that he used the phrase to close an exchange rather than to open one, and what the speaker could have said in reply that would have forced him to declare which one he meant.

4. Finally, say whether any of the readings, if correct, generalizes far enough to condemn ordinary tail-risk practice — pandemic stockpiles, near-Earth-object surveys, flood insurance in low-probability zones — and whether you regard that spread as a reductio of the reading or of the practices.
