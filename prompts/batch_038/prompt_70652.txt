So I'm three years into a philosophy PhD and I've got a summer fellowship application due for an institute that does history of economic thought — two pages, and the project is Hume on money. I have a half-formed idea that I need turned into an actual proposal. Here's the seed, scribbled in the margin of the essays at 30,000 feet. Hume looks like he's running two mechanisms that pull against each other. On one side there's the automatic adjustment story in the balance-of-trade essay: a country that loses too much of its specie becomes cheap relative to its neighbours, foreigners start buying from it, the money flows back, so no nation can permanently drain itself through trade — plus the general quantity-theoretic point that once an increase in money has fully spread, prices rise in proportion and nothing real has changed. On the other side there's the transition story in the money essay: gold and silver arriving bit by bit spreads through the economy unevenly, and while it is spreading it wakes industry up, puts idle hands to work, emboldens merchants — which is why he ends up recommending that a sensible magistrate should try to keep the quantity of money increasing if possible, because it keeps a spirit of industry alive. My worry: the policy recommendation lives entirely inside the transition, and the transition is exactly what the adjustment mechanism exists to close off. If inflows are self-reversing, then "keep money growing" either means fighting a battle you can't win or it means Hume's anti-mercantilism is a long-run thesis with a short-run mercantilist policy bolted onto the front of it.

I want the proposal to turn that into a question with a determinate answer, not a mood. Part of what I need you to do is tell me how to sharpen it: is the transition a real short-run non-neutrality claim, a claim about the uneven distribution of new money over time rather than its aggregate amount, or just a rhetorical concession to his mercantilist readers? Please build in the awkward bits that I think make the project non-trivial rather than a survey. Hume explicitly denies in the interest essay that the rate of interest depends on the quantity of money, and instead gives three non-monetary causes, which sits badly with anyone who wants to read the transition story as a monetary-stimulus story. The transition also seems to require idle labour and idle resources, so it's doing double duty as an employment argument. And there are two neighbouring puzzles I don't know what to do with: the passage on raising the denomination of coin, where he treats revaluation as a fraud on creditors that gains nothing abroad, and the public credit essay, where he's gloomy about paper credit and the national debt in a way the quantity theory alone doesn't obviously explain — there his worry looks like it's about credit resting on opinion and confidence. I want the proposal to say which of these it actually uses, which it brackets, and why; my advisor keeps telling me I'm writing four projects and calling it one, and honestly she's not wrong.
