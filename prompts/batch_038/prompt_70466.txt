Forty years of condensed matter physics left me with a habit of asking whether a problem is well posed before asking whether it has a solution, and I have lately applied that habit to Bradley's regress, which I encountered in his logic and have been unable to put down. As I understand it, the argument is this: a judgement that a stands in R to b is no more unified once R has been admitted as one further constituent alongside a and b; something must bind R to a and to b, whatever does so would itself require a binder, and the demand never terminates. My own proposal, offered in the spirit of a physicist proposing an order of parameters, is that the regress is a type error rather than a genuine puzzle. On this view relations are not terms of any sort but the structural features by which terms get posited at all, so that a fact is a whole prior to its constituents, and its constituents are recoverable only by abstraction or projection from it. Asking then for the binder is, on my reading, like asking for a further Hamiltonian to govern the Hamiltonian: the question is intelligible only if one already possesses what it claims to lack.

Please write an essay that takes this proposal seriously as one contender in the live controversy over the unity of the proposition, weighs it against the principal rival diagnoses — that the copula must be conceived as an essentially unsaturated or predicative entity rather than as an object; that unity is a primitive and therefore a legitimate explanatory stopping point; that the whole difficulty evaporates once we cease reifying propositions into a stock of constituents; and that no account can avoid the regress except at the cost of circularity — and states plainly which of these survives scrutiny. I am not looking for a survey that ends by admiring the problem from a safe distance. Commit yourself to a verdict: can a structure-first account of unity actually halt the regress, or is the demand for something that binds the binder ineliminable? Defend that verdict against at least one strong objection that you formulate yourself, rather than one you borrow. Write for a reader fluent in mathematics and physics but only generally acquainted with philosophy, and do not trouble me with references or a reading list.
