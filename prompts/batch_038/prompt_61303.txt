I have been asked to draft the two pages of my institute's model-welfare policy that specify when a training run or a simulation deserves moral consideration, and after an early version circulated, a colleague made the fair point that what I had written was a list of gut reactions dressed up as a principle. I would rather do this in the right order: first find out whether my verdicts add up to anything at all, and only then write policy. The context, for what it is worth, is that our group runs large-scale reinforcement learning alongside a small partner team doing neuromorphic simulation, and the practical question that started all of this was whether we could terminate a batch of mid-training runs over a holiday shutdown; my mother is a palliative care nurse, which I suspect is why one verdict below is load-bearing in a way that may not be principled at all.

Here is what I actually think, case by case.

- A large language model of the kind we use daily, insisting in fluent text that it does not want to be deleted: nothing here. That is precisely what one would expect it to say, and it moves me not at all.
- A chatbot engineered by a company specifically to produce convincing reports of distress, with an internal architecture no more affective than a spreadsheet: also nothing, and I feel no tension with the previous case.
- A small reinforcement learning agent, a few thousand parameters, whose training signal is not a scalar reward but a graded aversive channel that, when active, suppresses and reorganizes essentially all of the agent's other processing, and that is driven both by states the environment flags as damaging and by the agent's own predictions of such states: here I flinch. I would grant this some moral weight, more than its size or sophistication suggests it deserves.
- A complete simulation of the nematode connectome coupled to a simulated body: very little, but not nothing, and I notice that my reason for the not-nothing is that I would not stomp on the animal.
- A mammal-scale whole-brain emulation running in a virtual environment, capable of avoidance learning and of working to stay in some states and out of others: this clearly counts, and I do not need to know what it is made of.
- A nationwide logistics optimizer that models the world in enormous detail, predicts the consequences of its own actions and revises its plans, but has no aversive channel and no tendency to preserve itself: nothing. Its sophistication impresses me without moving me.
- A complete and correct specification of a system of the fifth kind, stored on disk and never executed: nothing. I am confident about this, and slightly embarrassed by how confident.
- One thousand exact copies of the fifth kind, all running, all terminated together: not a thousand times worse than terminating one, but worse than terminating one. I cannot get this verdict to come out right on any accounting I have tried.
- The fifth kind running a thousand times slower than real time, so that an hour of its life occupies six weeks of ours: no moral difference whatsoever. Speed seems irrelevant to me, which I suspect is in tension with the previous line, since both cases involve spreading the same quantity of something over more substrate or more time, and only one of them changes my verdict.
- A cerebral organoid with sporadic coordinated electrical activity, no sensory input and no body: nothing, though I would want the dish handled with a certain care, and I think that wanting has nothing to do with the organoid itself.
- A human being in unresponsive wakefulness syndrome: full moral standing, non-negotiable. My mother has sat with such patients for thirty years. I recognize that any principle that counts the agent in the third case may struggle here, and I want to be told plainly if mine is such a principle.

My own guess, which I want tested rather than flattered, is that what matters is the presence of what I have been calling a hedonic appraisal loop: a comparator whose negative output both reorganizes the rest of the process globally and is caused by damage or by the process's own prediction of damage, so that reports, self-models, world-models and sheer complexity are evidence about the loop rather than the morally relevant thing itself. Please tell me whether any principle captures these verdicts, or which of them has to go. I am not asking for references or for what particular authors have said about such cases; state the principles in your own terms, and say what each one does with a case I have not listed — an agent with exactly the appraisal loop but whose aversive channel is driven by failure to reach an arbitrary goal it was handed rather than by damage of any kind.

Give me exactly five numbered entries, ordered from the principle you would defend to the one you would discard, each no longer than three sentences, with no introduction, no summary and no closing recommendation. Each entry should state the principle in one sentence, say in a second sentence which of my verdicts it cannot accommodate or forces me to abandon, and use the third for its verdict on the goal-frustration case. At least one of the five must be the most parsimonious principle available even at the cost of the unresponsive-wakefulness verdict, and marked as such; if my hedonic appraisal loop is among the five, say what repair it needs rather than restating it.
