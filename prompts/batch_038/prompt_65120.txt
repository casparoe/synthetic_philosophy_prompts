I'm a developmental editor at a company that publishes high-school world history textbooks. Twice a year I get a batch of chapter drafts and the same argument, coming at me from both directions at once. Half our state reviewers say we've written people out of the story — every drought, plague and volcano gets top billing and nobody in the chapter ever actually decides anything. The other half say we're doing the old "geography explains civilisation" routine again, just with a climatologist's vocabulary. Frequently they're complaining about the same paragraph, which is the part that makes my job miserable.

I want a screening procedure I can run over a flagged passage before I write my note to the author. Constraints: about fifteen minutes per passage; I have a history degree and no training in philosophy or climate science; and I can't fact-check the underlying claims, because we have no science reader and the budgets gone. So the tests have to work on the shape of the causal claim in the sentence, not on whether the climatology behind it is right. And the author is a freelancer who gets paid by revision round, so every flag needs to come with a specific, line-level fix rather than a vague worry.

I have a hunch about which distinctions actually carry the weight here, but I don't trust my hunch, so take these or replace them with better ones:

- did the non-human factor start the thing, or did it decide who the thing landed on? A drought can begin a famine and still say nothing at all about who starved.
- the swap test: replace the eruption with a differently-caused version of the same shock — a war, a blockade, a tax change — and ask whether the rest of the sentence still holds. If it does, the climate isn't doing explanatory work and shouldn't be the subject of the verb.
- is there anyone left in the paragraph who could have done otherwise? I want to say a passage is deterministic when it contains no agent with a live alternative, but tell me if that's too crude.

For each step, give me what to look for, what counts as a pass, what a flag looks like, and what the rewrite actually does. Say what order to run them in, and which ones I can skip when I'm behind schedule.

Then run the procedure on three bits of plausible draft text:

1. A sentence attributing the wave of New England farm families heading west in 1816 to the cold summer that followed the Tambora eruption.
2. A passage saying that potato blight caused the deaths in the Irish famine of the 1840s.
3. A chapter opener framing the collapse of the Bronze Age palace societies as a systems failure triggered by a run of droughts.

At least one should come out clean and at least one should need a rewrite. Tell me which one you expect me to be personally attached to defending, and why.

Last thing: mark the points where the procedure just stops and it becomes an editorial call — how much causal weight a chapter as a whole gives germs and weather isn't something a test can settle — and be honest about how it will misfire. What does it over-flag, what does it wave through that it shouldn't, and does it lean toward one side of the reviewer fight by construction? No citations or reading lists please, I'm not going to chase them.
