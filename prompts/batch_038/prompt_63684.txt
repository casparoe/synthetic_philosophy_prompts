You are a philosopher of causation with a working knowledge of labour and compensation law. Think the problem through carefully before answering, and in particular spend some effort sorting out, for yourself first, which features of the practice I describe are strictly required by a given theory of causation and which merely happen to sit comfortably with it.

I am an occupational physician who has recently begun adjudicating workers' compensation claims in Japan, and my first two files have unsettled me. For a death from stroke or heart attack, I am told to apply a bright line: if the worker averaged 80 or more hours of overtime per month — in the month before the event, or across the two to six months before it — the work is treated as having caused the death; below about 45 hours the link is generally not recognized at all; in between, I weigh night shifts, business travel, and pre-existing hypertension. For a suicide following depression, I apply a different instrument entirely: a published table that lists types of workplace events (an accident the worker was responsible for, harassment, a forced transfer, a large change in duties), grades each weak, medium, or strong in psychological load, and measures the worker against what a comparable ordinary worker would bear. Certification then requires the work-related load to come out strong, and requires that neither private-life events nor the worker's own constitution explain the illness instead. In that table, 80 hours of overtime in a month is graded only medium: the very workload that settles a heart case on its own does not settle a mind case.

With that practice in view:

1. What account of causation does the hour-threshold rule presuppose — a dose-response picture, a difference-making or counterfactual one, a probabilistic one, or primarily an allocation of the burden of proof — and which rival accounts does the bright line quietly rule out?
2. What does the mental-disorder table presuppose instead, given that it grades events by their meaning to a typical worker rather than by quantity of exposure, and is the pair of instruments inconsistent, or are they two coherent views of causation applied to two kinds of harm?
3. Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined: which of these rules would come out exactly the same under accounts of causation that disagree with each other?
4. What does the apparatus as a whole presuppose about leisure — that long hours are harmful only above a dose and therefore permissible below it, or that they are a wrong which merely needs a body to become visible — and does the fact that compensation is triggered by death rather than by the hours themselves decide between those readings?
5. What would the rules have to become if the presupposed view were replaced by the rival you judge most defensible, as in systems that oblige larger employers to negotiate how staff may ignore out-of-hours contact without waiting for anyone to be harmed?

Answer as exactly five numbered items, one per question, each only a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion. In the fifth item, also state how confident you are in your reconstruction overall and name the single point you are least sure of.
