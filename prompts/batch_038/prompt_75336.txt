I am a novelist at work on a book whose central section is set in a British jobcentre, and for the past year I have been gathering material there rather than reading policy. What has arrested me is not the hardship, which I expected, but a peculiarity in the machinery. Claimants sign a document called a claimant commitment: a fixed number of hours a week spent searching for work, appointments with a work coach, a current CV, and — this is the part that interests me — a running log of the applications they have made, which they must be able to produce on demand. Where they cannot produce it, and no good reason is accepted, the payment is cut, in many cases by the entire standard allowance, and what remains available to them is a repayable hardship payment. Yet the office sits in a town where the vacancies are outnumbered many times over by the people looking, and everyone inside the building, the coaches included, appears to know that a large share of the applications logged will never be answered by anyone. The state is, in effect, paying people to write letters no one reads, and penalising them for not writing them.

What view of desert, or of the entitlement to subsistence, does that arrangement presuppose? I want the implicit theory reconstructed rather than named: what has to be true about the relation between contribution and claim, and about what a person's time is worth, for an evidentiary requirement of this shape to make sense at all. Then tell me what the practice strictly requires as opposed to what merely sits comfortably with it — deterrence, reciprocity, the formation of character through discipline, or something about the visibility of effort as distinct from its results. The tension I cannot resolve is this: the regime is defended in the language of getting people into work, but it measures conduct rather than outcomes, and it accepts a good reason for missing an appointment while remaining unmoved by the absence of any job to be had. Are those two halves of one theory or two incompatible ones? Finally, mark where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, and say concretely what the jobcentre would have to look like if its presuppositions were replaced — first by a frankly instrumental view, and then by a view on which subsistence is owed without condition.
