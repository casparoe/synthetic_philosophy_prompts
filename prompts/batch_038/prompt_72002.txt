The question the students were given was: In Two Dogmas of Empiricism (1951) Quine attacks two doctrines he takes to be shared by the empiricist tradition. (a) Name both. (b) Reconstruct in your own words the sequence of moves by which Quine argues that the first of them cannot be coherently characterized, paying particular attention to which class of statements he treats as unproblematic and which as troublesome. (c) Say what Quine puts in place of the two doctrines, and explain what this does to the claim that his view is still an empiricist one. About 300 words.

Here is the answer:

Quine's two dogmas are, first, belief in a sharp split between analytic statements, true in virtue of meaning and independently of fact, and synthetic statements, which are grounded in fact; and second, reductionism, the idea that every meaningful statement is equivalent to some logical construct upon terms referring to immediate experience.

He begins by noting that analyticity is standardly explained by way of synonymy: an analytic statement is either a logical truth or becomes one when synonyms are substituted for synonyms. Definitions don't supply synonymy, though, they presuppose it, since a lexicographer merely records an observed equivalence rather than creating one. Quine then tries interchangeability salva veritate — two expressions are synonymous if they can be swapped anywhere without changing truth value — but this only works in a language rich enough to contain a necessity operator, and necessity is in turn explained by appeal to analyticity, so the account comes full circle. The clearest illustration of the problem is his example of the field linguist who hears the natives utter a rabbit word and finds it could equally be rendered as rabbit, undetached rabbit part, or temporal stage of a rabbit; because the translation is indeterminate in just this way, synonymy can never be pinned down, and this is what does the real work in the essay.

The class Quine treats as unproblematic is the class of statements analytic through synonymy, such as the claim that all bachelors are unmarried, since here the predicate is contained in the subject in the way Kant described. His difficulty is with logical truths, because we have no way of fixing the meanings of the logical particles themselves, and hence no way of saying which sentences are true purely in virtue of logical form. For artificial languages he thinks semantic rules can mark off the analytic sentences, but this doesn't give a general account, since the rules are identified as semantic rules precisely by the fact that they list analytic truths.

In place of both dogmas Quine sets a holistic picture: our statements confront the tribunal of sense experience not individually but as a corporate body, a fabric whose outer edge is made of observation statements and whose interior holds mathematics and logic. Because the connection to experience is indirect, any statement can be held true come what may if we make drastic enough adjustments elsewhere in the system, and conversely no statement is immune to revision — he notes that revising the law of excluded middle has been proposed as a way of simplifying quantum mechanics. Our choices are governed by pragmatic considerations like conservatism and simplicity, which is why the two dogmas turn out to be at root one and the same. But since it is the whole system that gets tested, and since the total field is underdetermined by experience, Quine has really abandoned empiricism and landed in conventionalism: what is left is the picking of a framework, and evidence has dropped out of the picture altogether.
