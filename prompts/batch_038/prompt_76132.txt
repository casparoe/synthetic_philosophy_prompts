Three weeks ago I took a job as the animal welfare technician at a land-based salmon farm, which sounds much grander than it is — mostly I write SOPs, walk the tanks with a clipboard, and get shouted at when the oxygen drops. The company just got permission to put a shrimp unit in one of the spare buildings, and last week they handed me the stunning-and-killing protocol for both species and told me to make it "defensible" before the audit. Since then I've been reading at night and arguing with my flatmate, and the thing that's bugging me isn't any single answer, it's that my own gut reactions to these cases don't look like they come from one place. I grew up fishing with my grandad and I still eat fish, so I'm not coming at this from a vegan starting point, and I'm not looking for permission or absolution. I just want to know what I'm actually committed to.

Here are my verdicts, in the order they came up. A salmon that gets a hard percussive blow to the head in the harvesting room: fine. The same fish, same life, left on a wet deck gasping for ten minutes: clearly not fine — so the difference can't be about the death, since both are dead in the end, and it can't be about the life either, since it was the identical mediocre life. Carbon dioxide stunning in a bath, which I'm told is standard practice and arguably better than nothing: worse than the percussive blow, because they thrash and try to get out for a while first, even though the endpoint is the same. Chilling shrimp down in an ice slurry: not fine. But we use chilled water to sedate the salmon before handling them and I think that's fine — same cold, opposite verdict, and I can't articulate the difference. Pinching off one eyestalk from a shrimp broodstock female so she spawns more often: to me obviously wrong, more wrong than the crowded grow-out tanks, which makes no sense because it's a short, small injury versus eighteen months of bad conditions. Using cleaner fish — wrasse, lumpfish — in sea pens to eat the lice off the salmon, where a big fraction of the cleaners just die and get eaten: worse than running a hydrogen peroxide lice bath that stresses the salmon, which surprises me, since the salmon are the animals I'm more sure about and the bath is being done for their benefit. Catch-and-release on a barbless hook, fish swims off fine: worse than farming a salmon in a crowded pen and then stunning it, even though nobody dies. Wild shrimp suffocating on a trawl deck versus farmed shrimp of the same species killed the same way: I think the farmed one had the worse life, but I'd rather eat the wild one, and I can't dissolve that. Feeding fishmeal made from small wild fish to farmed salmon: lots of individual wild animals die per farmed animal, which makes farmed salmon worse than I assumed, but not impermissible — whereas if exactly the same body count went into producing shrimp I think it would be impermissible, so headcount seems to matter only sometimes. Octopus farming: should be banned outright and I wouldn't eat it, but I honestly don't know whether that's because they're cleverer, because I'm more confident they're sentient, or because a captive octopus's life is worse — and it seems perverse that I'm confident about the octopus and agnostic about the shrimp whose tank I'll literally be standing in. And then: culling deformed fry at the hatchery with a quick blow or an anesthetic overdose is fine and even a kindness, leaving them to starve or be eaten is not, but killing a healthy fish for a cooking video when nobody needs it is also not — so "it was going to die anyway" does work for me and "there's no point to it" also does work for me, and those feel like two unrelated principles wearing the same coat.

My own half-formed guess is something like: badness equals amount of suffering times duration, discounted by how confident we are that the animal is sentient at all. That gets me the fry and the octopus, but it obviously fails on catch-and-release, and it can't explain the ice-slurry versus chilled-salmon pair, and I suspect it can't explain eyestalk ablation either. So: can you give me one principle, or a small number of rival candidates, stated precisely enough that I could actually use it to write the protocol? I want it clause by clause with the case that motivates each clause, so I can see what's doing the work. Then tell me what it says about cases I haven't listed — specifically whether it's permissible to farm decapods purely to be ground into feed for farmed fish; whether a disease outbreak that wipes out 40% of a sea pen counts as a welfare harm of the farming system or just bad luck; whether switching to a slower-growing, hardier strain kept in slightly better conditions is a welfare improvement if it means more total fish-days of lived life; and whether a supermarket holding live lobsters in a tank and boiling them to order is relevantly different from what I'm about to do in the shrimp unit. And please be blunt about which of my verdicts above the best principle forces me to give up — I'd rather hear that two of them have to go than be handed a baroque five-clause theory that saves everything. Say which is the better deal: the complex principle that keeps all my intuitions, or the simple one that costs me a couple.

One more thing, and it matters to me for this job. Please separate what's actually settled from what's genuinely contested among people who work on this stuff, and don't hand me a contested view dressed up as consensus. I want to know which of my disagreements are empirical — is this animal sentient, does this method actually cause pain or is it just reflex — and which are normative, i.e. given that it hurts, does it matter and how much. I've noticed that people on both sides of this say "the science is clear" about completely opposite things, and I can't check the literature on a phone in a wet room, so if you're unsure whether something is settled or is live debate, say that you're unsure rather than smoothing it over. Same for the numbers — if you're estimating how many wild fish go into a kilo of farmed salmon, or what fraction of cleaner fish die, flag that it's an estimate and roughly how shaky it is.
