I'm in my third year of a philosophy PhD and this term I'm co-teaching one of those "science and its critics" seminars with the physics department — half the room are experimental postgrads who are (fairly) sceptical that anything we do touches what they do at the bench. Last week we got onto method and one of them trotted out the line everyone puts in Feynman's mouth: "philosophy of science is about as useful to scientists as ornithology is to birds." I've never managed to find where Feynman actually says it, and I suspect he didn't, but that's not what I want to ask about. What I want to ask about is the arguement he then built on top of it, roughly: (i) the analogy holds because in both cases the practitioners have a fluent competence and the people who reflect on it produce nothing the practitioners need; (ii) birds can't in principle consult ornithology and scientists can't in practice, because method is picked up by apprenticeship rather than by reading about method; (iii) so philosophy of science is at best entertainment for the philosophers; and (iv) so when philosophers weigh in on interpretation, or on what counts as evidence, or on whether some field is a science at all, they're describing a practice they have no standing to correct. He also conceded — and this is the bit that's stuck with me, because I thought it was genuinely clever — that there is one way ornithology helps birds, namely that certain species are still here because ornithologists lobbied for their habitats, but he waved it away as ornithology turning into politics, i.e. not the analogy's business.

Can you audit the thing for me? I'd like the correspondence table first — what's playing the bird, what's playing flight, what's the ornithological literature, what's the habitat, what's the field station — and then the disanalogies, sorted into the ones that actually bite against (iii) and (iv) and the ones that are just local colour. I'm especially suspicious that the analogy is doing descriptive work in (i)-(ii) and normative work in (iv), and that the slide happens somewhere around (ii); can you tell me whether there's a version of it that's honestly descriptive throughout, and what it would then license. Also: is the habitat concession really extraneous, or does it show the frame can't carry the weight, since the one thing ornithology demonstrably does for birds is change what happens to birds from outside? And there's the obvious worry that uttering the line is itself a claim about one discipline's standing to judge another, so does it self-refute, or is that just tu quoque cheapness of the sort I'd be embarrassed to deploy in front of them?

Some context on where we're going with the course, in case it changes your answer: next week they're reading Weinberg's chapter against philosophy from his book on the search for a final theory, and then the Hawking and Mlodinow gesture of declaring philosophy dead for having failed to keep up with physics, and I want to be able to say something better in response than "well, Einstein read Mach." So finish up by telling me what a better analogy would be, if there is one. My half-formed hunch is that the right comparator isn't a descriptive discipline studying an animal that can't talk back, but something more like medicine or conservation, where the subject can receive the discipline's findings and change what it does in response — but I'd like that either developed into something with the same bite as the birds line or shot down, because I don't want to hand them a weaker analogy than the one they started with on Thursday.
