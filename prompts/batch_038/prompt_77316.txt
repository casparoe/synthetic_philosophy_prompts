I work on distributed systems — replication, partitioning, consensus — and I have been reading about commissurotomy patients: the epilepsy surgery cases where the corpus callosum is severed, a stimulus is flashed briefly to one visual field, and the verbal report from the left hemisphere comes apart from what the left hand can demonstrate it saw. I have no philosophy training, so please define every term of art in the clause where you first use it, and describe any philosopher's position in your own words rather than assuming I recognize it. No references, bibliography, or reading list: I cannot chase them down, and I would rather have the reasoning than the pointers.

What I want is a designed case, not an exposition. Construct a thought experiment that isolates one variable — the temporal offset between the moment some content becomes available to one hemisphere and the moment it becomes available to the other — and holds everything else fixed.

Constraints on the design:

- The patient is an ordinary commissurotomy patient of the kind actually studied, with the usual asymmetry between speech and left-hand response. No duplicated persons, no uploads, no exotic machinery. The single idealization you are permitted is that some spared indirect pathway between the hemispheres transfers content at a fixed, adjustable delay. If you need anything beyond that, stop and justify it.
- The scenario proper is at most 150 words; everything after it is analysis.
- Report behavior is constant across conditions. Whatever the offset, the patient says and does exactly the same things at the same times, so the two views below cannot be told apart by observation. I want the disagreement forced into the open rather than buried in the data.

The two views, which must deliver different verdicts in the case you build: (a) a synchronic unity view, on which what a subject has at a time is a single phenomenal field, and states belong to one subject only if they are co-conscious — mutually present at the same moment; (b) an accessibility view, on which what makes for one subject is that each stream can take up, remember, and act on the other's content over time, so that unity is diachronic and comes in degrees.

Then give me:

1. A condition matrix spanning five settings — an intact-callosus control on a normal subject, an offset small enough that two events would ordinarily be experienced as simultaneous, an offset just past that, an offset of a second or two, and complete disconnection — and for each, the verdict of each view on whether there is one unified field at the crucial moment and on how many subjects of experience there are, with the criterion each view is applying stated explicitly. Tell me at which offset the two verdicts first diverge.
2. Whether there is a principled reason for the threshold to sit where you put it, or whether the synchronic view inherits a sorites problem: same content, offset nudged a millisecond at a time, no fact about when one subject becomes two.
3. Which single feature of the case is doing the work, plus two ablations: what the verdicts become if the offset is removed entirely, and if instead of one patient with an adjustable delay you use real patients who differ in how much commissural tissue is spared.
4. The best attack available to whichever view comes out worse — the move its defender would actually make, not a strawman.
5. Since the two views agree on every observable in the case, an honest verdict on whether the dispute is substantive or merely verbal, and if verbal, what would have to be added to make it non-verbal.

So you know what counts as cheating: the case is for deciding whether "how many subjects of experience are there right now?" — the counting question that the split-brain literature made central — can have an answer that varies continuously with a timing parameter. A scenario that stipulates the verdicts rather than deriving them from each view's own criteria fails. So does one whose description smuggles in a view's vocabulary: "the patient is having two experiences at t" is not neutral phrasing. Keep the scenario description theory-neutral.

Finally, an assumptions register: list the empirical claims your design leans on — transfer through spared pathways, the window within which humans treat two events as simultaneous, what such patients can and cannot report — one line each, with your confidence that it is actually true. I cannot verify anything, so I need to know which parts of the scaffolding are idealization and which are established.
