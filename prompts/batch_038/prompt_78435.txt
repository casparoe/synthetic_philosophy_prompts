I spent forty years as a physicist on the safety side of materials — flaw populations, fracture mechanics, and later probabilistic risk assessment for reactor components. Since retiring I have taken up philosophy, and more recently the arguments about aligning artificial agents, where I keep meeting a comparison that strikes me as both persuasive and overworked. It runs roughly as follows: nobody certifies a pressure vessel or an airframe on its average performance; we insist on bounds that hold in the worst case, with margins; therefore we should regard benchmark averages of AI systems as essentially worthless and demand worst-case guarantees of their behavior before deploying them. The comparison is recruited to license a fairly strong conclusion — that anything short of a guarantee holding across all inputs and all environments is not safety engineering at all, but marketing.

I should like the analogy audited rather than repeated. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly: what in the AI case plays the role of the applied load, of the flaw population, of the partial safety factor, of the test article that can be loaded until it fails, and of the certification authority empowered to say no. Then the disanalogies, sorted into those that are fatal to the intended use and those that are merely inconvenient. I would particularly value attention to what the analogy quietly imports from my old discipline and may not find in the target — for instance, that the load is exogenous and statistically stationary, that the failure mode is physically characterizable in advance, that destructive testing of the actual article is possible, and that a guarantee is backed by an institution with liability and the power to ground the fleet.

Here is a complication of my own making, which I should like taken seriously rather than waved away. In practice we never possessed a true worst case. The characteristic strength of a material was a low percentile of a fitted distribution; the bounding flaw size was set by what our inspection could actually detect; the target failure probability was a number negotiated with a regulator, in airworthiness something of the order of one chance in a thousand million hours of flight for a catastrophic condition. Our worst case was a statistical construction dressed in deterministic language, calibrated to a level of risk a society had agreed to tolerate. Does that concession destroy the analogy, or does it merely relocate the question — to which distributional assumptions are licensed, over what class of environments, and who is entitled to calibrate them? If the latter, does the demand for worst-case guarantees survive, or does it collapse into a dispute about how heavy the tail of the assumed distribution may be?

Finally, if the bridge-and-airframe comparison cannot carry the weight placed on it, is there a better one? I am not after a survey of the literature, which I can no longer keep pace with, but the analysis itself.
