I do compliance work for a firm that builds things in about a dozen countries, and I've been trying to write down the rule I actually apply when I sign off on stuff, because the handbook's line — anything of value given to influence an official decision — turns out to be useless. Half of what my colleagues consider ordinary business would fail it and nobody thinks any of that half is wrong. So here are my real verdicts, including the ones that embarrass me.

A contractor's wife invites my counterpart at the ministry to her sister's wedding, seats him at the family table, and the family hands him an envelope of cash. Fine. I'd actually be suspicious of him if he refused.
The same contractor hands the same official an envelope of cash in a parking garage. Not fine, obviously.
A supplier sends the head of procurement a bottle of whisky at New Year, worth maybe sixty dollars. Fine.
The same supplier sends the same bottle three days before the tender closes. Not fine — and this is the one that breaks my brain, because I'd bet the tender was already decided either way and nothing about the bottle changed.
A regional governor routes a road through the district that elected him, adding twenty percent to the cost. Fine, that's what he was elected to do.
That same governor steers the guardrail contract to his brother's firm. Not fine.
A council member's daughter gets hired by a developer with permits pending before her mother's committee. Not fine.
The same daughter gets hired by the same developer six years after her mother left the committee, at market salary, and she's genuinely good at the work. I want to say fine, but I notice I would never approve it in writing.
A mining company pays for the village chief's son to go to university, with nothing pending and no promise made. I said fine once and I still don't know if I was right.
A mining company pays for the village school's roof. Fine, definitely.
An MP takes a paid directorship and declares it in the public register. Legally fine, and my gut says not fine, and I can't tell you why my gut says what it says.
A junior official refuses a gift from an elder who has been hosting and feeding him for years, and after that the elder's whole network treats him as an outsider. I feel the refusal was wrong and not just imprudent. That is a verdict I cannot defend to anyone at work.

So. Where am I going wrong, or am I going wrong at all? The pairs I can't live with: same bottle, same two people, verdict flips on timing alone. Same money, same two people, verdict flips on which ceremony it happens inside. And the governor — I let him send an entire road to his own district but not a guardrail contract to his brother, and I can't state a principle that does that work without either condemning all the road-routing (which I don't want to do) or approving the brother (which I very much don't want to do).

There's a second worry underneath. When I'm sitting in the country where the wedding envelope is a normal thing inside a relationship everybody can see, I judge one way, and when I'm home I judge another, and I can't tell whether I'm applying one standard with two inputs or two standards and flattering myself about it. If it's two, I want to know which one is the mistake, and I don't want to be told the answer is "context" unless you tell me what about the context is doing the work.

What I want from you: a single principle, not a checklist, that comes as close as possible to my verdicts. State it precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I haven't seen. Then show me which clause is doing the work in each of the pairs I flagged — bottle, envelope, governor, the daughter. Then tell me what the principle says about two cases I didn't list: a company paying an official's father's hospital bills five years after the official left office, and a lobbyist taking a regulator's junior staff out to dinner once a month for years with nothing pending at any of those dinners. And if staying coherent forces the principle to overrule some of my verdicts, say which ones and why I shouldn't just patch it with exceptions — I can already feel the patching impulse and I think it's the wrong instinct.

Bottom line in your very first sentence: does a principle like this exist, and roughly what is it. Then the reasoning. No preamble, no restating my cases back at me.
