Write an internal quarterly memo of about 600 words from the People Analytics team of a mid-size freight company to its executive committee, in the relentlessly upbeat house style of corporate data science ('we let the data speak').

The memo reports two findings from the same operational dataset, and treats them in opposite ways.

First: drivers in the flagship safety-coaching program have a lower accident rate overall but a higher rate at every depot separately — the program was rolled out earliest at the long-tenure depots, where accidents are rarer for reasons having nothing to do with coaching. The memo leads with the aggregate, calls it the bottom line, and mentions the depot breakdown only as an interesting quirk of the rollout schedule.

Second: shipments handled by the new automated dispatch system are late more often overall, but among shipments carrying the mid-transit risk flag they are less often late in every region. That flag is raised both by weather and port congestion and by the dispatch system itself whenever it reroutes a shipment. The memo celebrates this as the 'apples-to-apples' comparison and demotes the aggregate to a footnote about unusual volume.

Neither move may be criticized, glossed, or explained anywhere in the text, and the words confounder, collider, and Simpson's paradox may not appear. A reader who knows the causal structure should be able to reconstruct from the memo's own numbers precisely what went wrong, and should recognize that the two errors are mirror images of each other; a reader who does not should still get a good joke about 'data-driven' authority. Close with a one-sentence caveat from the team's only statistician, set in smaller type, which the cover note quotes out of context as a full endorsement.
