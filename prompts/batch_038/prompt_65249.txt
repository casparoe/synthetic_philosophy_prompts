I convene a standing workshop on disagreement in practical ethics, and this term's two resident participants have argued for months about moral compromise between value systems without producing a single document. One is a committed defender of the gains-from-trade picture, in the line of Toby Ord's account of moral trade and of the bargaining and parliamentary models of moral uncertainty that follow Bostrom: where two parties weight the dimensions of a shared option space differently, there is generally a compromise each ranks above the contest that would otherwise settle the matter, and refusing to trade is waste with a moral name. The other is a committed opponent of that picture, in the tradition of the corruption and incommensurability objections (Anderson, Sandel; compare Margalit on rotten compromises): the arithmetic of the trade misdescribes at least one of the value systems, the baseline it improves upon is itself a threat to do what the threatening party believes is wrong, and a party that signs the compromise thereby comes to value something in the wrong way.

Both will sign one joint statement, if someone else does the drafting. Write that statement. Begin with the document itself, with no preface, no commentary, and no restatement of the task.

Use this case, so that the agreed claims are not vacuous. A single statute must be enacted, and there are four options, scored by each camp on its own 0–100 scale. Gridlock, leaving the existing regime in place: welfarists 45, ecologists 40. A welfarist statute alone: 100 and 0. An ecologist statute alone: 0 and 100. A negotiated compromise, under which suffering reduction is targeted at the species and processes the ecologists rank lowest, refugia are preserved, and a standing committee seats both camps: welfarists 62, ecologists 58. The trader camp holds that absent agreement the floor vote is a 50/50 contest between the two bills, on which reading the compromise is preferred by both to the fallback; the opposing camp holds that this baseline is inadmissible, and that descriptively gridlock is at least as likely as either bill.

The statement must contain:

1. A numbered section of claims both camps can sign, including at least one non-trivial claim about this case, at least one claim about what makes compromise possible or impossible in general, and at least one claim fixing the status of the statement itself, i.e. what signing does and does not commit a party to.

2. A disagreements table with columns for the issue; the trader formulation in wording its own camp accepts; the anti-trader formulation in wording its own camp accepts; a neutral formulation both can sign, or an explicit note that no such wording exists; and a status flag marking each row as a genuine disagreement, a verbal one, or unresolved.

3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling one would move at least one camp. I expect at least these, though you may reformulate or add: (a) whether a party's willingness to enact a statute it believes is wrong can generate legitimate bargaining power, or whether the contest is a baseline no morality should honor; (b) whether the terms of trade survive exposure of the normalization, given that rescaling by variance across feasible options, by range, or by credence-weighted spread across value systems may move the compromise out of the region where both camps prefer it to the fallback; (c) whether a camp can jointly recommend an option while disowning the exchange rate it embodies, and what the difference is between signing and acquiescing under protest.

4. For each crux, the concrete case, argument, or piece of evidence both camps accept as bearing on it, with each camp's prediction recorded in advance.

5. A closing section stating what each camp would count as evidence or argument against its own position, and whether either believes any such thing exists.

Where you conclude part of the dispute is verbal, say which part and why, and supply the stipulated vocabulary that removes it, including a distinction between compromise on outcomes and compromise on decision procedures, and between a value system's ranking of options and the mode of regard in which those options are held.
