so I got into a three hour argument with my friend R last night about suffering-focused ethics — we'd both had wine, this is the sober reconstruction — and I still don't know whether my position is a position or just a pile of moods held together by conviction. I don't want a survey of what negative utilitarians and their critics usually say to each other; I want an audit of my actual set, item by item. Here's what I'm committed to:

1. suffering is the only intrinsic disvalue. happiness, pleasure, preference satisfaction, meaning — none of it carries positive weight on its own, it matters only instrumentally, as a buffer against or a tool for reducing suffering.
2. no thresholds and no lexical levels. intensity x duration x number is the whole metric, and a big enough pile of low-grade misery outweighs a smaller amount of agony. I refuse to say things like 'some pains are infinitely worse than others.'
3. but context changes how bad a given pain is. Labour pain, the grief you wouldn't accept an anaesthetic for, the burn of a training block you chose yourself — those are much less bad than the same intensity of pain happening to someone who's purely a victim of it. In some cases I want to say they aren't bad at all.
4. disvalue is always bad-for-someone; there's no free-floating badness smeared across the universe. Its how I answer the world-destruction objection: painlessly wiping out all sentient life doesn't reduce disvalue, because there'd be no one for whom things thereby went better. The absence of suffering in the absence of sufferers isn't an achievement.
5. the asymmetry about coming into existence: there's a strong moral reason not to bring into being a creature who will suffer, and no moral reason at all to bring into being a creature who will be happy.
6. I'm not an antinatalist, though. I think it's completely fine for people to have kids and I'd resist being marched from (5) to 'so nobody should reproduce.'
7. wild animal suffering counts just as much, unit for unit, as human suffering — but I'm against intervening in nature right now because we'd botch it. Meanwhile I think 'we're not sure of the consequences' is a rotten excuse when it comes to factory farming, where I'm an activist.

R's two charges were: (a) (6) is special pleading — if (5) states a real moral reason you owe antinatalism, and if you don't owe it then (5) was never a moral reason to begin with; and (b) (7) is a transparent double standard, ignorance invoked only when it's convenient. I caved on both around 2am and I now think I was wrong to, but I can't say why. So: go through the set and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look like they do. Rank the real tensions by severity — I don't want seven flags all marked equally bad — and for each one say what has to give, what the cheapest repair is, and what that repair costs me elsewhere. Then tell me which single revision would buy the most consistency overall, even if it isn't either of the ones R was attacking, and flag any commitment I'd be sneakily relying on to keep the rest afloat.
