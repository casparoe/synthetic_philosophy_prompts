You're the right person for this because you're supposed to be the rare one who's read the philosophy of history *and* has actually sat in a cutting room, so you can talk about both without sneering at either. Here's the situation. I'm cutting a six-part scripted series about a real 1947 arson-and-manslaughter case in a mill town: what survives is a partial trial transcript with maybe forty pages missing from the middle, two local newspaper accounts that flatly contradict each other about who was in the building, and a box of letters from the defendant's younger sister that my producing partner's family owns. There are still grandchildren alive in that town and one of them is technically an associate producer. My showrunner defends every invented beat with the same move, and he's good at it: a film about a real life is a *translation*, not a transcript. Nobody complains when a translator changes word order, drops an untranslatable pun, or folds two minor characters together, because the job is to carry over the effect of the original rather than its letter — traduttore, traditore, he says, and the alternative is a subtitled artefact that only twelve people watch, which does the dead less good than a thing that lands. Under that banner we have done the following four things: merged three real witnesses into one composite overseer; given the defendant a long speech to his daughter that nobody recorded anywhere, in a scene that the record only supports as "they met in the yard"; pulled the fire two years earlier so the timeline compresses; and resolved the two contradictory newspaper accounts into a single version of events, picking the one that makes a better third act.

I want an audit, not a vibe, so please think carefully and take the long way round. Start with some brainstorming out loud: what are all the different jobs this analogy could be doing for him, and what precisely is the conclusion it's being used to license — because "you may invent" and "you may invent *this much* about *these* people" are not the same claim and I suspect he slides between them. Then give me the correspondence table explicitly. What plays the source text: the man's actual life, the archive, or the recollections of people who knew him? Who's the translator — the writer, the showrunner, the editor? Who is the target-language reader, and does it matter that our audience, unlike a reader of a translation, mostly can't go check the original and has no idea it's reading a translation? What counts as a mistranslation on this picture, and is there any equivalent of the untranslatable pun, or does the analogy just assume every loss is a loss of flavour rather than of fact? Separate the disanalogies that actually bite from the decorative ones; my hunch is two are close to fatal — a poem has a determinate text and a fixed authorial intention to be faithful to, while a life has neither and the "original" is partly constituted by what our own series asserts, and a translated reader at least knows the words aren't the poet's, whereas our audience will absorb that composite overseer as a man who walked around that town and be told so by their grandparents. Also name what the analogy quietly imports that the target doesn't have: that there's a single intact original sitting somewhere, that fidelity is a matter of aesthetic effect, that the person being rendered has consented or at any rate has no interests at stake in how it goes, and — this is the one that keeps me up — that the harms of a bad translation fall on the reader rather than on the author. Does his conclusion survive once those are on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole way?

One more thing, and this is the part I most want you to dig into. A friend of mine who restores frescoes for a living says I've got the wrong analogy entirely: what we're doing is closer to inpainting a damaged wall painting, where the professional ethic is that your additions have to be distinguishable from the original up close, reversible by whoever comes after you, and held to the minimum needed to make the whole legible again — she does the hatched-fill thing where a loss reads as continuous picture from across the chapel but screams "patch" when you put your nose to the plaster. Build that correspondence table too, and then use it as a test: which of our four inventions does it permit, which does it forbid, and what would the film equivalent of the hatch marks even be — a title card, a different lens, an on-screen archive layer, the grandmother's voice saying "that's not how I remember it"? Be honest about whether distinguishability and reversibility are achievable in a medium with no footnote layer, where the composite is indistinguishable from the man at every viewing distance and the audience cannot un-watch what they've absorbed. And if you end up thinking both analogies are wrong and something else fits better — a court deposition, a portrait commission, a cover version, a will being read aloud, a séance with a really good production designer — make the case for it and show what it would require of us concretely. Aim for something like 1200 words, rigorous over breezy, and its fine to conclude that my showrunner is right about some of the four; I'd rather be argued down than comforted.
