Ok so I've been going through my intuitions about fakes one case at a time and they really do not add up. Here they are, roughly in the order I hit them.

(1) A forger paints a "newly discovered Vermeer" so good that the top experts certify it. It hangs in a museum for forty years and people are floored by it. Then the fraud comes out. Verdict: the thing is worth much less now, and I think that's right — even though nobody's visual experience of it changed by one iota.

(2) Same forger, same hand, but he sells them openly as forgeries at a specialist auction, three grand a pop, nobody deceived. Verdict: I'd happily call these good paintings, I'd put one on my wall. But I wouldn't call them great and I wouldn't pay museum money. So the discount in (1) can't just be the fraud, since (2) has no fraud and still gets discounted.

(3) An old master's damaged canvas gets an 18th-century corner repainted in period style, and nobody can now tell which strokes are which. Verdict: I don't discount the untouched 95 percent. Forgery feels local here, which contradicts (1), where the whole object took the hit.

(4) Reverse of (1): a superb painting everyone assumed was the master's turns out to be by his brilliant assistant, an otherwise unknown hand. Verdict: still a great painting, I'd still pay a lot — but noticeably less. This is the one that bugs me most. If value is about achievment, why is an unknown person's identical achievement worth less?

(5) An old artist repaints from memory a work of his own youth that he sold and lost track of. Genuinely his hand, no deception about who painted it (though he passes it off as the original canvas). Verdict: I don't want to call this a forgery at all, and I also think it's worth less than the original. Which is embarrassing, because authorship is exactly what I said mattered in (1) and (4).

(6) A model spits out an image pixel-for-pixel indistinguishable from a lost drawing by some great artist — a drawing that never existed. Verdict: I feel nothing is lost here. No victim, no tragedy, and also no credit due. But with the human forger in (2) I did want to credit something. So it isn't just about the marks on the surface.

(7) A museum burns down; the lost works are reconstructed from good photographs by master craftspeople and shown with full disclosure. Verdict: I'd go, I'd be somewhat moved, but I'd call the show documents rather then artworks.

(8) Wine. A famous-vintage bottle refilled with excellent but younger wine, resealed, sold as the vintage. Verdict: I'm way less bothered by this than by (1), even though the structure looks identical — the pleasure was exactly the same before and after the revelation.

My tentative principle is that value attaches to achievement, and achievement is a relation between the object and the particular person-in-a-particular-situation who made it — so it isn't in the surface, but it isn't a famous name stapled on either. But (4), (5) and (8) all seem to break it, and (3) breaks something else entirely.

Could you state a principle, or a small set of clauses each with the case that forces it, that gets as many of these as possible? Precise enough that I can tell when it's being stretched. Then tell me what it predicts for cases I didn't list: a Strad versus a modern copy that beats it in a blind listening test, the arguments about whether cleaning old frescoes reveals them or strips them, a signed baseball, an anonymous medieval cathedral whose architect is entirely unknown. And be honest about which of my verdicts your principle can't save and has to overturn, and which of them you think are just snobbery dressed up as insight. If I have to lose something I'd rather lose (8) than (5), but tell me if that's backwards.
