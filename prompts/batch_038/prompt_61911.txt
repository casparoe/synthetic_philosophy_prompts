I'm putting together the final for an upper-level seminar called "How Do We Know That?" — archaeological inference, basically, co-taught by me and a colleague from anthropology. The students have already slogged through the standard material on site formation processes, taphonomy, the so-called Pompeii premise, and the logic of analogy, so I do not want a question that lets anybody score by defining or summarizing any of it. I want a document-based essay question built around a made-up excavation report (deliberately fictional — if it were a real site, half the class would just reproduce a Wikipedia digest of it), and I want the difficulty to come from a genuine crux rather than from the volume of material. The crux I have in mind is how far you can actually get from a pile of small standardized objects to a claim about an *institution* — a market, a taxing authority, a palace that guarantees and polices standards — and where exactly that inference breaks. My colleague the ceramicist thinks the storage jars in the burned room settle the whole thing, which is precisely the shortcut I want the marking scheme to punish.

Here's basically the data sheet I'll hand them. Fictional mound, "Sarnu Höyük," phase IIb, a single occupation horizon dated by radiocarbon to the mid-second millennium, in a river valley with dry farming plus some irrigation. Finds: 43 small stone and fired-clay objects, all undecorated and uninscribed, with no seals or iconography in the domestic contexts. Masses cluster at ~10 g (n=12), ~38 g (n=17), ~77 g (n=9), and ~153 g (n=2), tolerances within about 5 percent, plus three that fit no series at all (13 g, 27 g, 55 g). The 10 g group is perforated through the middle and shows cord-polish around the perforation; none of the larger ones are perforated. Contexts: 26 come from house floors in a residential quarter with no large storage facilities; 13 from a destruction fill above a burned room containing 14 pithoi with charred barley and irrigation-weed seeds, two of them lying next to stone cylinder seals; and 4 from a pit that cuts the IIb floor and is dated by its own pottery roughly 300 years later — that pit holds the only two objects made of stone sourced about 200 km away, and one that is a reworked fragment of an earlier statue. Two comparative notes on the sheet: known loom-weight assemblages elsewhere in the region are much sloppier in mass than this one, and spindle whorls in the same region typically fall in the 5–40 g range. I left out the cross-tabulation of mass against context on purpose, because I want to see if anyone notices that gap.

Please write: (1) the exam question itself, answerable in 75 minutes and worth 25 marks, with the data sheet assumed to be in front of the student; (2) a model answer of about 700 words; and (3) a marking scheme of five or six must-hit points, with marks per point totaling 25 and one line each on how students typically blow that point. I want the A/B boundary drawn sharply and behaviorally — my TA's position is that any question I write can be answered in one line by saying "context is everything," which is true and useless, so the scheme should make explicit that reciting that gets you a C. The classic B answer I want it to catch is the one that handles the rival hypotheses beautifully (loom weights? spindle whorls? curated heirlooms? an intrusive pit?) and then quietly slides from "okay, at least some of these were balance weights" to "therefore a central authority enforced the standard." An A answer should see, among other things, that tight standardization can be produced horizontally by repeated dyadic exchange with nobody policing it, and that pooling objects from three contexts of possibly different date may be manufacturing the very clustering the question asks them to explain. The question should force them to seperate "these were used for weighing" from "the standardization was imposed and maintained by some governing body," since those are two very different inferential leaps that students collapse constantly. Don't ask for citations, a bibliography, or a reading list anywhere in the exam or the model answer — the data sheet is all they get and I dont want them padding. American English throughout, please; our department style sheet is aggressively American. End your response with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that gives, in one sentence, the strongest claim about institutions this dataset actually supports.
