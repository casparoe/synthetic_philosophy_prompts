Been reading LW and the EA forum on and off for close to a decade, and I keep watching proverbs do argumentative work nobody bothers to check. Latest instance: a long thread about whether to donate now or invest and donate later. The give-later case as I understand it is basically arithmetic — money compounds at something like 5–7% real, so waiting only loses if the price of the best available good rises faster than the portfolio does. The give-now case is that cheap wins get picked off by other funders, plus you might die, change your mind, or lose control of the money somewhere along the way. I know the abstract distinction between risk-adjustment and pure time preference, what I dont have is a clean mapping from one sentence onto it.

One guy ended the back and fourth with 'a bird in the hand is worth two in the bush,' and everyone sort of nodded like it settled the matter. I want the disambiguation. What are the distinct precise claims that sentence could be making here — a menu, from the trivially true through the substantive to the outright false? For each one tell me which discounting mechanism it licenses (pure time preference, probability that the later good never materializes, rising marginal price of doing good, a commitment problem with your own future self, liquidity/opportunity cost, something else I haven't listed), whether it can be written as an inequality between two rates or whether it resists formalizing, and whether it survives a substitution: swap 'two in the bush' for a diversified index fund plus one specific well-known charity, and 'the hand' for cash you'd wire this afternoon.

Give me the whole thing as a table with these columns, in this order:

- reading — a short name for it
- precise_claim — the sentence restated as exactly one claim
- discount_mechanism — which mechanism from above, or "none"
- formal_version — the inequality between rates, or why there isn't one
- verdict — platitude / defensible / contentious / false
- gets_him_to_donate_now — yes / no / only-if, and if only-if, spell out the condition
- survives_the_substitution — yes / no / weakened, one clause of explanation

Then two short paragraphs after the table: which reading he most plausibly meant, and where exactly the slide happens. Please also flag any reading that is true but does no work at all, because I suspect there's at least one everyone agreed with that has zero implications for the actual question. No citations or reading list — I couldn't check them anyway.
