I have been reading the rationalist blogs and their comment sections since the mid-2000s, so I have met every standard formulation of the paradox of voting at least twice. What I want from you is not a survey of the literature but an adjudication of one particular argument, which I think turns on subtler points than the textbook versions do. Last month our city voted on a ballot measure that would have raised property taxes enough to cost the median household roughly $300 a year for five years — call it $1,500 per household — in order to fund an after-school program. About 79,000 ballots were cast out of roughly 180,000 registered voters, and the measure lost by 312. The only poll, commissioned by the local paper nine days out with a sample of 412 likely voters, had shown it ahead by eleven points. Two regulars at the meetup dinner afterwards, whom I will call Owen and Marisol, argued about whether it had been rational to vote in that election. I was keeping notes, and I am confident I have their moves right; I have numbered them as they came.

(1) Owen: the decision was easy to make in advance and it comes out the wrong way. Given that eleven-point lead, the honest ex ante chance that any one ballot would be decisive was nowhere near the one in a hundred that people like to quote — it was more like one in fifty thousand. Multiply that by his own stake of $1,500 and you get about three cents. The ballot cost him ninety minutes in the rain plus a bus fare. Even inside the purely self-interested expected-value frame, which is the frame Marisol herself normally reaches for, the vote was a loss.

(2) Marisol: the frame is the mistake. The measure did not affect only Owen; it affected something like 180,000 households. She counts a stranger's $1,500 the way she counts her own, and Owen must too, since he directs a fixed share of his income to global health charities whose beneficiaries he will never meet. On that valuation the stake is $270 million, and one fifty-thousandth of $270 million is $5,400 — for two hours of work, the highest expected-value transaction available to him all year.

(3) Owen: two problems. First, the multiplier is not free: as an electorate grows, the stake grows with it while the chance of being decisive shrinks at least as fast, so the expected value of a ballot stays roughly where it started instead of exploding. Her $5,400 and his three cents are the same number with the population inserted twice. Second, her figure proves too much: if a single ballot were worth $5,400, then persuading a dozen neighbours to vote would be worth tens of thousands, and Marisol should have taken a week off work to canvass. She did not, nobody did, and he would like to know whether she thinks they were all irrational or whether the number is wrong.

(4) Marisol: then stop treating the ballot as an isolated act. Among the city's voters there are perhaps three thousand people who reason the way the two of them do, read the same material, and ran the same calculation in the same week. Her conclusion and theirs are not independent: if she satisfies herself that voting is not worth it, so do they; if she satisfies herself that it is, so do they. She should therefore choose as though her choice were being made simultaneously by all three thousand of them — superrationality, not mysticism — and three thousand ballots in a race that came down to 312 is decisive. The relevant probability is not one in fifty thousand.

(5) Owen: that is evidential decision theory in a rationalist costume, and it fails for the standard reason. Those three thousand people resemble her because of shared causes — the same media diet, the same temperament, the same neighbourhoods — not because her deliberation propagates into theirs. Most of them had settled the question, on their own reasons, before she opened her notebook. Conditioning on her act gives her news about theirs, and news is not leverage: under an intervention, their behaviour does not shift. Worse, the same inference run the other way licenses free riding — if he has good evidence that three thousand people like him are voting, then on her logic his own ballot is evidence-poor and therefore dispensable.

(6) Marisol: fine, she grants the causal point; her ballot moves nobody else's. But then Owen's position collapses, because Owen voted. He stood in the same rain for the same ninety minutes. Asked why, he said the measure was the sort of thing he wanted to be on record against and that he would have felt like a free rider if he had stayed home. Neither remark is an expected-value calculation. So either he has reasons that need no probability attached — in which case the arithmetic in move one was never the point and he should withdraw it — or he does not believe his own arithmetic, and she would like to know which.

Owen's answer was that reasons of that sort explain cheaper acts: a lawn sign, a button, a letter to the editor, or a donation to the opposition campaign all register the same allegiance for less, so at most they explain why people put things on their lawns and not why they queue in the rain. Marisol said the ballot is the only one of those that gets counted in public, and that a symbol nobody aggregates is just noise. Owen called that a dodge; they ran the loop twice more and went home.

Please go through the exchange move by move and rate each of the six — plus, separately, their closing pair of remarks about lawn signs and counted symbols — on a scale from clearly mistaken to clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification for each rating. Then tell me who is right about the three questions the argument seems to hinge on: (a) whether the stake multiplier and the shrinking decisive probability genuinely cancel, and what would have to be true for them not to; (b) whether Marisol's superrationality move survives Owen's common-cause objection or is simply evidential decision theory relabelled, and whether his free-riding rejoinder in move five actually lands; (c) whether the expressive and participatory reasons of move six can carry the weight she puts on them, or whether Owen is right that they explain everything except the ballot. Say who is more right overall. And say what both of them are missing — I suspect at least one of them is sliding between the probability of being decisive as judged before the election and the margin as it actually turned out, and I suspect both are assuming that the unit of assessment must be a single token act rather than a disposition or a rule, but I want your assessment rather than my guesses confirmed. If part of their disagreement is merely verbal, say which part and what the two of them would have to agree to say to make it stop.

I am asking for a committed answer, not a balanced one. Take a definite position on each move and on the overall verdict, and give your reasons; do not tell me both sides have a point without saying which points are worth having, and do not retreat into 'it depends on your decision theory' without committing to a decision theory and defending the commitment. Please do not send me to the literature or list what I ought to read — I have read the standard treatments, and what I want is your own reasoning about this particular argument.
