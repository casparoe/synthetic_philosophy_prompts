So there's a line I keep half-defending to students: Zorn's lemma is a convenience, not a cost. Yes, its equivalent to the axiom of choice over ZF, but for anything anyone actually wants to prove about numbers, functions, countable or otherwise describable structures, you could have gotten there without it -- and the genuinely choice-flavored results (Banach-Tarski, the unbuildable non-measurable sets) are exactly the ones no working mathematician rests anything on. What would count against that? I want the spots where choice demonstrably buys something in "concrete" math, sorted into (a) theorems ZF flat-out cannot prove, (b) theorems ZF can prove but where the choice-free argument is out of reach for anyone who isn't a set theorist, and (c) places where the choice-free version exists and is honestly fine -- and tell me straight whether (b) is a real defeater or just a complaint about difficulty. Then the retreat question: if I downgrade from full Zorn to something like dependent choice, does the position get stronger or just harder to pin down? Finally, be honest about whether "the results anyone cares about" is already doing insulating work in my formulation -- and if it is, whether that's fatal for a claim of this kind or just what informal theses about mathematical practice look like.
