My reading group is doing a popular book on honesty and deception, and the chapter on the church fathers has this long section on Augustine that I want to sanity-check before we meet Thursday, because half the group is already treating it as settled. Here's roughly what it says: "Augustine wrote two treatises on lying, one early and one near the end of his life, and between them he staked out the most extreme position in the history of ethics — no lie is ever permitted, regardless of consequences. But he's not a crude absolutist. He grades lies by seriousness, and at the very bottom of his list is the joke, the lie told in jest where nobody is actually taken in, which he considers harmless enough that it isn't really a sin at all. Notice also that his reason for banning lying has nothing to do with the harm lies do to the people you lie to. It's that speech was given to us in order to make known what's in our minds, so lying turns language against its own purpose and corrodes the liar's soul — which is exactly why the harmless white lie is still wrong even when it helps everybody. And he leaves the escape hatches open: if a man comes to your door hunting someone you're hiding, you owe him nothing, and silence, refusing to answer, and technically-true-but-misleading speech are all available to you. That last permission makes Augustine the grandfather of the mental-reservation loophole the Catholic casuists later ran with. As for scripture, he deals with the Hebrew midwives who lied to Pharaoh by pointing out that God rewarded them, which shows he knows how to bend when life is at stake. So he ends up in the same place as Kant on the murderer at the door, and for the same reason, which is why the two are always taught as a pair — and why neither is any use for thinking about lying to protect people."
