**QUESTION 3 (25 marks)**

Aumann's result that Bayesians with a common prior cannot agree to disagree is standardly stated under the assumption that the agents' posterior probabilities are *common knowledge*, rather than merely mutually known to some finite order.

(a) State the result and sketch its proof.

(b) What exactly is lost if common knowledge is replaced by mutual knowledge to a high but finite order?

(c) In a finite game of perfect information such as the centipede game, does common knowledge of rationality secure the backward-induction outcome? What should we say about a player who finds herself at a node which the analysis implies will never be reached?

**CANDIDATE'S SCRIPT**

Aumann's theorem concerns agents who are Bayesians in the technical sense. Each begins with the same prior probability distribution over a common, finite state space; each is equipped with a partition of that space, representing what she can distinguish; and each updates by conditionalising on the cell of her partition that contains the true state. The theorem says that if two such agents' posterior probabilities for some event are common knowledge between them, then those posteriors are equal. Agents with a common prior cannot agree to disagree: they cannot both know, in the common-knowledge sense, that they assign different probabilities to the same event.

It is worth being clear about what the theorem does not say, because it is often mis-summarised. It is not the trivial observation that agents who have seen exactly the same evidence must agree; of course they must. Its content is rather that private information makes far less difference than one would expect: two Bayesians who have been given quite different signals must nevertheless arrive at the same posterior, so long as each of them knows what the other has concluded. Persistent disagreement is therefore not a stable resting place for rational agents, and wherever we find it in practice we should look either for a failure to conditionalise properly or for agents who do not in fact share a prior.

Knowledge is modelled as a partition. Agent i knows E at a state if the cell of i's partition containing that state lies entirely within E. "Everyone knows E" is then the event at which every agent knows E; mutual knowledge of order k is defined by iterating this operator k times; and common knowledge is the infinite conjunction — everyone knows that everyone knows that everyone knows — for every finite depth. The proof of the theorem runs through the finest common coarsening of the two agents' partitions, which is the partition whose cells are the units within which knowledge can be commonly had. Consider the event that agent 1's posterior for A is p and agent 2's is q. If that event is common knowledge, it must be a union of whole cells of the common coarsening. But on any such cell, neither agent's posterior for A can vary, and each is simply the prior probability of A conditional on that cell. So p and q are both equal to that quantity, and hence equal to each other. The common prior does the work at the final step: without it, two agents could conditionalise on the same cell and still differ, because they would be starting from different distributions over it.

It is tempting to think that the infinite regress in the definition of common knowledge is a formality, and for most purposes it is. What the argument requires is not the infinite conjunction as such but enough depth that neither agent can locate a state in which the other might be wrong about her being wrong; and in the models that are actually written down the depth required is small. The literature on almost-common knowledge bears this out. In Rubinstein's email game, two players wish to coordinate their actions conditional on a state of the world, and confirmation of that state is sent by a chain of messages, each of which may be lost with some small probability. Rubinstein's point is that for any such probability, however small, the players behave essentially as they would if the state were common knowledge: the epistemic situation in which knowledge is merely very deep is continuous with the epistemic situation in which it is common, and any discontinuity one hears about is an artefact of taking a limit rather than a fact of economic substance. Common knowledge is thus best understood as a convenient idealisation, the limiting case of a family of epistemic situations all of which support the same conclusions to within any desired accuracy. It follows that the theorist who objects that common knowledge can never be established by unreliable private messages has not shown that the conclusions of epistemic game theory fail; she has shown only that they hold approximately.

The centipede game puts the idealisation under more pressure. Two players alternately choose whether to take or to pass, and the pot grows as they pass; taking ends the game and gives the taker the larger share. If the game is finite and this is common knowledge, and if both players are rational and this too is common knowledge, the backward-induction analysis applies. Reasoning from the last node, each player prefers to take rather than pass whenever the game reaches her; the player before her therefore prefers to take rather than pass; and so on back to the first node. Player 1 takes immediately, and both receive less than they could have had by passing for many rounds. This is the familiar puzzle: the argument is valid, and yet its conclusion is unattractive for both players, and in experiments people pass.

Suppose, then, that we are watching such a game and player 1 passes. What should we say? The backward-induction argument has established that passing is inconsistent with common knowledge of rationality, so the observation refutes it: player 1 is not rational, or at any rate player 2 can no longer believe that he is. Player 2, being rational herself, should revise accordingly. She should treat player 1 as someone who is not playing the game as the analysis describes him, and therefore as unpredictable. But a player who cannot rely on her opponent to take when it is his turn to take cannot rely on the pot reaching her again, so player 2 should take at her first opportunity. The general moral is that common knowledge of rationality is a fragile assumption — fragile not because it demands an infinite chain of reasoning, but because a single piece of behaviour off the predicted path destroys it, and once it is destroyed what the player at the unexpected node ought to believe depends on which assumption she gives up instead: that her opponent knows the payoffs, that he is of the standard type, or that he never errs. The framework itself supplies no answer to that question.
