I write a fortnightly column on evidence, statistics, and public disagreement for a general-interest magazine, and each spring I teach a two-week intensive module, Disagreement and Its Discontents, in a graduate program for science journalists. The module closes with a supervised take-home that candidates have three hours to complete. I would like you to write one question for that assessment on the common prior assumption, together with a model answer of about 700 words and a marking scheme.

The question must be unanswerable by reciting Aumann's agreement theorem. Everyone in my module has read a summary of the result, so an item of the form explain why rational Bayesians who share a prior cannot agree to disagree would be pure recall. Please build the question around the following case, which I invented and which candidates will be given as the stem:

Two rival publications each employ a full-time forecaster. Both subscribe to the same commercial wire services and the same regulatory data releases; neither has private access to anything the other lacks. Each month both go on the record with a probability for one binary proposition — that a particular drug will receive approval before the end of the calendar year — and each publishes a short note explaining what moved them. Both are competent, both are honest, both know the other is competent and honest, and all of this is a matter of public record. One was trained as an epidemiologist, the other as an econometrician, and each maintains that their training is what their starting point reflects. In fourteen months their published numbers have never coincided. My editor now proposes a consensus protocol: the two will continue alternating monthly publications until their numbers match, at which point the magazine runs the agreed figure as its house forecast.

The question should ask whether the agreement theorem implies that the protocol must terminate, and whether the fourteen months of divergence show that at least one of the two forecasters has been irrational throughout. Specifically, I want the item to force candidates to:

(a) separate three things that are routinely run together — the common prior as a modelling device, common knowledge of posteriors as a premise of the theorem, and the claim that persistent disagreement is by itself evidence of irrationality;

(b) see why the inference from both know the other's number, and both have said so in print, to therefore they have common knowledge is a non-sequitur, and say what common knowledge actually costs;

(c) consider whether the protocol is rescued by repeated public announcement rather than a single exchange, and under what conditions such a back-and-forth converges — including the possibilities that it takes arbitrarily many rounds or fails to terminate at all;

(d) say what is at stake in the assumption itself: why economics absorbed the idea of personal utility but resisted the idea of personal probability, and whether genuinely heterogeneous priors describe a real possibility or merely redescribe the same behaviour in a different vocabulary.

The candidates are writers, not mathematicians. They should be able to answer in prose, with at most a toy example involving a handful of states; nothing in the question or the model answer should require measure theory. If the item can carry a brief second part asking why the same reasoning is used to argue that mutually beneficial speculative trade should be impossible, that would be a bonus, but only if it does not bloat the question.

Please deliver: the question exactly as it would appear on the paper; a model answer of about 700 words of the kind I would want to read from my best candidate; and a marking scheme out of 25 that names the must-hit points as discrete items with marks attached, states what a mid-range answer earning around 15 does that the first-class answer does not, and lists three mistakes that should cost marks together with the specific confusion each one signals.

No reading list, please. Name a result or a paper only where the name does argumentative work, and only if you are confident the attribution is correct.

Finally, tell me how confident you are in the technical claims the model answer makes — in particular anything about what the agreement theorem does and does not require, and anything about the convergence of iterated announcement — and single out the one point in the whole package you are least sure of, so that I can have it checked before the paper goes out.
