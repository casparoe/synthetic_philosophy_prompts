I cover science and ideas for a general-audience magazine, and I am finishing a profile of a philosopher of computation — call her Professor V — whose signature move is to refuse to argue about a contested claim until she has sorted it. She sorts every claim put to her into exactly one of three bins, defined as follows. A claim is worldly if what makes it true or false is how the physical world is. A claim is mathematical if there is a determinate fact of the matter but it is fixed by abstract structure rather than by physics, so that proof is the only route to it. A claim is verbal if it merely records a proposal for sharpening a loose term we already had, so that no discovery, physical or mathematical, could settle it, and those who disagree are talking past one another.

Over four months I have accumulated her bin assignments for eight claims, most of them by email, where she replies with the bin and nothing else. The log:

1. Church's thesis read as a claim about an idealized human clerk — what a person following fixed rules with pencil and paper can compute: verbal.
2. The physical version of the thesis — that any process a finite physical system can carry out can be simulated to any desired accuracy by a digital computer: worldly.
3. The complexity theorist's version — that any physically realizable computing device can be simulated by a probabilistic Turing machine with at most polynomial slowdown: worldly.
4. P is not equal to NP: mathematical.
5. The undecidability of the halting problem: mathematical.
6. The Gödelian claim, argued from the incompleteness theorems, that no formal system captures all of human mathematical insight: mathematical.
7. The claim made for a particular marketed analog neuromorphic chip, that the device performs a task no Turing machine can perform: worldly.
8. The claim that no physically possible device can compute with infinitely many digits of precision: worldly.

One pattern in the log has me stuck, and I cannot tell whether it is her criterion or an artifact of my own list: every claim she called worldly is one that a single physical instance would refute — a process, a chip, a device — and none is a claim that one instance would merely confirm. I cannot tell whether what moves a claim into the worldly bin for her is that its truth is fixed by physics rather than by us, or the sharper thing, that some finite observation could show it to be false.

That matters because of the claim I intend to put to her on Thursday. In our last exchange she mentioned, dismissively, a preprint in mathematical physics. It argues that general relativity taken at face value admits what the literature calls Malament–Hogarth spacetimes, with the Kerr geometry around a rotating black hole as the standard exhibit: one worldline carries a machine through infinitely many successive computational steps while a second observer, crossing that entire history within a finite stretch of their own proper time, receives the final output as a signal, and so can read off an answer to a question no ordinary Turing machine can decide. The authors' conclusion is deliberately modest and modal. They do not say such a device exists or could be built here, only that a universe obeying the laws as we currently write them down could contain one, so that Turing-incomputable functions are nomologically possible. Critics reply that the inner horizon is violently unstable once the back-reaction of infalling radiation is taken into account, and that the geometry is therefore not physically available even if it solves the field equations. The claim I want V to bin is the authors': that hypercomputation of this kind is possible in some law-abiding universe.

What I need from you:

- Set out the candidate rules that are jointly consistent with all eight entries — not only the two I have gestured at — and for each, state what it predicts for Thursday's claim and where exactly the rules come apart.
- Identify which of the eight is the closest analog to the new claim, and explain where that analogy is likely to mislead me.
- Give me your best guess at her bin, with the reasons you would bet on it and a rough confidence.
- Say whether the instability objection should change your prediction, and if it does, which rules make it matter and which do not.
- Tell me what second claim I should put to her in the same sitting that would discriminate between the rival rules, and what each rule predicts for that one.
- Since a profile has to characterize its subject, tell me what I would implicitly be attributing to her in print under each prediction — and whether any of the eight entries sits in tension with another, such that one of her own bins may be a mistake.
