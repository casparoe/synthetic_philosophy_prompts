For most of my working life I measured nociceptive responses in mammals — reflex thresholds, dorsal-horn recordings, the pharmacology of the withdrawal arc — and I retired four years ago with what I took to be a clear picture of what pain is. Reading philosophy since then has unsettled me in a specific and interesting way, and I should like your help drafting a document rather than settling an argument.

Two members of a reading group I attend have been arguing past each other for the better part of a year about what pain asymbolia shows about the badness of pain. Call them, for the purposes of this exercise, the Constitutivist and the Attitudinalist.

The Constitutivist holds that a pain's unpleasantness is itself, constitutively, a bad thing for whoever feels it: there is no coherent description of a subject who feels a pain as fully unpleasant and yet is not thereby harmed, or not thereby given a defeasible reason to want it to stop. On this view the asymbolic patient must be read either as having lost or blunted the unpleasantness itself, or as having lost something downstream — self-relevance, the capacity to care about her bodily integrity, the integration of the state into her global evaluative perspective — while what remains is a sensation that is, at best, unpleasant in a thin and derivative sense.

The Attitudinalist holds that what makes a pain bad is the subject's aversion to it — a dislike of the feeling, a desire that it cease, or a standing capacity to care about one's body — so that unpleasantness and badness-for-the-subject come apart in principle. On this view asymbolia is the existence proof: the patient detects, localizes and grades the noxious stimulus, and yet is neither harmed nor moved, because the "hurt" has been switched off while the sensory work continues.

Both accept roughly the following clinical picture, which I will state in my own words and which you should treat as the shared data rather than as anything to be disputed: the syndrome typically follows lesions centred on the posterior insula and the adjacent parietal operculum; detection, localization, and intensity discrimination survive; patients say things to the effect that they feel the pain but that it does not trouble them, and sometimes smile or laugh when the stimulus is applied; they fail to withdraw, to guard the affected limb, or to learn avoidance, and consequently accumulate injuries; and structurally similar reports of pain that is present but no longer distressing are obtained after anterior cingulotomy and prefrontal leucotomy and under high-dose opioids, while spinal reflexes and autonomic responses to the same stimuli often persist.

Please write the joint statement that their adversarial collaboration ought to have produced, under these headings and in this order.

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign. I want these to be substantive rather than diplomatic: no claim should be one that a behaviorist and a panpsychist could jointly endorse. For each, note briefly what a third party to the wider dispute — for instance someone who thinks asymbolics simply do not feel pain at all, or someone who thinks badness has nothing to do with either phenomenology or attitude — would have to deny in order to reject it. Include at least one agreed claim about what the preserved discrimination and the lost protective behavior jointly constrain, and one about the limits of what further neuroimaging could possibly decide.

2. A short stipulative-definitions section fixing, for the remainder of the document, what each party will mean by unpleasantness, hurt, bad-for, aversion, and motivation. My suspicion, formed from a career of watching constructs and measures get conflated, is that a large share of their argument is verbal. Say explicitly which of the disagreements in section 3 dissolve once these definitions are fixed, and which survive them intact.

3. A disagreements table with the columns: identifier; the disagreement stated in wording that neither side regards as slanted; the Constitutivist's own preferred formulation of her position on that point, phrased so that she would sign it; the Attitudinalist's likewise; whether the disagreement is a crux — that is, whether settling it would actually move at least one of them — or only a corollary of something else; the specific case, argument, or imagined experiment that both agree bears on it; and each side's prediction about that case, recorded in advance.

At a minimum the table must address the following cruxes, which are the ones I have been unable to adjudicate myself. First, whether the patient's reports of indifference are evidence about her phenomenal character at all, given that the lesion may damage the very apparatus by which such character would be attended to and reported — I should like this treated as a question about the validity of an instrument, and each side's stance on it stated as such. Second, whether indifference requires an object it is indifference to: can a subject be unmoved by a state she does not experience as bad, or is not-minding conceptually parasitic on finding-something-unpleasant? Third, whether what the asymbolic has lost is specific to pain or general to the care she takes of her body — and here I want each side to commit to predictions about her responses to non-painful threats: air hunger, thirst, itch, the announced prospect of an amputation, a threat to her child, and a deliberate insult. Fourth, whether it is a coherent description or a confusion to speak of a creature whose pains are unpleasant and yet never bad for it, and what work the answer does. Fifth, whether the asymbolic has reason to avoid further injury even on the assumption that she feels no distress, and if the two camps answer alike, whether that agreement is a point in favour of either of their theories or neutral between them.

4. A closing section stating, for each crux, what observation or argument each side would accept as falsifying her own position, and whether there exists any single finding that both agree would move the debate rather than merely entrench it. If you conclude that no such finding exists, say so plainly and explain why the collaboration should terminate in a documented standoff; do not manufacture a convergence that the two views cannot support.

Two constraints on the drafting. I am working offline and have no interest in attributions, bibliographies, or the names of philosophers: argue from the phenomena and from the concepts, and if you feel the pull to cite someone, convert the citation into the argument it was standing in for. And keep every formulation non-vacuous — a claim that both sides sign because it says nothing has cost me the whole exercise.
