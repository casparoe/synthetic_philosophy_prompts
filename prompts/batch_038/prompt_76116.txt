I'm 68 and spent my working life as a limnologist with a state water survey — lakes, mostly, oxygen profiles and algal counts, the unglamorous end of ecology. Since retiring I've been reading philosophy rather greedily, and I'm in a Tuesday book group that started on Hume's second Enquiry and, as these things go, drifted into the Treatise, which is a lot denser than I expected. The stretch I'm stuck on is the section in Book 3 on why promises oblige — I think it's Part II, section V, though I'm going from memory and my paperback's pagination isn't worth citing. I don't want general Hume commentary; I want help getting the argument in that one stretch straight, because reading it I feel like I agree with every sentence and still can't say what he's actually established.

Here's the bit in my own words, so neither of us has to fight the eighteenth-century punctuation. He says the obligation of a promise isn't something you find in nature the way you might find the wrongness of cruelty — it depends on a convention, a practice people have grown into over time. Then comes the move that has me: a promise can't work by a bare act of will, because you cannot will an obligation into being. A resolution to do something doesn't bind you, and wanting to be bound doesn't produce bindingness any more than wanting to feel gratitude produces gratitude; obligation is a matter of sentiment, and sentiments aren't at the disposal of the will. What the spoken formula does instead is create an expectation in the person you're addressing, and the convention — together with our approval of people who honour expectations they've deliberately raised — is what turns that expectation into something you're stuck with. So the bindingness comes from outside, from the practice and other people's sentiments, and the speaker's intention does far less work than we imagine. Somewhere near there he also uses the case of two men pulling the oars of a boat, who cooperate by convention without ever promising anything, and then argues a promise adds something over and above that.

Now my problem, which isn't academic and is why I care. Three years ago I promised a colleague, Don, in a hospital room, that I'd get our thirty-one years of lake data into a public archive with his field notes intact, and finish the summary volume he never got to. He died nine months later. Nothing enforces any of it. The survey has been reorganised twice since, half the notebooks are in a box in my garage, and no one still alive would ever know if I quietly let the whole thing lapse — my younger ex-colleagues think it's a poor use of my time anyway. So on Hume's account, what exactly is binding me here? The convention is obviously still running and I suppose my keeping this promise is one drop supporting the practice in general, but this particular promise pays nothing to anyone, and the expectation he makes so central has no one left to hold it. I've started calling these orphan promises (my wife says I coin terms like its a job), and I want to know whether the practice-and-utility story is even the right machinery for them, or whether something else in his kit does the work — the sentiment, the fact that I'd despise myself, the way a general rule keeps its grip even when this instance of it is useless. Walk me through which of his moves I'm leaning on, and be blunt about whether my case is one his framework handles gracefully or one it should predict doesn't bind and therefore gets wrong. And if the honest answer turns out to be that it's just me feeling bad plus everyone approving of people who don't, say so plainly — I'm trying to work out whether the moral-sentiment talk in that section is load-bearing or decoration.
