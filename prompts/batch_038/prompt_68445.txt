Please take on the role of a working philosopher who also writes jokes for a living — someone equally at home with the secondary literature on moral revolutions and with tightening a punchline until it actually lands. Think carefully before producing anything, and show me the process: I want a short brainstorming stage first, not just a finished bit.

Here is the situation. I am a junior, and this semester my AP European History class has been working through the abolition of the Atlantic slave trade, while my school's philosophy club has been reading the honor-code account of moral revolutions — roughly the Williams/Appiah line that dueling, footbinding, and slavery did not end because someone finally produced a decisive argument. The arguments were already on the table, and in some cases had been sitting there for a century doing nothing at all. What changed was honor: the practices stopped being what a gentleman, or a respectable family, or a Christian nation did, and once they became faintly ridiculous rather than merely wrong, they collapsed remarkably fast — frequently at the hands of the very people who had the largest stake in the old code. I am giving the club's final talk of the year to about fifteen students and two teachers who have read considerably more than I have, and I would like to open with a joke instead of a summary nobody wants to hear.

For the brainstorming stage, give me four or five candidate angles, one or two lines each, stating for every one of them precisely which piece of the theory the punchline turns on and who or what the target is. Then choose the strongest and write it out in full. The constraints matter: it must be deliverable aloud in under about twenty-five seconds; no walk-into-a-bar framing and no pun on any philosopher's name; the joke should be one that a listener cannot fully enjoy without knowing the theory, so that the payoff is proportional to how much of the argument you have actually followed; and it has to be fair, in the sense that the theorist himself could laugh at it rather than wince at a straw man. My favorite angle, if you can make it work, is the self-undermining one: a theory whose content is that long careful arguments are not what moves anyone, delivered in the form of a long careful argument. Please do not attach a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations, since I have no way of checking them, and do not explain the joke after telling it. Alongside your chosen version, give me the runner-up and one line of delivery advice, specifically where to pause.
