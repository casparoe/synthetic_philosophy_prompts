I retired six years ago after thirty-five years in experimental physics, and I have been reading philosophy of science with more time at my disposal than I ever had before. Our reading group has just come through a stretch on the epistemology of disagreement — the conciliationist line, on which learning that an epistemic peer dissents should move me substantially toward their view, and the steadfast line, on which I may rationally hold my ground because my reasons for thinking I assessed the evidence competently are not undone by the bare fact of dissent — and I should like both positions tested against an episode from my own side of the fence rather than against thought experiments about splitting restaurant bills.

Take Alfred Wegener and continental drift. I supply the facts so that you need not: Wegener was trained in astronomy and made his career in meteorology and polar research; he advanced the drift hypothesis in a lecture in Frankfurt in January 1912 and in book form in 1915, arguing from the fit of the Atlantic margins, from matching geology and fossil faunas on continents now separated by ocean, and from paleoclimatic evidence such as glacial deposits in present tropical latitudes. The orthodox alternative was permanence — fixed continents and ocean basins, with submerged land bridges to explain shared faunas and a cooling, shrinking Earth to explain mountain belts. In the mid-1920s a symposium of American petroleum geologists in New York took up the hypothesis, and the response on the record was overwhelmingly hostile; the papers appeared in a volume a couple of years later. The most damaging objection, however, was physical rather than geological: Harold Jeffreys argued that the Earth's outer layers possessed far too much strength to permit continents to plough through ocean floor, and that the forces Wegener could name — tidal drag, and a tendency to slip toward the equator — came up short of what the motion required by orders of magnitude. Wegener died on the Greenland ice in 1930 with the hypothesis generally rejected. Arthur Holmes pressed mantle convection, driven by radioactive heat, as a mechanism from the late 1920s onward; paleomagnetic measurements in the 1950s yielded apparently incompatible polar-wander paths for Europe and North America, which the symposium S. K. Runcorn convened in 1956 read as evidence of relative motion; and by the mid-1960s seafloor magnetic anomalies, seafloor spreading, and a reconstruction of the continental fit at the shelf edge rather than the shoreline had converted the whole into plate tectonics. Treat my chronology as approximately right but not load-bearing: if you think a date or an attribution is wrong, say so in one line and carry on.

What I want is the analysis, not a retelling. Begin by mapping the episode onto the concepts with some discipline — who counts as a party to the disagreement, in what respect each was or was not a peer of the others, what each party's total evidence actually consisted of at the time, and whether the conciliationist's standing assumption that peers share the evidence and diverge only in weighing it holds here at all. Then say what each position prescribes: what Wegener ought to have done with his confidence when effectively every specialist in the relevant data dissented, and what steadfastness permits him to retain.

Three complications interest me most, and I would rather you wrestle with them than smooth them away. First, peerhood seems to fragment across the components of the dispute: Wegener was arguably the peer of the geologists in physical reasoning and their inferior in stratigraphy, while Jeffreys was a peer in the physics and no party to the geological evidence. Does the conciliationist apparatus have anything to say when two people are peers on one part of a question and non-peers on another, or does such a case simply fall outside its stated scope — and if the latter, is the theory then unfalsifiable in precisely the cases worth examining? Second, the near-unanimity of the geological community was not, I suspect, the aggregate of many independent assessments: it flowed from a common framework and a common training, so that its errors were correlated. Tell me which way that cuts. Correlated error should discount the weight of a consensus, yet the dissenter cannot determine from inside whether the consensus is correlated or genuinely independent; if there is any test he could apply in advance rather than a rationalization available only afterward, I should like to see it. Third, much of the quarrel concerned evidential standards rather than the hypothesis — whether a claim lacking a mechanism may be believed at all. If that is what the parties disagree about, is it still the kind of disagreement these theories were built to handle? Note also the uncomfortable decomposition: Wegener was right about the phenomenon and wrong about the forces; Jeffreys was right about the forces and wrong about the phenomenon. Break each party's commitments into components and say which were rationally held then, and which the eventual outcome retroactively licenses or condemns.

Finally, and I insist upon this: hold hindsight in abeyance while doing the normative work. State what each side ought to have believed without knowing how the story ended, and only then ask what the vindication reveals about the theories — whether it confirms steadfastness, embarrasses conciliationism, or exposes something neither was designed to accommodate. Where a theory is silent, say that it is silent. I have no use for an episode compelled to speak.
