So I'm a freelance science-and-ideas writer — long reads mostly, plus a fair bit of broadcast chat — and for the last eighteen months I've been circling the same territory from different angles: how anyone decides what actually explains what happened in the past. I did a piece on the row over a museum's slavery galleries, another on the cliometricians feeding parish registers into regression models, and a third on all those books that promise to explain the twentienth century in one grand sweep. Reading around for those, I fell down the philosophy-of-history rabbit hole, and now a foundation that gives six-month fellowships to non-academics has invited me to send a two-page proposal. Deadline's in a fortnight and the idea is still wobbling. Here's the wobble, as best I can put it.

The old covering-law row starts with Hempel's 1942 paper: a historical explanation is really just an explanation sketch, a truncated appeal to general laws that you'd have to fill in to make it respectable. The reply from the other side — Dray on explaining an action by showing it was the appropriate thing to do given what the agent believed, Danto on narrative sentences that can only be understood from a later vantage point than the events they describe, Mink and White on narrative as a mode of comprehension rather than a copy of one — is that Hempel has misdescribed the activity. My hunch, which I can't yet defend, is that the two sides have been talking past each other about the explanandum rather than about the method. When a historian says she's explaining the French Revolution, or why printing took off in one region and not another, or the demographic aftermath of the Black Death, "why did an event of type T occur?" isn't the only question on the table. There's at least one other — following a particular course of events and seeing how it hung together — and there's a third sitting between them, which I think is where the interesting fight is: relevance. Given a thousand true things about 1789, which of them explain anything, and how would you tell? Hempel's model is notoriously bad at answering that (the relevance and maximal-specificity problems), and narrative appears to answer it by unity of plot, which then looks like an aesthetic or at best pragmatic criterion dressed up as an epistemic one. So my question is roughly: is narrative unity doing genuine work as a criterion of explanatory relevance, or is it only repackaging whatever relevance the underlying causal claims already carried?

Can you turn that into the actual two-page proposal for me? I need the question stated sharply enough that it could have a determinate answer rather than dissolving into a mood; why the existing positions don't already settle it — and please be honest if you think they do; and the method. On the method I'd want you to think hard about what a method even is for someone like me who isn't a professional philosopher. My instinct is a handful of case studies: take three or four real historical explanations apart and ask, of each, what would count as completing it. But you should say whether that's the right move or just journalism wearing a philosophy costume, and why the obvious alternatives — straight conceptual analysis, or a survey of what historians say they're doing — are worse. Then: what a result would look like at the end of six months, what would count as the project failing in a way I could recognise from the inside, and a candid note on whether this is too big or too specialised for a non-academic in the time available, and if so what I should cut. Skip the citations and the literature review, I'll add those myself. British English throughout please, and don't be afraid to tell me the idea is weaker than I think it is.
