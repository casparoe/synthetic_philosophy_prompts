Longtime lurker on the rationalist side of the internet, so apologies in advance for the format: I've got a set of gut verdicts plus a rule I invented to explain them, and I want somebody to break the rule.

Topic is Chesterton's fence — when you're allowed to tear down a practice whose rationale nobody can state. I know the standard moves: Chesterton's point that the reformer who can't see why the fence is there is exactly the wrong person to remove it, Burke on the latent wisdom stuffed into inherited arrangements. What I don't have is a line, because my verdicts don't look like they come from one place.

My rule, which I'll call the Beam Test: a practice gets to stay only if someone can name a thing that breaks when it goes — right now, not originally — and the naming has to happen before removal, not as a story you tell after the damage shows up. Three clauses. (a) Burden on the remover. (b) The function has to be current, not historical. (c) Because the practices still standing are a selected sample — we only ever meet the ones that survived — the prior should lean toward there being a beam even when nobody can point at it. That last clause is my favorite and also the one I most suspect is doing sneaky work.

Cases:

1. HOA rule against parking an RV in the driveway. Nobody at the meeting could say where it came from. I said tear it down, no investigation needed. Still think that was right.

2. Two laws, same state, same year — I'm making them up so nobody has to look anything up. A Sunday ban on selling margarine, and a requirement that milk be sold only in sealed containers. Nobody alive remembers why either exists. My verdicts: repeal the margarine one on the spot, and don't touch the milk one until somebody has actually demonstrated it does nothing.

3. The ham parable. Cut both ends off. Why? Because my mother did. Why? Because her pan was too small. Reason surfaces, practice dies, and I feel good about it.

4. Now the one that wrecks me. A monastery, again hypothetical, where the monks eat in silence; the archive shows the rule went in during the 1640s to stop arguments at table; the arguments were about something long dead. So the original function isn't merely unknown, it's known and obsolete — and I still want them to keep the silence. Same shape as 3, opposite verdict.

5. Shellfish taboo where everyone's forgotten the reason. Keep it until there's real toxin testing, then drop it with zero regret. Compare 4: I don't feel zero regret there and I can't say why.

6. Village footpath that bends for no reason anybody can state. Widening it feels reckless to me even though I cannot name one thing that would break. But that's the same epistemic situation as 1, where I was happy to bulldoze. The difference I reach for is that the path's reason might be physical and unrecoverable — a spring, a dead hedge line, a boundary — while the RV rule's reason was somebody's taste. Except somebody's taste is also a reason, and I don't actually know that the RV rule wasn't about something physical too, like drainage or the sight line at the corner.

7. School requiring two years of Latin. Someone finally explains the current function: it sorts kids into tracks and signals something about their families. The beam is named, it's load baring, and what it's holding up is a thing I think is bad. I want it gone. So (b) can't be the whole story, since "there's a function" and "keep it" came apart.

So: is there one principle that gets most of these? I'd rather have a slightly ugly principle that keeps 3/4 and 1/6 both distinct than a clean one that flattens them, but tell me if thats just me refusing to update. State it precisely, say which case each clause is there to handle, then tell me straight which of my verdicts it can't save and which one you're overturning. And give me its ruling on a case I haven't listed — ideally one you construct so that it splits your two best candidate principles, so I can go check my gut on it and get actual information instead of more agreement.

Last thing, and I mean it: don't be diplomatic. Say where you think this reasoning is most likely to fail, and say what would change your mind — what argument or what kind of evidence would move you to a different ruling on 4, or make you scrap clause (c) outright rather than repair it.
