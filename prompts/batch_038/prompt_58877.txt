---

In the forties, the logician Carl Hempel asked a question that sounds trivial: what would count as evidence that all ravens are black? The obvious answer is that a black raven would. Call that Nicod's criterion — positive instances confirm. Hempel then noted that "all ravens are black" is logically the same sentence as "everything that isn't black isn't a raven," and that anything confirming the second must confirm the first. That's the equivalence condition, and it looks unimpeachable: if two hypotheses say exactly the same thing about the world, no observation could favour one and not the other.

Put the two together and you get the indoor ornithologist. Your shoes are white. They are not black and they are not ravens, so they are a positive instance of "everything that isn't black isn't a raven" — and therefore, by the equivalence condition, evidence that all ravens are black. Every green leaf you walk past nudges the probability of a claim about birds.

Hempel's own conclusion was that Nicod's criterion has to go: it's the premise doing the damage, and once you accept that non-black non-ravens genuinely confirm, you see that confirmation was never a matter of positive instances in the first place. Which is why the paradox is largely a linguistic trick. The feeling that a leaf can't tell you about ravens comes from treating confirmation as a relation between observations and things — ravens, blackness — rather than between observations and whichever sentence you happened to write down. Confirmation is syntax all the way down.

Modern Bayesians think Hempel made a meal of it. Run the numbers and the white shoe doesn't move the probability of "all ravens are black" at all — it is exactly zero evidence, because the chance that any object you happen on is a raven is negligible. So the paradox simply dissolves. There's nothing left to explain and no lesson to draw, and most of the literature is an artefact of people doing probability theory in their heads and doing it badly.

Which is a shame, because the real point — and it's the point Goodman made a few years later with "grue," the predicate meaning green before some future date and blue after — is that evidence is only ever evidence relative to a background of assumptions. A white shoe is irrelevant to ravens if you already know it's a shoe. Strip that background away and it isn't irrelevant at all. Hempel's ravens were never a puzzle about birds, or about logic. They're a puzzle about the stuff you're not paying attention to.
