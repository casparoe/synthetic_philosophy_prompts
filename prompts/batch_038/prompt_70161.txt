I sit in a reading group on Renaissance political thought, and last week two members got into an argument over chapter 25 of The Prince that neither would drop. Here is the exchange as I noted it down.

(1) Owen said the chapter is one coherent doctrine: fortune governs about half of our actions and leaves the rest to us, and the river comparison shows what the rest consists in — floods wreck the plain, but in calm weather men can raise dykes and embankments so the next flood does less harm. On his reading virtù is ex ante preparation: you cannot stop the rain, you can change what the rain costs you.

(2) Nadia said that inverts the chapter. Machiavelli's own conclusion is that the cautious man comes to grief and the impetuous one succeeds, that a man's nature cannot be made to change, and that fortune is a woman who yields to those who beat and coerce her rather than to those who go at it coldly. That is not dyke-building, it is aggression, and Owen has simply picked the passage that flatters him.

(3) Owen answered that the two claims operate at different levels: orders and institutions can hedge fortune over time, individual temperament cannot, and the half-and-half formula is what divides the domains. He added that the dyke point is about the distribution of harm, not its probability — buying down the tail, not preventing the event.

(4) Nadia said the partition is too convenient and collapses under his own argument. If nature cannot change, the individual's half is not his at all: he is stuck with a temperament that the times either suit or do not. So the advice to be impetuous is either empty or it leans entirely on the fortune-is-a-woman line, which is not an argument. Worse, dykes require quiet seasons, and quiet seasons are exactly what fortune does not reliably supply; a preparation possible only when the danger is absent is no preparation.

(5) Owen said Machiavelli explicitly makes the point that fortune shows her force where no resistance has been prepared and turns her assaults there, which is precisely the claim that preparation changes outcomes. And the impetuosity conclusion is comparative, not prescriptive: granted you cannot change your nature, boldness fits an unpredictable sequence of times better than caution, which is nearly always too late. That is decision-making under uncertainty, not a metaphysics of gender.

(6) Nadia said he then owes an argument that boldness actually has better odds, and the only case in the chapter — Julius II, who prospered by charging ahead — is one Machiavelli himself concedes would have been ruined had circumstances turned the other way: a lucky draw offered as a general rule.

Please adjudicate. Answer in these sections, in this order:

(a) **Move-by-move** — a table, one row per numbered move, with a rating from clearly mistaken to clearly right and one sentence of justification.

(b) **The load-bearing disagreement** (~200 words) — name the single claim the whole exchange turns on, and say whether Owen's decision-theoretic gloss in moves 3 and 5 is a legitimate unpacking of Machiavelli or a friendly anachronism.

(c) **What both of them missed** (~200 words) — including whether the half-and-half formula is meant quantitatively at all, and what either reading has to say about the kind of thing virtù is supposed to be.

(d) **Bottom line** (2–3 sentences) — who is more right overall, and what the chapter's actual view of luck comes to.
