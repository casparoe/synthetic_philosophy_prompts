I'm on my university's gift acceptance committee and Thursday we decide on a $40M offer to endow an institute. The donors terms: the research agenda gets fixed in the charter in perpetuity, no future board may amend or dissolve it, enforcement sits with the donor's descendants forever, and if we breach, the endowment reverts. Our policy says we accept perpetual restrictions only when they're "consistent with the institution's long-term mission," which is undefined and useless here. Two camps on the committee. One says refusing is arrogance — we know future administrations will be captured by whatever is fashionable or fundable, and the entire point of the restriction is to bind people who aren't in the room. The other says a restriction nobody alive can release isn't a commitment, it's a dead hand.

Draft the written opinion the committee circulates before the vote: a clear recommendation (accept as offered, accept with specific modifications you spell out, or decline), the reasoning behind it, a serious treatment of the strongest argument from the side you end up against, and — this is the part I actually need — a test the committee could apply to the next perpetual gift that tells us when binding our successors is legitimate precommitment and when it's just control from the grave. The test has to be something a lay board member can apply without philosophy training.

Lead with your recommendation in the first sentence, then give the reasons; no preamble and no restating the question. American English.
