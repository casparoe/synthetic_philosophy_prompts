I chair our university's academic integrity board — five of us, I vote too — and for two years I've been keeping a private log of the meetings where *how* we voted seemed to change the answer. My gut reactions are not consistent and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. No references or reading lists, I just want the reasoning worked through.

Our standard is conjunctive. A student is responsible if (a) the work was submitted as their own, (b) a prohibited source was used, and (c) they knew or should have known it was prohibited. Then a seperate question of sanction, where an enhanced sanction applies if EITHER there's a prior finding OR there was serious harm to a third party. We usually vote element by element and then the chair applies the rule; sometimes we just vote straight up on the finding. Which we do has never been written down.

**Case 1.** Three members sat (I recused). Votes on (a): Y, Y, N. On (b): Y, N, Y. On (c): Y, Y, Y. So every element carried, but on the straight-up question only one of the three voted responsible. We used the elements and found the student responsible. I think that was wrong — you shouldn't hand down a finding of responsibility that two of the three people on the panel voted against.

**Case 2.** Same panel size, and as it happens the exact same pattern of votes. But here the contested element was what "prohibited source" means — specifically whether an AI writing assistant counts under a rule drafted in 2011 — and whatever we say about that carries into every later case this year. We again went element by element and again found responsibility, and I think that was right, because the interpretation is the thing we're actually authoritative about and it would be worse to publish a verdict with no majority view of the rule behind it. I notice I've just said opposite things about two identical vote patterns.

**Case 3.** Enhanced sanction. Prior finding: Y, N, N. Serious harm: N, Y, N. On the straight-up question, two voted for the enhanced sanction and one against. I said no sanction: each of the two was relying on a different ground, and a majority of the panel rejected each ground. But a majority did think at least one of the grounds was made out, just not the same one, and I don't know why that doesn't matter to me. It doesn't, but I want to know why.

**Case 4.** Same rule, and again no ground had a majority: fabrication Y,N,N; unauthorized collaboration N,Y,N; improper citation N,N,Y. But this time all three voted for the sanction on the straight-up question. I said that was fine and we published it. Compared with Case 3 the only difference is that it was unanimous, and I can't tell whether that's a real difference or just a feeling.

**Case 5.** Two halves, and I judge them differently. (a) Element (c) came up, one member is a lawyer, the other two said openly in deliberation that they'd go with her, recorded Y,Y,Y. Fine by me, that's how collegial bodies work. (b) The disputed element was whether a similarity report's matches amounted to copying; the two members who aren't from CS voted Y without following the argument, the CS member voted N. Not fine — that majority was noise and it decided the case. But in (a) the "majority" was really one person plus two abstentions dressed up as votes, which is what I objected to in (b).

**Case 6.** (a) Afterwards a member told me he'd actually thought there was harm but voted N on that element, because he knew that if it carried we'd have to find responsibility and he was against the finding. Honestly? Fine. He voted for the outcome he thought the board should reach. (b) Last year a member did the same thing but announced it at the top of the meeting — "I'm voting no on element two to kill this" — and I ruled the vote out of order and re-ran it. I still think that was right, but if it's legitimate to do it, it should be legitimate to say you're doing it, and the only thing that bothered me was the concealment. I can't defend that.

**Case 7.** A censure motion with three live options: no action, censure, censure-plus-removal. An amendment to add removal carried 3–2, and then the amended motion lost to no-action 2–3, so we did nothing. I've since worked out on a spreadsheet that the five members' rankings form a loop, and that if I'd taken the questions in the other order we'd have ended up censuring, or censuring and removing. Everyone voted sincerely on the question in front of them each time. I said fine — amendments are how deliberation works. But I chair, and I set the order, which makes me the person who picked the outcome.

So: is there one account of when a body should vote on its reasons and when it should vote on its result that gets all of these, or close to it?
