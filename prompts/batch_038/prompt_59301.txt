I chair the disputes panel of a housing co-operative of some hundred and twenty households, and over eight years we have built up a body of rulings on whether assurances given between members are to be treated as binding. A new matter comes before us next month and I would like to settle my own thinking about it in advance. What follows is a faithful summary of our record and of the case we now face.

The rulings, in the order they were made:

1. A member told her neighbour in the corridor that she would keep the shared store cupboard clear of her belongings until the neighbour's daughter left for university in the summer. The neighbour, on the strength of that, turned down a storage allocation from the council. When the member filled the cupboard in April, we held her to her word.
2. At the summer barbecue, a member told a group of us that they could of course have his car for as long as the rail strike lasted. There was laughter all round, including his own. When a member presented herself for the keys a week later, we recorded that nothing had been undertaken.
3. A member agreed to take over a departing member's arrears after being told the sum was four hundred pounds. It was fourteen hundred, and the person who told him knew that. We released him.
4. A member gave an undertaking to a dying resident that the resident's cat would be cared for in the house she had loved. The resident died the following morning. Nobody remained who could hold the member to it, nobody would have suffered had she simply let the matter drop, and nobody had rearranged their affairs in reliance upon it. We held that she was bound, and minuted that she was bound.
5. A member wrote out a solemn undertaking to himself, folded it into a Bible, and later asked us whether he was obliged by it to keep it. We told him we had no jurisdiction and that nothing had been promised.
6. A member undertook to sign her tenancy over to her partner after being told that otherwise a report concerning her would go to the police. We held that there was nothing to keep.
7. A member undertook to convert a garage into a bedsit and let it privately to a friend. We said the undertaking could not stand, whatever either party wanted, and that we would not enforce it against the member either.

The new case: in March, a long-standing member showed a prospective leaseholder around the block. Asked directly whether the service charge would rise, she said that it would not — the roof had just been done, and there would be no increase for five years. She holds no office, sits on nothing, and had no authority of any kind to speak for the co-operative. The true position, which the management committee knew at the time, was that an increase had already been budgeted for the following year. The prospective leaseholder took the flat, and states that she would not have done so otherwise. She is not claiming money. She asks that the co-operative hold her service charge at its present level for five years, on the strength of what she was told.

I would like the following, in this order.

First, set out the accounts of what makes an assurance binding that are genuinely consistent with all seven of our rulings — not merely with most of them, and not with the ones in isolation. I suspect there is more than one, and I suspect that the ones which first come to mind each fail on at least one item in the list; identify which, and on which.

Second, tell me what we should decide. Which of our seven precedents is the closest analogue to the new case, in what respect it is close, and why its closeness nonetheless does not settle the matter.

Third, if our record genuinely underdetermines the outcome — and I expect it does — say what principle we would be committing ourselves to by ruling one way, and what different principle we would be committing ourselves to by ruling the other, and which of those two commitments is the more defensible for a body that will have to live with its own precedents.

Finally, propose one further hypothetical case, of the sort that might plausibly come before us, on which the surviving accounts would deliver opposite verdicts, so that we could decide it deliberately and thereby settle which principle we are in fact running.

Please give me a committed answer throughout: take a definite position on the ruling, name the principle you would adopt, and defend the choice, rather than presenting the options symmetrically and leaving me to select. If you think one of my seven summaries is ambiguous in a way that matters to the analysis, say so and say how you are resolving it; and if you believe the whole framing of the question — that there is a single principle we are running — is mistaken, say that too and argue for it, but still tell me how you would vote.
