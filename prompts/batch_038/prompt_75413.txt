I have no academic training in philosophy or classics, and nothing I say below should be read as scholarly. For the past couple of years I have been working through Zeno's paradoxes on my own, using free public-domain translations and popular discussions, and I have recently got badly stuck on Aristotle's response to the dichotomy argument in the Physics: the digression that falls in the middle of Book VIII's argument about whether motion can be continuous, which I believe is around 263a4–263b9, though I may have the page reference slightly wrong. In the free online edition I have been using it is labelled Book VIII, Part 8. I have now read that passage perhaps fifteen times and I still cannot tell what Aristotle thinks he has established. I would be grateful for help.

Since I would almost certainly garble a translation I have not collated, I have not reproduced the wording. Instead I have set the passage out below as my own running paraphrase, in six steps. Please treat this as evidence of what I take the text to say rather than as the text itself, and correct me wherever my paraphrase distorts, flattens, or reverses the argument.

1. Zeno's argument rests on a false assumption: the assumption that it is impossible to traverse, or to come into contact with, infinitely many things one by one in a finite time.
2. Length, time, and anything continuous are called infinite in two different ways: infinite in respect of divisibility, and infinite in respect of how far they extend, or of their termini.
3. It really is impossible to come into contact with infinitely many things one by one in a finite time. But it is possible to come into contact with things that are infinite in respect of divisibility, because the time in which this happens is itself infinite in that same respect. So the infinite plurality is traversed in a time that is infinite in divisibility, not in a time that is infinite in extent.
4. Hence it is impossible to traverse an infinite distance in a finite time, and this holds whether the parts are equal or unequal, since infinitely many parts would require an infinite time.
5. One should not suppose that the infinite is used up by taking some particular part of it.
6. The passage then turns to a moving thing that comes to a standstill at the halfway point. Aristotle seems to say that if the thing halts at each half it cannot both be moving and have completed its motion in the same time, and that in that case the contact with the infinite happens only accidentally, or incidentally, in a finite time.

I keep oscillating between two readings and cannot settle between them, which is the main reason I am asking.

The first is what I call "the task-list reading." On this reading, Aristotle's point is not about infinity at all but about what counts as performing an act: the halves are never marked off as separate journeys unless something stops at them, so Zeno's list of infinitely many tasks was never a list of things the runner had to do. The appeal to two senses of infinite is then decorative, and the real claim is that an undivided run contains no completed sub-runs.

The second is what I call "the matched-infinity reading." On this reading Aristotle grants that the runner does contact infinitely many things, and argues that this is harmless because time is infinitely divisible in exactly the same way as the distance, so every sub-distance is matched by a sub-time and no total ever has to be completed. Here the appeal to the two senses of infinite is the whole engine.

Both readings strike me as unsatisfying. The first looks like a change of subject, since Zeno's argument does not require the runner to pause, and the halves seem to be there whether or not anyone stops at them. The second looks like it concedes Zeno's premise and then simply observes that time has the same structure as space, which is precisely what makes the puzzle puzzling rather than what dissolves it. Step 6, with its talk of accidental contact, looks like an attempt to keep both, and I cannot see how.

With that in mind, here is what I would like help with.

(i) In step 2, does the distinction between the two ways of being infinite do any argumentative work against Zeno, or is it only another way of saying that a finite line has infinitely many merely potential parts? If it does work, what exactly does it rule out?

(ii) Step 3 seems to me to restate the symmetry that generates the paradox instead of resolving it. If the distance has infinitely many parts and the time has infinitely many parts, why does the pairing help? Is Aristotle's claim that no total ever needs to be summed, or something else?

(iii) The words I have paraphrased as one by one, in steps 1 and 3, appear to me to carry a lot of weight. Do they? If so, does the runner contact the halves one by one or not, and what would settle it? I suspect the whole passage turns here and I cannot see which way.

(iv) In step 6, is the notion of accidental or incidental contact about the runner's relation to the midpoints (he passes through them without halting, so they are only incidentally stages of his journey), about his relation to the infinite plurality, or about both? If both, is it one claim or two?

(v) Is the reply circular, or a mere redescription? Put sharply: does Aristotle anywhere deny that the runner traverses infinitely many things, or does he only deny that the runner traverses them as so many separate completed journeys?

(vi) How much of this depends on the doctrine that the infinite exists only potentially? Would the argument survive for someone who rejects that doctrine and holds that a finite line determinately contains all of its points whether or not anyone divides it?

(vii) What would Aristotle, on this passage, say to the answer I meet constantly in modern discussions, namely that the remaining distances form a series converging on the whole distance, so there is no arithmetic absurdity anywhere? Is his reply a rival to that, a crude anticipation of it, or a response to a different question? Relatedly, does the convergence answer address the worry about completing infinitely many acts, or only the worry about the sum of the distances? I have a suspicion these are two different worries and that modern treatments answer the second while thinking they have answered the first, and I would like to know whether that suspicion is defensible.

(viii) Which of Zeno's other arguments would the same move touch, and which would it leave untouched? I have in mind the arrow, the Achilles, and the moving rows.

For the deliverable, please begin with a short correction pass over my six steps, flagging anything I have got wrong, conflated, or invented, and telling me if I have misplaced the passage within the Physics. Then answer (i) through (viii) in order, in prose. Then give me a verdict in one paragraph: the task-list reading, the matched-infinity reading, or a third reading I have not thought of, together with what I would have to give up in order to accept it. Finish with a plain paragraph on what someone in my position should conclude about whether Aristotle solved the dichotomy or only relocated it.
