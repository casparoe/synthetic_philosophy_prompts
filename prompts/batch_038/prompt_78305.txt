I am a staff software engineer at a mid-size infrastructure company, and for the past two years I have run an optional weekly hour called "deep dives," in which one of us explains a single idea to about a dozen other engineers. Nobody in the room has studied philosophy or logic; my own background is distributed systems. Next week it is my turn, and the topic I have chosen is the Church–Turing thesis — but specifically its epistemic status, because that is the part I find genuinely confusing and that I suspect nobody in the room has ever considered.

What I want the session to accomplish: an engineer who currently treats "Turing complete" as a checkbox on a language feature sheet should leave able to say what kind of claim the thesis is — a theorem, a stipulative definition, a conjecture about the physical world, or some combination — and able to say what would count as evidence against it.

Please do not explain the thesis to me. I have read enough summaries; what I lack is a plan for explaining it. I would like you to design the forty-five minutes.

1. The dependency structure. Give me the order the components must be introduced, and justify each placement by naming what has to already be in place for the next piece to mean anything. I should warn you that the obvious opening — draw the tape, the head, the transition table — is exactly how I bombed the last time I attempted this at a meetup. Tell me what, if anything, has to come before the machine, and whether the machine should carry much weight at all in a session whose point is the status of the thesis rather than its content. I would also like the room to arrive at the informal notion of a mechanical procedure on their own, before they see any formal model, so that the identification feels like a discovery rather than an announcement; tell me how to arrange that without turning the session into an exercise.

2. One carrying example. Not three illustrations — one scenario I return to repeatedly that does most of the work. Tell me what properties it must have and what it must conspicuously lack. My instinct is that the halting problem is the wrong carrier here, since it is a result about limits and my session is not about limits, but I cannot articulate the reason and I may be wrong.

3. The misconception map. This is what I most need. For each predictable confusion, give me the sentence a person in the room will actually say, and the point in my sequence where it has to be headed off — including the cases where the right move is to defuse it before it can form rather than to answer it afterward. The ones I already expect: "isn't it proved, given that every model of computation anyone has come up with can be simulated by a Turing machine?"; "so it's just a guess, then?" (someone said precisely this to me last time and I had no answer); "quantum computers break it"; "what about the argument that human mathematicians can't be Turing machines?"; and "LLMs are Turing machines, so they can't really do X." Tell me which of these I should let the room raise, which I should raise myself on purpose, and which is a trap that will eat the final twenty minutes if I engage it honestly at 4:30.

4. Whether to split the thesis. I have read that people distinguish a mathematical version — an identification of an informal notion, roughly "effective calculability," with a formal one — from a stronger physical version about what the world itself permits. I do not know whether that distinction is standard, or whether I am about to state it incorrectly, and I would rather you correct me than let me bluff. If it belongs in the session, tell me where and in what plain words; if it does not, tell me what to do instead when the quantum computing and real-valued analog computer questions arrive. Relatedly, tell me where the term "effective calculability" should first appear, and whether I should use it at all in front of an audience that has never seen it.

5. A closing check. One question I can put to the room, or one sentence I can ask each person to write down, that would tell me within a minute whether the status question landed or whether they have merely picked up trivia about 1930s logic.

6. What to leave out. I cannot cover everything. Name the two or three substantial pieces of this topic that a well-prepared person would be tempted to include and that I should deliberately omit, and give me the exact sentence to say out loud acknowledging each omission without appearing ignorant of the material. Finally, tell me how to handle a question that goes past what I know; I will get at least one from a colleague who reads logic for fun, and I want a graceful move that does not involve faking an answer.

Constraints: whiteboard only, no slides, twelve people, forty-five minutes, and no live coding. Please do not recommend readings, papers, or a bibliography of any kind — I will not prepare from that, and neither would the room. What I want is a plan detailed enough that I could hand it to another engineer and they could run the same session.
