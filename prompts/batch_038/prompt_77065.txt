I run a small research program on invertebrate welfare; my own training is in population ecology — mark–recapture, occupancy models with imperfect detection, sweep-net and Malaise-trap abundance estimation — and for three years I have been drifting toward a view I now want stress-tested rather than defended. The view: even if the probability that any given insect is sentient is low (I work with something like 5–20 percent, which I do not think is generous), the number of insects alive at any moment is so vast — I use 10^19 as a round working figure while conceding that honest estimates span at least two orders of magnitude — that expected insect suffering exceeds the suffering of all farmed and wild vertebrates combined, and should therefore dominate the priorities of anyone trying to reduce animal suffering. I typically add two supporting premises: most wild insects die before adulthood, and usually slowly, by predation, parasitism, desiccation, or starvation; and the interventions that might reduce that suffering (substitutes for insect- and fish-derived protein in aquaculture feed, humane killing methods for insects reared as feed, choice and timing of pesticides, eventually population- or habitat-level management) are cheap relative to the moral weight at stake.

I want the exposure of this view mapped, not a polemic on either side. Go through it kind by kind and tell me which pressures are decisive and which are merely awkward. (a) Cognition and nociception: what result would count as evidence against insect sentience rather than merely failing to confirm it, given that the indicators validated in vertebrates and decapod crustaceans — avoidance learning of noxious stimuli, opioid sensitivity, motivational trade-offs, self-administration — have no established insect analogue, so I worry the view cannot be tested even in principle. (b) Abundance and demography: if the standing number is out by a factor of a hundred, or if most insect deaths turn out to be near-instantaneous, or to occur at stages with no plausible substrate for affect, does the conclusion collapse or merely shrink, and what would a field ecologist have to measure to settle it? (c) Welfare theory: does the view stand or fall with hedonism about well-being, since a preference- or desire-frustration account seems to leave an insect with nothing that a brief death can harm — and is a theory of welfare entitled to aggregate across so many extremely short-lived organisms with no psychological continuity to speak of? (d) Decision theory: the charge that multiplying an enormous population by a small probability yields a Pascalian structure that is fragile to how the probability is estimated, to how outcomes are compared across scales, and to whether expected-value maximization is the right rule under this depth of uncertainty at all. (e) Feasibility: whether the view risks anything operationally when almost none of the interventions it recommends can presently be implemented at the scale its own numbers imply.

For each item, tell me what a committed defender of the scale argument would themselves concede as counting against them and what they would dismiss as beside the point; I want that line drawn honestly rather than in the view's favour. Then give me a straight ruling on falsifiability: is there any conceivable evidence that would move a reasonable advocate off this position, and if the view has been qualified into near-insulation, is that a defect given that its role is not theoretical but to justify spending money and changing policy? Say also what this view predicts that its rivals do not — I have in mind the rival who holds that sentience is improbable and therefore that numbers are irrelevant, and the rival who thinks the correct frame is uncertainty about moral status rather than expected suffering. Finally, address a structural worry of mine head-on: the same template of huge population times small probability would license equally urgent claims about nematodes, copepods, springtails, and rotifers, and about plants if one nudged the probability upward slightly. Is that a reductio of my view, or a sign that it is simply being applied consistently to cases people find distasteful?
