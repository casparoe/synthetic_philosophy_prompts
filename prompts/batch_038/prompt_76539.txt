I spent thirty-four years as a physical chemist, most of that time working on oxidation kinetics and thermal runaway at a national laboratory — which is to say, I earned my living from the behaviour of fire upon combustible matter. Since retiring I have begun reading philosophy seriously for the first time, and I came rather unexpectedly upon al-Ghazālī's treatment of causality in the *Incoherence of the Philosophers* (the seventeenth discussion, the one that turns on whether the disruption of causal sequence is possible), and thence, in trying to establish whether I had understood him, upon Hume. I am to speak to our local philosophical society in the spring on what a working chemist ought to make of occasionalism, and I have set down below the position I have arrived at. Before I commit it to paper in public, I should like a candid audit of it, because I suspect it hangs together less well than it felt while I was assembling it.

My commitments:

(1) Fire does not burn cotton through any necessity belonging to fire. Contact is the occasion of the burning rather than its producer, and what we dignify as a causal power in a body is, on examination, nothing in the body.

(2) Hume was right about the impression of necessary connexion: we never meet with it among the objects, and the sense of necessity is a habit of the mind bred by constant conjunction.

(3) The uniformity of nature is a custom or habit of God's (*ʿāda*, I believe the word is), adopted freely and interruptible at will. A miracle is therefore not the violation of a natural necessity — there being no such thing to violate — but a departure from custom.

(4) Nothing theological may be admitted as an explanation of anything in my laboratory. A rate equation with fitted parameters is the whole of a chemical explanation; a colleague who wrote "by divine decree" where the activation energy belongs would have explained nothing whatever. I do not see this as in any conflict with (3), though I understand others do.

(5) Causation is difference-making: *c* caused *e* if and only if, had *c* not occurred, *e* would not have occurred.

(6) But absences and omissions are not causes. What is not there cannot bring anything about. When a plant logbook records that "the failure to run the nitrogen purge ruined the batch", I have always read that as loose talk for the presence of oxygen together with the presence of an oxidisable intermediate.

(7) Between a real cause and its effect there must run a continuous process. Causation that leaps a gap, spatial or temporal, I cannot make sense of; after three decades of closing material and energy balances I regard the demand as more than a professional tic, though I concede that a working habit of mind is quite capable of dressing itself as a principle.

(8) I have also formed a view of my own, which I should like properly tested rather than merely encouraged: that stochastic physics suits the occasionalist far better than deterministic physics ever did. A nuclide with a fixed half-life has, on my account, no power whatever over when it decays; the half-life is a statistical description of a custom, not a disposition resident in the nucleus. Determinism at least left bodies a semblance of lawlike behaviour to be robbed of. A bare decay constant never pretended to any.

Two cases from my own practice, which I should like judged against (5)–(7) in particular:

*Case A.* In 1994 a batch was spoiled overnight because a nitrogen purge had not been run on a vessel holding an unsaturated intermediate. Had the purge been run, the batch would have been sound. I wish to say that the spoilage was caused by the oxygen that was present together with the intermediate, and that the missing purge caused nothing at all.

*Case B.* A standby cooling pump would have started automatically on a low-flow alarm. An operator, seeing the alarm first, started it by hand perhaps twenty seconds earlier. The reactor stayed cool, and the inquiry report credits him with saving the plant. I am confident that he did; yet on (5) it is not clear to me that his act caused anything, since the reactor would have stayed cool had he kept his hands in his pockets.

What I want is an audit, not a defence. Take the eight items as a set and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against one another and which merely look as though they do — I expect some of both, and I would much rather know which is which than be handed a list of undifferentiated worries. Order the real tensions by severity, and for each say what would have to be surrendered or reworded to remove it, and what that surrender would cost me, both in the philosophy and in the practice. Then tell me which single revision would repair the most damage, and what it would leave exposed. I should also like to be told, separately and bluntly, where I have mis-stated al-Ghazālī: I read him in translation, I have compressed a subtle argument into three lines, and I would far rather be corrected now than in front of the society. Please write in British English, and do not trouble yourself with references or a reading list — I have the books on the shelf beside me, and it is argument I am after, not apparatus.
