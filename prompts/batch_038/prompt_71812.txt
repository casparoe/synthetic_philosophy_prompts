I lead the appraisal function at a national infrastructure agency. We assess roughly thirty investment proposals a year, with turnaround expectations of about ten working days from receipt to recommendation, and my team is composed of transport economists, hydrologists, and financial analysts — competent with spreadsheets, largely untrained in welfare economics or intergenerational ethics. For the past decade we have applied a flat real discount rate to everything, drawn from a legacy template that offers three rates (3.5%, 5%, and 7%) and instructs the analyst to present all three, with the unwritten convention that the headline figure is the one under which the proposal survives. That practice has now become untenable. Our board has adopted two new requirements: for any scheme whose service life, containment obligation, or benefit stream extends beyond a hundred years, we must state and defend the rate or rates applied to the long tail rather than extrapolating the headline figure; and no scheme may be rejected on the sole ground that its benefits fall beyond year 250. The board has also added a cross-generational incidence statement to the standard template, requiring us to report what share of a proposal's discounted net benefit accrues to people who will not be born for at least a century. A chief economist signs off on my numbers before they go to a sub-committee, and the sub-committee is increasingly asking questions my analysts cannot currently answer.

What I need is a written decision procedure my staff can actually execute — an ordered list of questions, each with a statement of what counts as a sufficient answer, a default to fall back on when the answer is unavailable, and an explicit marker wherever the procedure is really handing a choice to a person because the issue is one of values rather than of fact. It should be grounded in whatever the theory of social discounting genuinely licenses: the Ramsey-style decomposition of a social rate into a pure time-preference term, an elasticity of marginal utility, and an expected growth rate in consumption; the unresolved dispute over whether the pure time-preference term is an ethical parameter to be set normatively or an empirical one to be read off observed behaviour; the treatment of uncertainty in future growth such that effective rates decline as the horizon lengthens; the distinction between discounting money, consumption-equivalents, and utility, and the error of applying one where another is required; the relative-price problem when the thing being valued (avoided flood damage, lost habitat, containment failure) does not grow at the same rate as consumption; and the opportunity-cost objection that a social rate is disciplined by the returns available on displaced private capital. I want the procedure to say plainly where an analyst is entitled to stop with the agency default, where a sensitivity band must be reported instead of a point estimate, and where the matter escalates to me or to the chief economist because no amount of further analysis will settle it. It must also handle the case where expected growth simply cannot be estimated over three centuries, and it must state its own failure modes: which steps are judgment calls wearing procedural clothing, what the procedure can be made to produce by a motivated analyst, and what it is structurally incapable of deciding at all. Please include a short justification template — the paragraph an analyst writes to accompany a recommended rate — with the slots the paragraph must fill. I would like names invoked only where a name is doing actual work in a step of the procedure; I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or any suggestion of sources to consult, since my team will not consult them.

Finally, run the procedure end to end on three live files, showing the numbers you use at each step rather than only the conclusion. First: a deep geological repository for intermediate-level waste, with capital and construction costs concentrated over fifteen years, a containment obligation running indefinitely, and a small non-zero probability of a release reaching the biosphere between 500 and 2,000 years hence. Second: a tidal defence scheme with an eighty-year design life, recurring maintenance, and benefits that rise with the income of the city it protects. Third: a peatland and coastal wetland restoration programme whose costs are entirely near-term and whose measurable carbon and biodiversity benefits are, on our own modelling, almost entirely post-year-250. For the third case I want the procedure's honest output even if it is a rejection, and even if the rejection turns out to conflict with the board's rule that a scheme cannot be rejected solely because its benefits lie beyond year 250 — my suspicion is that the arithmetic and the new floor will collide, and I would rather learn from you exactly where and why they collide, and which of the two the procedure should treat as binding, before the sub-committee discovers it on its own.
