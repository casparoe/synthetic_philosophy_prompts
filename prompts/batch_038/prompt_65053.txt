I cover forecasting, prediction markets and quant-ish social science for a mid-sized digital outlet, which means roughly once a week I end up with the same problem: two credentialed people hand me two different numbers for the same question and I have to write something. Right now my default is a coward's both-sides paragraph. My editor wants a house standard and honestly so do I, but I want it grounded in something better than my gut.

The thing I keep circling back to is Aumann's result about how two rational Bayesians who started from a common prior, and who commonly know each other's beliefs, can't end up disagreeing — no agreeing to disagree. I've read the usual grumbling: the common prior assumption is a modeling convenience no real beliefs satisfy, priors get picked post hoc to make the model behave, and common knowledge never actually obtains outside a proof. Fine. But it seems to me there's a usable procedure buried in it anyway. A disagreement is only worth reporting as a contest — only settleable by more evidence — if the gap survives a check that it isn't being produced by something other than a genuine difference in priors. If it doesn't survive, then the honest story is about priors, or about two people answering different questions, and no amount of new data closes it.

So: turn that into a decision procedure I can actually run. Numbered steps, the question I put to each source at each step, what counts as a sufficient answer, and where each step terminates — real disagreement (report the contest), prior gap (report the gap, not the contest), definitional mismatch (kill it or rewrite the question), or incentives (it was never a belief difference at all). I get maybe an hour per case, no math beyond arithmetic, and my deputy editor signs off on anything that implies one of the two people is simply wrong. Write it for somebody with zero formal epistemology, and if a step leans on a formal result, give me one plain sentence on what it says and one on what it doesn't say. No bibliography, no reading list, no names I'd have to chase down.

Cases it has to handle, because they occur constantly: one source has data access the other doesn't; the two are using different reference classes for the base rate; strategic shading — a forecaster with a book to sell or a calibration reputation to protect has reasons not to converge even when they should; the difference between me emailing A's number to B in private versus B responding on the record, and whether either is anywhere near the common-knowledge condition; and the case where both people insist the other is just biased. Also, I gather there's a weakened version of the theorem where near-common-knowledge buys you near-agreement instead of exact agreement — if that's right I'd rather build the procedure on that, since exact common knowledge is never what I've got. Tell me straight whether that's the correct move or a dodge.

Then run it on three disagreements: (a) two well-known forecasters at 65% and 25% on whether a country's inflation drops below its official target by the end of next year, where both have read each other's public writeups; (b) two epidemiologists who seem to be split on whether a rollout will reach protective population-level immunity, except one is reasoning about the trial population and one about everyone including the elderly; and (c) the one that'll annoy me — a piece I've already pitched and my editor liked, framed as two superforecasters facing off, where your procedure says the gap is priors all the way down and there's no horse race to report. For (c) tell me what the piece becomes instead, or whether it shouldn't run.

And don't hand me a menu. Commit: which of those escape hatches do you think is actually doing most of the work in the disagreements I cover, and should the procedure ever license me to print that one side is wrong? Give reasons. I also want the failure modes — where it will misfire, and in particular how a bad-faith source could use it to launder a just-wrong position as a mere difference in priors.

Start with your bottom line in the first sentence — what the procedure is and what it will and won't let me say — then the steps and the reasoning. No preamble, and don't restate the question back at me.
