I sit on the clinical ethics committee of a mid-sized academic medical center, and we are in the middle of rewriting the policy that governs how our clinicians treat advance directives. The committee chair has asked me to draft a short background paper explaining what our current practice actually assumes, on the theory that we have been applying these rules for two decades without anyone articulating the view of the patient behind them. I am a hospitalist, not a philosopher, so I will describe the practice as precisely as I can and leave the reconstruction to you. The draft under review has five features that I want treated as a single bundle, because the committee defends it as a package. First, refusals bind but requests do not: if a valid directive refuses an intervention, the team must withhold it even when it is life-saving and even when everyone believes the patient would choose differently today; if the directive demands an intervention the team judges clinically inappropriate, the team may decline and offer transfer instead. Second, a contemporaneous objection revokes: when a patient who has lost decision-making capacity resists — pushing away a feeding tube, repeating that she wants to go home — staff are instructed to treat that as a revocation and to stop, unless the directive contains an explicit non-revocation clause executed before two witnesses with a clinician's attestation of capacity at signing. In practice fewer than one patient in fifty has that clause. Third, the evidential bar is high: prior wishes must be established by clear and convincing evidence, meaning a written directive, a designated agent, or consistent statements reported independently by more than one person; hearsay about what someone once said at a funeral does not qualify. Fourth, the sequence is fixed — substituted judgment first, best interests only as a fallback — and substituted judgment is defined as the surrogate's task of answering not what would be good for this patient now but what this patient would have chosen. Fifth, in progressive dementia the draft instructs that values expressed while the patient retained capacity, including values about the kind of life the patient would consider worth living, take precedence over the patient's present apparent contentment, unless that contentment comes with capacity for the decision at hand; but in our separate psychiatric track, a directive may be overridden whenever the team judges it contraindicated, and a patient in an acute episode may revoke at will, with staff instructed to honor that revocation even when the patient plainly lacks capacity for the decision.

What I want from you is a reconstruction of the implicit theory, not an evaluation of whether the policy is good. Begin with the question of authority across time: what has to be true of persons for the earlier self to be entitled to bind the later one at all? Does this bundle require that the signing self and the demented self be the same person in some strong sense that carries authority forward, or would something weaker — a standing promise, a property claim over one's future body, a right to set the terms on which others may act toward you — license exactly the same rules? Then take the two clauses that seem to me to pull in opposite directions. The dementia clause looks committed to the view that a person's settled evaluative commitments outweigh the quality of the experience they are now having, while the revocation clause looks committed to the view that whatever the person in front of us expresses now is the last word; tell me whether one theory can generate both, and if so, what distinction it has to be leaning on that our draft never states. Do the same for the refusal/request asymmetry: is there a principled account on which a prior refusal binds absolutely while a prior request is merely advisory, or does that asymmetry reveal that what the policy is really tracking is the avoidance of a certain kind of wrong rather than the extension of the patient's will? And examine what substituted judgment, defined as a counterfactual question, presupposes about there being a determinate answer — including what the policy silently assumes when the patient has changed enough that the counterfactual has no truth value, which is, I suspect, most of our dementia cases.

Finally, I want the parts sorted rather than blurred. For each of the five features, say whether it is strictly entailed by the reconstructed theory, merely consistent with it, or compatible with several rival theories at once — that is, where the policy is philosophically underdetermined and the committee therefore has a real choice to make rather than an interpretation to discover. Flag which of the tensions above are genuine inconsistencies that no distinction can dissolve, and which are the surface expression of a distinction the draft has failed to articulate. Then tell me what would have to change — which clauses, which defaults, which evidential standards — if we replaced the presupposed view with a time-relative account on which the authority of the earlier self decays with the degree of psychological connection to the later one, and separately, if we replaced it with a view on which authority rests entirely in the present patient's welfare. Note anything the policy could keep unchanged under either replacement, since the committee would rather amend than rewrite. Please write in American English, define any technical term you use in a clause rather than assuming it, and give me the reasoning rather than a reading list or references, which would be of no use to me in a committee meeting.
