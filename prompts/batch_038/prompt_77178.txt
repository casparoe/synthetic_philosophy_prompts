I cover science and ideas for a general-interest outlet — neuroscience, psychiatry, AI, the usual beat. Lately I keep writing hedged sentences like "the brain activity that accompanies the decision" because I cant figure out what I actually believe about whether mental states do anything. So here are my gut verdicts on a pile of cases. I want to know if there's a single principle of causation behind them, what it says about cases I haven't listed, and which of my verdicts I have to give up.

1. My belief that there's beer in the fridge plus my wanting a beer caused me to walk to the kitchen. Uncontroversial, both are causes.

2. The neural event realizing that belief-desire pair also caused the walk. And I don't want to say the walk got overdetermined by two independent causes the way a victim does when two snipers both hit him at the same instant. It just doesn't feel like the sniper case. One of these is doing something the other isn't.

3. But when I try to say what, I get pulled in opposite directions. Pigeon case: you train a bird to peck at anything scarlet, then show it a crimson panel and it pecks. I want to say the panel's being scarlet caused the peck and its being crimson didn't — crimson is too specific, the bird would have pecked at lots of non-crimson things.

4. Mirror image, and this is the one that breaks my brain. A guy sprints for a bus because he believes it's the 8:47 and that it's the last one. Describe the physical side the way a neuroscientist actually does when pointing at a blob on a scan: "coarse motor-preparatory pattern N." Here the mental property is the more specific one, and I still want to say the mental property is the real cause and the coarse neural description is leaving something out. So in 3 specificity disqualifies a candidate cause and in 4 specificity is what qualifies it. Tell me how one principle says both, or tell me which case to abandon.

5. Counterfactuals help and then don't. If I hadn't believed the bus was the last one I wouldn't have sprinted — true, and that seems to license the belief as a cause. But if the firing rate in motor cortex had been a couple percent different, I'd still have sprinted — so the counterfactual fails for the physical realizer, and I absolutely do not want to say the neurons weren't causes. My body moved because my brain did stuff.

6. The string test — my own coinage, I don't think it's in anybody's paper. X causes Y only if you could wiggle X while holding everything X is made of fixed and watch Y change. My intuition: if you can't wiggle the belief without wiggling the neurons, the belief isn't an extra cause, it's just the neurons under a different name. Except 5 bothers me, since on the string test the belief flunks and I still want it to pass.

7. Phantom realizer — a hypothetical, not something I'm reporting. Suppose a cortical activation reliably shows up when people say they've decided to press a button, but silencing it leaves the behavior unchanged: a decoy, doing no work, like the shadow a puppet throws on the wall while the strings do everything. Verdict, held strongly: the shadow never caused anything, and if a reported intention is like the shadow then it never caused anything either.

8. Now the pair I can't reconcile with 7. Deep brain stimulation patients sometimes describe movements the device produces as accompanied by a vivid intention of their own — an intention that, in that instance, plainly produced nothing. I don't want to call that intention a shadow. I want to say it's a real mental event that got bypassed, and that with normal wiring the same kind of event would have produced the movement. But the "would have" is doing all the causal work there, which is exactly what I refused to let it do in 7. Same structure, opposite verdicts. I know that's a problem.

9. Group level, which is the beat I'm actually on. A company believes its market is shrinking and pivots. I want to say the company's belief caused the pivot, even though no single employee's brain state was that belief and every physical event involved was in someone's head or in a memo. If I say that, I have to explain what stops the neuron-level story of the pivot from being the real cause. Or I say nothing is excluded and both are causes — which is case 2 again, which I said I didn't like.

10. Machine level, because my editor will ask. An LLM's internal state that we'd naturally describe as "it thinks the user is asking about refunds" makes a difference to the next token it emits, and we can sometimes change that state by editing the prompt while leaving the weights alone. Verdict: that state causes the output. This one feels easier than the human cases, which makes me suspect my human-case verdicts are contaminated by something that isn't about causation at all.

So: is there a principle — one, or a small number of rival candidates — that delivers these? State each candidate precisely, say which of my cases motivated each clause, and give me its verdicts on cases I haven't listed. Specifically: a depression that costs someone their job versus the neural correlate of that depression; the pigeon case except crimson is what the bird was actually trained on; and whether anything can cause an outcome that would have happened anyway for a different reason.

Then be honest about the failures. Which of 1–10 does your best principle have to overturn, and which can only be saved by adding a clause so expensive that the principle stops being worth holding? If 3 and 4 can't both survive, I'd rather hear that flatly than get an epicycle for each. Tell me whether the string test in 6 is salvageable with a fix or is just a bad principle I should stop reaching for. And say plainly whether 9 is the same problem as the mind-body case rather than a cute extra — that changes how I'd write about any of this.
