So here's a problem I've got. I TA a Western Civ survey at a regional state school — 24 students, one 75-minute section, and we're in the second week of the Rome unit. The room has a projector that only works if you smack it, one whiteboard with a mostly dried-out tray of markers, and about a third of the class is over 30 and has opinions. On day one of the unit I asked cold, no warning, when Rome fell. Nine people said 476. The rest stared at me. Nobody had read the Gibbon excerpt I assigned. The syllabus then walks them through the two poles of the modern argument — Peter Brown's *World of Late Antiquity* on one side, Ward-Perkins's *Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization* on the other — plus a day on the non-political suspects (climate, plague, lead, whatever the archaeologists are fighting about this decade). What I've found is that my students can recite "it didn't fall, it transformed" back to me on the exam without having the slightest feel for why anyone would say such a thing, or what it would cost them to say it. They think the transformation people are just being contrarian.

What I want is one class period built around a fake curatorial committee. Framing: the history department has been asked to write the wall text for a display case titled THE END OF ROME, and the committee owes the museum (a) one date for the plaque and (b) one sentence of justification, and the museum is not going to accept a comma-spliced compromise. Here's the mechanic I've half-sketched and want you to finish and improve on: every student gets a secret card that isn't a fact but a *criterion* — one particular thing that has to still be true for Rome to count as existing (a western emperor on the throne; a single treasury funding a standing army; a merchant who can travel safely from Britain to Egypt; a Senate still meeting in the city of Rome; Latin as somebody's first language; an eastern court that calls itself Rhomaioi; somebody who can be taxed from a center; a road network that gets repaired; and so on). They score by getting the committee's official date close to the date their own criterion actually dies, plus a bonus if the final justification sentence comes out in their criterion's vocabulary — but they aren't allowed to say out loud which card they hold until a moment you design for me. Then at some midpoint I hand every group the exact same pile of evidence slips, so nobody can win on facts, only on which facts they decide are load-bearing. My chair wants active learning documented for program review, so if it has a name I can put on the form, bonus.

Give me the whole thing ready to run on Monday: the card list (I'm thinking 10–14, and please sanity-check the endpoint dates attached to each — I'm genuinely hazy on how late the Senate and the eastern self-designation survive, and I don't want to teach these people something wrong), group sizes, a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 75, materials printable on two pages max becuse we have no budget, a scoring scheme that works with poker chips or tally marks instead of real money, and the reveal structure. Then the debrief: map specific moments from the room onto the actual historiography — which students arguing for a single hard date were doing Gibbon without knowing it, which ones who kept dragging the conversation to pottery and living standards were doing Ward-Perkins, and what you say back to the kid who inevitably blurts out "wait, so there was no Rome to fall, that's obviously wrong." One committed design, please: pick your card set, pick your reveal, defend the choices; I don't want a menu of three variants with the work left to me.
