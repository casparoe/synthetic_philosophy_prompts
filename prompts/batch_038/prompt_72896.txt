I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine — a fortnightly column plus whatever long thing they'll let me file once a quarter — and right now I'm deep in a piece about what happened to Newton's papers after he died: the family's nervous filtering of the manuscripts, the Sotheby's sale in the thirties, all the alchemy and prophecy that came down out of the attic. It's definately the most fun thing I've written this year, but I've got a framing problem. Over the weekend I read a trade book by a smart, well-reviewed populariser, and there's a passage where the author sums up Newton's religion in about a page. I transcribed the gist:

"Newton was really two men. By day the supremely rational mathematician who derived the system of the world from a handful of laws; by night an alchemist and a hunter of prophecies, and by sheer volume of writing the second Newton buried the first. His faith was a deist's: a divine geometer who built the clock, wound it up, and left it to run, an architect you infer from the elegance of the building rather than a God who intervenes, answers prayers, or breaks his own rules. That's why the world of the Principia runs on law alone and why God shows up mainly in the pious coda bolted onto the second edition to keep the clergy quiet. The one doctrine Newton couldn't swallow was the Trinity, and he rejected it for the same reason he rejected other people's hypotheses about the heavens — he couldn't find it in the evidence, in nature or in the text once the scribal corruptions were stripped out. He also denied, so far as the papers show, that the soul survives death naturally. He knew exactly what all this would have cost him, which is why he hid it for decades, told almost nobody, and was happy to let more outspoken juniors say in print what he wouldn't sign his name to: a heretic lost his fellowship and his livelihood, and Newton was a careerist with a salary at the Mint to protect. The theology leaked into the physics exactly once, when he described space as God's sensorium — a sort of all-seeing eye in which the whole world is present to him — and even there he was doing mysticism in scientific dress. And he left a note computing that the world will end in 2060. Taken together the manuscripts show a mind with two watertight compartments: nothing in the theology explains the physics, and nothing in the physics was endangered by the theology. The faith matters to us only as a measure of how completely even the greatest intellect can wall off what it doesn't want to examine."

Can you audit this for me line by line? I want to know what a historian who has actually sat with these manuscripts would nod at, what they'd call a fair enough popular simplification, and what they'd say is wrong or straight backwards — because I'm about to paraphrase some of it and I don't want to launder an error into print under my own byline. A few places I specifically want you to press on: is "deist" defensible, or does it flatten something the General Scholium and the Opticks queries are doing? Does the clock-and-watchmaker business actually belong to Newton, or was it thrown at him in the fight with Leibniz? Was his rejection of the Trinity a piece of modern evidential thinking, or does that get his reasons backwards? Is "hid it because he was a careerist" the whole story, given who he did share manuscripts with? And the sensorium line — whose word is that, and is it an organ of perception in the everyday sense the book implies? On 2060, be precise with me about whether he was fixing a date for the end or ruling one out, and what the arithmetic was doing for him, since that's my hook. Last thing, and the one I care about most: does the closing argument survive the corrections? If a chunk of the theology turns out to be load-bearing for the natural philosophy rather than a walled-off private embarrassment, does the "compartmentalized genius" frame still hold, or do I need a different one — and if so, sketch me a sentence or two of what that would look like. Please flag anything you're not confident about instead of smoothing it over.
