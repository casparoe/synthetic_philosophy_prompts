So I've talked myself into a stronger version of the Aunt Hillary idea than most people are willing to hold — you know the Hofstadter dialogue where the Anteater's friend is an ant colony that's conscious. Most people read that as a thought experiment about levels of description. I take it literally, and I want you to attack it.

My view: a mature ant colony is a single cognitive subject. Not a mereological sum of little subjects, not a useful shorthand for "a bunch of ants doing things," but one mind. It has colony-level beliefs (about where the good feeder is, about which nest site wins), colony-level preferences, and the individual ants stand to those states roughly the way your neurons stand to your beliefs — they're real, they do the work, but they are not the subject of the belief.

What would count against that? Don't give me the generic "but the ants are individually dumb" line — I already think that's the wrong objection. Give me the defeaters that actually land, and run them against a concrete setup instead of in the abstract, because I think the abstract version of this debate is why nothing ever gets settled. Here's the setup I keep coming back to:

- One *Lasius niger* colony (common black garden ant), roughly 40,000 workers, living in two nest boxes joined by a closable bridge tube. A feeder sits outside each box.
- The colony reliably passes a foraging-choice task: give it two feeders differing in sugar concentration and it commits to the better one in a way that tracks the size of the difference, and it reverses when you reverse the difference. Commitment takes on the order of hours.
- Now close the bridge for three days in the middle of a task, so the two boxes have disjoint populations and disjoint chemical traffic. Test each box on its own: each independently performs the same choice task, degraded but clearly above chance. Reopen the bridge and the whole thing converges back onto a single commitment.

Under my view there was one subject, then apparently two, then one again, and nothing in between that looks like a subject being created or destroyed. Is that fatal for me, serious, or something I can absorb — by saying subjecthood is graded, or that identity over time doesn't have to be one-to-one, or whatever else is available? Tell me which, and whether the absorbing move costs me something I was relying on.

Other things I especially want pressed on, though add your own if these aren't the right ones to worry about:

1. Timescale mismatch. Colony-level commitments take hours; an ant's behavior takes seconds. If the colony's beliefs only cash out in behavior on a timescale at which no single organism needs them, what is the colony-level belief doing that "local rules plus current environment" isn't already doing? Is there a live causal-exclusion worry here, and does it bite against my view specifically or against every higher-level description including the psychological one?

2. Grain and binding. No individual ant has access to the colony-level state, and there doesn't seem to be anything in the colony playing the role of an integrated subject. Whether that's a problem depends on what you think integration actually requires, so say what my view is committed to about that. And check whether the cheap escape — "the subject just is the process" — smuggles back in the thing it was supposed to explain.

3. Interpretive charity inflation. If colony-level belief gets fixed by something like interpretive charity applied to the whole system, then every sufficiently complex self-organizing thing gets beliefs for free: a slime mold, a prediction market, a Wikipedia editing community, a robot swarm running a quorum rule. Am I committed to that promiscuity? If yes, is it a reductio or a feature I should own? If no, what non-question-begging cutoff do I owe, and do I have one? I'd much rather be told "you owe a cutoff and you don't have one" than be handed a dodge.

4. Whether the strong view risks anything at all. Every bad result seems absorbable: fails a task — well, no mind can do everything; splits in two — okay, graded subjecthood; colony-level states fully predictable from local rules — so are yours, that's the whole point. Walk through whether I've qualified the view into unfalsifiability, and then say honestly whether that's a defect for a claim of this kind or more like a framework choice that gets judged by explanatory payoff instead. I don't need it rescued. If it's well insulated and that's fine, say that; if it's well insulated and that's a problem, say that.

5. What my view predicts that the weaker rivals don't. The rivals I have in mind: (a) lots of small subjects and no big one, where the right vocabulary is distributed cognition without a subject; (b) the colony is a genuine system but only instrumentally a mind — an intentional stance we take, nothing more; (c) the superorganism story is right biologically, selection acts on the colony, but that's an evolutionary claim and gets you no subject. For each, name an observation that would distinguish it from mine. If there isn't one for some of them, say so plainly and tell me that's the real problem.

Also be straight with me about who concedes what. Which of your defeaters would a committed defender of the strong view grant as counting against them, and which would they wave off — and for the ones they wave off, distinguish waving off correctly from just waving off.

Format: table first, then a short verdict. Table columns, in this order:

- defeater — short name for it
- what would have to be true — the concrete finding or argument, stated so I could picture a paper reporting it
- severity — exactly one of: fatal / serious / awkward / cosmetic
- what my side says back — the best reply available to someone holding the strong view
- does the reply hold — "holds", "holds at a cost" (name the cost), or "fails"
- how you'd settle it — the observation or argument that would decide between the objection and the reply
- would a defender concede — "concedes", "concedes partially", or "dismisses"

Sort the rows worst-first by severity. After the table, three or four sentences of bottom line: is the strong colony-mind view exposed in a way that should make me downgrade it, and if so to what — one of the rivals in (5), or just a weaker version of my own view?
