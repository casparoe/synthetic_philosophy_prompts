I have spent about fifteen years reading rationalist blogs and forums, and the one habit I took from them is writing my commitments down explicitly so that other people can attack them. I am drafting a short statement of my views on moral worth — on what it takes for doing the right thing to redound to the credit of the person who does it — for an online reading group, and I would like a consistency check before I post it. I am not looking for a survey of the positions; I have read the standard arguments on both sides. I want to know whether the package below hangs together.

Here is the package.

1. Rightness is a matter of expected value. An act is right if and only if, given the agent's evidence, it maximizes expected good. I do not read this as a claim about what agents ought to calculate; it is a claim about what makes acts right.

2. Rightness and credit come apart. An agent can do what is right and deserve no credit for it, and can deserve credit for an act that turns out badly. Credit attaches to the agent's motives, not to the moral status of the act.

3. Credit requires responsiveness to the right-making features. An agent deserves full credit only if the features that in fact make the act right are among the features the agent is responding to — and responding to in the counterfactual sense as well: were those features absent, the agent's conduct would change.

4. That responsiveness need not be conscious, articulable, or accessible to introspection. I do not require a moral theory, a concept of rightness, or any insight into one's own mechanism. Someone raised to feel a visceral pull against preventable suffering, with no idea why, satisfies (3) as fully as a philosopher does.

5. Deliberating about rightness is usually counterproductive. The agents I most want around me are ones whose generosity runs on rails — habit, affection, standing in a community — rather than on continuous moral audit. I take this to follow from (1) together with the costs and biases of deliberation, not to be a concession to any rival theory.

6. Of two agents who produce the same effects, the one who acts out of affection for the people affected is more praiseworthy than the one who acts in order to be thought well of. The second has an ulterior motive; the first has none. Of everything on this list, this is the item that seems to me most obviously true.

Now three cases and my verdicts.

The auditor. Contributes a fixed fraction of her income every month, feels nothing about it, would rather keep the money, and has said so publicly. She picked the fraction by looking at the evidence, revisits it annually, and has twice redirected the money when the evidence changed. Verdict: full credit; she is my paradigm of a morally worthy agent.

The enthusiast. Gives the same amounts to the same organizations and is genuinely moved: he knows the people he helps by name and is brought to tears by the appeals. He has never once asked whether the intervention works. Which appeal reaches him first determines where his money goes, and he would give just as generously to something that did nothing, or did harm, so long as the story was equally vivid. Verdict: full credit, and I would rank him above the auditor, because in him the motive is native rather than installed.

The reputation-builder. Gives the same amounts again, and does it for standing. He is explicit about this and does not consider it a secret. His circle rewards results that get independently checked, so when the numbers come back bad he moves his money elsewhere; he has done this twice, unhappily. He reports feeling nothing for the beneficiaries and would prefer to keep the money, but he cannot keep the standing without the results. Verdict: some credit, but clearly less than either of the others.

I expect that a couple of these items look contradictory on a first pass and are not. So: audit the set. Tell me which pairs are genuinely in tension and how badly, ranked by severity rather than listed as equally troubling. For each genuine tension, say what would have to be given up, weakened, or reinterpreted, and what that reinterpretation costs me elsewhere in the package. Distinguish carefully between a tension I can dissolve by clarifying what one of the claims means and one I can only resolve by dropping something. Then tell me which single revision would repair the most damage at the lowest cost.

Since I assume you could be wrong about this, close the analysis by saying what would change your mind: which of the tensions you have flagged you would demote to a merely apparent one under pressure, which argument you have given is most likely to fail, and what a defender of the package as written would say in reply that you have not accounted for.

End with a line in exactly the form "Verdict: N", where N is the number of the commitment you would revise first. If the cheapest repair runs through one of the case verdicts rather than through a numbered commitment, write "Verdict: case" followed by the name of the case.
