I'm in the second term of a course on nationalism and the modern state, and our seminar has stalled on a dispute I'd like to settle with a properly designed case rather than another round of gestures. The question is whether Anderson or Gellner has the better causal account of the emergence of national consciousness. Every illustration either side raises changes too much at once — railways, mass schooling, conscription, censuses and a vernacular press all arrive together in the real examples — so nobody can say what the print market is doing on its own. Please construct a paired thought experiment that isolates it.

Specifications:

- Two territories, A and B, matched on everything that could plausibly matter: the same dialect continuum and the same administrative or sacred language imposed from above; the same terrain and transport technology; the same imperial administration; the same literacy rate and school system; the same conscription arrangements; the same census, map and museum apparatus; the same stage of early commercialization; the same distance from the metropolitan centre.
- One difference only. In A a vernacular print market consolidates: a handful of urban printers standardize a regional vernacular and sell newspapers and cheap serial fiction along existing trade routes. In B printing stays confined to the administrative language and to imports from the metropole, so no local vernacular press emerges.
- Realistic. This should be a pair a historian could argue about without straining, not science fiction, and the scenario itself should run to two or three paragraphs in total.
- Do not reuse Gellner's Ruritania, and do not build either territory as a transparent stand-in for one real country. I want the case judged on its structure, not on my recall of what actually happened somewhere.

What I need after the scenario:

1. Run A through Anderson's mechanism step by step — how a print market in a vernacular becomes a bounded, sovereign, imagined community; what the newspaper contributes that the novel does not, and vice versa; and what work the new sense of simultaneous, calendrical time is doing in the story. Give a rough timescale, and say what membership feels like from the inside to someone who will never meet most of their compatriots.

2. Give the same account's prediction for B: what unit does a population without a vernacular press imagine itself in instead — a religious community, a dynastic realm, a market region — and why the theory is committed to that rather than to something else.

3. The Gellner check, and the part I most want done honestly: with his variables held fixed as I have specified them, can his account generate any difference at all between A and B? If it cannot, tell me whether that is a genuine cost to Gellner or a sign that my list of fixed variables is not neutral — that I have already built industrialization, labour mobility and standardized exo-education into both territories and so begged the question against him before the print variable was ever introduced.

4. Bring in invented tradition. Describe one tradition A's nationalists would have to invent in order to give the new community a continuity it does not actually possess, and say which of Hobsbawm's jobs it is doing — establishing cohesion and membership, legitimating institutions and authority, or inculcating values and norms of behaviour. Then say whether the framework predicts that B invents traditions too, and if so, on whose behalf and in what direction.

5. Finish with a diagnostic. Name one observable that comes out differently in A and B on Anderson's account but identically on Gellner's, so that my pair is not merely each theory restating its own premises. Identify the single feature of the design that is doing the work, describe what the case looks like if I remove it, and give the strongest way a Gellnerian could attack the design itself rather than accept its verdict.
