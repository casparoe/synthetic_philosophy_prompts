The facts I'm working with: it struck on All Saints' Day while the churches were full, a tsunami reached the harbor about forty minutes later, candles lit for the feast set off fires that raged through the ruins, and estimates of the dead in the city run to thirty or forty thousand. Voltaire turned the disaster into an attack on the claim that this is the best of all possible worlds; Rousseau answered that much of the toll came from people's own choices, crowding into tall houses in a dense city.

Please analyze the episode through the contemporary problem of evil: the free-will defense, soul-making, the reply that God acts only by general laws, skeptical theism, and Rowe's evidential argument from apparently pointless suffering. Which of these does Lisbon genuinely put pressure on, and which is untouched by it precisely because it only claims that God might have reasons we cannot see? Is Rousseau's move a free-will defense aimed at natural evil, and how much harm can be shifted onto human choices before the natural-evil problem simply disappears? Does Rowe's argument gain anything from a real catastrophe of this size, given that his own example is a hypothetical fawn burning unseen in a forest fire, or does scale only add vividness? Finish by saying whether my teacher is right, or whether what the earthquake defeated was a much stronger thesis about this world than any live theodicy offers.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restating my question.
