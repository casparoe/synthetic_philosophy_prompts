I work as a reporter at a mid-sized regional newspaper, and I'm in an argument with my editor that I can't seem to resolve, because every way I state my objection sounds weak. Let me lay out the facts as carefully as I can.

Six weeks ago we obtained an original document which, if genuine, establishes that the county executive approved a no-bid contract with a firm partly owned by his brother-in-law. I want to be precise about why I take ourselves to know the document is genuine rather than merely to have been persuaded by it. It bears a handwritten signature and a notary seal; a paper supplier independently dated the stock to a run discontinued in 2019, consistent with the document's stated date; a former deputy clerk, whose identity we verified through two channels that don't touch each other, recalls entering the same wording in the official log at the time; and the county's records custodian confirmed on tape that the corresponding log page is missing from the public copy, which is exactly what the document predicts. Our lawyer cleared it. We published.

Since publication, the executive's office has released a stream of contrary material. My editor's position is that we are now in an unusual logical spot, and that recognizing it settles what we should do. His reasoning: we know the document is genuine. Therefore anything that surfaces against the story from here on has to be a lie, a mistake, or the product of pressure on whoever is supplying it — that is simply what follows from knowing. So the rational posture, he says, is to have decided in advance not to be moved: re-opening the story each time the other side issues a statement rewards the people manufacturing the statements and falsely signals that the question is live. When I objected that an affidavit could carry real weight, he answered that we already know what any such affidavit will say and what will have prompted it, so it cannot be different in kind from a press release. A colleague put it more bluntly: we did the hard work once, and now we are entitled to stop doing it.

My difficulty is that my replies sound worse than his. If I say we should keep an open mind, he correctly answers that this is either empty — of course we know, that is why we published — or an admission that we never knew, in which case the story was reckless. And there is a wrinkle that complicates my side further. Two items released after publication are precisely the sort of thing I would have predicted beforehand as manufactured under pressure. A third, however, I did not anticipate: it identifies a pending fraud suit against the paper supplier who dated our stock, and if that suit holds up it bears directly on one of my four legs, while leaving the other three untouched.

So I'd like your judgment on three questions.

First, is my editor's inference sound? Identify where, if anywhere, it fails: in the step from knowing to the claim that whatever arrives later will be misleading, or in the further step from that claim to the conclusion that we may disregard what actually arrives. If the step is valid but the conclusion doesn't follow, explain what has been quietly added.

Second, how should we treat the two categories differently — the denials we foresaw and that behave exactly as foreseen, versus the unforeseen item that attacks the reliability of one of our own grounds? Is there a principled line here, or is my instinct just squeamishness? Note that the unforeseen item is not evidence that the contract didn't happen; it is evidence about how good my evidence was.

Third, what standing policy should we adopt? Tell me concretely what we should commit to re-checking and on what trigger, what we should treat as settled and refuse to re-litigate, and how we avoid both the credulity of reopening a verified story every week and the shut-down posture my editor is recommending. I'd also like the answer to speak to the version of the argument our lawyer raised: that in a dispute over whether we published responsibly, "we already knew" is not a defense but an admission.

If there is a standard name for the puzzle I've sketched, say so, but I want the reasoning and a workable policy rather than a survey of positions or a list of references. Please write in American English.
