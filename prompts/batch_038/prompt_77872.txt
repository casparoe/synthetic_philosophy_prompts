I am a high school junior in an AP U.S. History course. During a discussion of Cold War foreign policy, a classmate ended an argument about whether the United States bears any special obligation to lead in world affairs by declaring that America has always been "a city upon a hill," that this is simply what the country is, and that the question is therefore settled. Our teacher let it stand. I know the phrase comes from a sermon John Winthrop delivered in 1630 to Puritan colonists bound for Massachusetts Bay, and I have since noticed that it reaches me most often in the twentieth-century form of a "shining city on a hill," so I suspect the same words are doing very different work in the two settings.

Could you set out, as a list, the distinct claims this slogan can be making, ranging from the purely descriptive to the strongly normative, and for each say whether it is a platitude no one could dispute, a contestable historical or moral thesis, or something false or not really a claim at all? Then tell me which of those readings, if any, would actually support my classmate's step from America's being an example to America's having a right or duty to direct affairs abroad, and whether the phrase's origin in a 1630 sermon rather than in political rhetoric constrains the readings available to someone invoking it today. Finally, I would like your judgment on whether, on the most charitable reading, the slogan functioned as a premise in his argument or as a way of foreclosing one.

Please state your bottom line in your first sentence and then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
