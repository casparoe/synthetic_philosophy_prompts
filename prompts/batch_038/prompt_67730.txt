Ok so my roomate is in his second year of law school and we got into it last night over a clause in a summer analyst agreement he signed, and three hours later I think he's won and I can't say why. The agreement says he'll stay the full twelve months and will train his replacement, and then two paragraphs down that either party may terminate the arrangement at any time on written notice, no cause needed. I said that second paragraph makes the first one not a promise at all — you can't promise to stay and simultaneously reserve the right to leave, that's just narrating what you'll probably do. He said fine, then on my own account he could have written the escape clause straight into the promise — "I promise to train my replacement, and I promise you this promise is off the moment I say it's off" — and there'd be nothing wrong with the second sentence, because if promises are just whatever you make them he was free to make that one. Then he built the whole thing out on a napkin and I couldn't find the exit:

(1) A promise binds the promisor only because of something the promisor did — he took the obligation on, in an act whose whole point was to put himself under it. Nothing else in the situation is normatively load-bearing.
(2) If the source of the binding force is his own act of assuming the obligation, then that same act fixes the obligation's content and its strength too; there's no further test, outside the act, for whether what he assumed is the sort of thing that can be assumed.
(3) So he can bind himself with a promise that carries its own off-switch: the content just is "I do this until I say I don't."
(4) But an obligation I can switch off whenever I feel like it gives you nothing you didn't already have. I have no reason to do anything in particular, you have no claim on me, and there's no such thing as my breaking it.
(5) So one and the same act both creates the obligation and empties it. Either it never really created one — which contradicts (1), since the force wasn't in the act after all — or obligations are things their author can create and void in a single breath, in which case "obligation" isn't doing the work we assumed.
(6) Therefore the idea that promises bind because the promisor willed them into existence has to go, and whatever replaces it has to locate the force somewhere other than his will.

My hunch is that something slips between (2) and (3) — (2) is about the content of what I take on and (3) is really about the status of the taking-on itself, which feels like a different level of thing — but I can't say that to him without him asking why the level distinction isn't just a way of protecting (1). And there's a second possibility I can't rule out: maybe (4) is the false premise, and an obligation with a reserved escape clause is a weaker-but-real one, since it still constrains how I'm allowed to leave even if not wether. Which would be news to me, because I'd have thought "you may leave whenever you like" and "you are bound" just contradict.

Pretend you're a philosopher who does promissory obligation and second-personal normativity for a living, and actually think before you start typing: open with two or three sentences on what kind of failure this even is — an equivocation, a level confusion, a scope slide, or a valid argument where the only question is which premise is cheapest to drop — and only then diagnose it.
