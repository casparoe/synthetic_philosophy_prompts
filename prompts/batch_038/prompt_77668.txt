I sit on the Programme Review Panel of our national research council's Centres of Excellence scheme, and in three weeks we decide the twelve-year renewal of the Centre for the Study of Cumulative Culture. The chair has asked each of the seven of us to circulate a short written opinion in advance, and mine has become the one I most need help getting right, because the panel is split down the middle and the case is unusually philosophical for a funding file. Under the scheme rules our options are narrow: full renewal for a further seven years at the current scale; termination with a two-year wind-down; or a conditional renewal at reduced scale with a mandatory mid-term review, which requires a majority and has been used twice in the scheme's twenty-year history.

The centre's founding charter states its central thesis explicitly and forbids its revision: no distinctively human cognitive competence requires postulating innate, species-specific learning dispositions, since everything is acquired by domain-general associative and social learning operating on information already present in the group. All adjustment over twelve years has therefore gone into the surrounding assumptions — transmission channels, teaching, artefact-mediated learning, fidelity thresholds, social structure. The record we have been given cuts both ways, and I want the memo to engage with it case by case rather than at the level of slogans. In Year 3 the centre predicted in advance, from a quantitative transmission model, the retention frequencies of intermediate variants of a weaving technique in a particular community, including a characteristic dip at one step; fieldwork confirmed it. In Year 6 a model led them to predict that a given archaeological assemblage would show a loss-then-reintroduction signature with a specific gap, and a field team found exactly that two years later; it remains the centre's most cited result. On the other side: in Year 2 a preregistered prediction that three-year-olds would acquire a two-step tool task by observation alone failed, and the centre introduced a guided-participation assumption afterwards, which could not be independently measured for four years and was then invoked to explain three further non-replications; in Year 5 a fidelity-threshold parameter entered the models, and an external reviewer noted that it was fitted to the same datasets it was subsequently used to explain, with no independent estimate ever produced; in Year 8 the centre absorbed a rival's overimitation findings with an auxiliary claim about learners' insensitivity to physical affordances, an auxiliary that was introduced to neutralise a difficulty but then yielded one confirmed prediction about copying errors in a corvid species; and in Year 11 a large externally run preregistered replication of the flagship Year 3 result failed outright, to which the centre replied that the replication protocol had stripped away the ambient scaffolding the theory holds to be constitutive of the skill, so it was not a test of the theory at all — a reply the director concedes is not independently testable as currently formulated, though a reformulation is promised. Over the last three years the centre has published no new transmission model, only reviews, position papers and a framework synthesis, and the two researchers who produced the Year 3 and Year 6 results have left. The main rival programme in the field — a nativist dual-inheritance approach at another institute, better funded and better cited — has absorbed the overimitation data and made confirmed novel predictions about childhood, but responds to its own failures by postulating new innate biases and has no account of the Year 6 archaeological result. A third, smaller group agrees on the anti-nativism but denies that the transmission models are doing any genuine explanatory work.

The panel divides as follows. Two members regard the file as closed: a decade of post hoc adjustments, an unfalsifiable response to a failed replication, and no live modelling programme, which on any reasonable reading of Lakatos amounts to a degenerating problemshift, and they will move to terminate. One member argues the opposite with equal confidence, and I think the memo must take this seriously rather than dismiss it: that Lakatos himself denies the possibility of appraising a programme at a single moment, that degeneration is only visible in hindsight and can reverse, that a programme is properly defeated only by a rival that explains the old successes and adds corroborated excess content — which the nativist programme plainly does not yet do — and that the council's own 2019 decision to wind down a different centre on degeneracy grounds now looks badly premature, since that group's central idea has become widely adopted elsewhere. This member adds that a funding council's remit is training, infrastructure and impact, not the philosophy of science, and that importing Lakatosian appraisal into a budget decision systematically penalises patient, unfashionable work. A fourth member is worried simply about leaving the field a monoculture. My own position is uncomfortable: I find the degeneration diagnosis persuasive on the Year 2, Year 5 and Year 11 items, but I cannot see how to state a rule in advance that condemns the Year 11 reply without also condemning the Year 8 auxiliary, which was introduced for the same kind of reason and then paid for itself with a confirmed prediction.

Please draft the opinion I will circulate, roughly a thousand to thirteen hundred words, addressed to the panel and usable as a basis for a vote. It should come to a clear recommendation among the three options, with a time frame attached, and it should say what I would need to see at the four-year point to reverse my own recommendation — stated now, in advance, as explicit tripwires rather than as a general call for evidence, even though I am aware that doing so is in some tension with the objection that programmes cannot be appraised at a moment. It should settle, or at least frame decisively, the specific question of whether the Year 8 payoff licenses the Year 11 reply, and it should say plainly whether the Lakatosian vocabulary is the right apparatus for a funding decision of this kind or whether something else should be doing the work. I want the strongest case against my recommendation stated by me, in its best form, in a section of its own, so that the two members who want termination and the one who wants full renewal can each find their own argument taken seriously and answered rather than ignored. Please avoid surveying Lakatos's philosophy for its own sake; assume every member of the panel has read the standard account and wants to know what follows for this file.
