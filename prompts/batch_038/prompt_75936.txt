I'm trying to write the methods section of a chapter on institutional persistence and I keep hitting the same wall: everybody says "path dependent," nobody says what would count as evidence that they're wrong. So before I commit to a definition I want the spec. Write out the desiderata — the requirements any adequate account of path dependence or lock-in has to meet — with the case or consideration behind each one, and then tell me which requirements are load-bearing and which are just the taste of a particular camp (the economists who want efficiency talk, the historical sociologists who want narrative sequences, the modelers who want it phrased as a property of a stochastic process).

Setup, so you know what "adequate" means for me: I want an account I can apply to a single real case, where I get one run of the process and no repeats, and where I want to be able to say something like "outcome X is locked in, and a feasible alternative Y at fork t would have stuck too." So the theory has to earn its counterfactuals rather than inherit them from a metaphor.

Cases it has to get right, beyond the usual suspects (keyboard layouts, videotape formats, the gauge of a railway):

1. A mid-sized city that tore out its streetcar network and never got one back. Everyone calls this lock-in. I want the account to be able to tell me whether that's a claim about the causal process that produced the outcome, or just a claim that reversing the outcome is expensive now — because those come apart, and I think a lot of the literature slides between them.

2. A standard that got adopted early, stayed, and where switching would cost more than it's worth — but where the standard is also, as far as anyone can measure, the best one available. My gut says the account has to be able to say "yes, path dependent" here, because otherwise it collapses into "path dependence = market failure." But then it has to not turn out that every stable thing in the world is path dependent, or the concept does no work at all. Those two pulls are the crux, I think, and I want to see how your list handles it.

3. Equifinality: two different early forks, same destination a century later. Most definitions seem silent on this. If they are, they can't tell me whether the fork mattered, which is the only question I actually care about.

Now the part I most want you to be decisive on: is there a subset of these requirements that can't be jointly satisfied? I suspect a triangle between (a) usable from one unrepeatable case, (b) licenses claims about paths not taken, and (c) doesn't smuggle in a welfare baseline for calling the locked-in outcome inefficient. If you think that's a genuine impossibility, say so and say which leg you'd cut. If you think I'm wrong, tell me where the triangle leaks.

Also: flag the single requirement that knocks out the most candidate definitions. I'd bet on granularity — what counts as a "state" and what counts as a "fork" seems to depend on how finely you carve, and if you carve finely enough then trivially every process is sensitive to its initial conditions, which makes path dependence true of everything and therefore of nothing. But argue for it or against it, don't just adopt my guess.

Lastly, briefly, no more than a short paragraph each and no bibliography: how do the main contenders do against your list — the increasing-returns / positive-feedback economists' version, the historical-sociology version that leans on reactive sequences instead of self-reinforcement, the taxonomy-of-mechanisms version (coordination effects, learning effects, adaptive expectations), and the skeptical economists who think properly documented cases of inefficient lock-in are close to nonexistent. Which survive, and which fail a requirement you'd have thought they'd clear.

Commit throughout. I don't want "some would emphasise X while others stress Y" — I want your ranking of the requirements, your reasons for it, and an explicit note on any place you think I've stacked the deck against a camp by how I've framed the cases.
