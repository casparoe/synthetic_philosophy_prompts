I'm a third-year philosophy of biology PhD student and I've been trying to make sense of why the adaptationism debate — the one that supposedly got kicked off by Gould and Lewontin's spandrels paper in '79 — still hasn't produced anything like a resolution. Everyone I read now concedes the other side's basic points. Adaptationists agree that not every trait is an adaptation, that some features are byproducts of how a body is built, that origin and current utility are seperate questions. The critics agree that plenty of traits are beautifully engineered for what they do and that selection does most of the heavy lifting in shaping them. And yet in our reading group, and honestly in most of the literature too, the two camps still talk past each other in a way that feels like it should be diagnosable rather than just hard. I have a working hypothesis I'd like you to attack rather than indulge: my guess is this isn't really a disagreement about how much selection explains, but about which default you're entitled to. One side thinks you get to say "selection did it" until someone produces a developmental constraint or a correlation with some other trait; the other thinks you get to say "byproduct" until someone produces actual evidence of selection. If that's right, the fight is about null hypotheses and evidential burdens, not about organisms. But I might be wrong, and I can think of at least two rival diagnoses — maybe they disagree about the unit of explanation (whole organism, single trait, developmental system), or maybe they're really arguing past each other about what a function claim asserts, the selected-effect versus causal-role business.

Here's the concrete case I keep circling back to, and you don't need to know anything about it beyond what I say here. I've been looking at work on a lizard where males have a bright blue belly patch. The patch gets used in territory displays, it shows up in essentially every population, and there's a decent story about females preferring it. But the same cells that make the blue pigment also makes a dark band down the spine, and the band appears to do nothing at all — no display role, no thermoregulatory story, nothing anyone has found. Developmentally the band comes first; the belly patch looks like a later modification of the same pathway. So is the patch an adaptation that dragged a useless band along with it, or is the band the real thing and the patch the byproduct that got recruited? The people who work on this system genuinely disagree, and both sides are perfectly happy to call the other's favorite trait a spandrel. Which makes me suspect my null-hypothesis story is tidier than the actual situation.

So: give me the diagnosis. Is this a verbal dispute, a methodological one, a fight about defaults, about units, about what "for" means in "the trait is for X," or is it a substantive question about the world that both sides are just answering badly? Engage with what each side actually says — the charge that the original paper attacked a strawman adaptationist nobody was, the counter-charge that the critics wanted design-talk out of biology for ideological reasons, the fact that even the architectural analogy got picked apart by people pointing out that the San Marco surfaces aren't technically spandrels but pendentives and that the builders chose them deliberately, which is kind of a perfect microcosm of the whole mess. And tell me whether the current consensus pluralism, where everyone announces they're a moderate adaptationist, is a real resolution or just mutual exhaustion dressed up as agreement. Then, given whatever diagnosis you land on, say what would count as progress: a distinction that needs drawing, a kind of study or formal result that could actually discriminate between the positions, or an argument that nothing could and we should expect this to run forever. I'm especially curious whether my lizard case is the sort of thing better data could settle, or whether it'd still be contested even with perfect data.
