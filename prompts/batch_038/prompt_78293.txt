Please write a satirical feature of roughly 900 words in the register of a British media trade magazine — headline, standfirst, two or three crossheads, and the habit of quoting circulation figures at some length — profiling the first twelve months of a newly launched national daily that has abolished authorship. Invent the paper, its proprietor, its spokesman, its numbers and everything said about it; no real newspaper, proprietor, editor or journalist should be named or recognisably sketched, and no quotation may be attributed to any real person or text.

The paper's founding principle is that a newspaper ought not to be written by anyone. It prints no bylines, carries no editor's name on the masthead, publishes no signed letters, and attributes every story, leader and correction to its readership taken as a whole. Its house editorial theory, which your piece should enact rather than expound, runs as follows:

- The readership is not a collection of people but an abstraction which comes into existence only through the paper's own activity; the paper is therefore not addressing an audience so much as manufacturing the thing that reads it.
- Nothing printed should ever be such that one identifiable human being could be held responsible for it. Responsibility is treated as a production error.
- Everything must be brought to the same register, so that no claim makes more demand on the reader than any other. A saint and a fraud are copy of equal length; the paper's proudest technical achievement is that a reader cannot tell which of its stories would cost them anything to believe.
- Reflection is preferable to passion, and talk about an event is preferable to the event. Ideal copy is that which can only be discussed and never decided; the paper measures success in the volume of remark it provokes rather than in anything done.
- Gossip is the truest form the age has, and the paper's job is to convert everything that happens into what people are saying about what happened.
- Reading is offered as the pleasure of holding opinions without the burden of holding them.

The comedy must come from the paper applying that theory consistently, cheerfully and without embarrassment — never from absurdity, and never from a narrator who thinks the paper is silly. Where a principle generates a practical impossibility (someone must sign the defence to a defamation claim; someone must be blamed in a correction; a prize must be collected in person; a source must be named or not used), let the impossibility stand as a flatly reported fact, unexplained and uncommented.

Please include, as invented reporting: the launch and its proprietor, who insists he has no views; the anonymous spokesman, of whom the paper will say only that he is not employed there; rising circulation after the paper stopped distinguishing between its news and its comment; a libel action the paper cannot lose and also cannot defend; a comment section which functions as the real editorial meeting, and in which the paper claims to find its leaders; the obituary desk, whose problem you should make plain without stating it; and the paper's flagship investigative series about a private man — his umbrella, the manner in which he removed his hat, and the fact that one of his trouser legs hung a little longer than the other — after which schoolchildren began following him home, and which the paper counts as its finest work, on the grounds that it converted a man nobody had heard of into something everyone had a view about.

Three further constraints. First, do not name, allude to, or footnot any philosopher, and do not quote any real text anywhere; a reader who knows precisely where that editorial theory comes from should recognise it purely from the machinery, and a reader who has merely spent a decade skimming aggregation sites should find it just as funny as a portrait of their own trade. Second, the cost of the principles must be felt exactly once, in reported detail, and neither the feature writer nor anyone quoted may remark upon it: no moral, no closing twist, no wink to the reader. Third, keep the piece deadpan throughout, in British English, and end it on a reported fact rather than a judgement.
