A panelist at a migration-policy talk I attended last month dismissed a proposal on the table — liberalize admission gradually, while making new arrivals wait several years before they can claim means-tested benefits — with a single line: 'You can have open borders, or you can have a generous welfare state, but you cannot have both.' No further argument was offered, and the proposal was treated as refuted. I would like that sentence broken into the distinct precise theses it might be expressing, indexed by the variables they turn on: which population of immigrants is at issue, which benefits, over what time horizon, and what kind of constraint is being alleged — fiscal, political, behavioral, distributive, or merely legal. For each candidate reading, tell me whether it is a platitude everyone should grant, a substantive but defensible empirical or normative thesis, or false, and what evidence or argument would actually settle it. Then identify which readings, if true, would defeat the waiting-period proposal rather than only its unrestricted alternative, and whether the slogan's force in that room depended on a reading its user would disown under questioning.
