So I TA an upper-level philosophy-of-economics seminar and I've been handed the job of writing the final exam, which is one essay question, 75 minutes, 25 marks. Last year's question was basically "is revealed preference theory empirically empty?" and it was a disaster — nearly every script opened with the same two moves: (1) utility is defined as whatever choice reveals, so the theory is true by stipulation and can't be tested, (2) insert a paragraph on instrumentalism and whether it matters. I want a question where that template gets you nowhere, because the interesting issue is that the emptiness charge looks way too easy given what the nonparametric results actually deliver.

Here's the apparatus I want the question built around (this is my own cooked-up case, treat it as given, don't try to source it). A subject makes 30 budget-constrained purchases under varying prices; the resulting price-quantity dataset satisfies GARP, so by Afriat's theorem there's some locally non-satiated utility function that rationalizes every observation. The same subject then gets the same bundles presented pairwise, with no prices and no budget framing, and produces a strict cycle, A preferred to B preferred to C preferred to A; and on a verbal report she says she prefers C to A. An economist I'll call the two-tier theorist says there's no tension at all: the agent has a latent ordering R that governs choice whenever a "salience condition" S obtains — roughly, whenever the choice is framed as a trade-off at given prices — and otherwise choice is driven by a heuristic H, say "take the option with the more conspicuous good." Market data reveals R; the pairwise cycle reveals H. My worry, and the thing I want students to actually argue about rather than gesture at, is whether the two-tier theory rescues revealed preference from the tautology charge or quietly empties it out completely: if any pattern of choices can be sorted between R and H, the theory predicts nothing about choice unless S and H can be pinned down independently of the very data they're supposed to explain. I'd like the question to make students say what S would have to look like for the theory to still have content, and to reach a verdict on it.

Could you write the whole thing? So: the exam question itself, self-contained enough that a student can read the case and the two-tier theory in about five minutes and needs no outside reading; a model answer of around 800 words that takes a side and defends it (I don't want a "both readings have merit" ending — pick a verdict about whether the theory is empirical, empty, or something in between, and about what utility is doing in it, i.e. whether it's an implicitly defined theoretical term or a substantive posit about the agent); and a marking scheme out of 25 built from five or six must-hit moves, each with its marks and a one-line note on the mistake that usually loses them. The mistakes I keep seeing are worth encoding: students treat a GARP violation as if it meant "the agent is irrational" rather than "no utility function rationalizes this dataset"; they think saying "unfalsifiable" settles the matter instead of asking whether the extra flexibility buys anything; they slide from Afriat's theorem to claims about uniqueness or measurability or about the agent actually having the utility function in her head; and they conflate the definitional reading of revealed preference with the empirical one without noticing that the two can't both be the target of the same objection. No citations, dates, or page numbers anywhere in the question or the answer key — students weren't assigned readings for this one, and I don't want a recitation exercise. Oh, and please keep the tone of the question dry and exam-like, not chatty; I'll paste it straight into the paper.
