For a paper in my decision-theory course I am trying to pin down what money-pump arguments actually establish, and I want to test an inference I keep relying on. Take a concrete pump: an agent prefers a red bicycle to a blue one, the blue one to a skateboard, and the skateboard back to the red bicycle, and is offered the three swaps in sequence, each for a ten-dollar fee, so that she finishes holding the bicycle she started with and thirty dollars less. My claim is that the loss arises only because she evaluates each swap in isolation against her current holding. An agent with exactly the same cyclic ordering who instead assessed the sequence as a whole, or who could commit to a plan in advance, would refuse the first swap and lose nothing. So the pump does not show that cyclic preference is irrational; it shows that node-by-node deliberation is, and transitivity turns out to be a constraint on the temporal organisation of choice rather than a requirement of rationality as such. Is that inference sound? And if it is, does it follow that an agent who is simply never presented with a trade sequence she can evaluate all at once has no rational defect in her ordering at all?
