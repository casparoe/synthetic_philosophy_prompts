I write a fortnightly column on research for a general-interest magazine, and I am preparing an explainer on observational learning and information cascades. Before I draft it, I want to be sure I understand several points that the popular summaries I have read consistently blur together. I am not looking for references, a reading list, or a survey of who published what; I want the conceptual machinery explained well enough that I could reconstruct the argument myself and recognise where the magazine-standard version of it goes wrong.

To keep the discussion concrete, here is the situation I have in mind, which I have invented rather than drawn from a paper. A regional network of some forty clinics is deciding, one after another over a couple of years, whether to adopt a new triage protocol. Each clinic's administrators can see only the adoption decisions of the clinics that moved before them, never the data behind those decisions. Each clinic has also run its own internal pilot. Pilot quality varies: most are small, but the distribution of pilot sizes has a long tail, and a few clinics have run pilots on thousands of cases. Adoption is worth it if and only if the protocol really is better; the clinics gain nothing from matching their neighbours, from looking decisive, or from coordinating, and they lose nothing if they are alone.

Against that setup, please explain the following.

1. There is an apparent contradiction in how cascades are usually described. On one hand we are told they are fragile, and that a single well-informed dissenter can shatter a settled consensus. On the other we are told that a cascade is sustained by rational actors who, once enough predecessors have moved the same way, ignore their own evidence no matter how strong it is. Explain how both can be true, and separate the two distinct senses of fragility that I suspect are being conflated here: fragility to a public act of dissent, and something like permanence against private information.

2. Do the arithmetic on my clinic case, with explicit numbers. Suppose the protocol is genuinely better or genuinely worse with equal prior probability, and that a typical small pilot identifies the truth with probability 0.65. After two concordant adoptions, what should a third clinic believe, and what is the minimum pilot accuracy at which that third clinic is able to withhold adoption on the strength of its own pilot rather than its predecessors? Please show the threshold, and then say what happens to it as the run of concordant adoptions lengthens.

3. Explain what the loss actually consists of. My instinct is that the standard phrase about an information externality is often used loosely, as though the harm were that a clinic's adoption somehow costs its neighbours something. Make precise what each clinic fails to contribute, why the amount of information the group accumulates can stay bounded even as the population grows without limit, and how this differs from a payoff externality, from congestion, and from conformity.

4. Return to the long tail in pilot quality. Explain what changes, and what does not, if pilot accuracy is capped at some level strictly below certainty versus if it can be arbitrarily close to certainty. I want the consequences stated separately for three things: whether a wrong consensus is permanent once formed, whether the truth is eventually identified as the number of clinics grows, and whether a public dissent can restart informative behaviour. If the distinction between a fragile consensus and a merely breakable one is doing real work, please say how the two come apart.

5. Tell me what an observer in my network could and could not infer from the decision sequence alone. Which rival explanations would produce an identical record of adoptions, and what specific additional observable in this setting would separate them? In particular, assess what would happen if one clinic chose to publish its pilot data rather than merely its decision, and what a cascade account versus a conformity account would each predict about the pattern that follows.

6. Suppose clinics cannot tell how good one another's pilots were. Explain whether the cascade logic survives that, and what higher-order uncertainty about signal quality does to the inference each clinic is entitled to draw from a predecessor's choice.

7. Close with a short list of sentences that popular accounts tend to write about cascades which are false or overreaching, each paired with a corrected version, and then the two or three claims I can safely make in print about my clinic network.

Please keep formulas to those that genuinely carry weight, define every term the first time it appears, and aim for roughly 1,500 words. Where the honest answer is that something depends on modelling choices rather than being settled, say so explicitly instead of smoothing it over.
