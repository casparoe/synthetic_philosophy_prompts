I am a high school junior preparing a presentation for my school's philosophy club on Bernard Williams's distinction between internal and external reasons. I have worked through his account of the subjective motivational set and the sound deliberative route from that set to an action, and there is one question my teacher could not settle for me: is the dispute between Williams's internalism and a value-based externalism ever a genuine disagreement about one and the same agent, or do the two sides simply talk past each other by using the word "reason" in different senses? To test that, I would like you to construct a thought experiment to the following specifications.

The scenario must satisfy all of these conditions:

1. One agent, one candidate action, set in ordinary life — a school, a family business, a small clinic. No science fiction, no demons, no predictors of any kind, and a setting my classmates could follow without a lecture beforehand.
2. The action is neither wrong nor harmful to anyone, and no other person has any stake in whether the agent performs it. The externalist in your story must be able to agree that performing it would be entirely permissible. In other words, the two views may not be allowed to diverge about the agent's obligations; the divergence has to be about reasons as such.
3. The agent is fully informed about the relevant facts, holds no false beliefs on the matter, and has a vivid imagination. The case may not turn on correcting a mistake or on the agent's failing to appreciate what his commitment really involves.
4. The action is motivated solely by what I understand Williams to mean by a ground project: a commitment that gives shape to the agent's life. It is not a means to anything else the agent wants, and it has no worth that anyone standing outside the agent's life would acknowledge. A passing preference will not do.
5. The verdicts must come out this way: Williams's internalism says the agent has a reason to perform the action; a value-first externalism, on which reasons are explained by the goodness or worth of the act itself, says the agent has no reason to perform it. Both verdicts must be generated by the theories as you state them, not smuggled in through how you describe the case. If a reader can see that you simply asserted one of the verdicts, the exercise fails.

Please avoid the examples already worn smooth in this literature: grass-counting, the artist who works for no audience, and the young man from a long line of soldiers who will not enlist.

Keep the scenario itself to a single paragraph of no more than 150 words. Then, in a compact numbered list: (a) trace the internalist's sound deliberative route from the project to the action, naming the element of the motivational set it begins from; (b) give the externalist's reasoning for denying the reason while conceding permissibility; (c) identify the single structural feature of the case that produces the divergence, and say what each verdict becomes if that feature is removed; and (d) explain how Williams himself would attack the case, and whether his attack succeeds given condition 3. Do not use quotations, citations, or reading lists anywhere in your answer, and keep the entire response under 700 words.
