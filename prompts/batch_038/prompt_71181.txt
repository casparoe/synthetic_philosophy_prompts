For years I have read the coordination-failure literature — Moloch, multipolar traps, races to the bottom — and I have grown used to a certain moral ordering: the bad equilibrium is the harm, and explicit coordination among the players is the escape from it. American antitrust law, as far as I can make out, runs those two things in exactly the opposite order, and I cannot construct a theory on which that is coherent.

Here is the pattern as I understand it. Please correct me where I have the law wrong, since I am not a lawyer and my reading is secondhand.

If the chief executives of the four largest airlines sat down and agreed to hold fares at a level each of them privately preferred, the agreement would be unlawful without regard to its consequences: the courts do not weigh the benefits against the costs, and the executives themselves face criminal liability. But if those same four airlines arrived at precisely the same fares without ever speaking — each noticing that a price cut would be matched, each concluding that starting a fare war would be self-defeating, all of them independently settling into the supracompetitive equilibrium — the result is lawful. Parallel conduct, without more, is not an agreement; courts require additional evidence, such as behavior that would be irrational absent coordination, before they will infer one. Consumers pay the same fares in both worlds. The only difference is whether a conversation took place.

At the same time, a firm that won monopoly power honestly and then charges whatever the market will bear commits no offense; enforcement goes after exclusionary conduct, not the enjoyment of the monopoly price. And at the other extreme, agencies have sometimes proceeded against a firm that merely invited a rival to fix prices, even where the rival refused and prices never moved — the bare proposal, with no measurable effect on anyone, drew enforcement.

So the structure that puzzles me is this: the equilibrium in which everyone is worse off is not actionable, the agreement that would lift the players out of it is a felony, and an unsuccessful attempt at that agreement can be punishable while the successful tacit version of the same arrangement is not.

What does this practice presuppose? Reconstruct the implicit theory of harm and of coordination as carefully as you can. Is the object of the law the price level consumers end up paying, the process by which that level was reached, the act of substituting collective judgment for the discipline of the market, or something about consent and the integrity of independent decision-making by rival firms? Which rival accounts of what makes a cartel bad does the agreement requirement rule out, and which does it leave open?

Then separate what the practice strictly requires from what merely sits comfortably with it. Is the demand for an agreement doing any normative work, or is it an evidentiary device — a bright-line proxy for harm, adopted because the tacit case is close to undetectable and because penalizing parallel pricing would penalize ordinary competitive conduct? Distinguish those two readings and say which the surrounding doctrine actually supports. Do the same for the treatment of vertical restraints, where comparable conduct is judged by its effects rather than by its form, and for the refusal to police unilateral monopoly pricing: are these pieces of one theory, or of several theories that happen to share a statute?

Finally, suppose we replaced the presupposed view with a straightforwardly equilibrium-based one, on which the law targets the supracompetitive outcome itself and an agreement is neither necessary nor sufficient for liability. What would have to change in practice — in what a plaintiff must prove, in what firms may legitimately do to learn about their markets, in where the line falls between lawful price leadership and unlawful coordination? Would that make these traps escapable, or only more expensive to sit inside? And I want a committed assessment rather than a survey: tell me which reading of the current practice you think is the correct one, and whether, read that way, the practice is internally consistent or is holding two incompatible theories of harm at once.
