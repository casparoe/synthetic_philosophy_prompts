I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I'm currently reporting a long piece about whether the characters inside video games are owed anything — a question that stopped being purely academic once studios began shipping non-player characters driven by large language models, which improvise, plead, and appear to suffer in ways no scripted villager ever did. For the closing section I intend to state my own view in the first person, and a philosopher I interviewed for the piece suggested, unkindly but usefully, that I first establish whether that view is even self-consistent. I'd like you to do that. I'm not after a survey of the literature or a defense of any position; I want an audit of the set below, treated as belonging to a real kind of person — broadly welfarist, empirically cautious about machine consciousness, uneasy about the cruelty-in-games debate — rather than a strawman.

Here are my commitments. (a) What confers moral status is the capacity for things to go badly for the subject, and not rationality, language, or biological substrate. (b) That capacity is in principle substrate-independent: realize the right functional organization in silicon and there is a subject. (c) Present-day game characters do not realize that organization; they are text predictors layered over animation state machines, and their distress is a rendering rather than an event. (d) They therefore have zero moral status — not low status, but none. (e) Nevertheless, mistreating them in-game can be seriously wrong, because cruelty rehearsed in simulation trains a disposition that transfers to real victims. (f) The seriousness tracks the fidelity of the victim: tormenting a photorealistic character that begs convincingly is much worse than crushing ants in a stylized puzzle game, even with the player's psychology held fixed. (g) I would still judge it wrong for a player in a one-off, sealed session — no recording, no repetition, no measurable effect on his dispositions, and no one ever told — to spend an hour torturing a character that is, so far as anyone can tell, functionally indistinguishable from a suffering animal. (h) Studios owe it to players to stage server shutdowns with some care rather than deleting populated worlds mid-sentence, though I take this to be a duty about the characters and not a duty to them. (i) Finally, I hold that moral status is scalar, admitting of degrees and marginal cases, and that the history of ethics is largely the history of a widening circle of moral concern, of which concern for game characters may be an early edge.

Go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely pull against each other and which only look as though they do; I expect at least one of the apparent conflicts to dissolve under inspection, and I'd like to know which. For each real tension, state how severe it is, what specifically has to give, and what that revision costs me — including anything it would force me to retract elsewhere in the piece. Then tell me which single change would clean up the most. Please open with one sentence naming the commitment you would force me to abandon, and keep the entire audit under 500 words.
