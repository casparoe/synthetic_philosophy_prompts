Our reading group contains two people who have been arguing past each other for a year. One is a committed futarchist: she holds that the electorate's proper role is to fix the target — a public definition of national welfare — and that speculators, trading in markets conditional on the adoption of rival policies, should determine which policies hit it. The other is a committed defender of representative democracy who insists that the fatal objection is not that conditional markets forecast badly but that they cannot be held to account, cannot give reasons, and cannot be turned out.

Please draft the joint statement their adversarial collaboration ought to have produced. I would like:

1. A numbered list of claims both can sign — about what conditional markets can and cannot price, about the reflexivity worry that traders may profit by bringing an outcome about rather than predicting it, and about the liquidity problem for markets on policies that are never actually adopted.
2. A table of the residual disagreements, each row worded so that the relevant side would accept the description of its own position.
3. The cruxes: the sub-questions such that settling them would move at least one of the two. I suspect one is whether a decision procedure's legitimacy supervenes on its expected accuracy, and another whether a public, falsifiable price record is more or less open to capture than a politician's unfalsifiable rationalisation — but do not simply adopt my guesses.

For each crux, specify the pilot, case or argument both would accept as bearing on it, and record each side's prediction in advance. Finally, state which parts of the dispute you judge to be merely verbal, and why.
