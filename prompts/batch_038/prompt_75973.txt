I have spent a couple of years reading about pain asymbolia on my own, and I have come to think the argument it has generated is not going anywhere. The setup is familiar enough: some patients with damage around the insula say they feel the pain but do not mind it. They report it cheerfully, they do not ask for it to stop, they sometimes laugh about it. One camp says this shows that unpleasantness is a kind of evaluation — the pain still feels like a pain, and what has gone missing is its being taken as bad, which on this view is just what makes pain bad in the first place. The other camp says it shows that unpleasantness is a kind of command — the pain no longer directs the body to stop, and the patient's equanimity is a downstream effect of that. Each side then produces cases the other has to explain away: the first points to people whose pain they call unbearable while showing almost no avoidance of it, the second to people who guard and retreat from something they insist does not feel bad.

My hunch, which I would like treated as a hunch and demolished if it deserves to be, is that the two camps are not really disagreeing about asymbolia at all. They are disagreeing about what the word 'unpleasant' is supposed to pick out — a felt quality or a functional role — and neither side has noticed that it is answering a different question from the other.

I would also add something I keep meeting in my own line of work and rarely see discussed. There are patients who say the pain is bad, say it in a tone that is entirely untroubled, and yet still guard the limb, still refuse the exercises that provoke it, still wince as the hand approaches. Report, distress about the feeling, and avoidance come apart three ways, not two. I suspect part of the stalemate is that everyone assumes only two of those can ever come apart, so each camp gets to treat the third as noise.

Please give me a diagnosis rather than a verdict on who is right, and argue from what the positions actually claim rather than from who said what — no references, I want the reasoning. I would like the answer in four sections, in this order:

1. "The dispute, stated fairly to both sides" — about 200 words, in terms that make clear what each side is committed to and what would falsify it.
2. "Diagnosis" — about 400 words: verbal, substantive, or two questions travelling under one label; which hidden premises are doing the real work; and whether my hunch survives contact with it.
3. "The three-way split" — about 300 words: whether separating report, distress, and avoidance genuinely advances the debate or is a relabelling that lets both camps claim victory.
4. "What would count as progress" — about 200 words: a distinction, an empirical finding, a change of question, or a reasoned case that none of these will move it and why.
