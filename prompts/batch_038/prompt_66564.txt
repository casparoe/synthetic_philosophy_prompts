Right, so for my EPQ I've been writing about whether everyday conventions — queueing, tipping, which side of the pavement you stand on — actually create moral obligations, or whether breaking them just makes you rude rather than bad. I've got to present it to the whole sixth form at the end of term, which includes a load of people who'd rather be anywhere else, so I want to open with a joke instead of a definition. Background: there's a queue outside the chip shop near my school that everyone in town just accepts is a queue even though nobody is standing in a straight line, and my mate Sam reckons it's pure manners so he skips it whenever the man behind the counter isn't looking. There's also an exchange student who spent about two weeks genuinely unable to tell that queue from a small crowd, which is the bit I find interesting, because he definately wasn't being a git, he just didn't have the rule.

Write me the joke. It has to be a real joke, setup then punchline, about 100 to 150 words, and the punchline has to turn on the actual philosophical content — specifically the idea that the person who skips the queue has done something more than break a social rule, because he gets his chips precisely by relying on everyone else holding themselves back, whereas the exchange student maybe hasn't done that at all even though from the outside the two look exactly the same. Don't make it a joke about philosophy being useless or about philosophers overthinking things, no puns on duty or on ought implies can, and please don't do the two philosophers walk into a bar thing. Keep it British: chip shop, queue, not a food truck and a line.

Then give me two alternative punchlines for that same setup, one that a normal 17 year old would laugh at without ever having read anything, and one that only really lands if you know the free rider argument. Label them clearly, and don't explain any of them afterwards — if the joke needs explaining I'd rather find that out now.
