Two cases, identical except for one number.

A reservoir supplies a town. A particular solvent renders the water permanently undrinkable and causes a serious illness in everyone who has drunk it, but only above a threshold: at 499 litres the water is perfectly safe, at 500 litres the whole town falls ill, and at 501 litres the whole town falls exactly as ill, the illness having no dose–response gradient of any kind. In Case A, 500 factories each discharge exactly one litre and the town falls ill. In Case B, 501 factories each discharge exactly one litre and the town falls ill. In both cases every owner knows the threshold, knows with certainty precisely how many of the others will discharge, has the same motive (the cheapest disposal method available), faces no detection or sanction, and is unable to communicate or co-ordinate with any of the others.

Here is the argument our reading group has been going round for three weeks.

(1) An act is impermissible if and only if, holding fixed what every other agent actually does, the world would have been better had the agent refrained. (I stipulate this because I want to be a straightforward act-consequentialist, and this is the criterion of rightness that view supplies.)
(2) In Case A, for each factory, the world would have been better had it refrained: the water would have held 499 litres and the town would have been safe.
(3) So in Case A every one of the 500 discharges is impermissible.
(4) In Case B, for each factory, the world would have been no better had it refrained: the water would still have held 500 litres and the town would have fallen just as ill.
(5) So in Case B none of the 501 discharges is impermissible.
(6) The only difference between the cases is that one further factory discharges. Motives, knowledge, volumes discharged, total illness and the identities of the sufferers are all the same.
(7) Therefore a single additional participant converts 500 impermissible acts into 501 permissible ones.
(8) And generally, for any harm whatsoever there is a number of participants beyond which no one's contribution to it is impermissible: wrongness shrinks as participation grows, and any harm can be dissolved simply by recruiting enough contributors.
(9) That is not believable.
(10) Therefore (1) is false, and acts can be impermissible even when they make no difference to how the world goes.

I hold (9) firmly and I do not want to give up (1), which is the whole of my moral theory; that is what makes (10) so disagreeable. Note also that the usual exits have been bricked up in advance by the stipulations. The owners are certain of one another's conduct, so there is no residual probability and no expected harm to appeal to; the illness has no gradient, so there is no marginal contribution to severity; and they cannot co-ordinate, so there is no joint agent available to carry the obligation. What is left, as far as I can see, is the uneasy thought that in Case B a factory that discharges does nothing worse than one that refrains — and if refraining is not required of it, it becomes hard to say what its discharging is doing wrong.

What I should like from you is a diagnosis rather than a verdict on the toy case. Which step should be rejected, and what does rejecting it cost, both for the theory and for the intuitions that motivated it? If your answer is that (4) is false, say what makes it false given the stipulations, and explain why the counterfactual in it looks so compelling if it is. Then tell me whether the stipulations are legitimate or whether they simply define a case in which (10) was guaranteed from the outset, so that the argument shows nothing about harms in the actual world. Finally: if (10) does stand, does it transfer to the cases the group keeps invoking — greenhouse-gas emissions, buying factory-farmed chicken, declining to vote — or do those differ from Case B in some structural respect that the toy case conceals?

Throughout, please keep separate what is settled among people who work on this from what remains genuinely contested, and mark clearly any point at which you are advancing a disputed position of your own rather than reporting common ground. I would much rather be told that a question is live than be handed a contested resolution as though it were the consensus.
