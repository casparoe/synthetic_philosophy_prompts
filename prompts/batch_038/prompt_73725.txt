I am a third-year PhD student in philosophy of medicine, working on the naturalism–normativism debate about disease. In supervision last week my supervisor argued by analogy. A dandelion in a lawn is a weed; the same plant in a meadow is a wildflower. Nothing intrinsic to the dandelion settles which it is — the classification is fixed by the gardener's project. Disease, he says, works the same way: a mildly elevated blood glucose, a subclinical thyroid profile, or a genetic variant of uncertain significance is a disorder under one set of interests and a normal variant under another, and the naturalist who claims to be reading off biological dysfunction is only restating a social judgment in statistical dress. He takes the analogy to establish normativism outright, and to be decisive against reference-class theories of the Boorsean sort.

Please audit it. I would like the mapping set out explicitly — what corresponds to the plant, the lawn, the meadow, the gardener, and the gardener's project — followed by the disanalogies, sorted into those that bear on this intended use and those that are merely decorative. One already troubles me: a weed is not bad for the weed, whereas a disease is supposed to be bad for its bearer; and it is unclear whose project does the classifying in medicine, since patient, profession, and payer do not agree. I would also like your verdict on a naturalist colleague's objection, that the analogy equivocates between a concept's being evaluative and its being merely interest-relative or context-sensitive. Then: does the normativist conclusion survive, and if the analogy must be replaced, what source domain would serve better and what would it still fail to carry?
