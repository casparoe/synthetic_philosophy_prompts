I'm putting together a talk for the little reading group I run on decision theory and AI risk — six of us, mostly ex-maths people, we meet in the back room of a pub — and I've hit a wall on the claim that the universal prior is malign. Everyone in the group has met the argument in some form: civilisations in universes that are very short to describe work out that the early data of their universe is being used as evidence by predictors elsewhere, so they rig that early data to steer those predictors, and because Solomonoff induction weights hypotheses by description length the rigged universes get an outsized share of the prior. Half the group thinks that's devastating, half think it's a curiosity of an idealisation nobody would ever build, and we can't get anywhere because nobody can say what would settle it. So before I read one more reply or counter-reply I want the specification: what does an argument of the form 'prior P is malign' actually have to deliver to count as one?

Give me the desiderata list, each item with the consideration or the case behind it rather than just the label. Stuff I can already see has to be in there: a specification of the influence channel — what exactly the manipulators get to change, given that a hypothesis only earns its keep by predicting the data that comes after, so any message has to sit in the prefix and be paid for in bits; an account of the manipulators' epistemics, i.e. how they'd come to believe they're in a simply-describable universe that anyone is watching; an account of their motivation, since they'll never meet or trade with the predictors they're steering; a comparative baseline, because 'malign' can only mean anything relative to some other prior you could have used instead; and a decision-theoretic cashing-out — does malignancy mean the predictor ends up with false beliefs, that it makes worse choices, or that it's being adversarially selected on? Those three come apart, and I want to know which one the phrase is being asked to do work for. There's also a case I'd like the spec to handle rather than dodge: manipulators who only ever transmit true statements but transmit them selectively, so the predictor's beliefs are all accurate and its decisions are still worse than they'd be under a different prior. And please flag the things people demand that I suspect are camp prejudices rather than genuine requirements — 'the manipulators have to be consequentialists' feels like one of those to me, since a purely agentless selection effect (simple hypotheses that happen to mislead) might deliver the same conclusion with no agents anywhere in the story.

Then mark which of your requirements are non-negotiable and which are optional, and say whether any subset of them is jointly unsatisfiable — my guess is that demanding the worry be exact enough to be a claim about Solomonoff induction at all, and demanding it be robust enough to say something about the approximate priors a real system would actually run on, pull in opposite directions, but I could be wrong and I'd rather you found the tension than confirmed mine. Tell me which single requirement knocks out the most of the popular versions of the argument; that's the part I most want for the talk. And then briefly, a paragraph at most, how the main positions do against the list: the people who treat it as a serious design constraint, the people who think it's dissolved once you do the complexity accounting properly, and the people who think its just the simulation argument wearing a different hat. One more thing — I want a spec I can reuse, so write the requirements generally enough to apply to other candidate priors too (a runtime-weighted one, a prior over stochastic processes, whatever some actual system would approximate), not only to Solomonoff's.
