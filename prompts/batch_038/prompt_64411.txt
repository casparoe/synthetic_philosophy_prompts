I manage the community-collections programme at a regional museum in a former industrial town. We receive between six and twelve offers of material a month — a dairy worker's shift notebooks, a bundle of court depositions from a Victorian poaching prosecution, a canteen worker's letters — and for each we must decide two things: whether to accession it, and whether it could ever carry a display in its own right. Our stated aim is history from below, but our working criterion is really "is it interesting, and does it fill a gap?", and the predictable result is that our holdings keep defaulting to the tidy and the powerful, because those are the people who left archives.

Microhistory, as I understand it, is not merely a preference for small subjects but a set of methodological commitments: that reducing the scale of observation can disclose structures a survey cannot see; that an atypical individual is sometimes a better window onto a norm than a representative one; that the traces of ordinary lives were usually generated by the authorities watching, regulating or prosecuting them; and that an individual's choices only become intelligible against the local system of constraints, obligations and strategies they were navigating. I want those commitments converted into a triage procedure.

Constraints: each offer is assessed by two curators and one volunteer, none of whom has training in historiography; we have forty minutes per offer, with the material itself and no more than a phone call to the donor; anything involving a living donor or a display commitment comes to me for sign-off.

Please write the procedure as an ordered set of questions: what counts as a sufficient answer to each, which questions can be settled from what is in front of us and which would need research we cannot do, where the procedure returns a refusal, and where it stops and hands the decision to a person because the issue is one of values rather than evidence. Say which of the distinctions above must survive the simplification and which can be dropped without distortion, and flag the steps that are judgement calls wearing procedural dress.

Then run it on three offers: a complete, continuous run of minute books from the local engineers' union branch, 1948–1979; a single undated notebook kept by a woman who worked in the same plant's canteen, mostly prices, shifts and gossip, with pages missing; and the personal papers and photograph albums of the family that owned the plant, offered by the grandson on the understanding that we mount a display. At least one of these should come out refused or downgraded — say which, and why, even if it is the one I would most like to accept. Finally, state what the procedure cannot do, and in particular what it cannot remedy about the fact that the people we most want to hear from are the ones who left the fewest traces.

Please write your answer in British English.
