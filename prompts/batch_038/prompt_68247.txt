Two years ago my younger brother underwent a complete corpus callosotomy for drug-resistant epilepsy. The surgery worked: he has been nearly seizure-free since. But I am a graduate student in philosophy of mind, and I have spent the past year reading the philosophical literature on what such an operation is supposed to mean for the unity of consciousness, and I have come away with the strong impression that the debate has been running for more than fifty years without anyone moving anyone. I would like a diagnosis of that stall rather than a survey of it.

Here is how the terrain looks to me. On one side is the reading that goes back to the original commissurotomy work with Sperry and his colleagues: once the commissures are cut, the patient has in effect two spheres of consciousness, each with its own perceptions, learning, and feelings, revealed by the lateralized testing — a picture flashed to the left visual field is denied by the speaking hemisphere even while the left hand reaches for the object or draws it, cross-cueing between hemispheres succeeds in ways the patient cannot report, and there is the occasional intermanual conflict. Nagel's early piece on brain bisection gave this reading its philosophical seriousness, and Parfit used the case in his work on personal identity as evidence that questions of the form how many persons are here can be indeterminate, or at least not the question that matters. On the other side are those who think the two-minds description is a category mistake: everyday behavior after callosotomy is strikingly integrated, these patients do not behave like two people outside the laboratory, the disconnection symptoms tend to be transient, the left-hemisphere interpreter described by Gazzaniga suggests that what we are observing is one self-modeling system generating a single narrative from partial inputs, and a good deal of recent work on interhemispheric integration — including subcortical and extracallosal routes — holds that the dramatic dissociations are artifacts of a highly artificial testing situation.

My hypothesis, which I want tested rather than flattered, is that the two camps cite the same well-known facts and reach opposite conclusions because they are not answering the same question. The disunity side, as I read it, is answering a question about the conditions under which there is a single stream of phenomenal consciousness; the unity side is answering a question about who owns the behavior — the subject of agency and self-ascription. What I suspect is doing the real work underneath, however, is a shared assumption neither side states aloud: that how many minds is a counting question with a determinate answer, such that settling the empirical facts about integration would settle the count. If that is right, the impasse is not evidence that the empirical question is hard but evidence that the question is being posed in a way that presupposes a single natural kind which may not be there.

So: is my hypothesis right, or is this something else entirely — a genuinely verbal dispute about the words consciousness and mind, a dispute about which cases count as data (the laboratory versus the life), a hidden empirical question about subcortical communication that would in fact decide the matter, or bedrock intuitions about the individuation of subjects that neither side can argue for? Please say what kind of disagreement it really is, support that diagnosis with what each side actually claims rather than with a tour of the literature, and then tell me what would count as progress given the diagnosis: a distinction, an experimental design, a formal result, a change of question, or an argument that none should be expected. If some real distinction would dissolve part of the stall, draw it out explicitly, and say which pieces of the debate survive it.

I am not looking for a balanced overview. Take a definite position on the diagnosis, commit to it, and give your reasons; where you are less than confident, flag that specifically and explain why, rather than hedging the whole thing. Please write your response in British English.
