I am a junior taking a dual-credit political theory seminar through my high school, and our term-long assignment is a paper that reads one work of literature through one live debate in political philosophy. I have chosen William Golding's Lord of the Flies and the debate over direct democracy and referendums: the open-air assemblies in Glarus and Appenzell Innerrhoden, where citizens still decide cantonal questions by a visible show of hands instead of a secret ballot; the Brexit vote; and the California initiative process. Nearly every reading of Golding I have encountered treats the island as a story about human nature, or as a thought experiment pitting Hobbes against Locke. I want to argue something narrower and, I think, less often defended: that the novel is also a careful study of what direct democracy's procedures do and do not survive when the demos is tiny, unbounded, and cannot promise another round of voting.

I would like your help building that reading, and your help finding where it breaks. So that you are not guessing at what I mean, here is the machinery I intend to use.

The boundary problem. A referendum can settle a question for a people only if the people is already fixed, since deciding who belongs to the demos by majority vote would itself require a demos. The island is a test case: the littluns are present at the assembly yet are never treated as participants, Piggy stands inside the ring and outside the count, and later Jack asserts that the conch has no force at his end of the island. Is that last move a claim about jurisdiction, an act of secession, or an admission that the rule was only ever a habit?

Losers' consent. The stability of a vote depends less on the winners than on losers who judge the procedure fair and expect another chance in the future. Jack calls for a vote to remove Ralph as chief, the vote fails, and instead of accepting the result he leaves and forms a rival tribe. Does the novel support the losers'-consent explanation, that Jack exits because he sees no future rounds, or is something else driving him, and if it is, does that weaken my thesis?

Irreversibility. A standing objection to referendums is that a bare majority can make a decision that is very hard to undo. What is the island's point of no return, and is the shattering of the conch the novel making that argument or only reaching for a symbol-heavy climax?

Competence against consent. Ralph wins the first vote largely because he is holding the conch and looks the part, while the boy with the practical knowledge is never a serious candidate. Golding seems to invite an epistocratic conclusion. But the hunters are better at the task the island actually sets, so the novel may cut against that conclusion too.

Please write criticism rather than summary: identify which scenes carry the argument, describe the moments you rely on instead of quoting them, and say plainly where the novel refuses to cooperate. The ending, with the naval officer and the cruiser, seems engineered to point away from any institutional lesson, and Simon's experience on the mountain is not a political claim at all. I also want you to answer the objection my teacher is certain to raise: that a group of frightened children with no ballot, no agenda, and no means of enforcement is not a case of direct democracy in any recognizable sense, so the entire reading is a category error. Finish by telling me what Golding adds to the referendum debate that argument by itself cannot supply.

My paper is capped at about fifteen hundred words, so please keep your response near that length, and do not include a bibliography or a list of sources. I cannot consult them from here, and I would rather have your reasoning than your references.
