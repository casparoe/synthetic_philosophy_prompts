I'm the animal welfare lead at a mid-size aquaculture group -- whiteleg shrimp ponds in one country, tilapia cages in another. Roughly forty proposals a year land on my desk from site managers and ops people: cut stocking density, put shelters in the grow-out ponds, change the harvest method, drop eyestalk ablation in the hatchery, try a species of crab we've never raised because a buyer asked for it. Every single one arrives framed as a welfare improvement, and I have no consistent way to say yes, no, or not yet. My team is two aquaculture biologists and me. None of us has a philosophy background and we get maybe one afternoon per proposal.

Write me a decision procedure we could actually run: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where the procedure stops with a no, where it hands the decision up to me because it's a question of values rather than facts, and where it should say we don't have the data and what we'd have to go collect. Ground it in whatever the welfare science on fish and crustaceans genuinely gives you -- behavioral trade-off studies, learning and conditioning, nociception as distinct from pain experience, operational welfare indicators -- and tell me which of those distinctions has to survive the translation into something a site manager can use, and which ones we can safely collapse without the procedure turning into theater.

Four things it has to handle, because those are the ones we actually get stuck on:

1. Evidence asymmetry. For the shrimp we farm there's almost nothing species-specific, and most of what exists is on relatives. When is it legitimate to generalize across species, when does absence of evidence mean go ahead anyway, and when does it mean stop? I want a definite rule here, not a shrug.

2. Proposals that change how many animals exist rather than how any one animal's life goes. Cut stocking density by a third and we raise fewer shrimp, each of which plausibly does better. Our ops director scores that as a loss, our sustainability people score it as a win. Should the procedure score it at all, and if so how, without becoming a population-ethics seminar?

3. Harvest. We chill in ice slurry, which our certification scheme still permits below a set temperature, but which the welfare researchers increasingly call bad, and electrical stunning looks better -- except the stunners are capital-heavy and only really works on a couple of our sites. The procedure needs to output an answer here, not a summary of the disagreement.

4. Something where the science points against a change I already favor. Put at least one case like that in the worked examples and let the procedure come out against me.

Then run it on three proposals: switching the whole group from ice-slurry harvest to electrical stunning; cutting shrimp stocking density by a third; and moving into a species of swimming crab we've never raised, where the buyer wants volume and there's essentially no welfare literature to recieve.

Be committed about it. I don't want a balanced tour of the options with me left picking -- take a position on the contested calls (precaution versus permissiveness under uncertainty about sentience, whether the numbers question belongs in the procedure at all, how much evidence an indicator needs before it's allowed to drive a yes) and give your reasons. If you think one of my four hard cases is a distraction and shouldn't be in the procedure, say so and defend that. American English please, and no references or reading lists -- I can't chase citations, just tell me what you think.
