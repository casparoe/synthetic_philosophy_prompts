Six weeks ago I was appointed to a seven-person review panel at a mid-sized research funder, and at our second meeting we produced a result that has left me uneasy enough that I have now been asked to write a short memo to the panel and our program officer explaining what happened and what our options were. Before I write it, I want to understand the underlying conceptual issue properly, and I would be grateful for a careful explanation rather than a survey of positions.

Here is the case. One proposal before us was governed by a funding rule with a conjunctive structure: a proposal is eligible only if it is both excellent on scientific merit and feasible within the requested budget and timeline. Voting member by member, four of us judged it excellent on merit and a partly different four judged it feasible; but only two of us judged both. So when the chair put the question of eligibility directly, five voted against and two in favour. The chair ruled that the vote on eligibility governs, since our regulations define the panel's decision as a recommendation on funding. Several members objected that the panel had just found a proposal excellent and feasible and then denied that it was eligible, and that this was straightforwardly incoherent.

What I would like explained:

1. What has to be true for a divergence of this kind to arise at all? I gather it is not an artifact of our particular votes, and that there are general results about when a body's judgments can be aggregated without this happening. I would like to know what those results assume, which assumption is doing the most work, and which one looks weakest or most worth giving up in an institutional setting like mine.

2. A colleague insists this is simply the voting paradox he met as an undergraduate — majority cycles — and that our panel had intransitive preferences. I do not think he is right, because as far as I can tell nothing cycled: each of the three questions received a decisive majority, and our ranking of the shortlisted proposals was transitive. Please explain what actually distinguishes the two phenomena, whether the distinction is deep or merely a difference in vocabulary, and what the difference implies about how each one should be handled. He also says that once you see it as a cycle the remedy is obvious — change the order in which the votes are taken — and I would like to know whether that advice transfers to our case.

3. Our panel additionally ranked the shortlisted proposals by a weighted score: each member assigns points on three criteria, and the points are summed. Another member argues that the same choice arises here — you can either sum each member's points and then compare totals, or take a majority on each criterion first and then sum the majority scores, and the two routes can select different winners. Is that correct? If so, is it the same paradox, given that a weighted sum involves no logical connection between the criteria and the total, or is it a structurally different failure with a different lesson?

4. Two members recused themselves from judging feasibility on this proposal, because of a prior collaboration with the applicant, but not from judging merit. The merit majority was therefore computed over seven of us and the feasibility majority over five. Does the framework make sense at all when the premises are decided by different constituencies, and what does that do to the analysis in (1)?

5. Finally, part of the objection at our meeting was not procedural but about whether the panel is the sort of thing that can hold a view. I would like to understand what turns on that: does treating a committee as an agent with judgments of its own change what it ought to do, or is it only a manner of speaking?

Two further requests. I have seen the terms doctrinal paradox and discursive dilemma used both interchangeably and as marking a real distinction; please tell me which, and whether the distinction matters for a case like ours. And I am not after references or a reading list — I cannot chase papers down at the moment — so please give me the reasoning rather than the sources.

Above all, I need you to be scrupulous about separating what is settled from what is genuinely disputed. Some of what I have read presents the premise-based procedure as the correct response to divergences like ours, and I suspect that this is a contested normative claim rather than a theorem. Please distinguish clearly among mathematical results whose conditions people agree on, normative proposals that remain live questions among those who work on the topic, and matters of mere institutional taste; and where you are inclined toward a view that is not the majority position, say so explicitly instead of writing as though it were the consensus.
