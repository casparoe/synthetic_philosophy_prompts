I co-run a political epistemology seminar — 16 students, mostly philosophy and econ, plus a couple from the policy school who have never heard the word futarchy, and honestly half the philosophy ones only know it from a podcast. We've got one 90-minute slot next week and I want them to walk into the problem rather than hear me describe it.

Design me a market activity. Here's the shape I have in mind, push back if it's wrong: two rounds, identical machinery, different decision. Round one they trade on something where the market genuinely works — the price converges and turns out right — so they get the "ok, obviously this beats a vote" feeling in their chests. Round two I want the same machinery to fail, and to fail in the specific way I actually care about, which is not manipulation and not thin trading. It's that the thing they're pricing is a proxy whose relationship to the target flips depending on whether the policy gets adopted. My candidate case: a university deciding whether to make first-year seminars pass/fail, with contracts paying out on the share of students who declare a major by the end of sophomore year. If its the policy itself that changes what that number means, the price was never evidence about what the council actually cares about, and the market can be confidently, consistantly wrong for entirely good reasons.

What I need: the trading mechanism (whiteboard and index cards, no laptops — I'm not running a live double auction with 16 people for the first time in my life), how private information gets distributed so there's actually something for the price to aggregate, a payoff scheme that needs no real money but still makes them care (I have course-credit tokens, a bag of chocolates, and low standards), and a minute-by-minute timeline that fits 90 including a debrief which maps each moment onto the concept without me turning into a lecturer. The takeaway I'm aiming at is that the objection isn't "markets are dumb," it's that a pricing target has to be causally insulated from the decision it's being used to make — and I want them to discover that, not be told it.

Two more things. Last year I ran a cruder version where they traded on whether a hypothetical mayor would win reelection, and it collapsed into a lark — nobody's estimate had consequences and the loudest kid just set the price. Diagnose that failure properly and tell me how yours avoids it. And be blunt about the feature of the view the game can't reproduce: I already suspect it's the oracle. I get to close out contracts against a ground truth real futarchy never has, and that might make my whole exercise a confidence trick.

Finally, tell me where you're least confident. If you think round one is doing no pedagogical work and I should go straight to the failure, argue that case. And say what would change your mind about the pass/fail example being the right vehicle — what would have to be true of it for it to backfire on me instead.
