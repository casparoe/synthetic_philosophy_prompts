I am a third-year philosophy PhD student working in the experimental philosophy of moral judgment; most of my week is spent designing, piloting, and pre-registering vignettes. I have run into a recurrent dialectical pattern in the non-experimental literature I read that I have not been able to name, and I would like to know whether an established concept exists for it before I keep describing it circumlocutiously in my own work.

The pattern seems to me to have two halves, and I want the concept, if there is one, to cover both.

First: a thought experiment is presented with a load-bearing detail left out. Not left out in the way all abstractions leave things out, but left out at precisely the point where the verdict is liable to turn. Readers close the gap silently, from their own background assumptions, and they close it differently from one another. In the discussion that follows, everyone talks as though they were reacting to one and the same case, so what is really a disagreement about how to complete the case surfaces as a disagreement about the intuition the case elicits.

Second: when an objector completes the case in a way that damages the author's argument, the author completes it differently, and offers this as clarification rather than revision. The case is filled in on demand, always in whatever direction defuses the objection at hand, and no one treats that as changing the example, because the new detail is said to have been there all along, or to be obvious, or to be beside the point.

Two instances. (1) A paper on moral worth argues that an agent who does the right thing effortlessly, without feeling any competing inclination, is not praiseworthy in the way an agent who overcomes temptation is. The case as described never says whether the effortless agent is aware of the temptation she does not feel. One objector says: suppose she is aware of it and simply unmoved, in which case the comparison with the struggler looks unfair; the author replies that the case was obviously meant to be one in which she is unaware. Another objector says: suppose she is unaware, in which case she cannot be faulted for lacking self-control either; the author replies that the case was obviously meant to be one in which the temptation is vivid and present to her. The conclusion survives both replies, and neither reply is logged as a modification of the example. (2) In my own work: a vignette asking whether a worker's decision to take a hazardous job is free. We initially left her outside options unspecified, and responses split sharply between participants who assumed she had savings and family nearby and those who assumed she had neither. We now fix the outside options in every version and the split disappears. The philosophical cases I read leave the same slot empty, but the literature reports a single intuition about them and argues from it.

What I would like from you:

1. Is there an established concept for this? The nearest things I have found are enthymeme, begging the question, moving the goalposts, No True Scotsman, Dennett's notion of the intuition pump together with the associated advice to keep varying a pump's details until the intuition either holds or breaks, the framing and wording effects my own field studies, and the general defence of idealization. For each of these, and for anything better that I have missed, tell me what it does cover in my description and what it fails to cover, and then say which one fits best.

2. If none of them is the thing, name it. I want a term, a definition with something like necessary and sufficient conditions, and a diagnostic I could apply to a case in print that tells me when an omission is the benign abstraction it almost always is and when it is this.

3. Give me boundary cases: one situation that looks like this pattern but is not, and one that is this pattern but would not ordinarily be flagged as such.

4. Tell me whether it is a defect at all. Is there a respectable view on which the incompleteness is productive, for instance because it lets one case be argued across several frameworks, or because it mirrors the underdetermination present in the real situations we theorize about? I would rather hear that case now than discover it in a seminar.

5. Say what, if anything, the pattern licenses on the critic's side: may a critic complete the case in the way least favourable to the author's conclusion and argue against that, or must critics wait for the author to commit?

Two things about how you answer. First, please be explicit about how confident you are that no established term exists, and remember that your failing to find one is only weak evidence that none exists; flag clearly which parts of your answer are reconstruction and which are reporting. Second, I would rather be contradicted than accommodated. If you think the request rests on a confused premise — if what I have described is really two separable phenomena that should not share a name, or is just enthymeme plus goalpost-moving with nothing left over once those are subtracted, or is a pseudo-problem generated by expecting more determinacy from a prose example than any such example can supply — say so plainly and argue it, instead of manufacturing a term because I asked for one.
