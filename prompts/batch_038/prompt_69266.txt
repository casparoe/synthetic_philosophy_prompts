I'm prepping something a bit unusual for our department's end-of-year gathering — we do a "philosophy comedy night" thing where faculty and grad students perform pieces, and I drew the short straw on the patristics slot. I've been teaching a seminar on the ethics of lying this term, and the week that went over best by far was Augustine's, mostly because the students couldn't believe how airtight and how bizarre his position is at the same time. So: write me an advice column, in the house style of a diocesan newsletter or a monastery's monthly bulletin, running an agony-aunt page answered by an invented Augustinian absolutist — call him something like "Ask Consentius" or pick your own pen name — who applies the no-lying-ever rule with total sincerity and total pastoral warmth. Around 800 words, five letters with headers plus answers. The funniest ones should be the ones where the correspondent's problem is genuinely urgent and the answer is scrupulously correct and completely useless: somebody sheltering a relative from people who mean him harm, a parent whose kid wants to know whether the dog is dead, a nurse fielding a question from a dying patient's family, and someone who wants to fake his way into a group that's exploiting his sister so he can pull her out. That last one should track the actual question put to Augustine about whether you may feign heresy to catch heretics who themselves thought feigning was licit under persecution — the answer has to be no, and the reasoning has to be his, not ours.

The jokes need to come from getting the doctrine right, so that someone who's read the two treatises laughs rather than winces, and so that nobody can accuse the piece of strawmanning a dead bishop. Work in: lying as the disease of the double heart, with speech being for showing what's in you rather than hiding it; the ranking of lies by gravity, where a lie touching doctrine sits at the top and a lie that harms nobody and protects somebody sits at the bottom but is still a sin and still needs penance; the exemption for jest on the grounds that nobody's actually being deceived; the sanctioned toolkit of silence, refusal to answer, answering a question with a question, and saying only what you actually believe; and the argument that you may not buy another person's bodily safety with your own soul's death, since the first is temporary and the second isn't. One correspondent should have already lied and be writing in for reassurance, and that answer should grade the offense instead of just thundering at it. Also have the columnist explicitly swat away the idea that you can say one thing while meaning another in your heart — the mental-reservation stuff that came later, which he'd hate — because that beat does a lot of work and it's exactly where readers expect an escape hatch. He should never once slip into consequentialist reasoning, never once admit an exception, and stay kind throughout; the deadpan warmth is what makes it land.

After the column, tack on a short editor's note of maybe 150 words, and here I want you to actually commit rather than hedge: take a definite stand on whether the absolutism survives the workarounds. Either argue that the permission to stay silent and to refuse to answer quietly does all the normative work and leaves the prohibition on lying as a distinction without a difference — or argue the opposite, that the view is internally coherent and it's the letter-writers' intuitions that are miscalibrated. Pick one, give your reasons in the note, and flag honestly where the column took liberties: whether the jest exemption is really as clean as the piece implies, whether misleading-but-true speech is genuinely available to him, whether the grading of lies is ordered the way I've described it. Don't hand me a both-sides summary at the end — I'll get enough of that from the seminar.
