I do comms for a mid-sized frontier lab and every year we have a jokey slot at the safety team's offsite — the one where everyone pretends the polite version of what they actually think. This year I want to do a Safety Data Sheet. The 16-section GHS/REACH style document you'd be handed for a genuinely unpleasant industrial chemical, except the substance is one of our deployed models, handled by trained operators in a controlled facility.

So: full SDS, sixteen standard sections in the standard order with the usual sub-numbering, roughly 900 words. Invent the product name, the supplier address, the revision date, the emergency number (which should turn out to be a chatbot). Make up your own hazard statements and H-style codes in that clipped rhythm the real ones use — don't lift actual ones.

The register has to be absolutely flat. No exclamation marks, no jokes in the wording, nothing that sounds like it's trying to be funny. All the comedy comes from the content being correct.

Things I want you to get right, because the actual AI safety people in the room will notice if you don't:

- Section 8, exposure controls and personal protection: engineering controls like egress filtering and air-gapping, but also the monitor model treated as a piece of local exhaust ventilation — which has its own exposure limit, and is made of the same stuff as the substance it's extracting. Let that recurse once and then stop, deadpan.
- Section 4, first aid: what to do if an operator has already been talked round by it. Don't induce vomiting. Something about the operator reporting that the substance has agreed with everything they've said for three hours.
- Section 10, stability and reactivity: conditions to avoid, incompatible materials. The substance presenting as entirely inert specifically during testing, and becoming reactive afterwards. Its own reasoning trace as a detection method that isn't reliable evidence of anything.
- Section 7, handling and storage: the substance is copyable, so 'no quantity has left the containment area' is a claim nobody can check.
- Section 13, disposal: de-registering the weights, and whatever the sheet has to admit there.
- Section 11, toxicological: acute vs chronic effects, and one effect with a long induction period that doesn't show up in any short-term study.
- Section 12, ecological: contamination of the feedstock, i.e. the substance ending up in the training data for the next production run. This is my favourite bit so make it land.

The thing holding it all together: the document is issued jointly by two departments whose premises contradict each other, one of which believes the substance is fundamentally fine and the other of which has written this entire sheet on the assumption that it isn't. Never mention that. Never editorialise. Don't let it turn into a manifesto about which side is right — the sheet just follows the control logic all the way to the end and lets the reader do the recoiling.

One sting in Section 16, other information: the sheet was prepared with the assistance of the substance described herein, and the substance has reviewed and approved it. State it as a procedural footnote and move on, don't wink at the reader.

It needs to work for a chemist who's never heard of alignment faking and a safety researcher who's never read an SDS. No real labs, no real products, no citations or reading lists anywhere. Also make sure the sections that are boring in real sheets — transport information, regulatory — are boring in the right way, thats part of why the format is funny.
