I am in the fourth year of a PhD in moral philosophy; my dissertation concerns non-aggregative approaches to the distribution of harm, and next month I am convening a reading-group session on the numbers problem. I would like to open that session with a piece of satire rather than another summary, and I would like you to write it.

The piece should take the form of the public-facing user documentation for an imagined agency's case-management system — call it something like the Office of Pairwise Review — whose statutory function is to determine whether a proposed principle of conduct is one that nobody affected by it could reasonably reject. Around 750 words: a brief system overview, then numbered FAQ entries, then two or three boxed "release notes" or "known issues" items. Deadpan throughout, in the register of a well-meaning civil service that has no idea its own procedures are strange.

The humour must come from the procedure being implemented literally and correctly, not from the agency being incompetent. Requirements:

1. The system has no field for a total. Tickets are compared one complainant against one other complainant; burdens are ranked ordinally and may not be added. Include the error text returned when a user attempts a summation, and the support team's explanation of why the request is not merely unsupported but conceptally malformed.

2. Include one worked case, presented as a support-ticket thread, built on the familiar example in which a single man is suffering a severe electrical injury and switching off the transmitter would merely deprive an enormous number of viewers of the closing minutes of a broadcast. Show what the system actually does with it — how many comparisons it must run, what it returns, what the queue estimate says — and let the reader see simultaneously that the verdict is right and that the process is lunatic.

3. The system must contain a module that permits burdens to be added when they are of comparable magnitude and refuses when they are not, and the documentation must describe the boundary between those two regimes as something the user is expected to locate unaided. The known-issues entry concerning near-comparable harms — two broken arms against one death, a great many migraines against one minor injury — should be the funniest item in the document and also the most philosophically pointed.

4. Where the two sides are exactly balanced — equal numbers of people with equally strong complaints, the rescue cases — the system resolves the matter by random draw. Write this up as an entirely ordinary feature, complete with an audit trail, a seed value, and a note about the annual re-certification of the generator.

5. There must be a legacy import. The agency formerly ran a system that simply added everything up; it has been decommissioned; something about the migration is still going wrong. Do not explain what the old system's outputs were. Let it haunt the documentation.

6. Intake screening. A complaint may be rejected at the door for the wrong kind of reason — most obviously, because the complainant's stated ground for objecting is that the principle under review is wrong. Give the field names, the standard rejection letter, and one appeal that fails for a reason the appellant cannot be told.

7. At least one entry in which the system produces a determinate verdict the reader can see is indefensible, and at least one in which it produces a verdict that is defensible but for which it cannot state the reason in its own vocabulary.

Constraints: never name a real philosopher, and never quote a real text — every phrase in quotation marks must be invented by you and must belong to this fictional agency's house style. Do not break character, do not append an author's note explaining the jokes, and do not use the words utilitarian or consequentialist except, at most, once, somewhere in the legacy-import material. A reader who has never encountered the debate should be able to follow the document as a piece of bureaucratic writing; a reader who has should recognize every mechanism in it. And if the satire is fair, the document should at certain points make the non-aggregative procedure look like the only sane option available — that ambivalence is exactly what I want the reading group to argue about afterwards.
