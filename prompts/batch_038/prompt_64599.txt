I'm two years into a PhD in a sleep lab where most of my actual job is waking people up at scheduled points in the night and coding what they tell you. Our PI did her first degree in philosophy and she still personally adjudicates every borderline coding call, so without really meaning to I've built up about a year of her verdicts, and they're weirdly systematic. Here's the pattern, roughly:

- Woken mid-REM, immediate detailed first-person report of a scene she was inside → counts as an experience, no argument, ever.
- Woken mid-REM, says "I wasn't dreaming, I was just thinking about the drive home" — flat, imageless, no scene → coded as thought, not experience.
- Morning questionnaire: "I dreamt a lot last night," but when probed cannot produce a single image, place, or event → does not count. Her margin note is basically "impression with no content."
- Woken mid-REM, gives a report; asked again fifteen minutes later, gives a longer and somewhat different one → still counts, with a flag that the second telling is partly built after the fact.
- A door slams in the corridor and turns up in the report as a car crash → counts, and she says stimulus incorporation is the only direct evidence we ever get that something was happening in there at the time.
- Lucid dreamer who runs the pre-agreed left-right-left eye signal from inside the dream and reports accurately what it meant → counts; she calls these the gold standard.
- Deep slow-wave sleep, nothing reported at any of three awakenings → no experience, and she sees no temptation to posit one.
- Hypnogogic flash of a face at sleep onset, remembered seconds later → counts as an experience, but she refuses to code it as a dream, which annoys the clinicians no end.
- Day-surgery patient under propofol who reports a fragmentary scene in recovery, has no memory of the intervening gap, and can't reproduce the fragment when asked again an hour later → does not count.

The new one landed while she's away until Monday and I want to predict her verdict before I hear it. Subject is a patient with dense anterograde amnesia who was in the building for a different protocol, and we got ethics sign-on to do a sleep night on her. At 4:10am, polysomnogram says REM, we wake her: full scenic report, she's inside it, first person, coherent, and the hallway ice machine that kicked on forty seconds earlier comes through as a vending machine in a hospital corridor. She also executes the trained left-right-left eye signal correctly and tells us what it was supposed to mean. Everything looks like one of her gold-standard nights. Then we wake her again twenty-five minutes later and she has no idea she was woken before, denies having dreamt anything, and by morning there's nothing — the only trace the first episode ever leaves is our audio file and my notes. So: the coupling evidence is as strong as we ever get it, the content is as good as it gets, and the retention is zero. She said the opposite thing about the propofol case, which looks superficially similar to me — report given, memory of the interval gone — so I genuinely can't see which way she'll jump.

What I want is: first, the candidate rules that fit every verdict in that list, and I'm pretty confident there are at least three — something keyed to scenic immersion, something keyed to real-time coupling with the world, something keyed to what survives to be re-accessed later, and maybe versions of Malcolm's and Dennett's positions from the literature sitting underneath one or more of them, though she may be running a rule that only makes sense if you've spent years staring at a polysomnagram. Second, what each of those rules predicts about the amnesia night, including whether any of them says she'd reject the question rather than answer it. Third, your best guess at her actual verdict and why you'd bet on it, and how the propofol precedent is doing the work either way. Fourth, one further case we could realistically run with the equipment we have — say what the sleep stage, the stimuli, and the report conditions would look like — that would separate the top two candidate rules, because right now I think they're tied. And fifth, since the coding has to go into a dataset whether or not she's back, what's the call that's most consistent with her own precedents and what principle would we be committing ourselves to by making it. Be explicit about how confident you are in each of those five answers, and single out the one claim you'd most expect to be wrong about — my guess is it's whatever you say about which rule the propofol case actually rules out.
