I cover science and data stories for a weekly, and every quantitative piece I file goes past our numbers editor, Priya, before it runs. I've got a decent feel for what she'll bounce, but this week's one has me stumped. Help me predict her call — and tell me what she's actually running underneath it.

Her record so far:

- Two hospitals, one with the better overall death rate. Turned out it was worse among both its mild admissions and its severe ones, because it took nearly all the transfers and was stacked with severe cases. She killed the "safer hospital" headline and made us run the split table.
- A university admissions piece: men got offers at a higher rate overall, women did equal-or-better in every single department, because women applied disproportionately to the departments with the lowest acceptance rates. She let the overall number run but said it couldn't carry any claim about bias in either direction, and it had to sit right next to the department table.
- A drug story where the survival advantage vanished once you looked only at patients who'd finished the full course. She wouldn't run the conditional version at all — finishing the course is something the drug itself influences, so the comparison was rigged, and the crude difference was the honest one.
- Charter schools: worse overall, better inside every income bracket, because low-income kids were much more likely to be in charters. Her verdict was that the brackets are the story and the overall gap is mostly an artifact of who ends up where.
- A study where adjusting for a big confounder cut an association roughly in half but never flipped it. She said that's ordinary confounding, everyone knows it's in there, don't dress it up as a paradox.
- A country comparison where one place had the higher crude death rate and the lower age-standardized one. She said both are true and which one leads depends on the question: standardized for "is it healthier to live there," crude for "how many people will die there this year."

Now the one on my desk: a hospital early-discharge protocol. Readmissions at 30 days are *lower* overall for patients on the protocol. But split by length of stay, protocol patients do worse in every single bucket — under two days, two to four, five-plus. Length of stay is the thing the protocol changes (thats the whole point of it), and it also tracks how sick you were when you came in. Protocol patients are massively concentrated in the short-stay bucket. The story's question is whether the system should expand the protocol, and I have to write a headline that either credits it or indicts it.

So: what does Priya say? Which precedent does she reach for first? Is there one rule behind all six of her calls, or two or three that fit them equally well and come apart here — and if they come apart, what's my best guess at her verdict and what tips it? Then give me the follow-up case I could bring her, some hypothetical third study, that would tell those candidate rules apart. I care less about the true answer on readmissions than about which principle she's going to apply, and what I'd be commiting the piece to if I frame it her way.
