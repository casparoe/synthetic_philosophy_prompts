I teach an upper-level seminar, sort of a "rationality and its limits" thing — 22 students, mixed philosophy and econ majors, one 75-minute block. I want to open the unit on self-defeat with a game instead of a lecture. Target is Parfit's hitchhiker: the spot where it's rational to have become the kind of person who pays the driver later, even though once you're in town, paying is the last thing you have any reason to do. I want them to run into that from the inside and only hear the name in the debrief.

What I've got: movable chairs, a whiteboard, index cards, envelopes. No projector, phones banned. No budget for real money — I have a bag of candy and I can promise up to 2 points on the participation component, but I dont want it turning into a bloodbath where people are optimizing for candy. Also, every one of them has played a Prisoner's Dilemma somewhere, so the game must not be recognizable as one; if somebody says "oh this is just PD" in the first two minutes I've lost the room. 22 is even, so pairs work, but tell me what to do if three people are out sick.

The part I care about most: the prediction has to be endogenous. I'm not going to stand up there and stipulate a near-perfect reader of souls. Build the forecasting out of the students themselves — slips they write and get scored on for accuracy, or something in that neighborhood — and make it publicly visible how accurate the predictors actually were, because the whole lesson depends on them seeing that their classmates could genuinely read them. Spell out the candy/points conversion seperately from the game points so it doesn't get tangled.

And I want the later-stage payoff to be truly dominant, not just tempting. A student who refuses to pay shouldn't be making an obvious mistake given where she's standing at that moment — that's the entire point, and I don't want a game that quietly punishes her for it.

Deliverables: full rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, the exact wording on the role cards and the prediction slips, the payoff scheme, and a debrief that maps specific moments from the room onto specific parts of the structure. Then tell me straight whether you can actually make this one-shot or whether it inevitably turns into a reputation game — if iteration is doing the heavy lifting, I want to know how much of the lesson is riding on it and how to say that out loud to the class rather than pretend otherwise. Also give me the frank list of what the activity cannot reproduce (noisy prediction, nobody in the room is genuinely transparent, lying stays available the whole time, whatever else) that I'm going to have to supply by hand afterwards.

Finally: what predictably goes wrong the first time this is run? I want failure modes, not reassurance — the student who writes a deliberately useless forecast, the pair that colludes before you've finished reading the rules, the round-two problem where they've already seen the machinery, the kid who just refuses to play. And what to do about each one in the moment. If there's a natural way to gesture at Kavka's toxin in the last five minutes without derailing everything, mention it, but don't force it.
