I am working through Thomas Reid's *Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man* (1785), Essay VI, "Of Judgment," Chapter 5, "The first principles of contingent truths," in the stretch where he treats testimony, and I want to check that I have not flattened his argument. What follows is my own close paraphrase, not a transcription of his wording; please treat it as a report of the reasoning rather than as the text itself. If you believe the discussion of testimony belongs to a different chapter or to a different class of first principles than the one I have named, say so before answering.

My paraphrase: (1) human nature contains an original propensity to speak truth, so that veracity is natural and falsehood requires an effort against our constitution; (2) paired with this is an original disposition to believe what we are told, which is prior to any reasoning about the reliability of particular speakers; (3) in children this credulity is unlimited, whereas in adults it is bounded by experience of deceit; (4) neither disposition is the conclusion of an induction, and we cannot assign a reason for either, so both belong among the first principles of contingent truths; (5) testimony retains its force unless we have positive grounds for suspicion, in which case its weight is diminished accordingly.

Three questions.

First, is (3) in genuine tension with (4)? If experience of falsehood progressively narrows the disposition to believe, does Reid not effectively make testimonial warrant a matter of inductive evidence about speakers, collapsing toward the Humean account he means to reject? Or can the two be reconciled while preserving an anti-reductionist reading — and if so, what exactly does experience limit: the existence of the presumption, its strength, or only the availability of defeaters?

Second, how much work is the pairing in (1) doing? Reid seems to treat the speaker's implanted propensity to truth and the hearer's implanted disposition to credit as complementary halves of one social mechanism. Does his account require that the speaker be the sort of being endowed with (1) for the hearer's presumption in (2) to get a grip at all?

Third, this is why the scope of (2) matters to me: a large share of the testimony I now encounter is produced by systems that have no constitution and hence no implanted propensity toward either truth or falsehood, though they are engineered for fluency and agreeableness. On whichever reading you favour, does Reid's framework have any principled handle on testimony of that kind — does the absence of the veracity principle withdraw the default credit, or does the hearer-side presumption operate independently of the nature of the utterance's source?

Finally, indicate where in my paraphrase you think Reid's own text is most likely to resist the compression.
