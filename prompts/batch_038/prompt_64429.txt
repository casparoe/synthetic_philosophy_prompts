I write novels — literary family sagas, mostly set in small coastal towns, the sort where three generations are all in the same room at Christmas and nobody says the thing they mean. I'm about 70% though my fourth and I've hit a problem that isn't plot. It's belief.

Half a dozen of my characters hold things that behave nothing like opinions. My narrator's uncle Sandy is certain, and has been certain for nineteen years, that the family boatyard was stolen from his father by his own brother using a forged conveyance; he's been shown the register twice and hasn't budged an inch. My narrator's mother is certain her youngest son — who went over the side in '98 and was never recovered — is alive somewhere in Aberdeen. Meanwhile the teenage granddaughter changes her mind about practically everything within a single chapter, correctly, on the strength of one bit of evidence.

The book keeps failing in the same way. I write a scene where somebody marshals the evidence and the other person sees the light, and on the reread it's a lie — not morally, just structurally. The reader can smell that I moved a belief because the scene needed moving. And when I refuse to move it, I get accused, in my own head, of writing characters who are simply thick.

I've been reading around Wittgenstein's last remarks on certainty — the ones that came out of him being cross with Moore about holding up his hands as a proof — and the idea that's stuck with me is that some of what a person holds isn't a conclusion they could be argued out of at all, but part of the frame that lets them have conclusions; that doubt only gets going because a great deal else stands fast; that a child doesn't verify these things but swallows them along with the language and habits of the household; and that riverbed picture, where the channel enquiry runs in can itself shift, but slowly, and never because somebody won an argument.

So: turn that into something I can actually run at the desk. A procedure, in order, that I apply to any scene where one character tries to change another's mind, fails to, or where I'm tempted to let them succeed. It has to work for someone with no philosophy training and about twenty minutes and a cold cup of tea — my editor gets the full draft in seven weeks and I have to hand her something coherent, not a research project. Give me the questions in sequence, what counts as an honest answer to each, and mark the places where it spits out a verdict versus the places where it just hands the decision back to me because it's a judgement about this book rather than about epistemology.

Then run it on three of mine:

1. Uncle Sandy and the register. I currently have a scene in the last third where his granddaughter reads the entry aloud and something in him goes. Tell me whether that's earned.
2. The mother and the son in Aberdeen. A police officer lays out the dental record; she thanks him and puts the kettle on.
3. The granddaughter's quick, correct changes of mind — the opposite error, where I've made an ordinary belief behave like bedrock purely for plot reasons.

Be blunt, and make sure at least one of the three lands somewhere I'm going to hate. If the procedure says cut the scene, say cut the scene, and tell me what the honest alternative is — given that on this account real belief-change is slow, undramatic and mostly happens offstage. Which is a genuine problem for a novelist: the whole recognition-scene tradition, Sophocles onwards, is a machine for converting a person with an argument on the page. Does your procedure condemn that outright, or is there a legitimate species of on-the-page conversion? I want that answered, not dodged.

Also, don't quote anything at me. I can't check quotations and a wrong one would end up in the book, so paraphrase in your own words and keep the vocabulary to two or three terms I can remember under pressure — no reading list, no bibliography, no "go and look up the remark where he says such-and-such". And flag clearly which steps of your procedure are you imposing a system on material that is deliberately unsystematic, since these are remarks rather than a theory. Finally: tell me where the thing will misfire in my hands. My suspicion is that it hands a pompous writer a licence to call every stubborn character profound, and that it can't distinguish a hinge from self-deception or from somebody lying to themselves. Say whether that suspicion is right, and if it is, how I police it.
