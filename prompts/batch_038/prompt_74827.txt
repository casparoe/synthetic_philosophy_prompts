Six weeks into a new job and already in over my head abit. I'm the in-house scientific reviewer for a group of seven IVF clinics, and the reason the job exists is that we signed a contract with a vendor who does whole-genome sequencing on embryo biopsies and reports polygenic scores back to us alongside the single-gene results. Marketing ran with it before I was hired. Now everything patient-facing goes through me: website copy, ads, consent forms, the slide deck coordinators use at consults. Call it eight claims a month, plus people cornering me with one-offs.

My background is clinical embryology. I took one genetics course a long time ago. I know heritability isn't what people think it is, but I can't out-argue a vendor's sales engineer in a meeting and I don't have a biostatistician to lean on.

What I need is a procedure my team can actually run, not an essay. Reviewers are nurses and embryologists, we get maybe twenty minutes per claim, and the medical director — a reproductive endocrinologist, great guy, allergic to memos — signs off on whatever comes out of it, so the output has to be a verdict plus a sentence of justification he can read standing up.

The procedure should be an ordered list of questions: what to ask, what counts as a passing answer, what a fail looks like, where it just stops at no, and where it hands the decision up to the ethics committee because it's a values question and not a facts question. It should say what to do when we can't get a number — the vendor won't release validation details, we just get a glossy PDF — and it should be honest about which steps are judgment calls dressed up as procedure. I'd rather know that going in than find out in a deposition.

Please build in these as test cases, since they're literally what's on my desk:

1. An ad saying their type 2 diabetes score "explains 15% of the variance" and that selecting on it "reduces predicted risk by up to 30%." Also from their training webinar, the line that a heritability of 0.6 means the score accounts for 60% of the difference between two embryos. I think that second one is just wrong but I can't say exactly where it goes wrong.

2. Their sales deck, which ranks embryos on "educational attainment," while the website says in bold that they do not screen for intelligence or behavior. My gut says the deck is worse than the website is letting on, and I can't articulate why in a way that survives pushback.

3. Our own consent form, which I helped write: "this test tells you which embryo is likely to be healthiest." I suspect this one is unsalvageable, and if it is I want the procedure to say so plainly.

4. One where I expect the answer to annoy me: we run PGT-M for a known familial BRCA1 variant, and after a bad press cycle leadership wants to ban the phrase "genetic selection" from all our materials, including that program. If your procedure passes it and I have to go tell them the ban is overbroad, fine, that's what I need it for.

One more thing. The ethics committee has a standing rule that no claim may rest on a statistic that's only true of populations and not of the individual patient in the room. Half of what the vendor sends me is variance explained. Tell me straight whether that rule is coherent and enforceable or whether it needs rewriting, because right now I'm applying it inconsistently and people have noticed.

And give me a two-sentence version for when a patient asks at consult what the scores really mean, that a coordinator can say out loud without lying.

American English please.
