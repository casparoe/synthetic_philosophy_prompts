I retired four years ago from a national laboratory, where I spent thirty-seven years in optical spectroscopy. My entire working life consisted of establishing what a thing was made of by measuring the light that failed to come through it. Since retiring I have read philosophy slowly and out of order, the way an amateur does, and last winter I reached Rousseau's 1758 letter to d'Alembert on spectacles, written against the proposal that Geneva should acquire a theater. I expected a Puritan tract and instead found two arguments that have lodged in me and that I can no longer argue with comfortably. I have not become a Rousseau partisan. But I no longer believe a sentence I have said at perhaps a dozen charitable dinners, namely that the arts make a community better.

The difficulty is this. I must give a speech in which that sentence would ordinarily appear, and I am no longer willing to say it.

Our town's 1908 opera house has been restored after eleven years of campaigning, and reopens in three weeks. The capital gift was made in my wife Eleanor's name, following her death in the second year of the restoration; the marquee carries it, which means the room will be looking at her name while it looks at me. I have been asked to give the opening remarks, eight to ten minutes, before a twenty-minute scene performed by the high school company, before the artistic director speaks, and before the ribbon is cut.

Eleanor acted for thirty-one years in the town's amateur company. She was given small parts and played them badly by any professional standard, and she was happy every single night of it. She also ran the costume cupboard, sold more raffle tickets than anyone, and drove the set flats. I want to be clear that the speech is not really about Rousseau. It is about her, and about a building, and about whether I can say an honest thing in a room where an honest thing could easily land as an insult.

The room will hold about two hundred and sixty people: the amateur company and their families, the restoration committee, the town council, roughly forty donors, my children and grandchildren, three professional actors from the regional theater forty miles away, and a professor of theater at the state university who wrote the grant application that paid for the roof. That professor and those actors will know exactly what I am doing if I do it, and I want them to feel honored rather than ambushed. Everyone else should simply hear a warm, occasionally funny talk about a town and a building.

I would like you to write the speech, with the following constraints, which I ask you to treat as load-bearing rather than decorative.

First, it must not claim that the theater will make the residents of this town kinder, more empathetic, or more united. That is the claim I have stopped believing, for the reason Rousseau gave: an audience's tears discharge an impulse rather than train it, and the pity we spend in the dark on people who are not there may be exactly the pity we then do not spend on people who are. The speech must not assert the opposite either. It must not tell two hundred and sixty people that they have wasted eleven years and that my money was misdirected. It has to find the third thing, and the third thing has to be true.

Second, Rousseau should be present in substance and nearly absent in name. Say his name at most once, and only if the sentence works for a person who has never heard of him. No survey of his life, no mention of Geneva's city council, no seminar.

Third, there must be one moment, plainly spoken in ordinary words, in which I say what I have come to doubt. Not a hedge about how some thinkers have worried; an admission. It must be brief enough that the room does not feel lectured, and it must not imply that anyone present is a fool for having worked eleven years on a building.

Fourth, I want Eleanor's thirty-one years of small bad parts to do the philosophical work, not merely the sentimental work. As I understand Rousseau, his objection bears on a class of professionals who sell their own appearance and live on the opinion of a paying audience, and his alternative to the theater is the open-air festival in which nobody is merely watching and everybody becomes a spectacle to everybody else. Eleanor was never in the first category and was, in the second, a veteran. The speech should get from Rousseau's objections to her without straining, and it should be able to say why the building we are opening tonight is nonetheless a standing invitation to do what she did rather than only to sit and receive. I do not want the conclusion to pretend that a proscenium house is a festival in a public square. I want it to hand the room the distinction and then leave the challenge with them, kindly.

Fifth, one sentence, no more, acknowledging that I did not give the money purely for the town's sake, and that wanting my wife's name lit up above the street is not so far from the appetite Rousseau thought the stage was built to feed. This should read as self-possession, not as self-flagellation, and it should not turn into a confession routine.

Sixth, you may use at most one analogy from my own working life, and it must carry argument rather than ornament. For what it is worth, the material is there: a spectrometer never shows you the light, only the deficit where the light was absorbed; everything I ever knew about a sample I knew from what did not arrive. If you use this, use it once, and use it on the question of what an audience is and is not doing when it weeps. If you cannot make it do that work, leave it out and use no scientific imagery at all.

Finally: it is to be read aloud by a seventy-three-year-old who is not a performer and who will be nervous. Plain sentences, nothing that requires a subordinate clause to be held in the air for eight words, two or three places where the room can laugh without laughing at anyone absent, and a last line that a person in the fourth row will still be able to repeat on the drive home. Ten hundred to thirteen hundred words.

After the speech, add four or five sentences addressed to me rather than to the room, setting out which of Rousseau's commitments the speech concedes, which it refuses, and where you have had to shade or simplify in order to keep a mixed audience with us. I want to be able to check that you have not quietly made me say something I believe to be false.
