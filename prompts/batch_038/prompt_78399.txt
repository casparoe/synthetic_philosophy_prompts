For the closing session of a two-day workshop on crustacean welfare in aquaculture — an audience that will include hatchery technicians, welfare scientists, and at least one regulator — please write a sestina.

The subject is one specific practice: unilateral eyestalk ablation of female Pacific whiteleg shrimp broodstock. One eyestalk is pinched off, enucleated, or cauterized, normally without anaesthetic, because the neurosecretory complex inside the eyestalk secretes a gonad-inhibiting hormone that keeps the ovary from maturing in captivity; remove that brake and the female will spawn within days, at the cost of higher mortality and worse offspring. The poem should not lecture about this, but it should be built on it, and it should be recognizable to anyone who has stood in a broodstock room.

The six end-words, in the order in which they first appear, are: **eye, shelter, reflex, trade, wound, stun**. Rotate them in the traditional sestina pattern (1-2-3-4-5-6 / 6-1-5-2-4-3 / 3-6-4-1-2-5 / 5-3-2-6-1-4 / 4-5-1-3-6-2 / 2-4-6-5-3-1), then gather all six into a three-line envoi: thirty-nine lines, unrhymed, in lines of roughly comparable length.

Each end-word must at some point carry its technical weight rather than only its colloquial one:

- **reflex** — the skeptic's reduction: withdrawal, tail-flip, and the grooming of an injured site can all be produced by circuits that need not feel anything, and nociception is not the same thing as pain;
- **trade** — the criterion meant to defeat that reduction: an animal that surrenders something it wants, a good shelter or a meal, in order to avoid a noxious stimulus is exhibiting a motivation rather than a fixed response;
- **shelter** — both the place a crab or prawn will subsequently work to avoid if it was shocked there, and the thing a half-blind ablated female loses her grip on;
- **wound** — the ablation site, and the rubbing, flicking, and hiding that a local anaesthetic given beforehand measurably reduces;
- **eye** — the organ removed as a means to an endocrine end, and also, crucially, the counterexample: a decapod sees perfectly well without anything resembling a visual cortex, which is exactly why the absence of a cortex-like structure cannot by itself settle the question;
- **stun** — what would be done instead, and the unresolved question of whether the available alternatives, from ice slurry to electrical stunning to splitting, are genuinely better.

Let the argument from analogy appear — the inference from similar responses to similar experience — and let it be pressed by the neuroanatomical objection, then let the objection meet the eye.

The form should be doing the philosophical work. The rotation is the debate: six terms returned to in a fixed order, each pass through them slightly re-lit, and no pass ever terminating. The envoi is where that becomes unbearable, because a hatchery must decide this season whether to keep ablating and a regulator must decide whether to mandate stunning, and both decisions have to be made out of exactly the same six words with exactly the same evidence and not one item more. Do not resolve the science. Do make vivid that the decision cannot wait for it to be resolved, and that this asymmetry is a real argument rather than an evasion — while also letting the reader feel what it costs to act on evidence one concedes is inconclusive.

Avoid sermon, avoid a sentimental first-person shrimp, avoid industry apologia; the technicians in the room are not villains and should not be written as such. Invent no study titles, author names, dates, or quotations — nothing in the poem should require looking anything up.
