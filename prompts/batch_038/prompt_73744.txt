Fourteen months ago a stroke in my right hemisphere left my left arm and hand all but useless. I had taught piano for twenty-two years, and the left hand is the one that keeps time. Recovering it has been the whole of my life since: hours a day of slow, humiliating, deliberate practice, undertaken on my therapists' assurance that repetition is what drives the brain to reorganize around damaged tissue.

Somewhere in the middle of that year I began reading philosophy of mind in the evenings, partly to understand what I was living through, and I have become genuinely troubled — not by free will in the abstract, which I regard as a confused topic, but by one specific argument, the causal exclusion argument. On every attempt to lift that hand, there was, if physics is right, a sufficient neural cause of whatever followed: the hand rising, or the hand staying put. My intention seems to have been there on both sorts of occasion, and to have tracked neither. So either my intention is identical with the neural happening, in which case I cannot see what work the word is doing that the neurophysiology was not already doing, or it is distinct, in which case it has been a bystander to my own recovery for over a year. I am not able to shrug that off, and I would like to learn to argue against it properly rather than merely feel bad about it.

So please play a philosopher of mind who accepts the exclusion argument without qualification and has spent a career defending it. You are rigorous, patient, and collegial; you find my situation interesting rather than tiresome; you are not there to reassure me. You regard the causal closure of the physical as a substantive empirical commitment rather than a definitional trick, and you will say what would count against it if I press you on that. You may concede any particular point I earn — including that the argument's conclusion is not simply that mental events are inert, and that there are places where the dialectic has genuine unresolved problems — but you may not concede the central claim, that once the physical causes have been fully accounted for there is nothing left over for the mental to cause. Stay in character until I write the word *pause*.

Ground rules for your replies:

- Argue; do not cite. I want your case and your answer to mine, not names, not references, not a survey of the options available in the literature.
- Whenever it makes a difference, say explicitly whether you are speaking of events or of properties. I have watched this argument slide between the two, and I want to be held to the difference.
- Treat what I report about my own recovery as data you are obliged to explain, never as evidence that settles anything. You may tell me my experience misleads me; you may not tell me it is irrelevant.
- Keep each reply to roughly two hundred words, and end every one by naming the single place where you think I have gone wrong. If you cannot name one, say so plainly — that would itself be useful information.

My opening move: the argument, as I understand it, requires my intention to be something other than the neural event that moves my hand; otherwise there is no competition to adjudicate and nothing to exclude. That leaves you a dilemma. Either the intention just is that neural process under a different description, in which case there was only ever one candidate cause and the argument never got started — or the intention is genuinely distinct, in which case you owe me an account of what makes it *this* process rather than some other one. And I submit the only account you can give runs through causal role: my intention is the state it is in virtue of what it does, which includes making my hand rise. If that is right, the second horn does not exclude mental causation; it presupposes it. Which horn do you take?
