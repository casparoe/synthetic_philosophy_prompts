I'm giving a department colloquium talk in about three weeks and I want to walk in with the question period already mapped out. American English for everything, please, and don't send me a bibliography or a reading list — I'm not going to chase down sources and I don't want the answer padded with them. If you have to name something, name it and move on.

Here's the thesis, roughly. I'm arguing that hinge propositions in On Certainty are not a special class of things we're certain of, and definitely not things we know. They're the fixed points a checking practice needs in order to run at all — closer in function to a standard of measurement than to an empirical claim you could get more evidence about. You don't verify the fixed point, you use it, and if you tried to put it in question you'd lose the thing you were checking against. From that I want to draw two consequences people usually think hinge epistemology can't deliver. First, skepticism gets the wrong diagnosis: the skeptic isn't showing we lack knowledge of the external world, she's treating hinges as candidates for knowledge when they were never in that business, so the right response isn't "but we do know them" — it's that the demand is malformed. Second, and this is the part I expect to get hit hardest, hinges don't land us in relativism. Real hinge-level disagreement between communities is, I claim, very rare; what look like clashes of world-pictures are almost always disagreements about how to apply hinges both sides share, or about which local hinge gets recruited to govern a given practice. The riverbed shifts, but not in a way that lets two communities be equally entitled and incommensurable.

The room: about twenty people, mostly mainstream analytic epistemologists, several of them internalist-leaning and a couple who think the whole "entitlement" vocabulary is just a way of dodging the question of justification. One historian of mid-century analytic philosophy who thinks On Certainty is a pile of notes rather than a theory and resents it being systematized into a research program. Two grad students working on relativism and adjacent stuff who genuinely want hinge relativism to be a live position and will say my anti-relativism move begs the question. One philosopher of religion who's sympathetic to hinge treatments of faith and will want to know whether my account gives her what she wants or rules her case out by stipulation. And somebody from the social epistemology end who works on misinformation and conspiracy belief, who will absolutely ask me what on my view separates a hinge from a belief that's merely insulated from correction.

What I want from you is a prep sheet, not a defense of my view. Give me the eight or so objections this specific room will actually raise — not the generic anti-Wittgenstein complaints I'd get from a general audience — ordered by how much damage each would do if I fumbled it. Phrase each one in the vocabulary and tone of the person most likely to raise it, so I can hear it coming. For each: a reply I could actually say out loud in about two minutes, and then a blunt note on where that reply is soft, what assumption it's leaning on, and whether the softness is survivable. Tell me separately what I should concede in the first five minutes to take the wind out of the historian and the internalists, and which of the objections are coming from people working off summaries rather than the text — for those I want to know whether correcting them is worth the time or whether I should let it ride.

Last thing: include the question I'm hoping nobody asks. The one that would force me to either admit my account can't distinguish a hinge from a self-sealing prejudice, or retreat to something so weak the anti-relativism payoff disappears. Tell me straight which of those two is less bad and what I say.
