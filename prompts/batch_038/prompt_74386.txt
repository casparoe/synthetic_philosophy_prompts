I've been asked to circulate a one-page statement of where I stand on the boundary problem ahead of a workshop where everyone's views get picked apart in advance, so I want a consistency check before I send it off. Here's the package:

1. The all-subjected principle is the right criterion for the franchise: anyone subject to a body's coercive law has a claim to a say in it.
2. The demos can't be constituted by a prior demos. The boundary has to get closed somewhere without further democratic authorization, and I accept that this closing is itself not democratically legitimated.
3. All-affected is still true at the level of justification, though: institutions should be designed so that everyone significantly affected has some channel of influence, even where that channel isn't the vote.
4. Children and adults under guardianship are subjects of the law but shouldn't vote; some trustee or proxy arrangement is the answer for them.
5. Affectedness is scalar, and I've been pushing a threshhold view to block the everything-affects-everything objection: you count as affected by a decision when that decision changes what you can reasonably expect from your own life.
6. Future people are affected in that sense, but we owe them no institutional voice, since there's no one there to hand it to.

Go through this as an audit, not a lecture. Which pairs genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Where's the cheapest repair, and what does it cost me?

Please use these sections in this order: "What holds up" (a few lines); "Real tensions" (the bulk — ranked worst first, each naming the two commitments, why their usual motivations clash rather than just their conclusions, and what has to give); "Only apparent" (short, and say what you'd tell someone who raised it); "Cheapest repair overall" (a paragraph); "Bottom line" (two sentences on whether the package is worth defending as it stands). I genuinely don't know where the crack is, and I'm not going to tell you which of these I'd sacrifice — make me choose.
