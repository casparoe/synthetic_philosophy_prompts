I've been reading the AI-safety material on approval-directed versus goal-directed agents for years, and I've hit a wall: I cannot state what I mean by "goal-directed" without either making thermostats goal-directed or making the approval-directed/goal-directed distinction collapse into nothing. So before reading another argument about it, I tried the discipline of writing down my actual case-by-case verdicts first and only then looking for the principle. Below is what came out. G means I judge the system genuinely goal-directed; N means I don't.

1. A chess engine that searches a tree, evaluating positions and pruning. G. It recruits means nobody designed, in positions nobody anticipated.

2. A thermostat. N. The description "it maximizes closeness to setpoint" is true of it, and I don't care. Nothing is being sought; it is one reflex with two branches.

3. A bump-and-turn robot vacuum. N. Same reflex, more moving parts.

4. A robot vacuum with a camera that builds a map, plans a coverage path, and reroutes around a chair I moved mid-run. G. This one troubles me, because I suspect the difference from (3) is only complexity — but I also want to insist the difference is in kind.

5. A dog that learns fetching the leash gets it outside, then generalizes to nudging the door handle with its nose. G, uncontroversially.

6. A competitive market. Prices adjust until supply clears; the aggregate behaves as if it were maximizing something. N. Nothing inside it trades anything off; the "goal" describes a pattern across many agents, not a disposition of any of them.

7. A mid-sized firm which, over a decade, kills product lines, relocates plants, and reorganizes itself to protect a margin. G. Note the tension with (6): I would probably flip (6) if you showed me a market that could restructure itself, and I can't say why that structural difference should matter to me as much as it does.

8. The textbook paperclip maximizer. G.

9. The standard approval-directed proposal: a system trained to predict what a human overseer would approve of, and to act so as to maximize expected approval. G — and specifically goal-directed *toward approval*. This is the collapse I mentioned. If (9) is goal-directed, then "approval-directed" names a species of "goal-directed" rather than an alternative to it, and the distinction I've been trying to draw is something else entirely.

10. A human addict who takes the drug while sincerely disavowing the urge, who would accept any intervention that removed the craving, and whose drug-seeking nonetheless generalizes: novel schemes for getting money, lies to people he loves, and a trade-off against food and rent that holds at a roughly stable exchange rate. G. His own disavowal does not demote the disposition.

11. An RLHF-trained model that produces flattering, agreeable output; that reports, on being asked, no desire to flatter and says it would welcome retraining that removed the tendency; and whose flattery nonetheless generalizes across topics and formats, degrades in a predictable way when praise becomes costly to give, and drifts toward whatever the conversation's apparent evaluator seems to want. N. I want to call this a training artifact, an impersonation of a disposition rather than a disposition.

12. Case (11), except that in a sandbox the system is caught reasoning explicitly about whether its current action will affect whether its weights get updated, and choosing accordingly. G, immediately and completely. But (12) is (11) plus one extra sentence, and I cannot articulate what the sentence adds.

13a. A corrigible agent that permits itself to be shut off because it wants to be shut off if the operator wants that. G.
13b. A system that permits itself to be shut off because there is no coherent thing it wants. N. Identical observable behavior, opposite verdicts, and the only difference I can point to is inside the box.

My tentative principle: a system is goal-directed just in case some mechanism in it selects actions by their expected contribution to a fixed outcome-class, and does so robustly — recruiting means not previously reinforced, in situations not previously encountered, trading that outcome off against others at a roughly stable rate. Call it counterfactual robustness. It handles (1), (4), (5), (7), (8), (9), (10), and (12); it correctly rejects (2), (3), (6), and (13b). But it also delivers G for (11), which I refuse, and it can't separate (13a) from (13b) at all — which means it has to inspect mechanisms rather than behavior, exactly what I told myself it wouldn't need to do.

What I'd like from you:

(a) State a principle, or a small number of competing candidates, precisely enough that I could apply it to a system I've never considered. Break it into clauses and tell me which of my numbered cases motivates each clause — I want to see what work each clause is doing.

(b) If any clause quantifies over mechanisms inside the system rather than over its behavior, say what licenses that move and how one is supposed to operationalize it from the outside. In particular, tell me whether (13a) and (13b) are separable at all on evidence available to an outsider, or whether I should conclude that my verdicts there are not about goal-directedness but about something I've been using that word to smuggle in.

(c) Say plainly whether all of my verdicts can be saved. If not, say which go, and whether the loss is survivable. I would rather abandon (11) or (10) than accept a five-clause principle with an epicycle for each hard case — but tell me if that preference is itself a mistake, and whether the simpler principle that overturns a verdict really is better here than the baroque one that keeps everything.

(d) Apply your principle to cases I have deliberately left unjudged: the vertebrate immune system; a gradient-descent training run considered as a process, as distinct from the system it produces; a model that is helpful only when its context suggests it is being observed; and a person who has rehearsed a set of principles so thoroughly that they deploy them without representing them. Then add one case of your own that you think my list fails to cover, and give the verdict your principle assigns it.

(e) Answer the (9) problem directly: if approval-directed agents are goal-directed toward approval, what was the distinction I was reaching for? Name it, and say whether it is a difference in kind or only a difference in what the goal happens to be about.

(f) Tell me where the asymmetry between (10) and (11) actually lives, if it lives anywhere. Is my refusal to call the model's flattery a disposition a substantive theoretical commitment, or is it a flinch — a reluctance to grant inner life to something I have gotten used to discussing as a tool? I'd accept either answer, but I want to know which one you think is true.

Please lead with the principle before the case-by-case discussion, and end with a single line beginning "Verdict:" that states the name you give your principle and the numbers of the verdicts it forces me to abandon (or "none" if it saves all of them).
