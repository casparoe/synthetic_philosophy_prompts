so I have this whole package of views about religion — religion as a natural thing you can study, not as a is-it-true question — and I want it stress tested before I write it up for the philosophy club at my school, because part of me thinks it all fits and another part thinks I'm cheating somewhere and I can't tell which part.

here's the package:

1. religion isn't an adaptation. it's a byproduct. we have an over-eager agency detector, we have theory of mind, we run both way past their proper domain, and gods and spirits fall out for free. no selection needed for the belief itself.

2. but once the stuff existed it got shaped. groups with expensive rituals, watchful moralizing gods, shared feast days and marriage rules out-survived and out-reproduced groups without them. so the *forms* religion takes are adaptive even though the underlying impulse isn't. I'm confident 1 and 2 fit together fine, btw, one is about origin and one is about what happened after — tell me if that's too easy.

3. none of this is a disproof. where a belief comes from tells you nothing about whether it's true, that's the genetic fallacy and I'm not committing it.

4. and yet — reading all this genuinely moved me. I grew up going to church and my confidence dropped a lot, and I think that drop was rational rather than a fallacy. my reason: call it the wiring test. if you'd hold a belief anyway just because of how your head is built, you need a reason to keep it that isn't the wiring. for religion I dont have one.

5. but I'm a moral realist, and the exact same story can be told about my moral intuitions — kin stuff, reciprocity, disgust, reputation — and I don't think that costs me anything. morality passes the wiring test.

6. also I expect religion to keep shrinking. science and states take over its jobs one by one — explanation, welfare, community, calendar — and eventually it's a minority hobby. astrology is the model: nobody needed a law against it, it just got embarrassing.

what I want: go through these and tell me which ones actually pull against each other and which ones only look like they do. rank the problems worst to least bad, don't just list them all as equally worrying, and for each one say what would have to go, what it costs me to give it up, and whether there's a rewording that keeps most of the commitment. then tell me the single change that cleans up the most.

bottom line in your first sentence — does the set hang together or not — then the reasoning. no preamble and no repeating my list back to me.

and if you think the framing is off, say so plainly instead of humoring me: like if the wiring test isn't the kind of principle that can be applied the way I'm applying it, or if auditing a personal belief package for consistency is a dumb thing to spend an answer on, just tell me that and explain why.
