I'm in a statutory interpretation seminar and for our last session we all had to post an "interpretive profile" so the rest of the group could take swings at it. Mine is below, and two people have already told me it's basically incoherent, which I dont buy but I also can't say exactly why. What I want is an audit, not a lecture on the history of the debate: go through the list, tell me which commitments actually pull against each other, how badly, and what the cheapest fix would be in each case, then rank them by severity. Please be willing to say that some of them are fine — I'd much rather be told "three of these only look bad and two are real trouble" than have the whole thing graded as a mess.

The profile:

1. What a court enforces is the semantic content the enacted text had when it became law — the content a competent speaker of the language, aware of the relevant legal and linguistic context, would have taken those words to express. Not what the enacting legislators expected, hoped for, or would have said if you'd asked them about your case.
2. Legislative history is inadmissible. Committee reports, floor statements, managers' colloquies, all of it. Two reasons: none of it went through bicameralism and presentment, and it's generated strategically — by staffers on the way in and by litigators on the way out — so it's both unauthorized and unreliable.
3. Same for congressional inaction. If Congress doesn't amend a statute after a court has construed it, that tells you nothing about what it means. Silence under conditions of gridlock is noise, not a signal.
4. Absurdity is a real limit, though. Where applying the text produces something no sane legislature could have meant, a judge shouldn't apply it.
5. Ditto scrivener's errors — if there's an obvious typo in the enacted text (a wrong cross refrence, a dropped "not," a duplicated clause), the court should correct it rather than enforce it.
6. "Purpose" is never an independent source of obligation. A preamble or a findings section is evidence bearing on what the operative words express, and nothing more.
7. A general statute from the 1930s regulating "any device" reaches a smartphone. Fixing the meaning at enactment doesn't fix the set of things the meaning picks out.
8. Stare decisis can be a good reason to leave a past misreading of a statute standing even once you're personally persuaded it's wrong — partly reliance interests, partly institutional peace, and partly that Congress can always step in and correct the error if it cares enough about it.
9. In criminal cases, ambiguity that survives all the ordinary tools gets resolved for the defendant.
10. Where a statute hands an agency rulemaking power and the operative text is ambiguous, the agency's reasonable reading should usually win. (I mean this as my view about how things ought to work, not a claim about what the doctrine currently says — I know that area has been getting rewritten.)

So: which of these is a problem for which? My suspicion is that some are fine and only look bad on contact, and that one or two of the ones that sound most high-minded to me are quietly leaning on something I haven't paid for — if that's true I want to know precisely where, and what the commitment is secretly relying on to do its work. Item 1 in particular feels like it might be smuggling, since "aware of the relevant legal context" is doing a lot of lifting there and I'm not sure I could cash it out. Also, for anything you say has to go, tell me what I'd lose by dropping it — which doctrines or results I'd no longer be able to justify — and then tell me which single revision would clean up the most damage across the whole list. Don't assume I'd rather protect the textualist-sounding items. I genuinely don't know which one I'd sacrifice, that's what I'm asking.
