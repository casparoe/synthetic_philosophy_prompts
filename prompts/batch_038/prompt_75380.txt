The question I have to teach this summer is whether the brain is a computer, and the difficulty is not that I cannot answer it but that I cannot see how to make the question mean anything to the people I am supposed to be answering it for. I am in the final year of a joint Philosophy and Cognitive Science degree, and I have volunteered for a forty-five-minute session in our department's widening-participation summer school. The room will hold about twenty-five seventeen- and eighteen-year-olds who have never studied philosophy; perhaps a third have written some Python at school, and I expect those to be the ones who talk. I have a whiteboard, a pack of index cards, no projector, and no prior relationship with any of them. Last year's version of this session, which I sat in on, failed in two instructive ways: it was captured within eight minutes by a debate about a man in a room shuffling Chinese symbols, and once that collapsed it slid into speculation about whether an AI will one day be conscious. I want to avoid both, but I do not yet know what to replace them with.

What I want the session to pay off is a distinction I only properly grasped in my philosophy of mind module this term. On one reading of the question — roughly, does the brain physically realise processes that can be described as computations — the answer looks uncomfortably close to trivial, and I have read both the argument that a sufficiently permissive account of implementation makes every open physical system implement every finite-state automaton and the reply that implementation has to respect causal organisation rather than mere pattern-matching. On the other reading — is the mind literally a digital computer running a program, in the way my laptop is — it is a substantive, empirical, and genuinely contestable hypothesis, and a great many intelligent people have denied it. My draft opening asks the room whether a thermostat computes, which I think gets at the trivialisation worry, but my flatmate's objection is that it backfires: if the answer is yes, everything in the room is a computer and the question evaporates; if the answer is no, I have simply taught them to define computation narrowly enough to protect their intuitions. I cannot tell whether that opening is a flaw or the whole point, and that is one of the things I need you to settle.

So please plan the session rather than explain computationalism to me. Specifically, I want: (a) the dependency structure — which pieces must be in place before which and why the order cannot be shuffled, working from the single thing I can safely assume the room shares, namely that a program takes inputs, does something to them, and produces outputs; (b) the one example I should build the whole thing around, with an account of what that example has to have and what it must conspicuously lack. My current candidate is the gradual replacement scenario — a person whose neurons are swapped for functionally identical artificial substitutes one at a time — which I intended to run as a live show of hands, re-voting at ten milestones up to a fully replaced brain and plotting the results on the board. Tell me whether that carries the idea or derails it, and if it derails it, tell me what to use instead and what the thermostat opening should become; (c) the misconceptions they will bring with them or form in the room, in the order I expect to meet them — I am counting on "computer means silicon and digital", "a computer must have been programmed by somebody", "neurons are just very small transistors", and "if the brain is a computer then it can be uploaded" — together with the precise point in the sequence at which each must be headed off and the one or two sentences that do the heading off; (d) a single question I can ask in the final three minutes that would separate the students who have actually grasped the two senses of the question from the students who have merely been agreeing with me for forty minutes; and (e) an explicit list of what I should refuse to explain at this level and how I should say so out loud without looking evasive, since I suspect both consciousness and the Turing test belong on that list. Do not send me or my students to any readings, and do not pad the plan with warm-up games. If you think forty-five minutes with this audience cannot honestly reach the two-sense payoff, say so plainly and give me the version of the session that can be run instead.
