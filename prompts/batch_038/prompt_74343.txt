For an independent study I am doing this year, I had to submit a statement of my own position on Ryle's distinction between knowing how and knowing that before beginning the essay, and my supervisor suggested I have it stress-tested first. It runs as follows.

1. Ryle is right that knowing how to do something is not a matter of knowing that certain propositions are true: if intelligent action required contemplating a proposition beforehand, we would need a further rule for applying that rule, and so on without end. I do accept, though, that most of what we know that is dispositional — retained and available to us rather than constantly before the mind.
2. Know-how is not mere capacity for success either. A novice who happens to turn out one perfect soufflé has not thereby learned to make one: her success did not issue from any grasp of what she was doing.
3. Know-how survives the loss of the ability to perform. A pianist whose hands are paralyzed in an accident still knows how to play Chopin.
4. The purest cases of know-how are ones the agent cannot describe — the poultry worker who reliably sorts day-old chicks without being able to say what she is looking at, and the skier in full flow who reports that she is not steering at all, only letting the run happen.
5. A chess program that outplays every grandmaster does not know how to play chess.
6. A coach's advice can genuinely improve my technique even though it reaches me as nothing but information.

I would like an audit rather than a survey. Which of these pull against which, and how damagingly? Please rank the conflicts by severity, say for each what would have to be given up and what that would cost the rest of the view, and then identify the single revision that would repair the most. Some of them may only look inconsistent, so please separate those from the genuine problems rather than flagging everything.
