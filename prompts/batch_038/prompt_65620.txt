I spent forty years in experimental physics and have only recently begun reading philosophy seriously; I now convene a small group of retired colleagues, all of us fluent in probability but none trained in the discipline. Our current topic is van Fraassen's Reflection principle — the idea that if I now expect my future self to hold some credence, I ought to hold at least that credence already.

Please act as a formal epistemologist working on diachronic rationality, and think the problem through before drafting. Begin with a short brainstorming section laying out the axes along which positions on Reflection genuinely differ: whether the expected change is caused by new evidence or by something that degrades judgment; whether the future self will retain what I now know; whether the principle governs belief or commitment; whether it is primitive or derived from conditionalization.

Then build a sorting quiz of ten to twelve items, each a concrete scenario in which the respondent simply states what he would now believe or wager, with three or four options. The cases must include a future self holding strictly more of the same kind of data; a future self made optimistic by a drug or by exhaustion at the end of a night shift; a future self who will have forgotten evidence I presently hold; a future self I expect to be converted by an argument I now regard as fallacious; and an Ulysses-style case in which my preferences themselves will change. No item may use the vocabulary of the debate — no "rational," "coherent," "Dutch book," "defer," or "should you update?" — since the point is to sort people by what they say about cases, not by labels they have picked up.

Close with a scoring key mapping answer patterns onto positions, which must include at least: Reflection as an unconditional requirement; Reflection as derivative of conditionalization plus diachronic coherence, so that doubting either undoes it; an information-monotone restriction; a source-based restriction keyed to how the change comes about; Reflection as a norm of planning rather than of belief; flat rejection; and a deflationary option on which it constrains only betting dispositions. Mark any combination of axes that leaves no position occupied, and say whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag any two positions the instrument cannot actually separate, and name the further item that would separate them. Where you are unsure that a position has an established name or a standard defender, describe it rather than attribute it.
