I am in the second half of a philosophy of AI seminar, and our unit this month is the symbol grounding problem: Harnad's original formulation, the Chinese Room, and the more recent argument that a system trained only on linguistic form has no way to arrive at meaning (the octopus-style thought experiment). For the last session each of us may submit a creative response in place of the usual paper, and I would like to write a piece of satire that the other students will recognize as having done the reading rather than as having skimmed an encyclopedia entry.

Please write a set of enterprise sales collateral — a product FAQ with a tier sheet, roughly 850 words — for an invented company, Veridical Systems, whose flagship offering is Grounding-as-a-Service: for an annual subscription, a customer's text-only foundation model is leased a robot body so that, as the copy tirelessly insists, its symbols finally mean something to the model itself rather than to its engineers. The voice is upbeat, jargon-fluent B2B marketing, deadpan from the logo line to the legal footer, and it never breaks character to explain itself or to wink.

The collateral must contain:

- Three subscription tiers — a haptic-only tier; a full sensorimotor loop with a world-model guarantee; and an enterprise tier adding an auditable causal ledger of every interaction the model has ever had with a physical object — each described in plausible spec-sheet bullet points.
- A comparison table against a rival, Scale Alone, whose pitch is that sufficient text at sufficient scale yields grounding for free, and which Veridical treats with thinly veiled contempt. Both positions should be exaggerated versions of claims people actually make, not strawmen.
- A compliance section on the Certified Grounded seal, awarded after an audit in which a human engineer signs an attestation stating what each of the system's symbols means. The reader should notice what this does to the vendor's central claim; the vendor should not.
- A troubleshooting entry reproducing a support ticket from a deployed customer whose robot now recoils from a hot plate but, when asked what hot means, returns the same paraphrase it produced before embodiment. Support classifies this as expected behavior, remediable by an add-on module.
- One short customer testimonial that is intended to be flattering and is not.
- An upsell for phenomenal content, listed as private beta with no timeline for general availability.

Two constraints matter more to me than the jokes. First, the copy should slide at least once between two worries that are routinely run together: whether a form-only system could ever acquire meaning in the first place, and whether a system's states are about anything for the system rather than for its designers. A specialist should be able to point to the sentence where the slide happens; a lay reader should simply find the paragraph funny. Second, invent every name, company, customer, certification, and standard — no real people or vendors, no citations, references, or bibliography, and no quotations from actual papers or talks.

The satire should also be fair: nothing in the document may be something a working embodied-cognition researcher would call flatly false, only something they would call selling too hard.
