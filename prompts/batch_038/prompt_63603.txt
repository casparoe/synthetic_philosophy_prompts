Six weeks ago I got talked into chairing the accreditation panel for a small UK charity that awards a quality mark to youth mentoring providers — about thirty of them, reassessed every two years. Six of us on the panel, all practitioners, no philosophers, two hours a month, and I have to minute and sign off every decision. We've already had one appeal and I can see another coming.

The row we keep having is a version of the old Euthyphro problem, though nobody in the room would put it that way: is a practice good because we give it the kitemark, or do we give it the kitemark because it's good? Providers manage to say both things at once in an appeal — that our criterion isn't actually justified, and that we're perfectly entitled to set our own standards.

Acting as a metaethicist who's thought hard about divine command theory and the Euthyphro, including the modified versions that try to escape the arbitrariness horn, build me a procedure the panel can run on a draft criterion in about fifteen minutes before we vote: the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, where it stops with "this can't be adopted as worded", and where it hands the call back to the panel as a question of our remit rather than of fact. It has to cope with: whether member consensus can be cited as support; whether we can just lean on a statutory requirement or a regulator's list instead of arguing; what happens to awards already made when we revise a criterion; and what an appellant can and can't claim we got wrong. Flag which steps are judgment dressed up as procedure.

Brainstorm the possible shapes of it first, then commit to one. Then run it on three live criteria: enhanced DBS checks for every mentor; a minimum twelve-month relationship; and that at least 40% of a provider's trustees have lived experience of the service. I'm attached to that last one, so tell me straight if the procedure says it can't stand as worded. Finish with what the procedure can't do.
