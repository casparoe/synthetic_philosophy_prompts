ok so I'm about three chapters into a history PhD and our methods seminar did the thing where everyone has to write down their actual explanatory commitments on one page so the rest of the room can attack them. Mine turned out to look weirder on the page than it did in my head and I'd like a check before I present it, because I'm pretty sure they don't all fit and I can't work out which one is the load-bearing problem.

Here's the list. (1) I'm a Braudelian — I think history runs at several speeds and that the fastest one, the event, the individual, the decree, the harvest riot on a particular Tuesday, is foam: real enough to look at, but explanatorily almost inert. (2) My chapter argues that a run of cold, wet summers in the 1690s did genuine causal work in a cluster of grain riots and one tax revolt in a single valley region, and I want to say the harvest failures were necessary conditions for what happened, not just ambient background. (3) I'm firmly anti-determinist: I think environmental determinism is not just wrong but a bit politically rotten, so my line is always constraint yes, determination no. (4) Methodologically I only trust serial evidence — price series, parish registers, tithe accounts, notarial acts — because that's the only way to see anything at the scale of a century; documents about one person's one decision are anecdotes. (5) Building on that, I hold that the archives of the slow layers are structurally silent about individual decision-making, so we simply can't know what any given intendant or magistrate did. (6) And yet I do think (2) is a real causal claim about a single case — I'm not benchmarking my valley against twenty comparable valleys, I'm arguing that here, in that decade, the weather made the difference.

So: which of these actually pull against each other, which ones only look like they do, and where's the cheapest place to cut? I want an audit, not a lecture — please rank the tensions worst to mildest, and for each one say specifically what would have to go, why it can't be patched by rewording, and what I'd lose by giving it up. Then tell me which single revision would clean up the most at once. Two requests: don't give me a tour of the Annales school (I've read my Braudel, that's where the views came from), and don't assume I'd sooner abandon (2) than (1) or the other way round — I honestly don't know which one I care about more, so please cost both directions. Also if you think one of these is just badly stated rather than wrong, say which and how it should be stated instead, but flag those seperately from the real conflicts.
