I just got handed the job of writing the risk section of our foundation's grantmaking policy. We fund first-of-a-kind stuff, so half the time there's no base rate for anything, and the board has split into two camps: one says if you can't put a number on a risk you shouldn't fund it, the other says the missing number is itself a reason for caution and that isn't irrational. I've been reading around Ellsberg trying to settle this and going in circles, so what I want is an essay that actually picks a side on whether ambiguity aversion is a genuine defect of rationality or something defensible.

Please don't just run the textbook urn. Work through three versions: (1) the standard one, 100 balls, red and black, ratio unknown; (2) the same urn, but you're told the mix was set by a fair randomizer and nobody, not even the person running the game, ever saw the result; (3) same as (2), except you get to draw one ball, look at it, and put it back before you bet. Where does the pull toward the known-odds bet survive, and does that pattern fit the popular line that ambiguity aversion is really distrust of whoever hands you the urn, or does it point somewhere else? I also don't want two things swept aside: ambiguity-averse people seem vunerable to getting pumped through a sequence of trades, and the effect reportedly shrinks a lot when you evaluate bets one at a time instead of side by side. Reconcile those with your verdict, or say honestly if you can't.

Then tell me what to actually put in the policy. Finish by naming the shakiest step in your own argument and what would flip you to the other camp.
