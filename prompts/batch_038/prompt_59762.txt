I am drafting a methods chapter on explanation in history, and I want the specification fixed before I engage with any theory: what an account of historical explanation has to deliver in order to count as adequate. The purpose that fixes the standard is a practical one — the account must be usable by working historians to separate a genuine explanation of a particular outcome (why a given regime collapsed, why a conflict began when it did) from a chronology that merely precedes it or a story that is merely vivid.

Set out the desiderata, giving for each the case or consideration that motivates it, and mark which are non-negotiable and which are the preferences of one camp in the covering-law versus narrative dispute. At a minimum, address: how the account handles explanations that cite what actors believed and wanted; whether it must license counterfactual claims about the events narrated; how it accommodates the fact that historians argue about explanations rather than accept them; what is owed when two incompatible narratives invoke the same generalization; and what consistency with the practice of competent historical writing itself requires.

Then rule on a requirement I have been pressing on my students without settling it — call it the swap test. If the particular events an explanation narrates can be replaced by others without any loss of explanatory force, the particularity was doing no work, and what looked like a historical explanation was a generic one in disguise. Is that a genuine desideratum, a serviceable heuristic, or a mistake?

Finally: is any subset of these requirements jointly unsatisfiable, and which single one eliminates the most otherwise attractive accounts? Score the standard contenders only in passing, if at all. Close by saying what would change your mind — which requirement you would drop first, and on what argument or example.
