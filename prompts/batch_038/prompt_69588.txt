I am putting together a session for a graduate seminar in philosophy of biology on the units of selection, and I want a clear conceptual exposition built around a case I have constructed for the purpose. The case is deliberately artificial; please work only with it and do not substitute the familiar examples (volvocine algae, eusocial insects, trait-group models) except where a brief contrast genuinely helps.

**The case.** Consider a mat-forming colonial invertebrate, which I will call M. Its relevant features are these:

1. A mat originates when a single larva settles and grows clonally by budding zooids. Zooids are morphologically differentiated into feeding, defensive, and gamete-producing types, but the differentiation is fully reversible: any zooid can dedifferentiate and become any type. There is no sequestered germ line.
2. Mats are routinely chimeric. When two mats of different genotypes meet, they fuse and persist as a single integrated entity with shared tissue and a shared circulatory system. Within-mat genetic variance among zooids is therefore high, and it changes over the mat's life as faster-budding genotypes gradually take over the interior.
3. Mats propagate by two distinct routes.
 - *Fragmentation.* A piece of the margin breaks off, drifts, attaches, and grows out into a new mat. Fragments vary from a few square centimetres to most of a mat, and a fragment's zooid composition is simply whatever sample of the parent's margin happened to detach. There is no size bottleneck and no filtering step.
 - *Larval release.* Specialized zooids produce larvae sexually; larvae disperse and each founds a new mat. A larva is a product of some zooids within the mat, not a piece of the mat: the only material continuity runs through gametes contributed by whichever zooids happened to be in reproductive phase, and any one larva represents only a small and unrepresentative fraction of the mat's genotype composition.
4. Mats nonetheless differ in ways that look group-ish. Mats vary in seasonal larval output and in fragmentation rate, and these differences track mat-level features — the thickness of the shared tunic, the density of defensive zooids along the margin — that are not simply the mean of zooid-level characters. Margin defense density, for instance, affects the whole mat's resistance to grazing and hence its fragmentation rate.

Members of my seminar disagree about whether M-mats are groups in the sense required for group selection, and I suspect the disagreement is really a conflation of two different demands. What I want from you is an exposition that separates them and then runs the case through both.

The two demands are:

- **(A) The demand that the group bear a group-level trait and therefore have group-level fitness.** Here I have in mind Sober's distinction between a genuinely group-level trait (the classic example being a group's sex ratio) and a merely aggregate trait (such as mean individual size), together with Okasha's contrast between MLS1, in which groups are just containers whose "fitness" is derivative from individual fitness, and MLS2, in which groups themselves have fitness and produce offspring groups.
- **(B) The demand that the group be a reproducer in the developmental sense.** Here I have in mind Griesemer's insistence that reproduction requires material overlap between parent and offspring — the offspring develops out of a bit of the parent, so resemblance is developmentally caused rather than merely copied — and Godfrey-Smith's further point that a bottleneck in the life cycle is what re-individuates an offspring, together with his taxonomy of simple, collective, and scaffolded reproducers.

My working hypothesis, which you should test rather than accept, is that M's two propagation routes pull these demands apart: the fragmentation route seems to satisfy material overlap while failing the bottleneck condition, and the larval route seems to involve a genuine bottleneck while involving no reproduction of the mat *as a mat*. If that is right, the verdict on M depends on which demand one takes to be constitutive of a level of selection, and the case would show that "is this group a unit of selection?" is not one question.
