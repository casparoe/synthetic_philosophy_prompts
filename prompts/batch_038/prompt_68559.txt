I am a doctoral candidate in political theory, writing a chapter on the gap between social choice impossibility results and actual institutional design, and I intend to use the first round of the 2002 French presidential election as its central case. What I want is a disciplined analysis of that election through Arrow's impossibility theorem — including, and this matters most to me, a candid account of how much of the analysis the theorem genuinely licenses. My working suspicion is that the usual invocation (Arrow shows there is no fair voting system, therefore arguments about electoral reform are futile) does no work at all in a case like this, and I would like to see whether that suspicion survives contact with the details.

Since you cannot look anything up, treat the following as stipulated. Sixteen candidates stood on 21 April 2002 under the Fifth Republic's two-round system: the first round is decided by plurality, and the top two advance to a runoff in which the winner must hold an absolute majority of votes cast. Turnout in the first round was 71.6 percent, a drop of nearly seven points from the previous presidential election, and for the first time under the Fifth Republic no candidate reached 20 percent. Jacques Chirac (RPR) led with 19.88 percent; Jean-Marie Le Pen (National Front) took 16.86 percent; the Socialist prime minister Lionel Jospin finished third with 16.18 percent, some 195,000 votes behind Le Pen. To Jospin's left and on his sovereigntist flank stood, among others, Arlette Laguiller (5.72 percent), Jean-Pierre Chevènement (5.33) and Noël Mamère for the Greens (5.25), plus Communist and Trotskyist candidates in the low single digits; together they outpolled Jospin by a wide margin. François Bayrou of the UDF took 6.84 percent. Two weeks later, nearly every eliminated candidate and most of the press called for a republican front against Le Pen, and Chirac won the runoff with 82.21 percent. Before the first round, opinion polling had treated a hypothetical Chirac–Jospin runoff as a dead heat.

With those facts in hand:

1. Mapping. State precisely what plays the role of the profile, of the aggregation rule, and of the social outcome here. Arrow's theorem concerns social welfare functions that yield complete, transitive rankings over a fixed alternative set, and its premises are unrestricted domain, weak Pareto, and independence of irrelevant alternatives, from which it follows that no such function can also be non-dictatorial. Be explicit about whether a single-winner election under one fixed rule is even the kind of object the theorem ranges over; if it is not, identify the family of objects that contains this election and on which the theorem does bear, and say what the difference costs the analysis.

2. Diagnosis. At least two distinct complaints about 21 April are usually run together, and I want them separated. One is that plurality violates independence of irrelevant alternatives, so Jospin's elimination is traceable to candidates who could not themselves have won. The other is that the two-round rule violates the Condorcet criterion: on the supposition that Le Pen would have lost pairwise to Chirac, to Jospin, and to Bayrou, a Condorcet loser nonetheless reached the decisive ballot. Say which complaint better captures what went wrong, whether the two can come apart in this electorate, and how far that supposition is licensed by the evidence available — in particular, what the 82.21 percent runoff does and does not establish about the pairwise structure of the first round, given that the runoff was contested by a larger and differently motivated electorate.

3. Domain restrictions. Arrow's result depends on the domain being unrestricted, and there is a family of conditions — single-peakedness and, more generally, Sen's value restrictions — under which majority rule becomes transitive and Paretian, non-dictatorial aggregation possible. Assess whether such a restriction was plausibly available to the 2002 French electorate. Note what I take to be the complication: the National Front's appeal ran on immigration, national identity, and law and order rather than on the left–right economic axis that otherwise ordered the field, and Chevènement's sovereigntism cut the same way. Does a two-dimensional space in which one bloc's support peaks off the main axis destroy the restriction, or merely relocate it? And if the restriction fails, what follows — is 2002 a near-instantiation of the conditions under which Arrow's theorem bites, or a case in which the theorem is true and idle?

4. What the theorem does not cover. Arrow is silent on incentives, so redo the case in a framework that is not. Under Gibbard–Satterthwaite the first round invited favorite betrayal, and the pressure toward the useful vote that followed 2002 is precisely that. Under clone-independence, the proliferation of left candidates is not a fact about preferences but an option available to parties, and I want a view on whether the left's fragmentation should be modeled as sincere diversity or as failed coordination. Consider also whether abstention can be rational here for a reason other than ordinary free-riding: methods that eliminate by transfer or by pairwise tally are vulnerable to the no-show paradox, in which a voter who prefers some candidate to every other is better off staying home. Does that mechanism have any purchase in a plurality first round, or does it belong only to the instant-runoff and Condorcet alternatives that reformers proposed afterward?

5. Verdict, in both directions. Say whether 21 April 2002 is a confirmation of Arrow's theorem, an anomaly for it, or a case it was never meant to reach. Then run the question the other way: if the French two-round system was chosen to optimize not Arrow-consistency but the public legitimacy of an absolute majority and the deliberative second round in which the republican front formed, should a social choice theorist count 2002 as a design failure at all — and what would have to be true for the answer to be yes?

Please reconstruct the argument yourself: no bibliography, no references, no appeals to what some named author says. Define any technical notion you introduce in a sentence or two, so that a reader outside social choice theory can follow.
