For two semesters our department's foundations-of-mathematics reading group has contained a standoff I have now been asked, as the neutral party, to write up. One member is a potentialist of an old-fashioned stripe: on her view a collection is legitimate only insofar as it can be generated, so the natural numbers form an endlessly extendable series rather than a finished object, and every apparent reference to a completed infinite totality is either a shorthand for such a procedure or else empty. The other is a set-theoretic realist: he holds that the potential/actual distinction is pre-Cantorian folklore with no work left to do, that the iterative conception gives us a perfectly determinate hierarchy, and that his opponent's refusal to say what the hierarchy is a totality *of* is the tell. Their debates have become unproductive in a specific way. He reads the hotel thought-experiment — the fully occupied establishment that accommodates a new guest by shifting everyone up — as a theorem about Dedekind-infinite sets, and she reads it as a reductio of the assumption that got it started; neither has ever written down what would count as evidence against their own position; and both are convinced the other is smuggling in a definition of size or of existence. I want the document this collaboration should have produced, drafted so that each of them could sign it without amendment.

Please deliver four things. First, a numbered list of claims both can sign, stated as precisely as you can manage: what each accepts about the hotel itself (I take it both should accept that the natural numbers are equinumerous with a proper subset and hence that the whole-is-greater-than-the-part principle fails as a general account of size, while disagreeing about what more, if anything, follows); what the diagonal argument requires by way of a completed collection of reals, as opposed to what it requires only of an arbitrary given enumeration; which results of classical set theory are merely unfamiliar rather than philosophically suspect; and what the two of them agree the notion of a potential infinity does and does not license. Second, a disagreements table with one row per live disagreement, each row giving the realist's position in wording he would accept unamended, the potentialist's in wording she would accept unamended, and an explicit verdict on whether that row is a substantive dispute or a dispute about what the word exists permits us to say — if any rows are verbal, say so and say which stretch of language does the work. Third, the cruxes: the two or three sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one of them, each paired with the argument, theorem, or case both agree bears on it and with each side's prediction recorded in advance. Fourth, a closing paragraph stating the residual disagreement in one sentence that both are willing to sign.

My own suspicion, which you should correct if it is wrong, is that one crux concerns the proof-theoretic results: does reducing the consistency of first-order arithmetic to primitive recursive arithmetic plus quantifier-free transfinite induction up to the first epsilon number vindicate the potentialist, by exposing exactly which infinitary resources arithmetic needs, or does it refute her, by showing those resources are ones she cannot in principle do without? And a second crux may concern the consequences of choice — non-measurable sets and the decomposition paradoxes that go with them: are these data about the ontology, or only about the poverty of our geometric intuitions? If you think the real crux lies elsewhere, in the status of impredicative definitions or of quantification over all sets, put it there instead and justify the substitution. No literature, no attributions, no recommended reading: I want formulations and argument, not a bibliography, and I will be circulating this to people who cannot look anything up. Hard limit of 1,200 words for the whole document, table included.
