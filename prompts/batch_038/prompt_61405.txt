I am the TA for a fourth-year decision theory course and I have one script left to mark; I would like a second read on it before I commit to a grade. Here is the exam question, worth 25 marks, and the student's response in full.

**Question 3.** State the money-pump argument against intransitive preferences. Does the argument succeed in showing that cyclical preferences are irrational? In your answer, discuss at least one proposal from the dynamic-choice literature about how an agent with cyclical preferences might avoid being pumped.

**Student's answer.**

Suppose an agent prefers A to B, B to C, and C to A. She begins holding A, and a trader offers her a sequence of swaps, each carrying a small fee ε. Since C is preferred to A, she pays ε to swap A for C; since B is preferred to C, she pays ε to swap C for B; since A is preferred to B, she pays ε to swap B for A. She finishes holding exactly what she started with, but with 3ε less. Every step in that sequence was, taken on its own, precisely what her preferences called for, and yet the final outcome is dominated: there was an alternative available to her all along — simply declining the first swap — that would have left her better off however things turned out.

Like the Dutch book theorem, the money pump is a consistency result rather than an empirical prediction: it shows that cyclical preferences guarantee a loss whatever else the agent does over time. That is why the argument is usually taken to establish transitivity as a requirement of rationality, and not merely as prudent advice.

There are background assumptions worth naming. The pump needs the agent to prefer more money to less, and it needs the ε-fees to be the only costs attached to the swaps. It also assumes that her ranking of two options is unaffected by which of them she happens to hold at the time; an agent with a genuine status-quo bias would decline the first swap and would never enter the pump at all. Finally, because ε can be made as small as we like, the loss the pump inflicts can be made as small as we like, which suggests the argument shows only that cyclical preferences are exploitable to whatever degree we stipulate, rather than that they constitute a serious defect in an agent.

The obvious reply to the pump is foresight. A sophisticated agent — one who sees the whole sequence in advance and reasons backwards through it — declines the first swap, because she knows exactly where it leads. So the money pump cannot establish that cyclical preferences are irrational as such; it establishes only that cyclical preferences combined with myopia are exploitable. McClennen's resolute agent reaches a similar destination by another route: she binds herself to a plan in advance, in the manner of Ulysses lashed to the mast, and then carries the plan out at each subsequent node instead of choosing afresh there. But the pump is set up so that nothing restricts the agent's later options, so there is no device for her to bind herself with, and resolute choice therefore collapses into myopia with extra steps. The upshot is that what the pump diagnoses is a failure of information rather than of preference: cyclical preferences are a defect only in agents who cannot foresee their own future choices.
