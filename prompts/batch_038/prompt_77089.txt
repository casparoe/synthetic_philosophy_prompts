ok so I run a small Kant reading group, six of us, and we've burned three meetings going in circles on the formula of universal law because everyone's using it to mean something different and nobody notices. I want to build a decision tree that sorts each of us into a named position — questions first, positions wherever they happen to land in the space, not a list of commentators with their views stapled on.

Three axes it has to turn on:

- where the contradiction lands when a maxim fails — in the conception, in the will, in the state of the world, or in the agent's standing as a co-legislator. I want the consequentialist slide (what happens if everybody did it) marked as a seperate fork rather than folded into contradiction-in-the-will, because half my group is unknowingly running a Singer-style generalization argument and calling it Kant.
- whose description of the maxim governs: the agent's own, the morally salient one, or the most specific true one. This is where "I'll steal on Thursdays" and the innocent-maxim problem live.
- whether the test generates duties or only vetoes them.

Plus one fork I haven't seen drawn anywhere, and I want you to tell me if it's real or a confusion: I've been calling it agent-side vs recipient-side universalization. In the case of the prosperous man who refuses all help, the contradiction seems to come from imagining yourself as the one who'll need help later, not from anything wrong with the maxim as a rule for acting. So does the test universalize what I may do, or also what I may have to put up with being done to me? If that's a genuine axis, which leaves are reachable only from the recipient side; if it's a confusion, say so in the item where it would have sat.

Leaves I want present whether or not you think they're attractive: the view that the formula does all the work and the other formulas are restatements of it; the view that it's a cheap screen and the formula of humanity carries all the content; the practice-parasitism / free rider reading; the practical-contradiction reading of the sort you get in O'Neill and Korsgaard; and the Hegelian leaf — empty formalism, nothing ever fails it except by smuggling in content.

Use these as the diagnostic nodes rather than as illustrations: the four Groundwork cases (suicide out of self-love, the lying promise, letting your talents rust, refusing all help), the murderer at the door, and two of my own — "I'll take up the oboe" (harmless, nobody else has any reason to, and yet it looks like it goes pointless if universalized) and "I'll do X on Tuesdays" (universalizes perfectly, which is exactly the problem).

Format: exactly five numbered items, no intro and no wrap-up. Each item is one fork — the question phrased so someone can answer it without knowing which position it leads to, the answer options, and the position or positions at the end of each branch, a couple of sentences each. In whichever item it fits, flag any two leaves your tree cant actually tell apart, and any combination of answers that comes out empty — and for the empty ones say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it's repellant, or because nobody's just bothered to go there.
