So — long story short, I've been talked into chairing my university's research portfolio review board, the body that every five years decides whether an interdisciplinary centre keeps its renewal money. Roughly three to four million a round, so people get quite worked up. What I've noticed is that the word "degenerating" has become a club: whenever someone wants a centre gone they say it's a degenerating programme, and whenever someone wants one saved they say Lakatos would obviously call it progressive. Half the board hasn't read a page of Lakatos and just hears a philosopher's name being swung around, and I don't much like it, because I think the methodology of scientific research programmes really is the right framework for what we're doing — it's the only account of scientific rationality I know that takes seriously that the unit of appraisal is a sequence of theories stretched out over time rather than a single paper, and that has any vocabulary at all for the difference between an auxiliary hypothesis that buys you something and one that's just a patch over a hole.

So what I want from you is an actual decision procedure I could hand to the committee: an ordered set of questions, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each one, that a smart non-philosopher with two hours and a dossier in front of them could run, and that keeps the hard parts of Lakatos instead of flattening them into "did they publish a lot." The things I most care about surviving the translation: the hard core / protective belt distinction and the negative versus positive heuristic — board members need to be able to say what a given centre's hard core is, and I want a test for whether they've actually located it rather than just picked whichever claim they'd enjoy defending; the difference between theoretical progress and empirical progress, since those come apart constantly and my physicists treat them as one thing; and above all the criterion for a novel fact, because that's where everybody cheats — they'll count something the programme was built to accommodate as though it had been anticipated, or count a restatement of the hard core in shinier notation as excess content. Give me whatever conditions Lakatos actually sets out for when a problemshift is genuinely heuristically powerful rather than merely defensive, in language a chemist or an economist will accept as a fair test and not as philosophy word games. And be explicit about which steps hand the decision over to a human being because they're judgment calls rather than matters of fact — I'd rather see those marked than buried inside a confident-sounding checklist. Then tell me what my procedure has to smuggle in that Lakatos himself wouldn't license: he's pretty clear that you can only appraise a programme retrospectively, that there's no such thing as instant rationality, and that hanging on to a degenerating programme for decades can be perfectly reasonable — and yet my board is being asked exactly the question he refuses to answer, which is when to quit. I want that gap owned up to, not papered over, plus a note on where the procedure will predictably misfire: on young centres that haven't had time to produce a problemshift yet, on fields where two camps genuinely disagree about what the hard core even is, and on programmes that spin off enormous technological and commercial value while anticipating nothing new.

Finally, run it on three dossiers and show your work at each step rather than just giving me the verdict. (1) A centre doing "quantum models of decision-making" — steady output, very heavily cited, trains lots of students, spun out a successful imaging company; but every failed prediction gets fixed by adding another free parameter, and I can't point to anything it anticipated that wasn't already in the founding papers a decade ago. I personally dislike this one and expect the procedure to say degenerating, so I want to see whether it can be made to say that on grounds that aren't just my prejudice. (2) A centre building a formal framework for biological self-organisation — no experimental confirmation of anything yet, but one genuinely surprising prediction made in year three that took until year nine to test and came out right, and mostly formal elaboration since. I like these people a great deal, and I want the procedure to tell me straight if I'm going soft. (3) A centre whose flagship theory was publicly demolished about six years ago and which has rebuilt around a different core since; half the board says falsified, cut it, and the other half says you can't hold the old theory against them because it's a new programme now. Does my procedure have the resources to settle that arguement, and if not, what does it owe the board instead? At least one of the three should come out as "no verdict yet" if that's the honest answer — and if it does, tell me what the board should do institutionally in that situation (a shorter renewal, an earmarked test, a named prediction with a deadline?) rather than pretending the framework hands me a yes or a no.
