I am in Year 13 and doing my extended project on the economic descendant of the Needham question: why modern industry took off in Britain rather than in China. Everyone I read agrees that there is a fact to be explained and disagrees about the explanation — cheap, well-situated coal; New World colonies that let Britain escape a land constraint; wages high enough to make labour-saving machinery worth inventing; political fragmentation and interstate rivalry; property rights and patents; a culture of experiment; or plain luck. My history teacher says the debate is hard but well posed, and that better evidence about wages or output in the Yangzi delta will eventually settle it. A friend says the question is nonsense because Britain and China are the wrong units to compare.

I want to argue a third position, and I would like you to make the case for it as strongly as you honestly can.

The position is this: the question is ill posed not because the units are wrong, but because nobody has fixed what is being contrasted with what. A question of the form "why P rather than Q" carries a hidden Q, and different choices of Q convert the same evidence into an answer or into no answer at all. Consider three contrasts. Britain against Qing China as a whole: coal geography and empire do real explanatory work. Britain in 1780 against the Yangzi delta alone: on one reading the two are close enough in living standards that there is no pre-industrial contrast left to explain, while on another, which puts English silver wages at roughly three times the delta's by 1600, the divergence is already centuries old and the question turns out to be about agriculture rather than industry. Britain against the Dutch Republic: the richest economy in Europe, with comparable commercial and financial institutions and no coal of its own, which makes the explanandum stop being about Britain and start being about Europe. Under each contrast a different fact does the explaining, and a fact that answers one contrast fails to answer another. My claim is that the historians are therefore not disagreeing about evidence; they are disagreeing about which contrast is the real one, while presenting the disagreement as a factual one — and since the contrast is never stated, no amount of new data can close it.

Please set that case out, and then take seriously the two objections I expect: that choosing a contrast is a merely pragmatic matter of how one phrases the explanandum, so nothing substantive follows; and that if this were right, every open-ended historical question would be ill posed, which proves far too much. Say explicitly whether the position survives them.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence, then give the reasoning, with no preamble and no restatement of my question. I do not want references or a reading list — just the argument.
