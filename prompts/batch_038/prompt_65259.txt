I cover science and ideas for a magazine, and I am working on a piece about the internal architecture of the Endangered Species Act, which strikes me as a text that has quietly taken positions on several questions philosophers argue about. I would like your help reconstructing those positions.

Here is the structure as I have it. When the Secretary decides whether a plant or animal warrants listing, the statute directs that the determination be made "solely on the basis of the best scientific and commercial data available"; economic effects are not merely downweighted at that stage but excluded from it. One step later, when critical habitat is designated for an already-listed species, the same statute directs the Secretary to consider the economic impact, the impact on national security, and any other relevant impact of specifying a particular area, and permits exclusion where "the benefits of such exclusion outweigh the benefits of specifying such area as part of the critical habitat," unless failure to designate would result in the species' extinction. Further along, an exemption process added in 1978 lets a cabinet-level committee weigh a federal project's benefits against the benefits of the alternatives consistent with conserving the species, and authorize the project to proceed even where it would jeopardize a listed species; in its most famous test, the Tellico Dam case, the committee declined to exempt, and Congress then exempted the dam by a rider in an appropriations bill. Meanwhile the unit being conserved is itself partly a legal artifact: the Act's definition of a species reaches below the taxonomic species to subspecies and to distinct population segments of vertebrates that interbreed when mature, so which entities appear on the list depends on administrative judgments about distinctiveness and significance rather than on discovery alone. And the operative listing standard is not a claim about the present but a probabilistic claim about the future risk of extinction, yet it is treated as a matter of scientific fact that politics may not touch.

So: what theory does this apparatus presuppose, and where does it presuppose nothing at all?

I want four things. First, the ontology: does insisting that listing rest solely on scientific data commit the statute to species being mind-independent kinds with determinate boundaries, such that a listing decision is a discovery about the world? Which rival accounts of species — species as historical individuals, as lineages, pluralism about species concepts, outright nominalism — does that insistence actually rule out, and which of them would license the practice just as comfortably, given that the Act's own definition of the conserved unit is partly conventional? Second, the axiology: what view of value makes sense of a scheme that insulates one question from money entirely and then answers a downstream question in dollars, national security, and commensurable benefits, with an extinction clause that appears to function as a lexical threshold? Is that a fact/value split, a two-stage decision procedure disguising a single value judgment, or merely a jurisdictional allocation between an expert agency and a political body? Third, the underdetermination: identify the parts of this architecture that carry no metaphysical commitment whatsoever — features explained by judicial review, tractability, or resistance to capture — and distinguish what the statute strictly requires from what merely fits it well. Fourth, the counterfactual: pick the rival view you think the practice most conspicuously excludes, and say concretely what would have to change in listing, in critical-habitat designation, and in the exemption process if that rival were adopted instead.

Begin with your bottom line in a single sentence, then give the reasoning; no preamble and no restating of my question. You do not need to supply references, a bibliography, or a reading list — I want the argument, not the sourcing.
