been chewing on the risk-weighted expected utility thing for a couple years now — came at it from the rationalist-blog side, so the Allais / Ellsberg / Pascal's-mugging reflexes were already installed before I ever looked at the formalism — and what I want is an audit of my own position, not a tour of the literature. here's the package:

1. the Allais common-ratio pattern is correct and it isn't a confusion or a framing artifact. sure million over the 10/89/1 gamble; then 10% for five million over 11% for one million once the common consequence is stripped out. somebody who flips there is tracking something real about risk, not screwing up arithmetic.

2. the way to capture it is a rank-dependent rule of the Buchak sort: order the outcomes of the act, then weight each increment by a function of the probability of landing at least that well, with that function convex so long odds on the upside get discounted more than proportionately. I take this to be the normatively right decision theory for an individual agent, not a description of how humans happen to be biased.

3. my utility of money is basically linear. I refuse to explain risk aversion as diminishing marginal utility of dollars, and I refuse to get Rabin'd into a total-wealth framing where turning down $110/$100 commits me to turning down a shot at a billion. the risk-sensitivity lives entirely in the probability weighting.

4. relatedly, decisions get evaluated on their own terms. if I'm facing two independent bets at the same time, I apply the rule to each one separately and do what it says for each. I dont want a theory where the verdict depends on whether I happened to think of them as one package or two.

5. act-wise dominance is not up for negotiation. if one act is at least as good in every state and strictly better in some, you take it, and any formalism that says otherwise is broken. this is bedrock for me — its how I check that a theory isn't smuggling in nonsense.

6. and this is what lets me handle Pascal's mugging without caving to it: tiny probabilities get downweighted more than proportionately, so for realistic muggings the weighting does the work and I never have to pretend my utility function is bounded.

a friend who does formal epistemology read the list and made two objections. (a) that 1 and 3 pull against each other, becuase if your utility of money is genuinely linear then the Allais preference is just a mistake with extra steps. (b) that 3 is empty anyway — "risk-averse weighting with linear utility" is concave utility written in different notation, so I've announced a rejection of something I've quietly kept.

so, go through it: which pairs among 1–6 are genuinely in tension, ranked by severity rather than dumped in a flat list, and for each one what specifically has to give and what that costs me. then tell me straight whether (a) and (b) land or whether he's the confused one. and if there's one revision that cleans up the most, name it — I'd rather drop a single commitment decisively than hedge across all six. assume I already know the math; I want the bookkeeping on my own position.
