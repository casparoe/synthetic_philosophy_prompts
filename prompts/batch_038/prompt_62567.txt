For years our logic seminar has played a standing game: someone proposes a procedure, and our supervisor says whether it counts as an effective method. The seminar's running joke is that this case-by-case intuition-mongering is the only evidence anyone ever offers for the Church-Turing thesis, so we would like to know what principle the verdicts are tracking. The record so far: ordinary long division on integers of arbitrary length, with unlimited paper and time, yes. A method whose next step depends on the clerk judging which of two continuations is more promising, where the clerk's judgements are perfectly consistent but nobody has managed to state a finite rule reproducing them, no. A method that enumerates proofs of a fixed axiom system until it hits a proof of a theorem that is in fact provable there, with no bound anyone can state on how long the search will run, yes. A machine that performs step n in half the time of step n-1 and reads off an answer at the limit, no, on the ground that no finite being could run it or check it. A method that needs a separate finite instruction sheet for each input length, the sheets not producible by any uniform procedure, no. A method exactly like long division except that certain inputs would require more paper than exists in the observable universe, yes, without hesitation. A method that lets the clerk choose freely among several admissible moves at each step but reaches the same final answer whichever choices are made, yes.

Next week's case is a Goodstein-style procedure: a wholly definite, finitely specifiable iteration on natural numbers that a clerk could carry out step by step and that does terminate for every input, but whose termination is not provable in first-order Peano arithmetic.

Predict the verdict, and do the rest of the work too: state which general rules the record is jointly consistent with (an idealized-clerk conception, a no-judgement or no-insight conception read either as applying to the procedure as given or to some equivalent of it, a straightforward Turing-equivalence conception, a conception that additionally requires the procedure's halting to be establishable within the mathematics we accept, and any others you think fit), and say why at least one familiar candidate such as physical realizability is already dead. Then identify where the surviving rules diverge on the new case, give your best guess with the reasons for preferring one rule over the others, and name a further case that would settle which rule our supervisor is actually running.

Please open with your predicted verdict in a single sentence, then develop the analysis. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write an email: no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no bold or italics, and no tables.
