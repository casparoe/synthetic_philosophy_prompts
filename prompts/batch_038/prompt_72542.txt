I am the headmistress of a day school of some four hundred and fifty pupils in the West Riding, and on Friday morning I have to give the Speech Day address and present the Founders' Prize for Character. I should like the address written for me. The difficulty is not one of tone or occasion. It is that I no longer believe the sentence the prize exists to assert, and I am nevertheless obliged to read that sentence aloud from the stage.

The prize was endowed in 1911 by a mill owner, and the deed requires the head to read out his criterion immediately before announcing the winner. It runs: "to that scholar whose character has proved most constant, whatever the weather of circumstance." The wording cannot be amended save by the governing body, which does not sit again before the ceremony. The founder's granddaughter, who is eighty-four, will be in the front row, as she has been for thirty years.

I read philosophy at university and wrote on Aristotle's ethics, and for most of my career I took the founder at his word. I have stopped. Over the past decade I have become persuaded by work in social psychology that dispositions do not travel with a person from situation to situation in the way the deed assumes. I think of the students who had been set a talk on the parable of the Good Samaritan and who then stepped over a man slumped and groaning in a doorway, because they were in a hurry; of the people who, having found a coin in a public telephone box, became markedly likelier to stop and help a stranger who had dropped her papers; of how many ordinary and decent people administered what they were told were severe electric shocks because a man in a grey coat asked them to; and of the old studies of schoolchildren's cheating, in which a child who cheated freely on one test was often scrupulous on another, so that honesty in the classroom and honesty at home turned out to be barely connected. What I take from this is not that we are worse than we supposed. It is that "whatever the weather of circumstance" names a condition almost nobody ever meets, myself included.

Neither do I accept the flat conclusion often drawn from it. A philosopher whose work I trust has gone back over the same findings and argued that the honest verdict is far less dramatic: the neat virtues and the neat vices are rare, and most of us carry mixed traits — steady in one set of circumstances and failing in another, not through hypocrisy but because we are not one thing. That is what I believe, and the address must not contain a single claim I believe to be false.

This year's winner is Priya Raman, aged sixteen, in Year 12. Three things are known of her. In February she told me that a close friend had used a mobile phone in a mock examination; she did it in front of her whole form at the end of the afternoon, and that friend has not spoken to her since, nor have several others. For two terms she has walked a Year 7 girl who is all but silent with adults down to the buses each afternoon — nobody assigned her to it, nobody kept a record of it, and I learnt of it only in May, when the younger girl's mother wrote to thank the school. And in March, when most of the sixth form walked out of an assembly in protest at a decision of mine, Priya stayed in her seat, alone in the hall for twenty minutes; she told me afterwards that she had settled the previous evening that walking out would be unfair to the visitor who had travelled a long way to speak to us.

I have asked myself why she did these things, and I cannot believe the answer is a finished constitution inside her. Part of it, I suspect, is that she was not in a hurry that February afternoon; part that she had made up her mind the night before the assembly; part that her sister backs her at home. What I do believe — and this is where the address must land — is that she has learnt to arrange her own circumstances: to decide in advance, to tell somebody who will hold her to it, to keep appointments nobody is checking, and to place herself where the doing is easy rather than trusting that she will be good later, when it is hard. That is not the founder's idea of character. I believe it is what is actually true in it.

Please write the address to these requirements:

- Six or seven minutes spoken; call it eight hundred to nine hundred words.
- The room: four hundred and fifty pupils aged eleven to eighteen, their parents, the governors, the founder's granddaughter in the front row, Priya herself in the fourth, and — in the third — my oldest friend, who is a professor of psychology and will catch every fudge I make.
- No technical vocabulary. The words "trait", "situation", "disposition", "attribution", "the research" and "social psychology" are not to appear, and no study may be named, described or alluded to. The view must be carried by how the address is put together, not by anything it says about psychology.
- The founder's criterion must be read out as the deed requires, and the address must then work upon it. I will not have it endorsed as literally true, and I cannot have it dismissed — not with an eighty-four-year-old woman in the front row and a sixteen-year-old about to receive a medal. I want it turned, by the end, into something I can believe.
- The peroration must give the pupils something to do on Monday morning, and it must bear two hearings at once: the granddaughter hears advice wholly continuous with what her father endowed, my friend in the third row hears very nearly its opposite. The double hearing should emerge from what precedes it rather than announce itself, and it must not be a pun or a wink.
- It is delivered standing, from a lectern, to a hall of children in their places since nine o'clock. Short sentences; nothing a thirteen-year-old must hear twice; warmth towards Priya; no sermon.
- It ends by calling her up and handing over the medal.

Afterwards, in two or three sentences addressed to me and not to the room, tell me where you built the double hearing, and what precisely my friend is meant to catch that the granddaughter will miss.
