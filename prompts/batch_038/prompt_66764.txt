I am three-quarters of the way through a novel set in and around a municipal museum of social and economic life in a central European city between the wars; the museum's real business is not its collection but a workshop on the top floor that turns statistics into pictorial charts for exhibition halls, schoolrooms, and workers' reading rooms. My narrator is the workshop's chief "transformer" — the person who receives a table of figures and decides what the picture will be — and a good deal of the book is meant to consist of the documents she generates: circulars, house rules, memoranda to the print shop, requisition slips for zinc blocks. My editor has just cut the subplot in which she had an affair, so one of those documents now has to carry more weight than I planned for it. What I need is a parody of a graphic house-style manual: the workshop's own standing instructions to its drawing staff on how to construct a picture language that a reader with no schooling, and no language in common with the designer, can nevertheless understand. Roughly 850 to 1,000 words, in a deadpan institutional first-person plural, as if approved by a committee and revised four times, with numbered rules, a short troubleshooting section written as questions from the print shop answered by the workshop, and a closing appendix of symbols retired from the repertoire. It should read as a slightly stiff translation from German, and the tone should be affectionate rather than cruel: these people are right about nearly everything they are wrong about.

The manual's doctrine must be the real doctrine of pictorial statistics as it was worked out in Vienna between the wars, and the rules you give should be the rules such workshops actually observed. Quantities are expressed by repeating an identical figure rather than by enlarging one, because a big man is not ten men and because the reader cannot tell whether an enlarged symbol is being compared by its height or by its area; each figure is stripped down to the fewest marks that will still identify it; nothing is drawn in perspective; the chart must explain itself without a caption; and the whole enterprise exists to make social facts — not treasures, not rarities — memorable to ordinary citizens, on the principle the museum liked to put in one of its own catch-phrases: to remember simplified pictures is better than to forget accurate figures. You may use that line. You may also note once, and without emphasis, that the director has conceded in print that what he is building is not a language but a "language-like technique"; the manual's authors regard this as a slander and wish it recorded that they are building a language. I would rather you invented the museum's name, the director's name, and the name of the municipal committee that funds it than attempt any history: I want the doctrine right, not the dates.

That last point is where the comedy should live, and it must never be announced. The joke is expressive poverty discovered from the inside: the workshop keeps being commissioned to draw things its own rules cannot draw. It cannot draw "not" — a chart of what did not happen is indistinguishable from a chart of something else — and it cannot draw "if," "or," "usually," "on average," "at least," "perhaps," "zero," "the same as last year," "mine," or "next." It cannot draw an averted war, a probability, a counterfactual, a denial, or a promise. Each failure produces a new rule, each new rule produces a new failure, and the appendix of retired symbols should quietly show the wreckage of at least three abandoned attempts — a figure with a bar struck through it, a figure with a question mark attached, a figure of a second figure pointing at the first. Meanwhile the workshop's philosophical patron insists, on principle, that every legitimate statement must be about observable physical things, which is awkward, because the museum's entire commissioning brief is unemployment, prices, class, debt, and war. And because the manual is itself written in words, the workshop must eventually confront the question of who is going to draw the manual: a rule for reading rules, which will require a rule for reading that rule. I want the reader to feel, without being told, the old difficulty that a picture seems unable to say that something is not the case, and the related difficulty of whether a repertoire of figures could ever amount to a notation at all rather than a family of likenesses graded by resemblance; readers who have thought seriously about depiction and about notational systems should recognise their own puzzles in the print shop's complaints. Please do not name any philosophers, do not supply real citations, footnotes, or a bibliography, and do not let the manual wink — the escalation should be entirely earnest, and the workshop should end, as such workshops do, by issuing one more rule.
