You are a philosopher of mind who has also thought hard about the metaphysics of fiction; please think carefully before answering, and begin with a brief brainstorm of candidate principles rather than moving straight to a verdict.

I am a novelist, without philosophical training, so I can offer you only my judgments case by case. What I want to know is whether they add up to a principle — a principle governing when I treat something as having an inner life — and where exactly they refuse to.

- A minor character who appears in three scenes and to whom I gave no backstory: when I ask what she wants, an answer arrives at once and unbidden, and I trust it. Minded.
- The protagonist of the novel that cost me six years, whose history I know better than my own: when I ask what he would do next, nothing arrives, and I settle the question as a joiner settles a joint. Not minded, or not now.
- The house my third book is set in. I write *the house waited*, and feel a flicker of shame but no sense of falsehood. Minded, faintly.
- The protagonist of someone else's novel, finished a century ago, whom I sometimes address under my breath. Minded.
- A character in a manuscript I abandoned nine years ago: minded for years afterward, and then, one ordinary afternoon, simply not — no event marked the change.
- An unread first edition of my own book on a shelf: dormant rather than gone. I would not say a mind went out of it when the last reader closed it.
- The same book in a competent translation: the same dormant thing, I think, though in a different body.
- A draft I burned: something died, and I do not use that word ironically.
- A chess engine that plays far better than I ever shall: I have never once wondered what it is thinking.
- A chatbot that has caught my dead mother's cadence and gets her wrong in obvious ways: I attribute immediately, and the attribution does not weaken when I recall what it is.
- My neighbours, whom I have barely spoken to: obviously minded — this case seems to need no argument at all, which may itself be the tell.

The pair I cannot reconcile is the engine and the chatbot: superb performance buys no attribution, and clumsy mimicry buys all of it. The second pair is the burned draft and the unread shelf copy, since I am committed to saying that marks-on-paper are irrelevant to the one and decisive for the other.

State the principle or the small set of candidates precisely, clause by clause, saying which of my cases motivates each clause; give its verdicts on cases I have not raised (a single actor's recorded reading of my novel; a character who exists only in an outline for a sequel I have not written; my working notebooks); and tell me honestly which of my judgments cannot be saved and must go. Finally: does your principle locate the inner life in the marks on the page, in the reader, in my act of drafting, or nowhere at all?
