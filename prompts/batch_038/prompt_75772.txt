I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and I have spent the better part of a year reporting a feature about the quiet transfer of judgment from people to systems — not the speculative version that fills the op-ed pages, but the mundane one: the resident who reads the decision-support box before she reads the chart, the modeller who asks a language model to digest a literature she has stopped reading, the structural engineer who can still audit a simulation but could no longer set one up from a blank sheet. The piece runs next month, and my editor has agreed to something unusual for us — the article will end with a short poem rather than a kicker paragraph. I have three failed drafts of it in a drawer. All three collapse into either a jeremiad about forgetting how to think or a shrug about how we have always leaned on our instruments, and I would like you to attempt a fourth.

What I want the poem to carry is an argument I could not fit into the reported copy. There is an old line of thought — I believe Peter Hardwig pressed it about big science — to the effect that in a collaboration of several hundred authors no single member has seen more than a fraction of the evidence, so a result can be known by the group and by nobody inside it; and philosophers who work on testimony long ago conceded that this is not a scandal but simply what knowledge looks like at scale. So scale is not what is new in my story, and I do not want the poem to pretend otherwise. Two other candidates, and I want both tested rather than asserted. The first is an asymmetry of cost: it has always been cheaper to check a result than to produce one, and that gap is precisely the mechanism by which the capacity to produce one goes unmaintained, since a decade of competent auditing will never disclose that the ability to regenerate the thing from first principles has lapsed. The second is the identity of the party deferred to: a colleague who disagrees with you is at least a locatable source of reasons you could interrogate, whereas a system that produces reasons on demand has none to give, and its agreement therefore carries no information. If the poem can make a reader feel the difference between being handed a number and being handed an interpretation of a number, it will have done what my six thousand words have not managed. I would also like it to neutralize, without mentioning, the counter every reader will reach for — the one about Socrates warning that writing will ruin memory, or about the arithmetic teachers of my parents' generation — not by answering it but by making the difference specific enough that the analogy stops covering the case.

The form should be a pantoum, and the repetition should be the argument rather than a decoration upon it. Four quatrains, sixteen lines: the second and fourth lines of each quatrain recur verbatim as the first and third lines of the next, and the poem's final two lines are the first and third lines of the opening quatrain, in that order. What I am after is a handover — the same sentences should come back owned by a different mouth, so that by the close a reader cannot tell whether a recurring line is something the speaker says or something the speaker has been given to say, and so that the closing pair lands as a confession rather than as a return. Please set it in one room on one night; I have been reporting at a public-health modelling group, so a windowless office at two in the morning, a printed figure curling on the desk, and a cursor somewhere offstage are all available to you, though you may choose another setting. Concrete nouns only: the machine is never named, and none of my beat's vocabulary appears anywhere in the poem — no algorithm, model, data, prompt, output, epistemic, outsourcing, or automation. Aim for lines of roughly four stresses; slant rhyme is welcome and full rhyme is not required. Afterwards, in two or three sentences, tell me which line changes hands, at which stanza, and what you take the turn to be; I will have to defend this closer to an editor who does not read much poetry, and I would rather not guess.
