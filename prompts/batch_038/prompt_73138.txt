Last night a friend and I argued for four hours about a study I ran, and this morning I still cannot tell who won. Here is the background, then his argument, and then what I want from you.

**Background.** I design typefaces. Some years ago I became interested in the old Letter Spirit work — the attempt to model how a designer can produce a wholly new drawing of a letter and yet be drawing *the same letter* — and I built a small apparatus in that spirit. In my apparatus every glyph is constructed on a coarse grid of square cells, each cell either filled or empty, so that glyph-space is finite and discrete: two glyphs are neighbours just when they differ in exactly one cell. I took an uncontroversial lowercase 'a' as one endpoint and a shape that is uncontroversially no letter at all as the other, and I built a path between them of 43 single-cell flips, each flip selected from a pilot so as to be as unobtrusive as possible. All 44 glyphs were then shown to 61 participants, twice over: once for a rating from 0 to 100 of how much the shape is an 'a', and once for a forced verdict, yes or no.

The results are smooth. Mean ratings fall monotonically, with no discontinuity anywhere along the path. Yes-fractions drift from roughly 95% to roughly 5% across the middle twenty-odd steps, and no single step sees a drop of more than about ten points. For every adjacent pair inside the transition zone, at least a third of participants return the same verdict for both members. Shown two consecutive glyphs side by side, participants can usually identify the differing cell; shown one glyph at a time, they cannot say where along the path the shape ceased to be an 'a'.

**My friend's argument.** He calls step 2 the no-hinge principle.

1. Glyph-space is connected: between any glyph that is an 'a' and any glyph that is not, there is a path of single-cell flips.
2. No single-cell difference is, by itself, the difference between being an 'a' and not being an 'a'. For one cell rather than one of its neighbours to carry the whole weight of a letter's identity would be arbitrary; and when pressed, I could neither point to such a cell nor say what would make it the decisive one — for any candidate flip I can imagine a script in which it matters and a script in which it does not.
3. Hence for any two neighbouring glyphs on my path, one is an 'a' if and only if the other is.
4. Hence, by induction along the path, the terminal glyph is an 'a' if and only if the initial one is.
5. But the initial one is an 'a' and the terminal one is not — a contradiction. Since the path, the endpoints, and the data are not in doubt, what has to go is the assumption that the endpoints were determinate in the first place. There is no fact of the matter that this shape is an 'a' and that one is not; there is only graded resemblance to paradigms, plus a practice that draws a line somewhere in the gradient.
6. Nothing in step 2 referred to letterforms. No property whose instances are assembled from small local parts can have a sharp boundary in the space of those parts. So nothing determinately is a chair, a heap, a red patch, a city, a word of a language.
7. Therefore the project I admired — stating what every 'a' has in virtue of which it is an 'a' — is not merely unfinished but impossible: there is no such property to find.
8. And my craft judgements inherit the result. When I say that one of my 'a's works and another does not, I am not reporting a fact about the shapes; I am registering where my practice happens to have drawn its line.

I cannot accept 7 or 8, and 6 alarms me. I want to keep the data, and I want to keep the conviction that certain 'a's I have drawn are simply wrong rather than merely unconventional. I have a suspicion that step 2 is being asked to bear more than it can hold, or that something happens between 4 and 5, or that 6 changes the subject without announcing it — but after a day of staring at this I no longer trust my own diagnosis.

So: is the argument valid as written? If it is not, identify the step that fails and say exactly what it equivocates on or assumes without warrant, and explain why that step looked so secure — particularly in light of the curve my data actually produce. If it is valid, tell me which premise is least costly to abandon, and price the alternatives properly: what I pay for insisting that there is a sharp cut-off somewhere on my path that nobody can locate; what I pay for saying the cut-off shifts with context or with judge; what I pay for treating letterform membership as a matter of degree; and what I pay for conceding step 6 and living with it. Finally, I want to know whether the property I need as a working designer — the one that decides whether a new drawing joins the character set or is thrown out — is even the property my grid experiment was probing.
