I've been reading rationalist blogs since roughly the Sequences era, so I come to this already primed with the standard story: Dutch books for credences, money pumps for preferences, coherence as non-exploitability, and so on. A friend of mine runs a decision-theory reading group and has been drafting an essay that tries to explain the money-pump argument to that audience without any mathematics. She sent me the central paragraph and asked whether it was safe to publish; I said yes on a first read, because it matches what I already believed. She then showed it to a graduate student in her group, who came back with "two of these claims are simply wrong, one is a philosophical position dressed as a result, and something is doing work the paragraph never acknowledges," without saying which was which. Since I cannot tell you where the trouble is, I want an audit rather than a defense.

Here is the paragraph as it currently stands. "Suppose you prefer an apple to a banana, a banana to a cherry, and a cherry to an apple. Someone who knows this can hand you an apple, then offer to trade it for a cherry plus a dollar, then trade the cherry for a banana plus a dollar, then trade the banana for the apple you started with, plus a dollar. You accept each trade because at each point you are getting something you prefer, and you finish with the apple you began with and three dollars less in your pocket. The loss is guaranteed rather than merely probable, and it depends on no deception and on nothing you failed to notice, so intransitivity is not a cosmetic flaw in a preference ordering but a mechanism that hands your resources to anyone patient enough to run the cycle. The same reasoning does the work for the rest of the standard axioms, which is why the whole edifice of expected utility can be motivated dynamically: any violation of any axiom is a vulnerability that shows up as a sure loss, and that is why rationality is best understood as a constraint on what can be done to you rather than as an intrinsic property of a preference ordering. Notice also that seeing the trap coming does not help. A fully sophisticated agent who knows in advance that she will be offered the entire sequence is pumped just the same, because each trade still looks worth taking when it is offered, and this strengthens the argument: it is not addressed only to the careless. Nor does the argument require the cycle to consist of strict preferences. It works equally well when some of the comparisons are ties, so the familiar sorites-flavored case — a cup of coffee with one more grain of sugar, indistinguishable from the last, all the way up to a cup you find undrinkable — is not a counterexample to transitivity but an instance of the same pathology."

What I want is a careful going-over. Take the claims one at a time and say, for each, whether a decision theorist would sign it as written, sign it with a qualification, or reject it; state the qualification or the objection; and where a claim fails, say what would have to be added to the argument to make it go through, or whether it cannot be repaired at all. I am most suspicious of two moves myself — the claim that foresight provides no protection, and the claim that indifference cycles are covered by the same reasoning — but I would rather you assess those than take my word for them, and I would especially like to know whether the paragraph contradicts itself between the sentence that says the pump depends on nothing you failed to notice and the one that says sophistication is no help. Then tell me what the graduate student meant about unacknowledged work: which premises the argument is quietly leaning on, whether about how choices relate to preferences, about what happens at each node when the agent is merely indifferent, about the size of the payments and whether they leave the ordering intact, or about what the person running the pump is permitted to offer, and whether a defender can afford each of those premises or has to argue for it. Finally, tell me what survives. If the offending sentences were cut or rewritten, would the conclusion she actually wants — that an intransitive agent is defective by the lights of practical rationality, not merely unlucky in her trading partners — still follow, and would the broader claim that rationality just is non-exploitability still be worth asserting? Please write this as continuous prose, the way you would in a letter or a long email: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, even though you are in effect moving through the paragraph sentence by sentence. I am comfortable with the vocabulary, so do not spend words defining terms like sophisticated choice or the independence axiom; spend them on the audit.
