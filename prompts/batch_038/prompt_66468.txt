I spent thirty-one years as a structural engineer inspecting bridges and retaining walls, and since retiring I have been reading, entirely on my own and with no training, about how complex societies come apart — chiefly the eastern Mediterranean at the end of the Bronze Age, the Classic Maya lowlands, and Rapa Nui. I read them for a selfish reason: my younger relatives keep telling me that our own predicament has no precedent, and I keep suspecting that it does. Last month our town historical society asked me to give a talk, and I argued that these three cases share a shape — a society continues to look intact from the outside, its monuments and markets and official records carrying on as usual, while the systems holding it up quietly degrade underneath, so that a shock which would otherwise have been absorbed produces a loss wildly out of proportion to itself. Afterwards a retired schoolteacher in the second row, who reads far more than the rest of us put together, told me politely that my entire framing was out of date. There is no such thing as collapse, he said, only change; what I had described was transformation wearing a gloomy label. He did not use identical words every time, and by the end of the evening the same idea had been recruited for at least three separate jobs: to insist that the Maya cities were not abandoned in any sense that matters, to insist that the Rapa Nui islanders did no lasting damage to their own island before Europeans arrived, and to suggest that anxiety about climate change is a modern fashion for seeing catastrophe where there is only rearrangement.

What I want is a map of what that sentence can actually be claiming. Please write out, as a numbered menu, the distinct precise theses a person might be asserting when he says that collapse is not a thing that happens but only a name we give to change we dislike — running from the versions that are true and empty, through the ones that are substantive and genuinely debatable, to the ones you believe are false. For each entry, give me: the thesis stated in a sentence or two of plain English; what the world would have to be like for it to be true; your own verdict on whether it is true; and which of the three jobs above it would actually accomplish, if any of them. I expect several of the differences to be verbal rather than historical — over what it takes for the same society to persist, over whether collapse names a process or an end state, over whether the concept requires that someone be made worse off, over whether a sharp fall in population and the loss of writing count as collapse even if the survivors themselves report that life got easier — so please say plainly, for each thesis, whether it is a claim about the past or a claim about the vocabulary of people who study the past, and whether it is meant to be compatible with real catastrophe or to make catastrophe impossible by definition.

Two readings I would like included whether or not you think they are the popular ones: the view that collapse is real but is a property of institutions and administrative systems rather than of populations, so that the disappearance of a palace economy can be a catastrophe for scribes and a reprieve for farmers; and the view that collapse names a rapid drop in a society's level of sociopolitical complexity, with no built-in claim about anyone's welfare either way. Then tell me where each of those leaves my talk — whether either one makes my central claim about hidden degradation and disproportionate shocks false, or merely mislabelled. Finally, flag the pairs of readings that people habitually slide between in mid-sentence, because I suspect that is precisely what happened in that room, and I would like to be able to say so without sounding like a pedant. Please do not hand me a bibliography, a list of recommended books, or citations I cannot look up; argue it out from the logic of the claims and from whatever you already know of these cases, and if you must use a technical term, define it in the same breath.
