I'm auditing a reading group on machine consciousness attached to a university AI-ethics centre; my background is one intro philosophy course and a few years of engineering, so I can follow the arguments but I can't tell when a competent-sounding author is quietly sliding from one claim to a neighbouring one. Last week we read the penultimate chapter of a recent trade-press book about whether artificial systems might have minds, and the chapter pauses to set out higher-order theories of consciousness before using them as a springboard for two conclusions. Here is the relevant passage as I transcribed it:

> Higher-order theories say that a mental state is conscious just when the mind has a thought about that state: you consciously see red only because some part of you registers that you are seeing red. The view was developed mainly by David Rosenthal, who insists that these higher-order thoughts are themselves unconscious, which is why no infinite regress threatens. It follows that consciousness is a matter of self-monitoring rather than of what an experience feels like, and that is why the theory appeals to neuroscientists hunting for a measurable marker: any system sophisticated enough to keep track of its own states, including a well-designed artificial one, is conscious on this account. Critics reply that the theory makes consciousness both too cheap and too intellectual. Too intellectual, because a fish or an infant lacks the concepts needed for a thought about a state, and so would have to be unconscious. Too cheap, because a higher-order state could misfire and represent an experience the subject never had, in which case the theory is committed to a conscious experience of nothing at all; since that is absurd, higher-order theories have to be abandoned, and the alternative is to hold that consciousness is built into first-order states themselves.
