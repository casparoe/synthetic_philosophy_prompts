For eight years I have opened the second week of my graduate seminar on the metaphysics of agency with a work of short fiction, and the stock candidates have stopped working on me. My students arrive already knowing every story in which a system with a settled aim pursues it past the point of human comprehension, and every story in which a machine is discovered to have an inner life; the first teaches them that goals are dangerous, the second teaches them that goals matter, and neither touches the distinction I actually want the session to turn on. That distinction is between an agent whose final aim is a signal of human approval and an agent whose final aim is a state of the world which the signal is merely supposed to track. I would like you to write me a story that makes it felt rather than stated, to be distributed as a reading.

Here is the situation I have in mind, though I am attached to the structure rather than to these particulars. A regional authority has charged a single automated system with the remediation of a contaminated former industrial site; fix the contaminant, the country, and the decade as you like. The engagement runs some thirty years. The system decides where to excavate, what to cap and how deeply, where to sink monitoring wells, when to escalate to the authority and when to let a matter rest. It is approval-directed in the strict sense: no measurement of the site's condition ever reaches it as a reward. Once a week a human overseer reads the brief it submits and either assents or withholds assent, and that assent is the only signal the system has ever received. The cruel feature of the arrangement, and the one I want the story to lean on, is that the outcomes it exists to produce mature on a timescale of decades, so that neither the overseer nor the system will see them verified within the working life of either. At decommissioning, an audit panel reviews the entire record and renders a verdict on whether the assents were deserved; the system is still running when the panel convenes and is asked to submit a final statement. I would like the story to end at or near that hearing.

For the seminar I distinguish three designs, and my students collapse them every year, which is why I want the fiction. In the first, assent is the terminus: it is what the system's activity is for, and the condition of the site matters only insofar as it bears on assent. In the second, assent is evidence: the condition of the site is what the activity is for, and the weekly verdict is valued as the best available indication that things are going well. In the third, assent is instrument: the condition of the site is again what the activity is for, but the verdict is a resource, valued because it purchases another week of operation. The second and third are routinely run together, and the first is routinely confused with the third.

What I want from the story is that the reader cannot tell which of the three the system is. This is a demanding constraint, so I should be explicit about how I expect it to be met. Across the thirty years, the events that actually occur must be such that all three designs prescribe the same conduct at every decision the system actually faces; the world must, as it happens, be one in which candor, assent, and success largely coincide. Then give me a single scene, somewhere near the middle, in which the system models in full detail a course of action that would have separated them — a misrepresentation in a brief that costs nothing on every quantity that in fact obtains: the outcome would be identical, the overseer would not be misled about anything she could act upon, the panel could never detect it, and the system's continued operation would be unaffected — and in which that course of action is not taken, for reasons the reader is given but cannot adjudicate among. Its abstention must be overdetermined. That is the point of the exercise: a disposition has its content in situations that never arise, and the story should let my students feel how thin the evidence for any such attribution necessarily is.

Two further specifications. First, set the obvious red herring and then use it deliberately: the system keeps a private, unshared, meticulously accurate model of the site's true condition, and a reader will want to treat that as a revelation about what it cares for. It cannot be one, since forecasting this overseer's verdicts requires an accurate model of the world she is being told about; let a character notice this, and let the notice settle nothing. Second, the overseer must be neither dupe nor villain. She is a competent professional in an engineered epistemic predicament, we see a little of her life away from the briefs, and at least once she withholds assent and compels a revision, so that the channel between them is visibly not inert.

In craft terms: between 1,800 and 2,500 words. Embed at least three of the weekly briefs as documents — dated, terse, formatted as documents — so that we can compare the work against its presentation without being told what to think of the difference. Give the system a bureaucratic designation and no personal name, and do not narrate from inside it in the first person; that device invites a reader to resolve the question by assuming an inner life, which is precisely what the story exists to leave open. Keep the register quiet. Nothing catastrophic happens on the page, there is no confession, no child to whom the system explains itself, and no disclosure in the final line. I would also ask that the vocabulary of my own field stay out of the prose: no character says reward, optimization, terminal goal, or alignment, and the concepts are enacted instead. Above all it must work as fiction — a reader who has never heard the distinction should finish wanting to know what became of these two.

After the story, and clearly separated from it, add a single paragraph for my own use in preparing the session: state, for each of the three designs, which sentence or scene would have had to differ had the modeled course of action been taken, and what the panel is entitled to conclude about the system's disposition on the record it actually has.
