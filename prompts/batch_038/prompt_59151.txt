I run the forecasting platform at a mid-sized freight company. In three weeks I am chairing a two-day internal workshop whose stated purpose is to decide how our models' forecasts should be scored and how they should be reported to the rest of the business. About sixty people will be there: roughly half are machine-learning engineers who build the models, and the rest are actuaries, risk analysts, and two economists from a university group we work with. I have been asked to give the ten-minute opening remarks. I want them built around an idea I arrived at from the engineering side, with no philosophical training, and I want your help both checking that I have understood it and writing the remarks.

The idea comes from Keynes — not the macroeconomics, but the earlier book on probability, which a colleague with an economics degree pointed me to. Here is my understanding, which I expect is mangled in places:

- He separated two things I have been treating as one: the probability of a conclusion, and the weight of the evidence behind it. They are different quantities and they can move in opposite directions.
- He thought a great many of the probabilities we actually rely on are not numbers at all, and that some of them cannot be ranked against one another — not that we have failed to rank them, but that there is no ordering there.
- Separately, in something he wrote in the 1930s, he argued that for certain questions about the future there is no probability to be calculated even in principle, and that people nonetheless act, on something other than calculation.

I have also been carrying two glosses I now suspect are wrong: that on his view more evidence makes a conclusion more probable, and that the practical upshot is to defer a decision until the weight is high enough. I would rather be corrected by you now than by an actuary in the second row.

Two deliverables.

First, before you write anything, tell me what is actually wrong in those three points and two glosses, in terms an engineer can hold onto. Do not send me to sources and do not give me a reading list; I am not going to read a 1921 book before this workshop and I will not be citing anything in the remarks. If the second point and the 1930s point turn out to be the same point, or if I have credited him with something he did not hold, say so plainly. Also answer this, in the same place: I have a nagging sense that what I am describing already has names in statistics — effective sample size, epistemic versus aleatoric uncertainty, interval-valued or imprecise probability. Tell me where those mappings hold and where they break down, because I do not want to give a talk that turns out to be a renamed version of something the room already does correctly.

Second, write the remarks, under these constraints:

1. The distinction must be load-bearing rather than decorative. The talk should be organized around the fact that our reporting practice collapses two axes into one, and it should end somewhere other than where it began.
2. Build it on an artifact everyone in the room knows. Our dashboard shows one number per lane and a 90 percent interval. A forecast for a lane we have run for four years and a forecast for a lane we opened six weeks ago can render identically. We currently paper over this with a rule that a lane needs thirty days of history before it goes live, a threshold somebody picked in a meeting. Last spring we quoted a confident transit-time estimate for a new cross-border lane on the strength of almost no relevant history, and the backtest did not catch it, because the backtest could only ever score us on lanes resembling ones we had already seen. That belongs in the talk in two or three sentences, without turning into a postmortem.
3. Half the room built the metrics. Calibration is a real achievement and it measures something true; the remarks should say precisely what it measures and what it does not, and should leave people prouder of their work rather than defensive. Do not write a talk whose thesis is that our numbers are meaningless.
4. I know one person there will object that a Bayesian model with a hierarchical prior already handles this, since the posterior widens when the data is thin. I do not know how to answer that. If there is an answer, put it in the talk. If there is not, do not fake one: tell me, and write around it.
5. Mixed audience. The economists and the actuaries know Keynes; the engineers have never read him and will disengage at the name. He is named once, early, and everything after that has to stand without him. No biography, no history lecture.
6. The 1930s point is the hardest thing to say to a room whose job is to emit numbers. I think it is the truest thing in the material and I want it in there, but it has to land as a statement about where our numbers are load-bearing and where they are decoration, not as permission to shrug. If your honest judgment is that it cannot be said accurately in ten minutes to this audience, cut it and tell me you cut it and why.
7. No quotations from Keynes anywhere in the remarks. I cannot verify them, and I am not willing to have an invented sentence read aloud by someone who trusts me. If you judge one of his phrases indispensable, give it to me separately and flag explicitly that you are unsure of the wording.
8. Voice: I am an engineer, not a professor. Plain declarative sentences, a few concrete figures of the kind I would actually have, no rhetorical questions in the closing, no in conclusion, no exhortation to be more thoughtful. It should be something I can deliver without embarrassment, including the sentences where I say what we have been getting wrong.
9. About eleven hundred words, which is roughly ten minutes at my pace. End on a single question the room can put to any proposal over the two days, something they can literally ask of a slide, rather than on a summary.
