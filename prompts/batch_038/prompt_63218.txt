I retired four years ago after a career in statistical mechanics, and I have been reading philosophy seriously for the first time — slowly, and with the disadvantage of having no teacher. I have just reread War and Peace with the sections on war in Hegel's philosophy of right open beside it, and I find myself unable to settle whether the novel is a rebuttal of that view of war or an unintended illustration of it. I should like a critic's reading rather than a summary of either author.

Here is what I take Hegel to be claiming. War is the moment in which the ideality of the particular is made actual: property, life, and private interest are shown not to be absolute, and the individual has a substantive duty to risk them for the sovereignty of the state. Prolonged peace, on his comparison, does to a nation what a prolonged calm does to the sea. But immediately afterward he seems to withdraw much of this, conceding that the point is only a philosophic idea, a justification of providence, and that an actual war requires a different kind of justification. Does that qualification do real work, or is it a fig leaf? And if it does real, how much of the notorious reading of Hegel as a theorist of war survives it?

Now set the novel against that. Tolstoy's own theory of history, in the second epilogue, is explicitly a calculus: abandon the search for causes, take the infinitely small elements — the individual wills — and integrate them. Napoleon is an instrument with no more freedom than the last of his soldiers; Kutuzov prevails by refusing to will anything and submitting to the current of events. As a physicist I want to know which of two determinisms this is. Statistical mechanics yields macroscopic regularity from microscopic freedom while imputing no purpose whatever to the aggregate; Hegel's reason, working behind the backs of the passions it sets in motion, imputes an end. Is Tolstoy's integration purposive or merely statistical? If it is merely statistical, on what ground can he insist that Borodino was monstrous rather than inevitable?

Please work through the scenes where the two views actually collide. Prince Andrei wounded at Austerlitz, looking up at the sky and finding the victor who rides over him insignificant — is that a refutation of the world-historical individual, or exactly what Hegel would expect of a man who has not yet grasped that his injury is a moment of the whole? Moscow abandoned and burned by its own inhabitants looks to me like the purest possible case of Hegel's ethical moment in war, the particular sacrificed to the universal, except that no ethical self-consciousness seems to be present in anyone who does it: is the novel granting Hegel his phenomenon while refusing his interpretation of it? And what of Pierre at the firing squad, and his conviction after captivity that no arrangement of the world can be made to yield happiness — is that a political claim Hegel's framework cannot accommodate, or a private one that simply falls outside it?

One further thing. My recollection, which may be wrong, is that Hegel also holds that war is waged against states rather than against private persons and their domestic life, and that the possibility of peace is preserved within war itself. If that is right, then the looting, the executions of suspected arsonists, and the butchery of the retreat are not merely horrible but philosophically interesting: are they a counterexample to Hegel's account of how civilized war must be conducted, or events his account simply declines to describe? I would like you to say which, and to tell me plainly if I have misremembered the constraint.

I am not asking for references or a survey of the secondary debate; I want your judgment. Be faithful in both directions. Where the Hegelian reading forces the novel — and it plainly is also a book about a first ball, about childbirth, about a brother's death, about family life in its last chapters, none of which has anything to do with the ethical health of nations — say so and say what is lost by the imposition. Where I have stretched Hegel to make him a foil, say that too. End by telling me what the novel contributes to the question of whether war can have value that an argument, however good, could not contribute: I suspect it has something to do with what it is like to be inside an event that theory can only describe from outside, but I cannot articulate it, and I suspect it is the reason I could not put the book down.
