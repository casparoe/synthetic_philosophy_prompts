I'm in the second year of a slightly odd masters program that makes me take economics courses and philosophy-of-science courses in the same building, and last week something clicked that I can't unsee. In my asset pricing seminar we went through a run of papers where the field's basic framework gets contradicted by the data — the standard example being that long-run stock returns are way higher than any plausible model of people smoothing their consumption would predict, plus a pile of return patterns (momentum, value, low-vol, whatever) that the simplest market models say shouldn't be exploitable. And what happens every single time is that nobody proposes giving up the framework. Someone adds a new ingredient — a taste for insuring against rare disasters, habit formation, one more "risk factor" — and that ingredient gets published as a contribution in its own right, and the framework walks away unharmed. It got so routine that a few years back some people counted up how many such factors had been published, found hundreds of them, and argued that new ones should have to clear a much higher statistical bar than the usual two-sigma rule, precisely because rescuing the model had become too cheap. Someone in the seminar said, half-joking, that this makes the whole apparatus unfalsifiable, and the professor's reply was roughly: no, because a good rescue predicts something new that you can go check independently, whereas a bad one is ad hoc — it exists only to make the anomaly disappear. That distinction seemed to satisfy everyone in the room and it did not satisfy me at all.

Then two days later, in my methods class, we did the famous Friedman essay on the methodology of positive economics, which I gather is still the semi-official statement of how the profession thinks about itself. The doctrine there is that whether a theory's assumptions are realistic is beside the point — firms don't literally solve marginal conditions, they just behave as if they do, like a champion billiard player who behaves as if he knows the differential equations — and the only thing that matters is whether the theory's predictions are accurate. I kept waiting for someone to notice that this cuts directly against what happened in the other seminar. If predictive accuracy is the only currency, then a contradiction between prediction and data is just a strike against the theory, full stop, and the question "was that rescue ad hoc or independently motivated?" shouldn't even be a question, since a rescue either improves predictions or it doesn't. And on the same view, the big selling point of behavioural economics — that its assumptions are psychologically more honest about how people actually choose under risk, unlike the axiom-violating experiments going back to Allais — shouldn't count as a selling point at all, because realism of assumptions was declared irrelevant. Yet in practice people clearly treat the ad-hoc/progressive distinction as doing real work, and they clearly treat psychological realism as a virtue. So the profession seems to be running two incompatible accounts of what makes a theory worth keeping, and only ever states one of them out loud.

Can you act as a philosopher of science who has actually spent time inside an economics department, and take this apart properly? Think it through slowly — before you settle on an answer, brainstorm the full range of theories of confirmation and of scientific progress that could license the seminar behaviour, including ones that aren't the obvious Lakatosian move (someone told me once this is what Lakatos meant by a research programme and its protective belt, but I never got past the slogan, so tell me whether the practice really is Lakatosian or whether that's a stretch). Then: reconstruct the implicit theory the practice presupposes as precisely as you can; seperate what it strictly requires from what merely fits it comfortably, since I suspect several very different views would license the same behaviour; say which rival accounts of evidence and of theory-choice the practice quietly rules out just by existing; and tell me whether the two halves — the seminar norms and the Friedman self-image — can be held together honestly, or whether one of them is really just window dressing. Finally, describe what would have to change on the ground if you swapped the implicit view for strict instrumentalism: journal norms, what counts as a contribution, seminar etiquette, hiring, the way textbooks present superseded material. Please also flag where you think my description of the practice is unfair or just wrong — I'm writing my thesis on housing supply regulation so this is largely procrastination, but I'd rather know if I've caricatured the field. And end by saying what would change your mind: which argument or piece of the practice, described differently, would push you to a different verdict, and where in your reasoning you're most likely to be going wrong.
