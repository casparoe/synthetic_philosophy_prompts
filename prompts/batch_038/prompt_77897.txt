Bit of an odd one, this. I'm a novelist, three quarters through a book, and one of my early readers — she does philosophy for a living — says Ive wandered into a well known puzzle without realising it, and wouldn't tell me which. So I want to know whether my protagonist's epistemic set-up actually hangs together, or whether something in it is doing work the rest of it forbids.

Nora's brother vanished eleven years ago. Before part three she knows he's still alive — I mean knows, not hunches, and I want the reader to grant her that. She'd also cheerfully admit she could be wrong; she's not claiming certainty, and I take it knowledge doesn't require certainty. Then a body turns up with his watch and his dental records, and she doesn't move an inch. What I'm committed to:

1. she knows he's alive beforehand, and she knows that she knows;
2. her not budging is meant to read as rational — admirable, even — and definately not as grief or denial;
3. the evidence really is strong. I'm not fudging it into bad evidence; the reader should feel it genuinely points the other way;
4. no cheap outs: no unreliable narrator, no repression, no head injury, just a clear headed woman weighing things;
5. it seems to follow from the above, and I want to keep it, that she could have known all along that anything she later learned against his being alive would be misleading — so she was entitled to plan for that in advance.

Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they clash? I half suspect I'm worrying about the wrong pair, so say so if that's the case. Then rank the real conflicts by how fatal they'd be to the book, and tell me the cheapest revision: which commitment has to go, what dropping it costs the story, and whether the body-in-the-morgue scene still works afterwards. I've no idea where the problem is, if there is one.
