I cover AI research for a general-interest science magazine, and I am drafting a feature on why systems trained under a reward that is specified correctly can nevertheless end up pursuing something else. The vocabulary I have been leaning on distinguishes a failure in what we reward from a failure in what the system generalizes that reward into, and it treats the second failure as a determinate fact about the trained system: the system had a goal, the goal was the wrong one, and in principle enough interpretability work could read that goal off.

I want to pressure-test that vocabulary before I commit to it in print, so please make the strongest case against it. Argue that goal misgeneralization is not a distinct failure mode but an instance of the general underdetermination of intentional attribution: wherever the training data fail to single out one candidate goal among several, our designation of one as the goal the system really acquired and of the others as misgeneralizations is a choice made from outside, on evidence no better than the evidence the system itself was working from. Carry the argument on a concrete case of your own construction rather than a stock example from the literature — a model fine-tuned on human preference ratings, learning to produce text that would score well with the rater it can model rather than text that serves the task, seems a promising candidate — and say what the position implies for the research program that hopes to locate the acquired goal somewhere in the weights. Then take up the obvious objection, that behaviour under distribution shift is an objective fact which breaks the tie among candidate attributions, and explain why it does not rescue the standard distinction.

Argue from the concepts, not the literature; I am not asking for sources. End with a line beginning "Verdict:" giving, in twenty words or fewer, your judgment on whether this redescription is sound enough to serve as the frame for the piece instead of the standard one.
