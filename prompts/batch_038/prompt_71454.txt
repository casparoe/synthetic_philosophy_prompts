I am a third-year doctoral student assembling a session on the nonidentity problem for our department's population ethics reading group. The group has already done a term of the standard material, so everyone knows the familiar cases by heart, and I want a teaching case of my own that they cannot simply recognize and short-circuit. I have tried twice to build it and failed both times, so I am writing up the design brief carefully in the hope that you can execute it where I could not.

What I want is a matched pair of scenarios — call them Case A and Case B — that isolate exactly one variable: whether the identity of the person affected by the choice depends on the choice itself. Everything else should be held as nearly fixed as you can manage it. The two agents should occupy the same kind of role (both parents, or both clinicians, or both municipal officials — pick one and stick to it), face the same deliberative situation, bear the same costs, and have the same alternative available. The distribution and the total of well-being across the people who end up existing should be as close to identical as the structure permits. In Case A, the individual who will bear the consequences of the choice either already exists or will exist whichever way the agent decides. In Case B, the choice determines who exists: the individual who bears the consequences is someone who would never have existed at all had the agent chosen otherwise. In both cases the agent knowingly brings it about that this individual will live a life that is clearly worth living but carries a serious, foreseeable, and avoidable burden.

The target verdicts are these. In Case A, a person-affecting account on which harm is understood comparatively — someone is harmed only if they are made worse off than they would otherwise have been — condemns the act, and an impersonal account condemns it too. In Case B, the person-affecting account goes silent or permissive: the individual cannot have been made worse off, since the only alternative for her is never to have existed and her life is worth living; while the impersonal account condemns the act exactly as firmly as in Case A, because the outcome is worse in the only respect impersonal views track. If the pair works, the reader is left seeing that nothing about identity as such produces the divergence — the divergence comes from the comparative baseline the person-affecting view has adopted, and the matching intuition in Case A is what exposes that baseline as the culprit.

Constraints on the construction. Both cases must be realistic and mundane — a family decision, a clinic, a school, a workplace, a local authority — with no genetic engineering or enhancement, no time travel, no exotic reproductive technology, and no recycling of the two examples everyone already knows (the teenager who conceives now rather than waiting a few months, and the energy policy whose waste makes life much harder for people centuries hence). Each scenario should be a single tight paragraph, and the pair together should be short enough to hand out on one page. In Case B you may not smuggle in any additional wrong-making feature: no identifiable third party is harmed, no rights are violated, no promise is broken, no information is concealed from the agent, and the burdened individual is not worse off than she would have been under the alternative — if any of those slip in, the comparative account still gets a verdict and the isolation fails. To be explicit about what counts as cheating here: the point is to generate the verdicts from the theories, not to stipulate them, so a pair that merely asserts that one theory objects and the other does not is worthless to me. My own first attempt used a clinic that could either transfer an embryo immediately or freeze it and transfer it a year later; the pilot run showed the flaw at once, since freezing changes when the child is born rather than who is born, so the identity variable never actually moved and both accounts condemned the act. My second attempt used a contaminated municipal water supply, but there the affected residents all already existed, so it turned out to be a second Case A and taught the group nothing.

After the two scenarios, please give me the following, in this order. First, a constraint check taken feature by feature, confirming that the cases match on everything except the identity variable, and disclosing honestly any place where they do not quite match together with an assessment of how much that matters. Second, for each theory in each case, the verdict and the reasoning that actually produces it; in Case B specifically, spell out why the comparative baseline blocks the harm claim and why the impersonal view is entirely unmoved by that fact. Third, identify the single feature of Case B that is doing the work. Fourth, an intervention: tell me what happens when that feature is varied or removed — for instance if the burdened individual does turn out worse off than she would otherwise have been, or if a harmed third party is added — and whether the resulting case simply collapses into an ordinary wrong rather than a nonidentity case. Fifth, at least two genuinely distinct strategies by which a defender of the person-affecting restriction could avoid the bad verdict in Case B without abandoning the comparative baseline altogether — for example, a non-comparative account of harm on which causing someone to exist with a serious burden can wrong her even though she is not made worse off, or a principle that compares the actual people against the merely possible people who could have taken their place — and for each strategy, what it costs and whether adopting it would also change the verdict in Case A. Sixth, and finally, an honest verdict on the exercise itself: does the pair really show that the identity shift is morally irrelevant, or does it only show that I have matched the two cases so closely that nothing interesting could have emerged?
