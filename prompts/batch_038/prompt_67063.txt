Retired microbiologist here, 35 years in a soil lab, only started reading philosophy properly about two years ago — mostly Kuhn, because everybody kept telling me he was writing about me.

Here's my problem. I'm in a reading group led by a guy much more steeped in this stuff than I am, and over the last few months I've been quietly collecting his calls on which episodes in the history of science count as real paradigm shifts. Copernicus displacing Ptolemy: shift. Lavoisier and oxygen finishing off phlogiston: shift. Aristotelian physics to Newtonian: shift. Quantum mechanics, 1900 to 1930: shift. Germ theory beating miasma: shift, though he allowed it was a messy one that dragged on for decades. Neptune turning up where the wobble in Uranus's orbit said it would: not a shift, that's normal science at its finest, the framework solving its own puzzle. Wegener getting laughed at in the 1920s and then plate tectonics winning in the sixties: also not a shift — and that's the call I keep going back to. His reasoning was that nothing about the concepts changed, geologists before and after meant the same things by rock and age and ocean floor and would have been convinced by the same evidence, all that happened was that somebody finally went and got the data. Prions: a shift, but only inside one small field. PCR and the sequencing machines: not shifts, instruments.

So he has a track record and I can't reverse-engineer the rule from it.

Now, my case, and the reason I care is that I lived through it. Between roughly 1995 and 2015 my field went from being about isolating organisms in pure culture — grow it, stain it, run the biochemical tests, name it — to being about sequencing everything in a sample and asking which genes are in there. Details I think matter:

- Nothing in the old framework ever came out wrong. There was no anomaly. The cultured bugs did exactly what they had always done. What happened is that the new machines showed us how much we'd been missing — turns out maybe one percent of what's in a gram of soil will grow on a plate.
- But the two camps genuinely could not compare results. Hand them the same sample and one says forty species, the other says nine hundred, and they aren't disagreeing about a fact, they're using "species" to mean different things: a phenotype-and-breeding lineage versus a sequence-similarity bin. My old collaborators' line was that the new number wasn't wrong, it just wasn't about anything they recognised.
- The textbooks were rewritten, and not by addition. The isolation-and-identification chapters largely vanished, and a grad student in 2015 was trained on worked examples that had no counterpart in 1985.
- Conversion was not generational. Most of the people I watched switch over did it in their forties and fifties, definately on the strength of argument and not because the old guard died off, and two of my oldest friends became the best sequence people I know.
- Underneath, what counted as evidence never budged: controls, replication, statistics, identical on both sides. And the big theories — evolution, genetics, biochemistry — were untouched and agreed on by everybody.

What's he going to say about this one? Five numbered points, one or two sentences each, no preamble and no summary at the end: (1) three or four different rules that fit all of his earlier calls equally well, (2) what each of them predicts about my case, (3) your best guess at his verdict and which rule you'd bet on, (4) which of his earlier calls is the closest analog to mine and why he might resist the comparison, and (5) one further question about my episode, something I could go and check, that would seperate the rules.
