I've been reading a popular history of free trade — a well-reviewed one, by a professional economic historian — and its chapter on the Wealth of Nations spends a good deal of time on Adam Smith's account of the British Empire. Here is how it summarizes him:

'In the Wealth of Nations, published in 1776 while Britain was already fighting its American colonies, Smith did something no defender of empire had expected: he ran the accounts. The colonies, he noted, contributed no revenue and no military force to the defense of the mother country; all Britain got from them was a monopoly of their trade, and that monopoly was upheld by a permanent garrison and naval establishment and by wars that were in substance quarrels over colonies. Add the interest on the debt contracted for those wars and you arrive at a sum larger than the whole value of the colonial trade it was supposed to protect. The monopoly injured Britain at home as well: it drew capital into slow, distant ventures, raised the general rate of mercantile profit, and made British consumers pay dearer for their goods. Smith's conclusion was flat — under the present system of management, Britain derived nothing but loss from her dominion over the colonies. What makes Smith remarkable is that he drew the moral and not merely the arithmetic. He told Britain to let the colonies go, to keep trading with them freely, and to stop paying for an empire that flattered national pride while draining the treasury. His much-quoted remark about Britain being a nation of shopkeepers is usually read as a sneer, and here it works as one: empire is just what a trading nation wants, and the colonial project was that appetite dressed up as statesmanship. Smith also gave the American grievance its most respectable intellectual defense, insisting that a people taxed without being represented were being robbed; and when he turned to the East India Company's rule in Bengal, he found the same disease in a more advanced form — merchants governing a territory, plundering it, and calling the result commerce. He was, in short, the eighteenth century's most lucid anti-imperialist, and his argument was moral before it was economic.'

I've read the early books of the Wealth of Nations but never the chapters on colonies, so I can't check any of this myself. I came away persuaded that Smith was essentially an anti-colonial thinker whose case against empire rested on principle. A friend who has read more Smith than I have insists that this is a modern projection onto an eighteenth-century argument about British national interest, but when I pressed him he couldn't say where the chapter overreaches, and I'd like to settle it properly.

Please go through the summary claim by claim and sort it into three buckets: what a Smith scholar would accept as accurate, what is a defensible popular simplification, and what is wrong or materially misleading. In particular I'd like to know:

1. Whether the cost-and-monopoly accounting in the first half is faithful to Smith's actual argument, including the claim about capital being diverted into distant trades and about consumers paying more.
2. Whether Smith really told Britain to let the colonies go, and what else he proposes in that connection that the chapter does not mention. If there is a second-best institutional arrangement Smith spends serious space on, describe it and explain how it changes the picture of him as an opponent of empire as such.
3. Whether the chapter's reading of the shopkeeper remark is right, and whom Smith's criticism is actually aimed at.
4. Whether Smith can fairly be described as defending the American claim about taxation and representation, given what his own proposal would require of the colonists.
5. Whether his treatment of the East India Company supports the "same disease" framing, or whether his remedy there runs in a different direction.

Then tell me whether the closing verdict — that Smith was an anti-imperialist whose argument was moral before it was economic — survives once the omissions are filled in, and offer me a corrected characterization of his position in two or three sentences I could carry around. Where the accuracy of a claim turns on Smith's exact wording rather than on the substance of his argument, flag that it does so instead of reconstructing his phrasing; I'm not looking for citations or a reading list, just an honest accounting of what the chapter gets right.
