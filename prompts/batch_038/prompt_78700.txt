I am finalising the stimulus set for a preregistered study on intentional-action attribution — the side-effect paradigm, but built from vignettes of my own rather than the familiar chairman pair — and I have a timing problem. Our design consultant, a philosopher who has rated every pilot item so far, is unreachable until Monday and the registration closes Friday. One item is unfinished, and our analysis plan branches on how she rates it, so I need a considered prediction of her verdict before she gives one.

Her ratings so far, in the order she saw them. Every item poses the same two questions: did the agent bring the highlighted outcome about intentionally, yes or no; and a blame-or-praise rating on a scale from 1, nothing of the sort is warranted, to 7, a great deal is.

1. A plant manager with thirty years on the same process approves a schedule change he is certain will foul the downstream watercourse. When the environmental engineer objects, he says he has no feelings either way about the watercourse; what he cares about is the quarter's numbers. — Intentional: yes. Blame: 6.

2. The same manager, the same kind of decision, except that a new catalyst makes the change certain to reduce the watercourse's toxicity. He says he has no feelings either way about that; what he cares about is the quarter's numbers. He does not understand how the catalyst works, and the improvement is a windfall he read about on the supplier's data sheet. — Intentional: no. Praise: 2.

3. As in item 1, but he is visibly distressed: the watercourse keeps him awake at night, he wishes there were another way, and the plant will not survive the year without the change. — Intentional: yes. Blame: 4.

4. He approves a change certain to reset the break-room shift clock to its factory default, so that for a week the bell rings ten minutes out. He says he has no feelings either way about the bell. — Intentional: no. Blame: 1.

5. He moves the annual maintenance shutdown so that it certainly overlaps the town's river festival, breaking a forty-year informal understanding between plant and town. Nothing illegal, no measurable damage, general irritation. He says he has no feelings either way about the understanding; he wants the maintenance window. — Intentional: yes. Blame: 3. She added a note in the margin here: she was not much inclined to blame him, but the word "intentionally" would not come off.

6. A master process chemist, the best in the sector, devises a tweak that will certainly cut the plant's effluent toxicity by ninety per cent. Asked about this, she says she has no feelings either way about the river; what she wanted was to solve the puzzle of the fouled heat exchangers. The toxicity drop rides along with the new reaction pathway: she foresaw it exactly, but could no more have set it at ninety per cent than at fifty. — Intentional: no. Praise: 2.

My own hunch keeps oscillating between two stories, one on which she is tracking whether there is anything to object to and one on which she is tracking whether the outcome is really the agent's own, and I cannot find an item in the record that separates them. That is presumably also why I cannot settle the last one.

The unfinished item. A chief metallurgist who designed a pressure vessel thirty years ago needs one more full-load fatigue run in order to generate the data that will force a fleet-wide replacement. He is certain the run will exceed the vessel's remaining life and wreck it beyond repair; the data come from the instrumentation, not from the wreckage. There is no non-destructive alternative that would yield the data in time. Nothing illegal occurs, no one is hurt, no professional standard is breached, the board is grateful, and nobody but him ever wanted the vessel preserved. Asked about the vessel, he says: I know exactly what that run will do to it; I could stop short and keep it, and then I would have no data; it is going to be wrecked, and I have made peace with that. He can set the load profile to within a kilojoule.
