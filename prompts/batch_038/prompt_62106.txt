I am presenting a paper at a workshop in moral psychology next month, and I should like a preparation sheet for the question period rather than a defence of the view. My thesis is a reading of Sartre on which bad faith is not a pair of contradictory beliefs housed in different parts of the mind but a single diachronic project: the agent maintains an unstable equilibrium on purpose, and what is in bad faith is the sustaining activity rather than any belief-state one is in. Radical freedom does real work on this reading rather than decorating it, since only an agent who chooses her situation in the strong sense could be answerable for keeping such an equilibrium going. My two central cases are the café waiter and the woman who fails to notice that her companion has taken her hand, and I argue from them that the equilibrium must be actively held rather than passively suffered. The room is mostly analytic philosophers of mind and action, several of whom work on self-deception in the deflationary tradition running from Davidson to Mele, one committed Frankfurtian, and a single Sartre specialist of a Heideggerian bent who will think I have sanitised the ontology. Give me the objections this particular audience will raise, in roughly the order they will come and in their own idiom — not the objections a Continental audience would offer — each with a reply I could deliver in about a minute, a note on what I should concede at once, and a note on where the reply is soft. Include the question I am privately hoping nobody asks. Please write this to me as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables. And be committed rather than even-handed — if you judge one of these objections to be fatal to my reading, say so plainly, identify which of my claims it kills, and tell me whether anything survives it. Write in British English.
