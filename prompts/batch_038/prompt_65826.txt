For my stats-and-society seminar we each have to write a piece of short fiction that stages Simpson's paradox without ever naming it, without a single equation, and without letting anybody give a lecture about confounding. Can you write mine? I've already sketched the setup with my TA so please stick fairly close to it.

A county workforce board has to vote on whether to keep funding Ridge, a fourteen-week HVAC bootcamp, or send everybody back to the old apprenticeship-placement office. Across all enrollees, Ridge people are more likely to be working in the trade a year later. But split the enrollees by whether they actually earned the state refrigerant certificate, and the old office wins in both groups — among the certified and among the people who never got certified. The wrinkle I want the story to turn on is that nobody in the room agrees about what "earned the certificate" even is. Ridge's director says it's something the program *produces*, so conditioning on it means grading both programs only on the people who already crossed the finish line. The county auditor says the certificate is really a stand-in for who signs up at all — the driven ones find their way to Ridge and also pass the exam — so the split is the only honest table in the building. The nasty part is that both of them can point at the exact same numbers, and the board can't run a lottery to settle it because the money runs out in six weeks.

I want the reader to *feel* why one of those readings is right rather than be told, and I want the decision to come out of somebody asking what would actually happen if you pushed all next year's intake through one program or the other — not out of what the subgroups look like sitting there in the spreadsheet. Make the analyst who has to present the numbers a person with skin in the game, maybe someone who went through the old office themselves twenty years ago and owes their career to it. And don't resolve anything with hidden fraud, a data entry error, or a surprise third spreadsheet — the numbers are all real and everyone is arguing in good faith, that's the whole point.

1200–1600 words, please. If you think the vote should come out against Ridge even though its aggregate numbers are better, that's fine, that's even what I suspect, but the story has to earn it.
