Act as a philosopher of mind who works on the temporal structure of consciousness and has argued both sides of the Cartesian theater debate with graduate students. Think the problem through carefully before answering; I want a diagnosis, not a survey of positions.

I have constructed the following argument against Dennett's multiple drafts model, and I have become uneasy with it, because its conclusion seems to establish far more than I can defend.

(1) Reports are produced by processes, and every process operates at a determinate time on determinate inputs. So whenever a subject reports c at t, c was available to the reporting process at t.

(2) For c to be available to a process at t is for there to be a fact of the matter about c's being present, at t, at some locus where that process can read it off.

(3) Multiple drafts denies that any content becomes present at any locus: it allows only a succession of parallel discriminations, each fixing its own content on its own schedule, with no privileged moment or place at which one of them becomes available to anything.

(4) So on multiple drafts there is no fact about which content was available at t, and hence nothing determinate for a report at t to be a report of.

(5) Yet reports are determinate and reliable: subjects report the apparent motion and colour change in the phi cases, the misplaced taps of the cutaneous rabbit, and the identity of a masked prime, and they do so at specifiable times.

(6) Therefore multiple drafts cannot account for report, and any adequate theory of consciousness must posit a determinate time-and-place at which contents become available — a theater in all but name.

I anticipate that the reply will invoke the Orwellian/Stalinesque distinction, but the two hypotheses in question agree about the neural events and about what subjects end up reporting, and my argument turns on neither, so I do not see how that distinction helps. If I had to choose, I would keep (5) rather than (2), which strikes me as close to analytic.
