Teaching the 1956 Oxford fight over Truman's honorary degree this week, and my students keep collapsing Anscombe's objection into "double effect plus absolutism about killing the innocent." I want a pair of cases that shows the intention/foresight line isn't just read off the causal structure. So build me two scenarios in which a harm is equally a means to the good the agent is after — same causal diagram, same practical certainty on her part that it will happen, same alternatives on offer — but where Anscombe's test comes out differently: in one she'd say the agent did the harm, in the other that she didn't. Constraints: mundane setting (a warehouse, a municipal office, a school farm — no trolleys, no bombing runs, no ICU morphine), and the difference has to show up only in practical, counterfactual facts — what each would do if the harm were blocked, what each would count as her plan failing — becuse that's where Anscombe thinks intention lives, not in what the agents say, feel, or confess afterwards.

Give it to me as exactly five numbered items, no introduction and no conclusion, in American English: (1) case A, four or five sentences; (2) case B, same length and same causal shape; (3) each agent's answer to the Why question plus the counterfactual that separates them; (4) Anscombe's verdict on each and which single feature is doing the work — and be honest about whether her own strictness, the thought that knowingly bringing something about makes it your doing, even permits splitting the pair; if it doesn't, tell me what the pair shows instead; (5) what a sophisticated double-effect theorist who's happy to talk about intentions under a description makes of the difference, and whether anything of Anscombe's complaint survives her.
