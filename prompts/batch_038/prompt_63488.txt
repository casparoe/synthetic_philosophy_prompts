I hung up the lab coat three years ago after something like fourty years doing dose–response work for a government toxicology unit, and I've rather fallen into philosophy late in life. I'm in a reading group that meets in the back room of the library — mostly amateurs plus one very patient postgrad who does the reading for us — and we've spent the last month on probability and explanation. Hempel on statistical explanation got me going: the business about a statistical argument having to be maximally specific, that you can't lean on the base rate for some broad class when a narrower relevant one is staring at you. Then we had Reichenbach, who as I recall gives almost the opposite instruction, put the event in the widest class you've got decent statistics for. Between the two of them I got the impression that everyone agrees there's a deep puzzle here — the reference class problem, I'm told it's called — but nobody agrees about what the puzzle actually is, and the treatments I've seen read like they were written by people who've never had to write a study protocol.

Which brings me to my own half-baked idea, and I'd like you to argue for it properly, because I suspect I've been making a much weaker case for it than it deserves. My thought is that reference classes aren't fixed by descriptions at all, and that's precisely why the puzzle looks insoluble. In my old life a probability was never attached to an animal; it was attached to a population that the protocol had constituted in the first place — this strain, this sex, this age band, this dose level, this route of administration, animals randomised, housed identically, fed on the same schedule, culled at the same timepoints. You don't discover the class by picking out the true description of the rat in front of you; you manufacture the class by designing the study, and you manufacture it in such a way as to guarantee that the members all got the same treatment under the same conditions. So here's the thesis I want defended: an objective probability claim is well-formed only relative to a population that could in principle be constituted by an intervention-and-design specification, and the embarrassment of endlessly many candidate classes is just the symptom of the fact that most true descriptions of a thing correspond to no such specification. Call it the protocol-fixed reference class, or find it a better name than I can.

So make the case for it, and make it a real argument rather than a balanced survey — balanced I can do myself. I want to know why this is more than pragmatism in a lab coat, i.e. why saying the class is fixed by the design isn't just conceding that probabilities are relative to whatever scientists happen to bother setting up. Then deal with the two things I got hammered for already. First: it seems to make a nonsense of probabilities where nobody could ever run a study — the odds of a given tumour in a wild vole, of life getting going on some planet, or of my own heart attack, and I'm seventy-one and the cardiologist quotes me numbers with great confidence. Second, and worse: that the view doesn't solve Hempel's problem so much as smuggle in the hard part, because deciding what counts as "the same conditions" is itself a relevance judgement, which is exactly what maximal specificity was supposed to discipline. And please be explicit about what has to be given up to keep the view — my own suspicion is that it costs you the idea that there's a single true unconditional probability for an individual event, and I'd much rather hear that argued than hidden behind a footnote. Don't bother pointing me at papers, by the way; my library card doesn't reach much these days and most of what I've read I've read aloud in the group. If you think the thesis is actually wrong in some specific place, say so and say where, but don't hedge the lot. Two or three pages worth would be about right.
