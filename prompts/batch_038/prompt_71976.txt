Last Sunday I drove forty minutes to a camera fair looking for a filter for a dead Minolta, ended up not finding it, and instead spent twenty minutes standing next to a dealer's table while two people I know — Rob and Ines, both from a reading group I'm in — argued about a third person's cameras. I was drafted as referee and I bombed it, so I want a second opinion. Here's the case they were arguing over, which I'm pretty sure I've got right.

Maya owns a camera I'll just call A. A dealer she knows, Theo, has two others, B and C. Maya's ranking of them goes in a circle and she's completely upfront about it: put A and B side by side and she takes B, because the lens is sharper and that's the difference she weighs; put B and C side by side and she takes C, because C has the original case and that gap matters more to her than the lens does; put C and A side by side and she takes A, because A is in much better physical shape and condition beats the case. She also says each of those upgrades is worth "twenty or thirty bucks" to her, so every one of them is worth more to her than a $5 fee. One afternoon Theo makes her three offers in a row, in this order, and she can see all three coming before she answers any of them: (1) trade your A for my B, free; (2) trade your B for my C, and pay me $5; (3) trade your C for my A, and pay me $5. If she answered them one at a time with no thought for what came next, she'd say yes to all three and end up back where she started — holding A, ten dollars poorer. What she actually did was take offer 1, then refuse offer 2 (so offer 3 never applied), and walk away with B and her money intact, which is a strictly better camera than she arrived with, obtained free.

Their exchange went like this:

1. Rob: That's luck plus a kindly dealer, not a vindication. Her preferences are the sort that let a stranger take $10 off her for nothing — if Theo had charged $5 on the first swap too, she'd have walked home with A and $15 gone. Being exposed to that drain is exactly what makes the circular ranking irrational, and the exposure is real whether or not it got exploited this particular time.
2. Ines: It couldn't have been exploited, because she could see all three offers coming. Reason backwards: she'd take offer 3; so at offer 2, taking the $5 swap means finishing with A and $10 out, which is worse than keeping B, so she refuses; so at offer 1 the free swap is pure gain and she takes it. Myopia is a processing limitation, not a defect in her preferences. And if this argument worked it would convict perfectly rational people too, since you can always hand somebody a run of individually-fine trades that add up to something bad.
3. Rob: Then look at what your rescue costs. At offer 2 she turns down a swap she says she prefers, for a fee she says is trivial. Her own stated ranking says take it. She only refuses because she's insuring herself against herself five minutes later, and needing to be protected from your own preferences is precisely the defect I'm pointing at.
4. Ines: She isn't choosing between B and C at offer 2. She's choosing between two packages — keep B and have no further dealings with Theo, versus take C now and then A for another $5. Preference has to be about the acts actually on offer, consequences and all, and once you rank acts nothing circular shows up anywhere.
5. Rob: Once you rank acts nothing shows up *at all*, because now her ranking swings with whatever Theo happens to be peddling and in what order. Same values, same three cameras: on one menu she prefers C to B, on another she prefers B-and-ten-dollars-kept to C-and-five-dollars-spent. If the objects of preference are acts, then "I prefer C to B" was never a claim about cameras, and the circle was in the offer menu, not in her. So pick one: either the circle is real and it can cost her money, or it isn't real and we should stop talking about intransitivity as though it's a thing a person can have.
6. Ines: Fine, make it harder for me and see whether the pump survives. Suppose Theo repeats the same three offers every Saturday forever instead of once. Then there's no last offer to reason back from, so she simply adopts a plan on day one: accept the free swap, refuse every paid swap, every Saturday, permanently. The plan is available, it's the best one open to her, and holding to it is rational — nobody gets pumped.
7. Rob: "Holding to it" is doing all the work and it isn't a reason. Every Saturday, node by node, the paid swap looks worth five bucks for a camera she prefers, and the only thing that stops her is a rule she wrote down on day one to dodge a trap. If resolving to override your preferences at each node is rational here, it's rational in any pattern whatsoever, including patterns that leave you worse off — that's stubbornness with a good press agent, not rationality. And notice you've conceded my point: the node-by-node preferences are the broken ones, which is why the plan has to steamroll them.

Can you go through moves 1–7 one at a time and tell me who's right at each, rather than just who wins overall? For every move I'd like a rough rating — clearly wrong, partly wrong, fair, clearly right — plus a couple of sentences of justification. Please actually check the arithmetic and the backwards induction in my setup rather than taking their word for it; I suspect at least one of them is leaning on a step that doesn't follow, and I suspect move 2's last sentence is doing something sneaky. Then tell me what both of them are missing: there's at least one assumption they each use in passing, about what it means for a swap to be "worth twenty or thirty bucks," that I don't think either of them is entitled to. I'm not after a literature survey or any names — just your verdict on these moves as they actually made them.
