For the past two years I have been adapting one of my own novels for television, which has meant handing scenes I wrote alone to a room of nine people; for the past three weeks I have been in residence at an institute where a handful of researchers work on the alignment of machine learning systems. I took the residency for the silence, but the lunches have proved more interesting than the silence. Several times the conversation has turned to a proposal they call humans consulting HCH, and to the related program of factored cognition: the idea that an ordinary thinker, given an idealized helper she may consult and who may in turn consult copies of herself, can decompose an unwieldy question into tractable sub-questions, settle those, and compose the answers into a judgment about matters no single unaided mind could hold all at once. They speak of this as extending understanding rather than diluting it.

One afternoon a visiting fellow — who published a novel before he published papers, and who I suspect enjoys the symmetry — dismissed the entire program in six words: "A camel is a horse designed by committee." The table laughed, the subject changed, and I have been carrying the line around ever since, because the same sentence and its near cousins are used against me constantly in television, and because I believe it is doing several distinct jobs at once, only some of which were available to him.

What I should like is a dissection, not a defense. Please lay out, as a numbered menu ordered roughly from the platitudinous to the radical, the different precise claims that proverb could be making. My own guesses — which you should correct, refine, and supplement, since I expect I have misdescribed several of them — are that it might mean that joint output converges on the median preference of the participants; that dividing a task loses something at the interfaces between the pieces; that a composition of locally optimized parts does not inherit any globally coherent aim; that the camel's ugliness is a failure of elegance rather than of function; or that the damage is done by bargaining among parties with divergent interests, quite apart from the division of labor itself. For each reading, tell me whether it is trivial, substantive and defensible, or simply false; what mechanism it posits; and what would count as evidence for or against it.

Then tell me which of these readings, if any, would actually license the inference he wanted — that a recursively decomposed epistemic process cannot preserve, let alone extend, understanding — bearing in mind that in HCH the sub-consultations are not negotiations among strangers with competing interests, but one inquirer conversing with an idealized extension of herself. Say explicitly where that disanalogy does the work and where it does not.

Finally, identify which reading the table most likely had in mind, and whether any of them condemns my writers' room rather than merely describing it.

Please write in American English.
