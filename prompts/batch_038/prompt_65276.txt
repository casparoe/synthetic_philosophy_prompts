ok so I've been trying to work out what Lewis's triviality results actually license and I want a sanity check before I build more on it. My understanding: since no proposition of the form A→C can have P(A→C) = P(C|A) except in degenerate cases, indicatives just don't express propositions at all — no truth conditions, and strictly speaking it's a mistake to say you have a credence in one. Counterfactuals are different, they get perfectly respectable truth conditions out of similarity between possible worlds.

But then something won't square for me: if indicatives are non-truth-conditional and counterfactuals are truth-conditional, they can't differ in content in any normal sense, so is the indicative/counterfactual distinction basically just tense and mood marking sitting on top of the same underlying claim? Say a lab batch got contaminated and we've since established the tech mislabeled the vials. One person says "if the tech didn't mislabel the vials, the contamination came from the water supply", another says "if the tech hadn't mislabelled the vials, the contamination would have come from the water supply" — are those really different claims, or one claim with a subjunctive on it? And relatedly, if there's no truth or falsity in the indicative case, how does modus ponens through it come out as valid inference rather than a useful heuristic?
