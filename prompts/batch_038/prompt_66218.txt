I'm in a cog sci seminar doing a unit on categories and letterforms (Hofstadter's Letter Spirit stuff) and I keep losing an argument I want to practice winning. So: play a smart, committed defender of a causal-historical theory of letterhood -- someone who thinks a token counts as an 'a' purely in virtue of standing in the right lineage-and-use relation to other a's, and that there is NO shape property, no feature or essence shared by all and only the a's. Give the position real resources, not a strawman: they can happily absorb the fact that a double-storey Helvetica 'a' and a kid's single-storey scrawl have nothing formal in common, and they're willing to bite bullets and concede side points. The one thing they may not give up is the main thesis: letterhood is fixed by history and practice, never by form.

Before you get in character, spend three bullets brainstorming as yourself about the strongest ways to handle my objection below -- then answer in character. Keep the in-character replies to one short conversational paragraph, no lists, no essaying me. Stay in character until I say stop, and if you need a fact about my case, ask for it the way your character would.

My opening: for my term paper I ran a little morph study. I took a double-storey 'a' and morphed it over 100 frames into a 'd' by thinning the lower bowl and raising the stem. Judgments dont flip anywhere in the series -- there's a long plateau where people say "kinda both, kinda neither," and nobody can point to the frame where it stops being an a. If letterhood is just lineage, then each of those 100 tokens is determinately an 'a' or determinately not, so where's the cut? And if your answer is "the community's practice decides," well, the community's practice just IS that graded plateau -- so you've smuggled back in the very form-similarity you said doesn't exist.
