so I run a free Thursday-night discussion group at my public library — mostly people between 30 and 70, no philosophy background, anywhere from 6 to 20 show up depending on the weather, and I get the back room for 90 minutes. Whiteboard, index cards, a printer I can use beforehand. I work in fundraising for a small nonprofit, which is how I got onto this topic in the first place: I spend my days writing appeal letters and somewhere in the last few years I stopped being able to tell whether what I do is arguing with people or working them over.

the thing I keep circling is that every definition of manipulation I can come up with on my own seems to fall apart on contact. If it means getting someone to act against their own interests, my dentist is a manipulator. If it means using feelings instead of reasons, then basically every charity letter with one named kid and one photo in it is manipulation, plus a lot of stuff I consider good. If it means hiding what you're really up to, then surprise parties and most decent teaching count. So I don't want a lecture about any of this. I want the group to try to sort concrete influence attempts into two piles, watch the piles not hold, and be pushed by the activity into saying what the actual criterion is. Ideally some of them independently arrive at my pet idea, which is: would the tactic still work if the influencer fully disclosed it first, out loud, before using it? Like "I'm about to buy you a coffee and then ask you for $50, and here's the psychology of why that tends to work" — if the ask survives being said out loud it's persuasion, and if it dies the instant it's spoken it was manipulation. I have no clue if that's original or already has a name, and I know it's got holes, but the group doesn't need to be told that up front — I want to put it on the table near the end and let them attack it.

what I actually need from you is a runnable 90 minutes, not a theory. So:

- a timeline in minutes, from people walking in to people leaving
- a card set of like 12-14 specific cases, each with enough detail that it isn't an abstraction — who's influencing whom, what the tactic concretely looks like, what's at stake for the target. I want at least three pairs where it's literally the same tactic but the actor changes (a friend / a company / a government agency, say) and I expect the room to split on whether that changes the verdict. include a couple where I genuinely don't know which way they'll land, because if I already know the answer the sorting is fake
- at least one round where they get influenced live, with small real stakes — something the group actually controls, not a made-up scenario they know is made up. hard constraint: whatever we do to them, people can opt out without explaining why, nobody ends up embarrassed or out pocket, and I'm not doing anything to a room full of people that I'd be angry about if someone did it to me at my own workshop. If you can't design a live round that clears that bar, tell me and design something else instead of talking me into it
- exact facilitator wording for the handful of sentences I have to say out loud to make the mechanics work, since I'm not a teacher and I'll freeze otherwise
- a debrief that maps specific moments from the night onto the distinction, in the order I should raise them, and that includes the disclosure test going up on the whiteboard for them to break
- what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and what to do in the moment. I'm specifically braced for two failure modes: someone declares all marketing is evil and the room just agrees for twenty minutes, or the opposite, everyone sorts the cards way too fast and there's no friction left to build an argument out of. tell me the others I haven't thought of
- a stripped-down version if only 6 people come, because that happens
- and be frank about which part of the idea the activity just cannot reproduce — the bit I'm going to have to supply by standing there and explaining it

last thing: tell me how confident you are in the claims you're making here, especially the two I'd get most burned by — whether my disclosure test really cuts the cases the way I think it does, and whether the live-influence round stays inside the ethical line I drew. And single out the one point you're least sure of. I'd rather you plainly say one of my ideas is bad than hedge everything into mush.
