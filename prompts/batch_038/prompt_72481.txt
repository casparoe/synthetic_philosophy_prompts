I am convening the closing session of a second-year interdisciplinary seminar on evidence, testimony and the boundaries of legitimate inquiry, and I want to build it around one well-documented episode rather than around abstractions. The episode is the career of Anatoly Fomenko's "New Chronology" in Russia, from his first interest in the subject in the early 1970s to its mass-market success after the collapse of the Soviet Union. Since you will not be able to look anything up, let me set out the record as I have it, and let us simply work from that. Fomenko is a highly regarded Moscow mathematician, working in topology and dynamical systems, with full academic standing; his interest was piqued by the writings of Nikolai Morozov (1854–1946), a revolutionary and self-taught polymath who had argued from prison that ancient history was far shorter than scholars supposed. Around 1980 Fomenko and colleagues published articles on "new mathematical methods in history" in peer-reviewed Soviet journals; they provoked controversy, drew a hostile review in a mainstream historical journal objecting that the constructions had nothing in common with accepted historiography, and won no historians over. By the early 1990s Fomenko had stopped trying to persuade specialists through journals and turned to books, in long-running collaboration with Gleb Nosovsky; the material eventually appeared in English under the title *History: Fiction or Science?*. The thesis holds that the conventional chronology was assembled by Scaliger and Petavius in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries out of duplicated and misdated sources; that real written history begins around 800 AD, with a near-blank two centuries thereafter; that most recorded events actually fall between 1000 and 1500; and that the classical worlds of Greece, Rome and Egypt are medieval phenomena projected backwards. Later popularisations add a global empire centred on Tartaria before 1600, and a conspiracy of falsification involving the Vatican, the Holy Roman Empire and the Romanovs. The methods are statistical: measures of similarity between the word-frequencies with which chronicles mention successive years, "dynastic parallelisms" that treat shifted repetitions in king-lists as the same rulers recorded twice, and the rejection of radiocarbon and dendrochronological dating as calibrated by the very written chronology they are meant to test independently. The chess champion Garry Kasparov promoted the work publicly in the 1990s, and one scholar has estimated that something approaching a third of Russians may be sympathetic to it.

What I want from you is a disciplined case analysis of this episode through the lens of the demarcation problem, running in both directions. First, set out the criteria you will actually use — not a shopping list, but the handful that you think bear on this case — and I would expect them to include Popper's falsifiability, the treatment of failed predictions through ad hoc auxiliary hypotheses, the "conspiracy of suppression" move in which contrary evidence is reinterpreted as evidence of the cover-up, the over-generation worry about a method that finds its signature pattern in any sufficiently large corpus, and Lakatos's distinction between progressive and degenerating problem-shifts. Then apply them to the episode, being precise about the mapping: who the relevant actors are (Fomenko, Nosovsky, the journal reviewers, the historians who declined to engage, the post-Soviet reading public, celebrity endorsers), what the options were at each juncture, and what counted as evidence for each party. Here I want you to confront the awkward point head-on rather than skate past it: Fomenko's central claims look eminently testable, and they have been tested — astronomical datings of eclipses and planetary configurations recorded in chronicles, radiocarbon sequences, tree-ring chronologies, numismatic and ceramic sequences, the stratigraphy of excavated cities, the manuscript traditions of the texts he calls late forgeries — and they have failed those tests. If a programme makes risky predictions that come out wrong, falsifiability alone plainly does not demarcate it, and I would like to know what does the work instead, and whether the difference between Fomenko's programme and ordinary science lies in its claims or in its response to their failure. Please also address the 1980s juncture specifically: was the peer-reviewed phase of the project pseudohistorical by your lights, or did it become so only when its author abandoned the corrective institutions and addressed a general public instead — and if the latter, is that an epistemic criterion or a sociological one?

Second, and just as important, use two control cases to test your criteria, because I suspect the seminar will otherwise collapse into "Fomenko is obviously wrong". The first is the Jesuit Jean Hardouin, who in 1685 published an edition of Pliny the Elder's *Natural History* arguing that most Greek and Roman texts had been forged by Benedictine monks, and who, when pressed for the monks' motives, said he would reveal them in a letter to be published after his death — a letter his executors could not find among his papers. The second is Newton, whose posthumously published *The Chronology of Ancient Kingdoms Amended* redated the Argonautic expedition, the Trojan War and the founding of Rome by arguments drawn from poetry and astronomy, and whose shortened Greek chronology is closer to modern estimates than the traditional one he attacked. Newton is nobody's pseudoscientist, Hardouin is nobody's scientist, and all three men share the premise that the received timeline is a construction. Tell me which of your criteria separate them, and whether any criterion does so without already presupposing the verdict. Finally, close by asking what the episode shows about demarcation itself: whether the "pseudohistory" label did genuine epistemic work in the Russian case or mainly social work, whether historians' refusal to engage was vindicated or merely convenient, and what a criterion set has to say to a public that finds the revision attractive for reasons — institutional collapse, distrust of official narratives, the prestige of mathematics — that no demarcation criterion addresses at all. I would like this as a continuous argument of roughly fifteen hundred to two thousand words with a few clear section headings, and please write it in British English. Do not append a bibliography or a list of further reading; the analysis is what I am after.
