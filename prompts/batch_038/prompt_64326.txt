I am putting together a two-hour seminar for a migration-ethics course that mixes philosophy students with American-studies students, and I have decided to anchor it on *The Grapes of Wrath* rather than on yet another case drawn from the Mediterranean or the Darién Gap — mostly because the Joads are the one displaced population the standard category cannot reach. They are citizens of the state they are entering, so they cannot be Convention refugees and nobody has ever proposed resettling them; and yet the novel is a sustained account of a people driven from their homes by drought and foreclosure, lured west by recruitment handbills printed in numbers wildly exceeding the jobs on offer, and then met at the state line by police — the 1936 LAPD blockade of the approaches from Arizona, Nevada, and Oregon is real, and my students should know it — and, inside California, by vagrancy arrests, deputized growers, burned camps, and the standing accusation of agitation. I want a reading of the novel through the contemporary ethics of refugee admission that takes both sides seriously: the question of who counts as a refugee when the criteria are legal but the displacement is not, and the question of who must take people in when the taking-in has, formally, already happened.

Here is the apparatus I want you to work with, and please push back on my framing wherever it does not fit. First, distinguish formal admission from membership. California legally cannot and does not exclude the Joads; what it withholds is work at a living wage, housing, sanitation, and the standing to appear as a claimant rather than a trespasser. I have been calling this admission without membership, and I want to know whether that distinction does real analytical work in the novel or whether I am imposing it: does Steinbeck actually separate the two, and is the federal camp at Weedpatch — elected self-government, hot water, the Saturday dance, the planted troublemaker defused by the committee, the deputy turned away at the gate — his picture of what membership would look like, or only a palliative that leaves the structure untouched? Second, run the allocation question. On a Miller-style connection account, the duty falls first on whoever created the displacement and then on those with special ties and spare capacity; on a Carens-style account, freedom of movement is the baseline and the burden of justification runs the other way; on a fair-shares or quota account, the issue is how a collective obligation is divided among many possible hosts. Tell me where each lands on the novel's events, and in particular whether the handbills — the deliberate over-production of a labour surplus — make California the connected party in exactly the way the connection principle wants, or whether Steinbeck's picture of the bank and the growers as a faceless collective that no one can locate destroys the principle's capacity to assign duties at all. Third, take up Parekh's argument that the design of a reception regime inflicts distinct moral harms even when it formally admits people: limbo, no right to work, encampment, being made to feel an intruder. Which of those does the novel document, and does it document any that her list misses?

Then the harder part, where I expect you to tell me I am wrong. The reading has two failure modes. One is that Steinbeck's sympathy is purchased with resemblance: the novel asks its readers to recognize the Okies as people owed refuge precisely because they are white, American, and churchgoing, while the dispossession of the Mexican owners of that same farmland a generation earlier is folded into a history of California agriculture rather than treated as a live injustice with living claimants, and the work the Okies were recruited into had long been done by Mexican, Filipino, and Black labourers the novel barely registers. Does the novel thereby endorse a likeness criterion for who counts — the very thing admission ethics exists to reject — and if so, is that a limitation to be diagnosed or a rhetorical strategy to be respected? The second failure mode is the ending. Rose of Sharon's act in the barn is conventionally read as solidarity, but under this lens it looks like the collapse of a political obligation into a private, bodily gift, given at the exact moment every institution has failed: the baby is dead, the camp is flooded, the state has done nothing. Is Steinbeck offering that as the moral of the story, or indicting a world in which it is all that remains? For the deliverable, roughly twelve to fifteen hundred words of criticism: no bibliography, no reading list, and no verbatim quotation — my students will have the book and you may not, and I would much rather you gesture at scenes descriptively and flag whatever you are unsure of than invent a detail or a line. Where you think the philosophical lens simply fails to grip the novel, say so plainly; the seminar is better served by knowing exactly where the analogy stops.
