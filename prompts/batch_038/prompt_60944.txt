I'm a third-year PPE student, and this term I'm in a seminar on the formal foundations of democratic theory, co-taught by a political theorist and a statistician. We've spent three weeks on the jury theorem and on the various things people have tried to build on top of it, and for the final session each of us has to bring in an argument of our own construction that we think the rest of the class will struggle to rebut. My seminar partner and I built the one below, aiming it at the proposal that political authority should be distributed according to demonstrated competence. The difficulty is that it now appears to prove considerably more than we set out to prove, and neither of us can locate the excess.

Here is the chain, with the stipulations we agreed to grant marked as such. (1) For any binary question with a determinate right answer, if each of n voters independently has probability p > 1/2 of choosing correctly, then the probability that the majority chooses correctly exceeds p, and rises monotonically toward 1 as n rises. We wrote this for odd n to avoid ties; tell me if that restriction is doing anything. (2) An epistocracy is a regime that restricts the franchise to a subset of citizens selected for higher competence on the questions the regime decides. (3) Stipulate that on the sorts of questions legislatures actually vote on, the ordinary enfranchisable citizen has competence p_c with 1/2 < p_c < p_e, where p_e is the competence of those an epistocracy would select, and that errors are independent within each regime. (4) By (1), at any fixed p above 1/2, majority reliability is strictly increasing in n; so a regime's reliability can always be improved by adding voters, however slight their edge over a coin flip. (5) Therefore, for any epistocratic body of size m with competence p_e, there is some n such that an electorate of n ordinary citizens deciding by majority vote is more likely to reach the right answer than that body is. (6) Actual electorates are orders of magnitude larger than any plausible epistocratic body — a senate, a supreme court, a panel of accredited specialists — so the n that (5) demands is not merely attainable but already realized in every large democracy. (7) Hence on every question to which the theorem applies, unrestricted democracy is more likely to be right than any competence-restricted regime, and selecting for expertise does not merely fail to add reliability but subtracts it, by removing voters from the count. (8) So if the tendency of an arrangement to yield correct decisions is the only truth-related reason for distributing authority one way rather than another, there is no truth-related reason to prefer epistocracy, and the epistemic case against democracy is defeated without consulting any evidence about how well informed voters actually are.

My unease attaches mainly to (7) and (8) rather than to the machinery. Taken together they seem to imply that civic education is a poor use of public money compared with simply enlarging the electorate, that the size of a decision-making body is the only design variable worth attending to, that appellate courts and epidemiological advisory panels should be replaced by mass votes, and that a hundred million people each barely better than chance outrank a genuine panel of experts. When we tried it out on friends, two objections came up and neither seemed to land. One said that the independence stipulated in (3) is precisely what fails in a mass electorate, since everyone is exposed to the same news; my partner replied that correlated error cuts both ways, because members of an expert body were trained at the same institutions and read the same journals, so if the objection has force it has force against both regimes at once. Another said that voters in large elections stop voting sincerely once they think their vote might be pivotal; my partner replied that each voter's chance of being pivotal in a large electorate is negligible, so nothing about the aggregation changes. I am not persuaded that either reply works, but I cannot tell whether the real trouble is a false premise, an unstated one, or a step that holds only under a reading the earlier steps have not earned. For what it is worth, I am most attached to (1) and (4), which look like nothing more than the theorem restated, and least attached to (8), which I now suspect was rhetorical from the start.

Could you work through the chain and give me a diagnosis? I want you to identify the step or steps you would reject, explain why each one looked entirely unobjectionable when we wrote it down, and price the alternatives — including, if you think one of the stipulations in (3) has to be surrendered, saying what the argument loses when it goes and whether anything can be salvaged in its place. Then tell me what would have to be added or assumed for an argument of this shape to succeed: is there a defensible version of it, and if so, what empirical work has that version now taken on? Finally, and this matters because I will be presenting the outcome in class, please keep separate what is settled — the behaviour of the theorem under its own assumptions, for example — from what is genuinely disputed among people who work on this seriously, and do not report a contested position to me as though it were the consensus. If you think a step is defective and competent people disagree with you, say plainly that you are taking a side and why. You do not need to cite anything; I will track down the sources myself.
