**Who Bears the Ambiguity? Lying, Misleading, and the Allocation of Interpretive Risk**

**1. The distinction as it is usually drawn.** The line between lying and misleading is standardly located in the speaker's belief. To lie is to assert what one believes to be false, intending one's audience to believe it. To mislead is to bring one's audience to a false belief by asserting what one believes to be true, and letting the false belief be generated by what the assertion invites the hearer to infer. Much of the subsequent debate concerns which side of that line is more culpable, and the dominant answer is that lying is worse — or at least that misleading enjoys a presumption of permissibility that lying does not.

**2. Why the standard explanation of the asymmetry fails.** The explanation usually offered is agential. The liar hands the hearer a false premise and so corrupts the hearer's reasoning at its input; the misleader hands the hearer something true, and the hearer's own inference — an exercise of the very capacity the liar bypasses — does the rest. I do not think this works, for two reasons. First, it cannot accommodate the distribution of our judgments. We treat some literally true speech as morally equivalent to lying: the ministerial statement drafted to be true only on a reading no reasonable hearer would supply, the witness's technically accurate answer, the seller asked whether the house has problems who reports that the roof was replaced in 2019. And we treat other literally true speech as unobjectionable: the negotiator who lets an opponent's optimistic reading of her last offer stand. If the agential story were correct, these should fall on the same side of the line, since in both the hearer's inference does the work. Second, the story makes the wrongness of misleading depend entirely on a feature of the hearer's cognitive activity rather than on anything the speaker did, which has the odd consequence that a speaker's conduct becomes more permissible the more gullible her audience is disposed to be.

**3. The proposal.** The morally relevant variable is not the speaker's belief-state but the allocation of interpretive risk: which party, given the norms governing this kind of exchange, is responsible for eliminating ambiguity about what is being conveyed. I will call the operative allocation the interpretive default of a context. Two defaults are worth distinguishing. Under what I will call a buyer-beware default, the hearer is expected to be on guard and to ask, and the speaker owes no clarification beyond the literal content of what she asserts. Under a drafter's default, the speaker — having unilateral control over how the exchange is framed — is expected to eliminate any ambiguity she can foresee the hearer falling into. The lying/misleading distinction then reappears as a special case: lying is what happens when a speaker violates the default by asserting what she believes false, and wrongful misleading is what happens when she violates it by designing an exchange in which a true assertion foreseeably misleads.

**4. Three things worth borrowing from contract law.** (a) Ambiguity in a contract term is construed against the party who supplied the words. The rationale is not that the drafter is a worse person but that the drafter is the cheaper cost-avoider: she could have written it clearly, and assigning her the risk creates the incentive to do so. (b) A statement that is literally true but that the speaker knows will convey a false impression in context is treated as an actionable misrepresentation; the law refuses to make literal truth a safe harbour for someone who designed the exchange. (c) Raffles v Wichelhaus, the 1864 English case in which cotton was sold to arrive by a ship called the Peerless from Bombay, there being two ships of that name on that route, one sailing in October and one in December, with the buyer meaning one and the seller the other. The court held there was no contract at all, because there was no shared meaning to enforce. This is the limiting case for my account: when neither party's reading is privileged, the exchange simply fails, and there is no default to fall back on. At present my paper has nothing to say about such cases, and I suspect that is a real gap rather than a marginal one.

**5. What the proposal predicts.** (i) The permissibility of a given misleading utterance should vary with the default rather than with the speaker's belief: the same sentence may be unobjectionable in arm's-length bargaining and impermissible from an insurer to a policyholder, or from a specialist to a layperson. (ii) The casuists' doctrine of wide mental reservation — where the qualification comes from the ambiguity of the words themselves or from the circumstances of time, place, and person, as opposed to strict reservation, where the speaker silently adds a qualification in his own mind — is not a piece of theological special pleading but an early attempt at the same analysis. Their operative test was whether the questioner had a right to the truth, which is a claim about entitlement rather than about mental states. (iii) Paltering, which the negotiation literature defines as the active use of truthful statements to convey a misleading impression and treats as distinct both from lying by commission and from lying by omission, should be condemned not because it is furtive but because it is characteristically performed by whoever is designing the informational structure of the exchange.

**6. Two objections I can see coming.** (1) Circularity. If the interpretive default is itself fixed by our moral verdicts about which deceptions are permissible, then the default explains nothing and merely redescribes the data. I currently have only a gesture at an independent ground: asymmetry of control over the terms of the exchange, together with the hearer's reasonable expectations. (2) Smuggling. Contract doctrine responds to efficiency and to the economics of information; morality may not, and borrowing the doctrine may import premises I have not argued for. I would add a third worry of my own: on this account the literal content of an assertion seems to do no distinctive work, which raises the question whether lying has quietly vanished from the taxonomy.
