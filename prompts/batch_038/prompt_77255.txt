I teach a small eight-week graduate reading group on the later Wittgenstein and on Kripke's reconstruction of the rule-following argument, and the group is deliberately mixed: three philosophers of language, a linguist, a cognitive scientist who suspects the whole debate is obsolete, and a lawyer who keeps trying to turn it into a question about legal interpretation. In week five the same three people will announce that they are dispositionalists, quietists, or platonists, usually on the strength of a label they have picked up second-hand. What I want is an instrument to run in week two, before any of the labels have been handed out, that sorts the participants by what they actually say about cases. The deliverable is therefore a diagnostic decision tree with a matching scoring key: five or six nodes, each a concrete case or claim with two or three answer options, arranged so that a respondent who has read only Kripke's book can work through it in about ten minutes without ever encountering the vocabulary of the positions it sorts them into. No node may use terms like "skeptical solution," "dispositionalism," "quietism," or "normativity of meaning"; those words should appear only in the leaf list at the end.

I want the branching order to do real work, and I have some constraints on it. The first split should not be about what constitutes meaning but about the diagnosis of the skeptic's move itself — whether the argument gets off the ground at all, or whether it presupposes a picture of what a meaning-fact would have to look like that nobody is obliged to accept. Later nodes must include: one on whether the force of the argument comes from the finitude of the speaker's past record or from something stronger, namely that even an ideally complete record of uses would fail to single out the addition function rather than a quus-style gerrymander that agrees with it on every case below fifty-seven and answers five to sixty-eight plus fifty-seven; one on whether the correctness of a new application is made true by our best judgments about it or holds independently of them; one on whether the worry is peculiar to meaning or generalizes to other normative practices, which you should test with non-arithmetic material — an ostensively taught colour word, a legal standard like reasonable care, a performance practice in which the correct rendering of a passage is fixed by nothing but the continuing judgments of practitioners; and one on the lone speaker, whether a single individual isolated from any community could so much as be wrong. Please also include a node probing the intuition that if the considerations generalize they should generalize to the skeptic's own vocabulary, so that the skeptic cannot state the conclusion in contentful terms.

For the leaves I want nine to twelve, each with a name if one is standard and "unnamed" if not, one sentence stating the meaning-constituting fact it posits or denies, and one sentence naming the single most awkward commitment it inherits. The familiar occupants should all be there — the straight naturalist answer built out of dispositions with idealization spelled out non-circularly, Kripke's own skeptical solution with its assertability conditions and its dependence on the community, the therapeutic dissolution on which the paradox rests on a mistaken picture rather than a discovery, the realist response on which a rule and its applications are not two items needing to be glued together, the view that locates content in normative statuses within a social practice of giving and asking for reasons, and the deflationary view on which a word's meaning is fixed by a basic acceptance property rather than by any reductive analysis. Alongside those, please include three occupants that surveys of this debate usually leave out, and tell me frankly if you think any of them is merely a redescription of one of the familiar leaves: a global error theory on which nothing ever means anything and ordinary meaning-talk survives only as a useful fiction; a community-relative view on which both the ordinary reading and the deviant reading are correct relative to different communal standards, so the skeptic and the speaker never actually contradict each other; and a view that denies meaning attributions are descriptive at all, treating them as expressive or practical moves rather than reports. Then flag every pair of leaves the tree cannot genuinely separate — I expect at least the skeptical solution and the therapeutic dissolution, and the two views that refuse to offer a constitution story — and say in each case whether the failure is because the positions coincide or because my diagnostic cases are too coarse to distinguish them. Finally, list the answer combinations that yield no occupant and diagnose each: is the cell empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is stable but unattractive, or because nobody has simply explored it yet?

Please organize the response under exactly these headings, in this order: "Design rationale" (about 120 words, explaining why you chose that branching order); "The nodes" (about 500 words, each node stated as a case followed by its answer options, in the order the respondent meets them); "The tree" (about 300 words, an indented flow from node answers to leaves); "The leaves" (about 450 words, one entry per leaf in the format above); "What this instrument cannot separate" (about 200 words); "Empty cells" (about 200 words); and "Scoring key" (a compact table of roughly 120 words mapping answer strings onto leaves). I am not after background exposition or a reading list — names of positions and people where they are standard are welcome, but please spend the space on the instrument itself.
