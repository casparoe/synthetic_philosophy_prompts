I convene a third-year undergraduate module on classical Indian epistemology and I am setting the unseen, closed-book final paper. I need one essay question, worth 25 marks, which candidates will have 45 minutes to answer, on the dispute between the Nyāya school and the Buddhist epistemologists over whether testimony is an independent source of knowledge. My students have done a full term on the pramāṇa frameworks and can write out the five-membered inference schema, but they have encountered neither of the two cases I want the question to turn on, so please do not build in any assumption of prior exposure to them.

The question must be constructed around two cases, and both must be stated in full in the question itself:

(1) A myna bird kept for years in a water-diviner's house has learned to repeat the sentence its owner used to say on finding a good spot: dig beneath the tamarind and you will strike water. The diviner is now dead. In a drought year, villagers act on the bird's squawk, dig, and find water. The bird has no beliefs and no intention to inform anyone; its utterance has simply proved a dependable indicator. Add the further detail that the villagers have never verified the bird's claims for themselves — their confidence in it rests on assurances given to them by the diviner, who was honest and skilful but is no longer available to be asked.

(2) A four-year-old is told by a stranger that the water in the far well is safe to drink. The stranger is in fact a careful and truthful hydrologist who has just tested it. The child has no way of assessing the stranger's competence, no history of corroborated instances, and no understanding of what testing a well involves; she simply believes him and drinks.

The question should then require candidates to do three things: explain, for each case, why it is put forward as a difficulty for the opposing account rather than for one's own; set out, in the five-membered format, the inference by which the reductionist says knowledge arises in one of the cases, and identify which member of that inference the Nyāya defender will attack; and finally adjudicate between the two accounts, considering explicitly whether a patched reduction — one that widens inference to cover unreflective, sub-personal dispositions of the sort the child exercises — is a genuine reduction of testimony or a change of subject that leaves the original dispute untouched.

Print the doctrines the question depends on as stipulations, so that nothing turns on recall: that the Nyāya school recognises four independent sources of knowledge — perception, inference, comparison and testimony — and takes testimony to be the statement of a trustworthy person; that the Buddhist epistemologists recognise only perception and inference and treat testimony as a species of inference from a sign; and that the five members of the Nyāya inference are, in order, thesis, reason, example (carrying the pervasion), application and conclusion. Gloss every Sanskrit term on its first appearance, and list the five members with their English labels, since candidates are working from memory under time pressure.

The question must not be answerable by listing the four sources and defining them, or by saying that Nyāya treats testimony as irreducible because its founder listed it separately. It should force candidates to say where each account locates the epistemic work — in the competence of the source or in the reasons available to the hearer — and to notice that the two cases pull in opposite directions, so that the pair does not by itself settle the dispute unless a further premise about the correct locus is supplied.

Please deliver: (a) the question exactly as it will be printed, including its preamble and stipulations; (b) a model answer of about 750 words; (c) a marking scheme out of 25 that allocates marks to named moves — with the mark value of each — and gives, for each move, one line identifying the mistake that most often costs candidates the mark, including at least the errors of reversing which case embarrasses which account and of writing the reductionist's inference with the conclusion that the speaker is trustworthy rather than with the reported state of affairs as its conclusion; and (d) a short paragraph stating what separates a first-class answer from a solid upper-second on this question.

Because the paper is closed book and unseen, include no bibliography, no citations, no sūtra numbers and no references to secondary literature, and do not require or reward candidates for naming individual authors. Do not attribute to either school any doctrine beyond what the printed stipulations supply. Write throughout in British English.

Begin your response with the exam question itself, as it would appear on the paper, with no preface, no account of your design choices and no restatement of this request, and follow it directly with the model answer, the marking scheme and the note on the first/upper-second boundary.
