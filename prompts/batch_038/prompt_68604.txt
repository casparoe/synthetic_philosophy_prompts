I'm in week three of a nuclear strategy seminar (undergrad poli sci, lots of Schelling, everyone says "credibility" like it's a substance) and I built this argument for my seminar paper. Now I think it proves way too much and I can't find the exit.

First, a definition I made up because I needed a name for the kind of threat I was after. Call a threat **self-undermining** if, in every world the threat could actually lead to — the world where the opponent backs down, and the world where they call you — executing it would leave you worse of than not executing, judged at the moment of execution, with all the information you'd have at that moment. So it's not a claim about whether making the threat was smart. It's a claim about what you'd want once you're standing at the fork.

The argument:

1. A threat deters a rational opponent only if their credence that you'll execute is high enough that the expected cost of the challenged action beats its expected gain.
2. A rational opponent's credence that you'll execute can't exceed their credence that executing would be choice-worthy for you at the fork, because at the fork you do what's choice-worthy at the fork.
3. Retaliatory strikes on cities are self-undermining. Once the first strike has landed, execution recovers nothing that was lost, deters nothing that hasn't already happened, kills millions more, and risks another round on top. And my definition covers both branches: if they backed down there's nothing to execute, and if they didn't, the harm is already sunk.
4. So a rational opponent should put credence near zero on your retaliating — and by (1), a threat you'll be credited near-zero on deters nothing, no matter how apocalyptic the punishment is. Zero times a catastrophe is still zero.
5. But the superpowers spent four decades and fortunes on precisely this: survivable second-strike forces, hardening, dispersal, boats on patrol. And no nuclear war happened between them. If (4) is right, all that spending was aimed at manufacturing a credibility that rationality makes unavailable, and the peace needs some other explanation — mutual terror, or neither side being sure the other side was sane.
6. And it generalizes, which is the part that worries me. My landlord's threat to keep the whole deposit over a scuffed floorboard is self-undermining (chasing me through small claims costs him more than the scuff). A TA's threat to report a paper that's three days late is self-undermining. A pledged overwhelming conventional response to a cyberattack on a hospital network is self-undermining. By (1)–(4) none of those deter a rational person. All of them obviously deter people, me included.
7. Therefore one of (1)–(3) is false. I want to keep (3) — I really don't want my paper's conclusion to be that vaporizing cities in revenge is rational, that seems like the single worst thing to walk out of this course holding. And (1) looks like arithmetic. So I get pushed to (2), but if (2) goes I have no account left of what makes any threat credible, which was the whole point of the thing.

Which step do I actually reject, and what does each exit cost? Like: if (2) goes, what's left of credibility talk — am I reduced to saying a threat works only when the opponent is confused? If (3) goes, what am I now committed to about retaliation, and does it have to be that retaliation is *good*, or just not-dominated in some wider sense? And if the fix is in my definition instead — I keep going back and forth on whether the quantifier over worlds in "self-undermining" is doing something sneaky — does tightening it make the definition stop applying to the nuclear case, or only to my landlord? Because a repair that saves the landlord case and loses the nuclear case would be kind of hilarious but not much use to me.
