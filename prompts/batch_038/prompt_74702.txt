You're a senior archaeologist who also does philosophy-of-science consulting for museums — the person who gets called when an argument about interpretation turns into an argument about evidence. Please think carefully about this one; it's subtle and a hedged answer is worse than no answer to me.

I sit on the interpretation committee of a regional museum. We're redoing the Neolithic gallery, the labels go to the printer in five weeks, and one display case has eaten three months of meetings. Draft the memo I'll circulate before Thursday.

The case (site anonymised on purpose — you don't need to know it, everything relevant is below, and please don't go inventing comparanda or a bibliography):

Object class: 47 small fired-clay anthropomorphic figurines, 6–11 cm, from a sixth-millennium BCE tell. All from one site; a single comparable example is known from a site 40 km away, in a domestic context.

Find contexts:
- 31 in the midden. Break surfaces fresh and unweathered. Break location recorded: 24 of the 31 snapped cleanly at the neck or waist, the other 7 irregularly shattered. Six refit pairs, each with both fragments within about 40 cm of each other in the same midden layer.
- 9 in situ on the plastered floor of a structure that burned down. Their bases carry a polish consistent with repeated handling. The incised chevron motif on the figurines' torsos also appears on a fragment of painted wall plaster from the same room.
- 7 in a small pit with red ochre. Complete, no usewear at all.
- Fourteen burials excavated at the site; no figurines in any of them. No building at the site has been identified as a shrine or temple.

Interpretive history: the 1960s excavation report called them cult objects and mother-goddess figures. Our curator of collections wants them in the Daily Life gallery as household stuff, possibly toys.

The complication. We have a co-curation agreement with a community council whose approval is required for any text about them. In the 1930s, the same expedition that excavated the site recorded a statement from community members describing a practice then current among them: small figures made each autumn, kept in the house through winter, deliberately broken and thrown onto the ash heap when the season ended. The field notebook also records the excavator's disappointment that the account did not support his goddess theory — he had hoped for confirmation. The informants had been shown figurines from the excavation in the storeroom.

The council's position: their account is knowledge, not belief, and the case belongs in Belief and Ceremony with that account stated plainly.

My committee is split three ways:
- Our field archaeologist says a 1930s account is evidence about a twentieth-century community and nothing more; using it as evidence about 6000 BCE is exactly the analogical leap the discipline spend thirty years trying to stop.
- Our community liaison says that's a double standard: nobody blinks when historians use much later written sources as evidence about earlier periods, and the only difference here is that the source is oral and the people who hold it are still around and can be offended.
- The director wants a decision and notes that the original excavator's family sits on the board and paid for the gallery through a bequest.

Our draft label policy — mostly written by me, now regretted — says: any interpretive claim on a label has to be a cable rather than a chain, i.e. several strands each carrying some weight on its own instead of one link per inferential step (that image isn't mine, it comes from the philosophy-of-archaeological-evidence literature); the words ritual, belief, cult and goddess may not appear unqualified; 90 words maximum; and the label must say which alternative reading the evidence actually rules out.

What I need from you, roughly 1000–1300 words:

Lay out the candidate readings of these objects and say what each one predicts about the specific data above — and in particular whether the three find-context groups are one phenomenon or three. If it helps you can sort candidates loosely along technomic / sociotechnic / ideotechnic lines (Binford's old split, more or less), but don't make me read a history of it and don't turn this section into processual-versus-post-processual throat-clearing. Method vocabulary earns its place only where it changes a decision.

2. Then a clear recommendation: which gallery, what happens to the physical case (one grouping or several?), whether and in what capacity the community's account enters the interpretive text, and a draft of the actual 90-word label. Write the label, don't describe it.

I specifically want the independence question handled head-on: is the 1930s account independent of the excavation whose finds it's being used to interpret, and does the answer change depending on which direction any contamination would have pushed it? Note what the notebook entry about the excavator's disappointment does to that calculation.

Flag any assumption you're making rather than smuggling it in. No citations needed — I'll add references myself. And commit: a memo that ends with all readings remaining equally plausible is actively harmful to us on Thursday.
