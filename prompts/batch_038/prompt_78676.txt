I am a philosophy PhD student working on the epistemology of markets, trying to establish what I actually believe about when trade is rational and when prices carry information. My verdicts:

(1) Two risk-neutral traders with a common prior over an asset's value, each privately informed: no speculative trade between them is rational. Any bet one would take, the other's readiness to offer is itself enough to make him refuse. Firm.

(2) Same setup, but their priors genuinely differ. Now trade is rational, and the fact of the trade tells neither party anything about who is right. Firm.

(3) A stranger offers me even odds on a local match; my own model gives the bet positive expected value. I decline, and declining is rational, because his willingness to offer is itself evidence. Firm — the case I trust most.

(4) A competitive market maker posts a bid and an ask to a flow of traders, some informed, some trading for reasons unrelated to their beliefs about value. He expects to lose to the first group and gain from the second, and the spread compensates. Rational, and the market healthy. Firm.

(5) Exactly (4), with the second group removed: everyone at the counter is either informed or knows she is not. Now I think his willingness to trade is unsustainable. Firm.

(6) Two colleagues with a common prior who know each other's credences on a policy question and still disagree. Their disagreement is fully rational, because neither has anything like common knowledge of the other's evidence. Firm, though I realize this looks like merely denying a theorem's hypothesis.

(7) A price that revealed everything the informed know would leave no one a reason to become informed. So informational efficiency must always be strictly incomplete — a substantive constraint on markets, not a curiosity about a limiting case. Firm.

The pair that troubles me is (3) against (4) and (5). If a counterparty's willingness to trade is always evidence, the market maker in (4) should treat every order as informed and the spread should swallow the market; yet I insist (4) is normal. And (5) says the whole difference is made by traders whose trades have nothing to do with their beliefs. That renders rational informed trade parasitic on non-informational trade, and I cannot tell whether this is a theorem, an artifact of my setup, or a sign that one of my verdicts is wrong.

My tentative principle, which I call the "announcement test": an offer to trade — or a bet, or an argument — is rationally acceptable only if it would remain acceptable to me after both parties publicly announced that they were willing to make it. It seems to handle (1), (3), and (6), and to explain why the market maker must quote a spread rather than his prior mean, but I cannot see what it says about (5) or (7).
