So I've been handed a 90-minute seminar slot in a second-year rational choice module, topic "money pumps and diachronic coherence," with essentially no guidance from the lecturer. The students have had exactly one lecture on expected utility and they know what a static Dutch book is — they've seen the thing where you offer someone a package of bets at once and the package guarantees a loss given thier quoted degrees of belief. What I actually have to get across is why the sequential version is a different and harder animal: the trades come one at a time, the agent can decline, and whether they get pumped at all depends on what they expect to be offered next and on what they're planning to do when it arrives.

Plan the ninety minutes for me. I want the plan, not the explanation — I know roughly how the argument goes, what I can't see is the order.

- Give me the dependency structure: what has to be in place before what, and why. My hunch is that "sure loss" versus "no free lunch" needs pinning down before any talk of foresight makes sense, but I genuinely don't know whether myopic choice and sophisticated choice should come before or after the pump that sophisticated choice seems to block. Argue the placement, don't just list it.
- One carrying example for the whole thing. I have a candidate: a fruit stall with three baskets, the agent trades A for B, B for C, C for A, each swap costing a small handling fee. Tell me what that example has to have and what it must not have — does the fee need to be small relative to the preference gaps? Does the agent know the whole sequence in advance, and what does the example become if they do? Is there a better single case, and if so what makes it better?
- The misconception map. My guesses: "the pump just shows these people are dumb, nobody has cyclic preferences in real life," "sure loss only counts if they'd take the whole package at once," "sophisticated choice blocks it so there's no problem left," and "therefore you must maximise expected utility, done." Tell me which of these are real, at what point in the sequence each one gets headed off, and whether one of them should be exploited rather than defused — I suspect the third one earns its keep for a while.
- Where transaction costs fit. There's a version of the objection that a pump only works because switching isn't free, and I don't know if that's a good objection, a bad one, or a good one that gets answered two minutes later.
- One closing question that separates students who got the diachronic structure from students who memorised "cycles bad."
- What I should refuse to cover at this level, and say so out loud: the general dynamic Dutch book theorem? the almost-sure-loss results for rank-dependent and other non-expected-utility theories? Ulysses contracts and precommitment? Pick, and tell me what I lose by cutting each.

Last thing: flag where I'm likely to say something wrong in front of them. I have a dim memory of a result to the effect that sophisticated choice stops blocking pumps once you add uncertainty about which trade comes next, or once the agent's own plans aren't credible to themselves, and I can't reconstruct why that's true — tell me if I'm making that up.
