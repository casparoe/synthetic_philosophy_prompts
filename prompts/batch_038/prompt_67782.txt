I'm co-teaching an MSc module this year on quantifying the long run — it's jointly run by the history department and the stats group, so half the room are historians who think dynamical models of the past are reductive, and the other half are data people who think narrative history is untestable, and I want a question that bites for both. For the unseen paper I need one essay question on Turchin's secular cycles and the structural-demographic theory behind them (population pressure, falling real wages, elite overproduction, state fiscal crisis, sociopolitical instability), worth 25 marks, answerable in two hours at about 1,500 words. It absolutely cannot be answerable by walking the examiner through the model's feedback loops, because every student will have that diagram memorised and will happily reproduce it. Students will have read Goldstone's *Revolution and Rebellion in the Early Modern World* and Turchin and Nefedov's *Secular Cycles*, but I don't want the question to depend on knowing the literature — it should stand on its own.

The way I want to do it is hand them a made-up but very concrete empirical result and make them say what it does to the theory. Something along these lines: a team fits a structural-demographic model to a 700-year series for a single agrarian polity and reports three instability peaks, spaced 180, 210 and 265 years apart; elite numbers grew faster than total output across the entire stretch, including the long calm centuries and not just in the run-up to the peaks; real wages rose rather than fell in the two decades before each peak; and the state's fiscal position only deteriorated *after* the first recorded elite mobilisations rather than before them. Please write the actual question wording around that — tighten the numbers or add a line of framing if that makes it fairer — and build it so that students have to get onto the points that really hurt: whether a predictor that is switched on all the time can discriminate peaks from troughs at all; whether jettisoning the immiseration leg while keeping elite overproduction is a legitimate refinement or an ad hoc rescue; whether a sequence with periods ranging from 180 to 265 years deserves to be called a cycle, and what a cycle claim would have to be tested against; whether the instability index is partly constitutive of the very events it is supposed to forecast; and what a theory this tightly bundled has to risk, given how few independent secular cycles the world record actually contains. I'd like the question to signal that a straight "it's unfalsifiable, so it's worthless" answer and a straight "the model is vindicated, wages are a detail" answer both lose marks, without saying why.

Then give me a model answer of roughly 700–800 words that actually takes a position rather than listing considerations — the sort of thing I'd hand back as the standard — with no citations and no suggestions for further reading, since it's closed book and I dont want a bibliography dump in it. After that, the markscheme out of 25 as exactly five numbered criteria, each one or two sentences long, saying what the specific move is, what it's worth, and the usual way it gets botched — no preamble and no closing summary, just the five numbered points. The question wording itself should sound like an official exam paper, but the model answer and the criteria can be plainer. British English throughout, please.
