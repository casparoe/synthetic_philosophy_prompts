I am a software engineer with no formal training in philosophy, classics, or historiography. I have been reading the closing General Observations of chapter 38 of Gibbon's History of the Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, the late eighteenth-century retrospective on why the western half ended, and I have got stuck on two of its paragraphs. I quote them below, with one omission marked. I understand the shape of Gibbon's project well enough; what I need is help reading these particular sentences, because I keep translating them into systems vocabulary and cannot tell whether the translation is faithful or is smuggling in ideas Gibbon does not have.

"The rise of a city, which swelled into an Empire, may deserve, as a singular prodigy, the reflection of a philosophic mind. But the decline of Rome was the natural and inevitable effect of immoderate greatness. Prosperity ripened the principle of decay; the causes of destruction multiplied with the extent of conquest; and, as soon as time or accident had removed the artificial supports, the stupendous fabric yielded to the pressure of its own weight. The story of its ruin is simple and obvious; and, instead of inquiring why the Roman empire was destroyed, we should rather be surprised that it had subsisted so long. The victorious legions, who, in distant wars, acquired the vices of strangers and mercenaries, first oppressed the freedom of the republic, and afterwards violated the majesty of the purple. The emperors, anxious for their personal safety and the public peace, were reduced to the base expedient of corrupting the discipline which rendered them alike formidable to their sovereign and to the enemy; the vigour of the military government was relaxed, and finally dissolved, by the partial institutions of Constantine; and the Roman world was overwhelmed by a deluge of Barbarians.

The decay of Rome has been frequently ascribed to the translation of the seat of empire; but this history has already shewn that the powers of government were divided rather than removed. [...] Yet the salutary event approved in some measure the judgment of Constantine. During a long period of decay, his impregnable city repelled the victorious armies of Barbarians, protected the wealth of Asia, and commanded, both in peace and war, the important straits which connect the Euxine and Mediterranean seas. The foundation of Constantinople more essentially contributed to the preservation of the East than to the ruin of the West."

My first question concerns the failure mode. My instinct is that "immoderate greatness" states a scaling claim: that imperial size multiplied independent failure modes faster than the empire's capacity to manage them, so that collapse was accumulated load exceeding capacity. But that is not quite what the sentences say. They say the causes of destruction multiplied with the extent of conquest, and then that the fabric yielded to its own weight only "as soon as time or accident had removed the artificial supports." That is not overload; that is loss of redundancy and deferred maintenance, where the load was always present and something compensating for it was taken away. Which picture does Gibbon hold, and does the architectural metaphor commit him to one? Related: when he says the causes of destruction multiplied, is he claiming there came to be more independent causes, or that existing causes became interdependent, so that a fault in one province propagated to others? Those are very different structures and the sentence does not seem to settle it.

Then there are words I suspect I am reading too modernly. Is "immoderate" chiefly about measure and self-restraint rather than magnitude, and does "greatness" carry size and excellence at once, with Gibbon wanting both senses in play? What is "the principle of decay" — a cause, a seed, a standing disposition? I noticed that earlier in the same essay he writes that honour as well as virtue was "the principle of the republic," and if principle means the same thing in both places, then "Prosperity ripened the principle of decay" is stranger than "prosperity led to decline": it says prosperity cultivated an internal animating cause of ruin that then acted on its own. Is that the right reading? What are "the artificial supports" concretely — walls and fortified frontiers, client kingdoms and buffer states, the legions' discipline, the balance of the constitution, or the civic loyalty described in the opening paragraphs? Is "time or accident" a hendiadys, or two genuinely different kinds of cause? And in the next sentence, what does "the military government" denote — the army as an organisation, or rule by the army as a political regime — and what does Gibbon mean by "the partial institutions of Constantine," where I cannot tell whether partial means biased in favour of some units, factional, or merely incomplete? Finally, the legions are said to have "first oppressed the freedom of the republic, and afterwards violated the majesty of the purple": what distinguishes those two stages, and is the second the point at which the army began making and unmaking emperors?

I also want to know what this passage is doing as an argument, since I am not sure it is one. Gibbon asserts that the decline was "natural and inevitable," and then says that instead of inquiring why the empire was destroyed we should be surprised it subsisted so long. That looks like a claim that ruin is the default and survival is what requires explanation, which would turn the book's own question around. Yet a few lines later he calls the emperors' expedient "base" and blames particular men — the hostile favourites of Arcadius and Honorius — for betraying the republic, which is the language of avoidable choices by accountable agents. Are inevitability and baseness compatible here, and what does Gibbon give up by keeping both? And if ruin really is the expected outcome, does the passage owe us an account of what was holding the structure up, as the mirror image of the preceding paragraphs in which he credits Polybius with explaining the rise through institutions? Does he supply one, or does he quietly change the subject from institutions to events?

Two further puzzles. The barbarians: the metaphor implies the fabric would have come down under its own weight once the supports went, yet the paragraph ends with the Roman world "overwhelmed by a deluge of Barbarians," as though an external agent delivered the collapse. Is the deluge part of the necessary sequence, one of the accidents that removed the supports, or merely the occasion of a failure already under way? And Christianity: this summary of causes never mentions it, although the paragraph immediately following turns to religion and to Christianity's share in the decline, and that is the thesis Gibbon is best known for. Is this passage a rival list of causes, a supplement to it, or the same account at a different level of description? Does the "natural and inevitable effect of immoderate greatness" render the religious explanation redundant, or does Gibbon treat them as operating on different timescales?

Last, I would like your view on whether the passage undercuts the idea that Rome fell at all. Gibbon insists the powers of government were divided rather than removed, and closes by saying that the foundation of Constantinople contributed more to the preservation of the East than to the ruin of the West. Taken together, does his framing here lend any support to the view that what happened in the West was a transformation and a transfer of institutions to successor rulers rather than a collapse, or does his language of ruin, deluge, and a fabric crushed by its own weight exclude that reading by construction? In short: is my maintenance-versus-overload distinction something I have found in Gibbon, or something I have brought to him?
