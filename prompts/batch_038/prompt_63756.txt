Six weeks ago I took a seat on the animal- and bioethics committee of a research institute, and the first two items on our agenda have turned out to be the same question in different clothing. One is a proposal to extend our welfare oversight — housing, nociception testing, humane endpoints — to cephalopods and decapod crustaceans, with a junior group asking whether the same protections should reach the insects used in our feed trials. The other is a human cerebral-organoid project: a dish containing some millions of human neurons, with no body, no sensory input, and no behavior of any kind. In both discussions one member keeps insisting that what we are really weighing is how much nervous tissue is on the table, and that everything else is sentiment. I do not think he is obviously wrong, and I do not think he is obviously right, and I have grown tired of the matter being argued one anecdote at a time. There is a further complication I should disclose: my sister was born with a congenital condition that left her brain very much smaller than typical. She is not someone I can describe as having less standing, and I want to know precisely what it would cost a theory to say so.

Before our next meeting I want the specification rather than the arguments. Adequacy is relative to purpose, so here is ours: a theory good enough to let a lay committee decide, under uncertainty and within a year, which taxa receive protection and whether the organoid work requires oversight at all.

Write as a philosopher who works on moral status and comparative cognition and who is genuinely at home in the neuroanatomy, not as a summarizer of the popular literature. Think carefully, and before you commit to anything, spend a few paragraphs brainstorming the candidate quantities that "brain size" could even denote — absolute mass, mass relative to body size, total neuron number, neuron number in some particular division, packing density, connectivity, capacity for information integration, something further — because I suspect much of the requirement list falls out of the differences among these.

Then give the desiderata: what any adequate theory of the moral relevance of brain size has to deliver. Each requirement should come with the concrete case or consideration that motivates it, and with an explicit statement of whether it binds every theory or is instead a preference of one camp. The cases I have in mind include, but are not limited to, the ones that embarrass us: absolute brain mass ranking whales and elephants above us; relative-size indices shifting their verdicts with the choice of baseline; birds performing flexibly with dense brains the size of a walnut; cephalopods distributing much of their neural machinery into their arms; the sheer numbers of small-brained organisms, which multiply any nonzero per-individual weight into something enormous; organoids, which have the human tissue and none of the rest. Include among your requirements the distinctions a theory may not collapse — between a property's being relevant in itself and its being evidence of something that is, between having standing and having how much, between a capacity and its exercise, between an individual's status and what aggregation does with it.

Two further things I want stated plainly. First, which single requirement does the most to eliminate contenders. My hunch is the demand that a theory reconcile a graded neural criterion with the equal standing we extend to every human being regardless of the size of their head, but argue the point rather than accommodate me. Second, flag any subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied: if a theory must be threshold-free because nature is a continuum and every cutoff looks arbitrary, and must also be binary because our regulations are, say so without softening it. I would rather discover that the list is itself an impossibility result than be handed a tidy set of criteria no view could ever meet.

Close with a paragraph each — no more — on how the main contenders do against the list: a sentience-based view that treats neural measures as evidence, a tiered capacity or personhood view, a view assigning graded status directly to some neural quantity, and the precautionary expected-value approach my committee is most likely to adopt by default. Score them; do not summarize them. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I will supply those myself. Where you rely on an empirical finding, state it qualitatively rather than with figures, and if you are not confident about a particular species or a particular number, mark your uncertainty and move on. I would far rather have a clean list of requirements than a confident-sounding one built on facts you are reconstructing.
