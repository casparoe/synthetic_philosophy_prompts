So I got into an argument with two roommates about price controls this weekend and it ended with me realizing I don't actually have consistent views, which is annoying. Going case by case, here's what I think. After a hurricane, capping the price of bottled water, plywood and generator rentals at pre-storm levels is right, and people who jack those up deserve to be fined. Same with insulin or an epipen — if the government caps what a company can charge for a drug people will die without, that isn't theft, that's the least it can do. Raising the minimum wage to $15 in my city: good, even though I've read the arguments that it costs some jobs at the margin. A living-wage clause for companies that contract with the city: good. Banning ticket brokers from using bots to hoard concert tickets and flip them: good. But an ordinary person who buys one ticket at face value and resells it for three times that on a resale site: totally fine, I have zero complaint about them. Uber surge pricing when it starts pouring and everyone wants a ride: fine. A plumber charging triple his normal rate to pump out a flooded basement during that same storm: not fine. Rent caps protecting an existing tenant whose landlord is trying to squeeze them out in a tight market: sympathetic. Citywide rent control that also applies to new construction: I think bad, and I can't fully articulate why. And farm price floors — the old setup where you guarantee producers a high price, get a surplus, and the government ends up buying or destroying the extra — I think those are just obviously dumb.

The obvious problem is that a minimum wage is a price floor and I'm for it, farm price floors are price floors and I'm against them, water caps and rent caps are price ceilings and I'm mostly for them, and scalping bans are sort of ceilings and I'm for one version and against the other. I've heard the econ-y moves people make here: elastic versus inelastic demand, monopsony power in a thin local labor market, search-and-matching frictions, the fact that a ceiling doesn't remove the shortage so much as re-ration it through waiting lists and queues and quality and side payments, the way a cap breeds a black market with worse terms for the buyer. I suspect my gut is secretly tracking some of that. What I want is the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that best fits these verdicts — stated precisely, with each clause tied to the case that motivated it. And I specifically want you to answer this: is the thing that rescues my minimum-wage-versus-farm-support split actually a moral distinction, or is it an empirical claim about elasticity and market power wearing a moral costume? Because if it's the latter, then what I have isn't a view about justice at all, it's a bet on the economics, and it'll flip the moment somebody shows me a local labor market that's competitive. Tell me whether I should be bothered by that or just own it.

Then run whatever principle you land on against cases I haven't mentioned: a legal cap on how much a hospital can pay a nurse during a staffing shortage; a rent-stabilized apartment that gets allocated by lottery in a city I'm thinking of moving to; a price floor under sex work; a ban on selling kidneys combined with a cap on what a hospital may charge for the transplant itself; capping the resale price of a game console that's impossible to find; a rule that lets a landlord pass through the cost of renovations in the rent. And be honest about which of my verdicts you have to overturn — I'd much rather have a simple principle that forces me to give up the plumber one, or the resale-ticket one, than some baroque thing with six clauses that saves everything and explains nothing. The case I keep going back and forth on is my own apartment, fwiw: my landlord is raising my rent 22% after doing a kitchen remodel I didn't ask for and definately didn't want, and I genuinely can't tell whether that's gouging or just... the market. Please write the whole thing in American English.
