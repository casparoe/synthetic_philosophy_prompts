I'm drafting a chapter for a collection on the philosophy of sleep and dreaming, and I want to settle my own view before I commit anything to paper. The chapter's question is the old one — are dreams experiences? — but I want it approached through the two most radical negative answers, which I suspect are much less similar to each other than people assume. On Malcolm's side (his 1956 paper on dreaming and skepticism, and the book that grew out of it), the claim is roughly conceptual: a dream report is not a report of an experience, there is no such thing as how long a dream took, and what makes "I dreamt that p" true is the impression one has on waking rather than any episode that transpired during the night. On Dennett's side (his 1976 paper of exactly that title, later reworked in *Sweet Dreams*), the claim is that the composition of a dream may be temporally independent of its recall — the so-called cassette hypothesis — so that dream content might become conscious for the first time when it is remembered, and so that a theory of consciousness had better not presuppose that dreams are experiences. Please write an essay that weighs these two negative theses against the affirmative picture, including the more recent literature on immersive experience and on the imagination model of dreaming, and that ends with a defended verdict rather than a survey. A conditional verdict is fine — for instance, that dreams are experiences in one sense but not in the sense that the skeptical argument requires — but it has to be a verdict, and it has to say which of the three positions I should sign up to and why.

Two things I want the essay to get its teeth into. First, do Malcolm and Dennett actually disagree with each other, or only with the folk? Malcolm presents his thesis as something no conceivable neurophysiological finding could overturn, since on his account the incoherence lies in the concept of a dream-experience; Dennett presents his as turning on what the brain might turn out to do, and he seems happy to concede that if the science showed content-specific activity throughout REM sleep the cassette hypothesis would lose its interest. If one thesis is a priori and the other is an empirical bet that happens to point the same way, then the apparent alliance between them is illusory, and I want the essay to say which of the two is doing the more serious work and which is the more defensible once the alliance is dissolved. Second, here is an objection a colleague pressed on me that I have not been able to answer. Suppose the decoding studies get much better than they now are: suppose that within a few years we can read out, in real time and at the resolution of a few seconds, the coarse category of a sleeping subject's imagery — faces, bodies, indoor scenes, movement — and suppose the readout tracks moment-to-moment shifts across a REM period, including across REM periods from which the subject wakes reporting no dream at all. It is tempting to say this refutes the cassette hypothesis outright, since content-specific neural states are present contemporaneously with the sleep stage rather than being manufactured at the instant of waking. But my colleague's point is that this cannot be the end of it: the same data are consistent with a subject having no experience at all while those states unfold, exactly as a blindsight subject's discriminations are consistent with there being nothing it is like for him to discriminate. So the inference from decodable content to experienced content already presupposes an answer to the question at issue. If that is right, the empirical result is either question-begging or it is doing something subtler than refutation, and I want the essay to say which. Relatedly, consider whether the underdetermination argument cuts both ways: if the impossibility of telling whether an experience occurred is enough to license Dennett's deflationism about dreams, does it not also license deflationism about the duration and content of ordinary waking episodes, which nobody wants to accept? Either the argument needs substantive premises about memory and consolidation — in which case it is no longer a conceptual insight — or it is inert.

Aim for roughly two thousand words, written for readers who have encountered both papers but have not thought hard about how they fit together. Do not pad it with a history of dream skepticism from Descartes onward, and do not end with a both-sides shrug or with the observation that more research is needed; if you think the empirical question is unresolved, say what the philosophical verdict should be anyway and why it is not held hostage to the unresolved part. I also want a short section near the end on consequences: if the essay concludes that dreams are not experiences, does the skeptical argument from dreaming actually lose its force, or does it survive in some weakened form — and if the conclusion turns out to have no consequences for skepticism at all, does that count against it? Finally, you do not have access to my library and I am not asking for a bibliography, a reference list, or any citations; paraphrase both authors rather than quoting them, and if you are unsure about a detail of either argument, flag the uncertainty in the text instead of inventing a supporting passage.
