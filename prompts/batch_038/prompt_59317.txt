You are a philosopher of perception and cognitive science who has spent twenty years presenting conceptual arguments to empirical audiences — vision scientists, developmental psychologists, neuroscientists — and who is unusually good at predicting, in their own idiom, exactly how a room full of experimenters will attack a philosopher's thesis. Think carefully before drafting, and begin with a short bout of brainstorming that I want to see: identify the three or four background commitments that this particular room shares (about what counts as an explanation, about the relation between tasks and capacities, about thought experiments) and work out which of those commitments generates the most objections to my argument. Then build the prep sheet around that diagnosis.

**The occasion.** In five weeks I give a forty-minute colloquium talk, with twenty minutes of questions, in a psychology department whose perception group is entirely empirical: psychophysicists, two people who work with patients whose congenital cataracts were removed in adolescence or adulthood, a computational modeler of sensorimotor learning, a developmentalist who works with preverbal infants, and one multisensory-integration neuroscientist. There are no other philosophers in the building. My host, who is sympathetic, has already told the group that I am coming to argue that their data settle a three-hundred-year-old question and that I am "probably wrong about how." The room will be curious, well-disposed, and impatient with vocabulary.

**My thesis.** Molyneux's problem — the puzzle about the man born blind who learns by touch to tell a cube from a sphere and is then made to see — is asked of a capacity that does not exist. "Transferring from touch to sight" decomposes into at least four separable competences: (i) matching a seen object to a simultaneously felt one at the same scale and orientation; (ii) matching a seen object to a *remembered* felt one across changes of scale, orientation, and viewpoint; (iii) sorting seen objects into kinds learned only by touch, with no felt exemplar present; and (iv) visually guiding the hand toward an object whose properties are known only by touch — transfer in the service of action rather than of identification. These come apart: they have different developmental timetables, they place different demands on acuity, memory, and coordinate transformation, and they can be gained or lost independently. My claim is that the affirmative answers in the literature are mostly answers to (i) or (iv), the negative answers mostly answers to (ii) or (iii), and that a large part of the historical dispute is an artifact of which competence a writer had in mind.

I then press further, and this is the part I most want the room to attack. I deny that the result is merely verbal or merely deflationary, because there is a residual question that is not indexed to any task: which of these competences, if any, is *constitutive* of possessing the concept of a shape. My answer is "none of them singly" — concept possession is graded and multi-competence, so the correct response to Molyneux is neither yes nor no, but that the question presupposes concept possession has a binary shape, and it does not.

**The deliverable.** A Q&A prep sheet, not a defense of the thesis. Please use these sections, in this order, with roughly these lengths:

1. **The Room** (~150 words). What this audience already treats as settled, what they will regard as a waste of their time, and which words of mine will trigger hostility on contact.
2. **Ranked Objections** (~1,500–1,900 words). Eight or nine objections, ordered by how much damage each would do to my talk if I answered it badly — not by the order in which they will arrive. For each, give me five labelled parts: **Source** (who in the room raises it — the modeler, the developmentalist, the cataract-researcher, the operationalist psychophysician, and so on); **The question as they will actually put it**, in their register, with the mild condescension an empiricist allows a visiting philosopher; **Damage** (score it 1–5 and say in one sentence why); **My reply** (about ninety seconds of speech, in my voice, no hedging filler); and **Where the reply is soft** (the follow-up that breaks it, stated plainly).
3. **Concessions Worth Buying** (~200 words). Three or four things I should grant in the first ten minutes that cost the thesis nothing and purchase credibility with this specific room.
4. **The One I Hope Nobody Asks** (~250 words). The objection for which I have no good answer at a podium, why it goes deeper than everything in section 2, and the honest holding action if it comes.
5. **Fallback Restatement** (~120 words). A compressed version of my thesis that survives every objection in section 2 — what I retreat to if the talk goes badly, stated so that it is still worth having said.

And do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, or any citations — I cannot use them, and I will assume anything you cite is confabulated.
