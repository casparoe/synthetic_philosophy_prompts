Six months into a novel about a family that owns a boatyard on one of the Great Lakes — shrinking town, one working traffic light, everybody knows whose dad drank — and I've realized I've been settling my characters' fates with a moral rule I can't actually state out loud. Which is annoying, because the two reviewers who were nicest about my last book were clearly philosophy people and I'd rather not get caught being sloppy about the thing the whole book turns on. So I want to do this properly: I'll give you my gut verdicts on a bunch of cases and you tell me what principle, if any, they add up to.

The bit I half-remember from reading on a delayed flight is a distinction between the luck that just happens to you and the luck you signed up for — option luck vs. brute luck, I think it's Dworkin's, but I wouldn't swear to it. That roughly matches me. Here's my running list, all from the same book so the cases stay comparable.

Ray turns the boatyard into a marina-restaurant on a leveraged loan after everyone in town tells him it'll fail. He understood exactly what he was risking and did it anyway. When it goes under: nothing is owed him, I feel fine about that. His sister Denise makes the same bet, same stakes, same information, same town telling her no — but she does it because eleven years of nursing their mother left her unable to picture one more year of it. When she goes under, I think something *is* owed her. Those two are the pair I can't reconcile: identical choice, identical knowledge, and my verdict flips entirely on where the desire came from. Their cousin never bet at all, kept his head down in the office, and got rich because the state rerouted the highway past his land: he keeps it, I don't feel like clawing back anybody's winnings even though that's pure dumb luck in his favor. Their mother, sick, no fault, no choice, her care eating Denise's whole twenties: obviously owed. A guy who smoked for forty years and now needs a transplant, advertised at since he was twelve: same place in the queue as anyone else. A guy who drives drunk into a culvert and needs the same transplant: behind the smoker. That one feels punitive and I can't defend it, but I'm not giving it up either.

Then there's a rule I invented for myself and I want to know if it's a real amendment or just a restatement in nicer clothes. I've been calling it the windshield rule: you can only be charged with a risk you could actually see through. Ray saw he might lose the loan. Nobody in that family saw that losing the loan would also cost their mother the house, because the house sat in a seperate trust that a lawyer had buried in the paperwork and nobody had read since 1994. So my instinct is that the bet counts against him up to the loss he could see, and past that line it's brute luck again. Same shape with Denise: she could see she might fail; she could not see that failing would lose her the custody hearing. And the kid, nineteen, who sells a painting he inherited for a tenth of what it's worth because he genuinely doesn't know what he's holding — I lean toward compensating him, on the grounds that the ignorance is the luck, but I don't trust myself there. One more, which breaks the rule: if Ray gets bailed out and then does the exact same thing again, I want the second round to get him nothing at all, including nothing for the part he couldn't see.

So: what's the principle? State it precisely, clause by clause, and tell me which case is doing the work for each clause. Is the windshield rule a genuine addition to the option/brute stuff or just a redescription of it? Which of my verdicts does your principle force me to drop — I already suspect the cousin and the drunk driver are the soft ones. And give me verdicts on two cases I haven't listed: the sister who stayed and nursed and never took a single risk in her life, and whether a life with no bets in it is owed something for the bets never taken; and someone who gets made whole and then, out of gratitude, passes it on to a stranger — does the compensation cancel the luck, or does it create a fresh claim somewhere? I need the answer to the second one for the last chapter, specifically whether the principle says the highway-lucky cousin owes Denise anything at all.

Finally, and this is the part people skip: tell me how confident you actually are in the principle you land on, and single out the one clause or verdict you're least sure about. I'd rather have a shaky honest answer than a smooth one, since I'm going to build an ending on top of it.
