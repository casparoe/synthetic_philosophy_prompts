I spent thirty-four years in infectious-disease surveillance, most of it working on case definitions, and I retired three years ago with the comfortable assumption that a definition is a measuring instrument: you fix it, you count with it, and the thing you are counting does not care. I have since been auditing philosophy courses, largely out of a sense that I was late to something, and next month the convenor of an evening class at a local further-education college has asked me to lead one session of seventy-five minutes on Ian Hacking's looping effects and the idea of interactive kinds. The room will be about fifteen people: several practising mental-health nurses and a couple of social workers, two people who have been patients in the system at length and have said so in advance, and the rest of us are retirees sampling philosophy for the first time. I have a whiteboard and no projector, nothing has been assigned in advance, and I would rather not hand out or refer to readings, since I know from experience that this group will not have looked at them and I will not be there to follow up.

What I want from you is a plan for the explanation, not the explanation; I can do the talking. I should tell you what I have sketched so far so that you have something to argue with. My draft order is: first, the contrast between a kind that is indifferent to being classified and a kind that is aware of being classified; second, the observation that once people know the classification they can conform to it, resist it, or reinterpret themselves by it; third, that this behaviour feeds back to the clinicians and statisticians, who then revise the classification; fourth, that the revised classification produces a somewhat different sort of person; and fifth, Hacking's suggestion that this is why the human sciences cannot aim at the predictive stability the physical sciences enjoy, the target having moved. Tell me where that sequence is wrong, what genuinely depends on what, and whether step five can survive in a room with no background at all or whether it belongs in the bin. I am also undecided about the case that carries the whole thing. My instinct is the episode of multiple personality in North America in the nineteen-seventies and eighties, because the loop there visibly opened, ran, and closed, and a completed loop is easier to see than a live one. But I can see the argument for a category the nurses in the room are diagnosing this week. Say which choice you would make and why, and say what the example has to contain — I suspect it must show movement in both directions, not merely people being damaged by a label — and what it must conspicuously lack, for instance that it must not be a case on which the room has already reached a verdict.

On misconceptions, my guesses are that the nurses will hear all this as a restatement of the self-fulfilling prophecy, which strikes me as a smaller and tidier claim than Hacking's, and that the rest of the room will hear it as the announcement that psychiatric categories are not real and were invented by people with an interest. There is also a third I dread: that the right conclusion is to stop applying labels, which the nurses cannot do and would be worse at if they could. Tell me which of these is likeliest, add the ones I have not thought of, and place each precisely in the sequence, because I take it that a misconception headed off too early is heard as a straw man and one headed off too late has already hardened. I would also like your advice on the two former patients: how to use lived material without turning either of them into an exhibit or inviting the room to adjudicate their history. Finally, give me one question I can put to the class in the last five minutes whose answers would distinguish those who have grasped the mechanism from those who have merely agreed with me, and tell me explicitly what I should refuse to cover at this level and say out loud that I am refusing it — I find that a declared omission holds a room together far better than a fudge. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence: the one structural decision you would make if you could make only one. Then give me the reasoning behind it, with no preamble and no restatement of my question.
