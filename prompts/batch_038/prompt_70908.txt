**The submitted answer, verbatim**

> A genuine moral dilemma, as the unit defines it, is a situation where an agent is required to do A and required to do B, can do A and can do B, cannot do both, and neither requirement is overridden by the other. The claim that such situations are impossible is usually supported by two principles. The first is agglomeration: if you are required to do A and required to do B, then you are required to do both A and B. The second is that ought implies can: you are only required to do what you are able to do. In a genuine dilemma you are required to do A and required to do B, so by agglomeration you are required to do both; but by definition you cannot do both, so by ought-implies-can you are not required to do both. Contradiction. So one of the premises has to go, and the impossibilist says it is the first one, the claim that both requirements hold.
>
> This is not a modern invention. Kant denied that duties can conflict at all: on his view, when two grounds of obligation pull in different directions, only the stronger one actually obliges, so what looked like a conflict of duties was really only a conflict among the considerations that could have generated them. Consequentialists reach the same result more cheaply, since they define the right act as the one that produces the best outcome. Exactly one act can do that, so the agent may face a hard choice and may regret what she gives up, but she is never required to do two incompatible things.
>
> Ross's account is the one I find most interesting, because it looks like a middle position and is not. Ross says we have many prima facie duties: fidelity, reparation, gratitude, non-maleficence, and so on. A prima facie duty is a duty that looks like a duty from where the agent stands; when the agent learns all the relevant facts, some of those appearances drop away and only the actual duty is a duty at all. So Ross is really on the impossibilist side. Prima facie duties can conflict with each other, but the actual duty, the one that is genuinely binding, cannot conflict with anything.
>
> The best evidence against that picture is what happens afterwards. In the dirty-hands cases discussed by Walzer, and in Williams on agent-regret, an agent who has done what was on balance right still owes an apology, or reparations, or feels a remorse that we judge appropriate rather than excessive. If the losing requirement had simply dropped away, as Ross says, there would be nothing left over to apologize for. The fact that something is left over shows that the agent was, all things considered, required to do both things and did only one of them. That is a genuine dilemma, and it is why the formal argument has to be wrong somewhere.
>
> One point about the formal argument. If we drop agglomeration, we can keep genuine dilemmas, but we lose ought-implies-can at the same time, since the agent is then required to do each of two things she cannot do together. There is no free lunch in deontic logic.
>
> My verdict on part (c) is that the dispute is mostly verbal. Both sides agree about what the agent should do and about which feelings are appropriate afterwards; they disagree about whether to keep the word "required" for one thing or for two. That said, the disagreement has some substance, because the two sides end up committed to different principles of deontic logic, and that changes how we reason about particular cases.
