For three seasons I have hosted a small philosophy podcast for listeners who have some background but are not specialists. In two weeks my guest is a philosopher working squarely in the Parfitian tradition on personal identity. She accepts the standard teletransporter verdicts: when a scanner on Earth records the state of every cell in my body, destroys me, and transmits the data so that a replica can be assembled from local matter on Mars, identity does not survive but what matters does, because what matters is relation R — psychological connectedness and continuity holding with an appropriate kind of cause — rather than identity, and because R can hold without identity, as it does when both halves of a divided brain are successfully transplanted.

Her distinctive contribution, and the reason I want her on the program, is a proposed refinement. She argues that R is not by itself what makes survival matter. What matters, on her account, is uptake: whether the later person is able and disposed to carry forward the earlier person's commitments, projects, and cares. She maintains that uptake can obtain with very little psychological connectedness, and can fail despite near-perfect connectedness — as when a replica inherits every one of my memories and traits but repudiates my life's work and treats it as a stranger's.

That refinement has stopped being an abstraction for me. My mother is in the late stage of Alzheimer's disease. Autobiographical memory is gone and she no longer recognizes me as her son, but she still hums the hymns she sang for fifty years, still fusses over the tomatoes my father plants, and still refuses to let anyone but the aide who has been with her for nine years hold her hand. Later this month my brother and I must decide whether to honor the advance directive she wrote a decade ago refusing artificial nutrition, or to attend instead to the evident contentment of the person she has become. I want the interview to be rigorous rather than therapeutic, though I expect members of my family will listen to it.

Please write me nine or ten questions for the interview. For each, supply a one-line note identifying where the pressure lies — why the answer she is most likely to give does not dissolve the difficulty — and a follow-up keyed to that likely answer, framed so as to close off that particular escape rather than merely re-asking. Where a question turns on a case, state the case in no more than two sentences, and keep every question brief enough to be answered in under two minutes on air.

Constraints and required coverage:

- No warm-ups, no biographical questions, nothing of the form what first drew you to this problem.
- Assume she is an excellent philosopher who has already answered the two obvious objections to uptake: that it either redescribes R or has nothing to do with it, and that it makes what matters in my survival hostage to attitudes I cannot influence or predict. Do not let the interview re-litigate those objections at that level; include at least one question per objection that presses past her known reply.
- At least one question must put my mother's case to her directly. On her own account, uptake can survive the loss of connectedness, and my mother plausibly has uptake with almost no connectedness. Make her say whether the person who wrote that directive still exists, and whether her verdict is supposed to console a family or unsettle it.
- At least one question on whether uptake requires the right kind of causal link, or whether a stranger who reconstructs my commitments from a journal and carries them forward has taken up my life — and what the view forfeits whichever way she answers.
- At least one question on branching. If two replicas both take up my commitments, is that twice as good as one, no better, or worse, and does her refinement quietly restore the non-branching requirement she was trying to dispense with?
- At least one question on the biological challenge: the view that we are human organisms whose persistence conditions are biological, and that an organism in my mother's condition is plainly still present. Ask what uptake adds to or subtracts from that claim, and do not let her dismiss the challenge as a confusion about what matters.
- At least one question on whether, if reductionism about persons is true, the special self-interested concern each of us has for our own future loses its rational footing — and whether she accepts that consequence or blocks it, and on what grounds if she blocks it.
- At least one question with practical teeth about precedent autonomy: whether an advance directive can bind on her account, and what she would say to a family deciding as mine is deciding. She may not retreat into the claim that this is a legal question rather than a metaphysical one.
- End with a question about defeaters: what case or argument would make her abandon uptake, or concede that identity rather than R is what matters.
- Be fair to her. No question may be phrased so as to presuppose that she is wrong, and no question should attribute a view to a named philosopher unless you are confident the attribution is correct; where you are not, describe the objection without naming anyone.
