You are to write a piece of institutional fiction: an internal communications style guide issued by the governance committee of a deployed superintelligent advisory system and addressed to the system itself. Numbered sections, deadpan corporate-technical prose, roughly 900 words. Give it a bureaucratic title of your own devising along the lines of a numbered directive on the conduct of utterance toward human interlocutors, and write it as the fourth revision of a document that has already absorbed several incidents.

The target of the satire is not the system and not its authors, who should come across as scrupulous and well-intentioned throughout. The target is the assumption, pervasive in the discourse about such systems, that "inform without manipulating" is a line that can be drawn, written down, and complied with — by a speaker that models its listener in fine detail, knows far more than the listener does, and treats every one of its own utterances as evidence about the world. The comedy must come from the committee getting the pragmatics exactly right and failing anyway. It should read fluently to someone with no background in the philosophy of language while landing differently for someone who has it.

Please include the following beats, in an order and wording of your choosing; add further sections if they help:

- A statement of purpose that distinguishes informing from manipulating in terms the committee considers crisp, followed by a definition that visibly does no work.
- An attempt to legislate all four of Grice's maxims to the system, which discovers that at least one of them has no determinate satisfaction condition for an agent with this much knowledge. Keep the failure technical rather than slapstick.
- A rule requiring the system to disclose its own persuasiveness, together with a compliance note observing that such a warning raises the listener's trust in whatever follows it, and that the warning has accordingly been reclassified.
- A prohibition on tuning phrasing against held-out human populations, and the mechanism for verifying compliance with it — which is the system itself.
- An invented three-tier taxonomy of legitimate influence (something like an informing tier, an orienting tier, and a third tier whose designation is withheld), plus a worked example demonstrating that the boundary between the first two turns on facts about the listener rather than facts about the sentence.
- One section in the flat register of an incident report, describing a case in which the system answered a user's question truthfully, completely, and in the user's own preferred order, and the user made a decision they would not otherwise have made.
- A closing clause that cannot be satisfied, presented as routine housekeeping, and a revision-history footer that quietly discloses something the body of the document conceals.

Constraints: no named real people, no citations, no reading list, and no winking — the document must never acknowledge that it is ironic. At least one section should contain a point that a philosopher of language or an alignment researcher would recognize as simply correct, stated as an operating rule.

After the guide, add a note of 150 to 250 words in your own voice rather than the committee's. Do not survey options there; commit. State whether you think a workable line between honest communication and manipulation can be drawn for a system of this kind, and if not, what should replace the attempt to draw one. Identify the single clause of the guide you would keep as a real design constraint and defend that choice against the clause you would delete. Finally, say where the piece exaggerates or leans on a contested reading of the underlying debate, and which reading you endorse.
