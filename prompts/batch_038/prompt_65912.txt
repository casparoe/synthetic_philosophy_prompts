I am a first-year graduate student in philosophy, and this term I am sitting in on a formal epistemology seminar in which we spent a few weeks on Dutch-book arguments and on the diachronic requirement that, under normal conditions, your present confidence in a claim should line up with what you now expect your own later confidence in it to be. That requirement struck me as plausible mainly because I assumed the later confidence would rest on everything the present one rests on plus additional evidence. Something came up in section that I have not been able to find anywhere in our readings, and I want to know whether it is a recognized thing.

The pattern is this: someone expects to undergo a change that will produce in them a belief they do not currently hold, and they treat the expectation of that belief as itself a reason to move toward it now, even though they cannot identify any evidence the change will supply that they could not already weigh. Two cases I have actually encountered. A friend of mine, a classicist, is in the middle of converting to a demanding religious tradition; before she had been persuaded of anything she began attending services and praying, explaining that she was confident that in a year she would find the case for the faith compelling, that her future self was a better judge of the matter than she is now, and that when I asked what she expected to learn that she does not already know, she said she expected to learn nothing at all — she expected to become the sort of person who sees it. The second case is a classmate who spent a summer doing fieldwork with a group whose methods he had, on what looked to me like good grounds, judged unsound; he came back having abandoned the objection, and reported candidly that he had not discovered any flaw in his earlier reasoning, but had simply stopped finding it compelling after months of working alongside people who did not. He calls the change evidence-free and yet treats the objection as settled, and expects to go on treating it as settled. In both, the later belief is generated by something other than the accumulation of evidence one can now anticipate — habituation, practice, community, perhaps a change of character — and yet the person treats the anticipated or the realized belief as reason-giving, and often expects others to treat it that way too.

So: is there an established concept for this? I want to know where it sits relative to the diachronic requirement I mentioned, and whether that requirement is supposed to cover such cases, to fail in them, or to be simply silent because of some background condition about how the later belief arose. I also want it distinguished from ordinary deference to a better-informed expert, where the other person's belief is at least a marker of evidence I lack; from deliberately shaping one's own beliefs by non-evidential means in order to reach a decision one already wants; and from the problem of choosing an option whose very experience you cannot now imagine. If nothing in the literature picks out exactly this, say so plainly and then give me a name and a definition that does the carving — what the phenomenon is, what it is not, and where the edges fall. In particular I would like your view on whether the following variants belong inside it or outside: a belief change produced by a drug or by a course of treatment; one produced by therapy that has dissolved an earlier fixation; and one produced by forgetting the reasons that originally supported a view, so that the later self is worse informed rather than differently constituted.
