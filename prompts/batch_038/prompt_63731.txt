I spent six years as counsel to a county executive, and I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about dirty hands in politics by listing the calls I was party to and my gut verdicts on them.

1. During a flood we got a nursing home evacuated by telling residents they would be back within two days. We knew it would be at least two weeks, and without that promise several people would probably have died in place. Verdict: it was wrong, I'd do it again, and I think we still owe those residents something.
2. A rumor was spreading that the water supply was contaminated. I told reporters we had no evidence of contamination — literally true, and it left out a great deal. Verdict: simply fine, no residue whatsoever.
3. The same office leaked a true but selective fact about a councilman's past to kill a ballot measure that would have gutted emergency services. The measure died; the consequences were better than in case 2. Verdict: not fine.
4. I let a deputy sign the order redirecting funds away from a program I knew was working, so that the blame would attach to her rather than me. Same outcome as if I had signed it. Verdict: considerably worse than signing it myself.
5. I put together a file on another councilman intending to use it if he voted against us. He voted with us and I never used it. Verdict: I was already dirty.

My tentative principle is that an act is a dirty-hands case when it is a genuine moral wrong that my public role required of me, and the requirement does not erase the wrong but only my standing to complain about having done it. Test that principle against all five cases and repair or replace it. I want the principle, or a short list of candidates, stated precisely, with the clause each case motivates and an honest accounting of which of my verdicts it cannot deliver — cases 2 and 3 look like the hard pair, but so might 4 and 5 — and tell me whether a simpler principle that overturns one of my verdicts is preferable to a baroque one that saves them all. Then say what the view you land on implies for two cases I have not listed: the official who refuses to do the dirty thing and lets the harm happen, and whether an institution should prefer to hire people who are willing to get their hands dirty. Please indicate how confident you are in the principle you end up endorsing, and single out the claim in your answer you are least sure of.
