I have been reading the rationalist and AI-alignment blogs for the better part of two decades — I arrived for the sequences and stayed for the interminable arguments about decision theory — and I am currently co-authoring a position paper with a colleague who came into machine learning from philosophy of language about eight years ago. He has a habit, in talks and in draft prose alike, of reaching for a single analogy to defuse what he regards as an over-inflated worry about inverse reinforcement learning. The analogy runs as follows: learning a reward function from demonstrations is simply Quine's radical translation, with the reward standing in for meaning. The field linguist observes a speaker's behaviour in the presence of stimuli and assigns meanings, the gavagai business included; the learning system observes a human's choices in the presence of states and assigns rewards. The optimality assumption — treating the demonstrator as approximately rational with respect to the reward one is inferring — plays the part of the principle of charity that Quine and Davidson both make indispensable to interpretation. And the familiar complaint that the reward is not identifiable, that indefinitely many reward functions rationalise the same behavioural record, is nothing more than the inscrutability of reference and the underdetermination of the translation manual, dressed in engineering clothes. From this he draws a comforting conclusion: reward ambiguity is no more alarming than the everyday business of interpreting other people, which we manage tolerably well, and those who treat it as a distinctive obstacle to alignment have forgotten that the problem is sixty-odd years old and survivable. His preferred formulation is that we should worry about it no more than we worry about the fact that nobody can prove his barista means what he takes her to mean.

Before I put my name to any of this I want the analogy audited properly rather than admired. Please lay out the correspondence explicitly — a table is welcome — identifying what plays the field linguist, the native speaker, the stimulus occasion, the overt assent and dissent, the translation manual, the totality of the available evidence, the charity constraint, and the rival manuals that are equally adequate to it. Then give me the disanalogies, but separated into two piles: those that actually bear on his reassuring conclusion, and those that are merely decorative, true of any analogy between a formal inference procedure and a philosophical thought experiment. I would like particular attention paid to the asymmetry in evidence: the linguist is confined to passive observation of behaviour with no independent access to the speaker's beliefs or to the world as the speaker takes it to be, whereas the learning system frequently has a dynamics model, can generate its own situations, and can ask questions — so does the analogy survive at all once active querying is permitted, or does it quietly assume the very passivity that makes Quine's problem so acute?

Beyond the table, I want four things judged. First: charity is compulsory for Quine but optional and tunable for the machine learner, since one can weaken the optimality assumption to noisy rationality, maximum-entropy behaviour, or explicit models of human time-inconsistency and bounded deliberation; tell me whether that flexibility is a difference in degree or whether it dissolves the mapping entirely, and what happens to the inferred reward when the rationality model is misspecified in a way the learner cannot detect. Second: he likes to say that the potential-based shaping equivalence class — the family of reward functions differing by terms that leave every optimal policy untouched — is the direct analogue of the rabbit-versus-rabbit-stage indeterminacy. Assess that identification, given that Quine's rival manuals are also supposed to be empirically equivalent, and say what, if anything, distinguishes the two cases and whether the distinction has any practical weight for a system that will act on the answer rather than merely converse with it. Third: consider whether the analogy smuggles in a picture of preferences as a settled fact about the human which the inference recovers, as opposed to a construction whose shape depends on the learner's representation, its priors and the distribution it was trained on; and whether it also smuggles in the linguist's disinterestedness, since the interpreter here has a stake, will optimise the object it infers, and may be run in circumstances far outside those that generated the demonstrations — including the case where the demonstrator's own world-model differs from the learner's, so that what looks like a reward error is really a belief error. Fourth, and most importantly: tell me plainly whether his conclusion survives once the disanalogies are on the table, or whether the analogy was carrying it from the start — and, if you think the comparison can be repaired into something that argues against him rather than for him, say so and show how. If there is a better analogy for inferring preferences from behaviour, offer one, together with an honest account of where it too breaks down and what it would license.
