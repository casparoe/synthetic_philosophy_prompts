I've been going back and forth on whether the "debate" approach to AI oversight actually does any work, and I want to stress-test it against a real case rather than the toy ones everyone uses. Rough setup, as I understand it: Irving, Christiano and Amodei's 2018 *AI Safety via Debate* paper — two superhuman systems play a zero-sum argument game, a human judge who can't verify the underlying claims directly just picks a winner, and the claim is that under optimal play true statements win because they're cheap to defend across a long game whereas lies need ever more supporting lies. Plus the usual objections: the judge can be rhetorically bulldozed, the judge might not even want the truth, and the honest side may need facts that are expensive to establish while the sophist only needs one cheap-sounding story. And on the other side of the aisle, the schemes that don't use an adversary at all — iterated amplification / IDA, where you repeatedly consult weaker-but-honest helpers and compose their answers.

Case: Challenger, January 1986. Three acts I care about (flag it if you think any of these are wrong, but don't get bogged down):

1. Night before the launch, on a teleconference, Thiokol engineers led by Roger Boisjoly recommended against launching below about 53°F, based on O-ring erosion and blow-by seen on earlier flights; the overnight forecast at the pad was around 18°F. A NASA launch manager responded to the effect of, when do you want me to launch, next April. Thiokol's management then went into a private caucus that excluded the engineers who'd presented the data, and reversed to a signed launch recommendation. The much-repeated line from that caucus is one manager telling the engineering VP to take off his engineering hat and put on his management hat.
2. About two weeks later, at a televised public hearing on Feb 11, Feynman clamped a sample of the O-ring material, dropped it into a glass of ice water in front of the commission, and showed it had lost its resilience.
3. Feynman's own appendix to the commission report, where he opened: "It appears that there are enormous differences of opinion as to the probability of a failure with loss of vehicle and of human life. The estimates range from roughly 1 in 100 to 1 in 100,000. The higher figures come from the working engineers, and the very low figures from management."

So:

- Map it. Who is the judge, who are the debaters, and what is the proposition at issue in each act? Note that the act-1 judge and the act-2 judge aren't the same body and aren't similarly motivated, and tell me what that does to the analysis.
- Which of debate's assumptions fail in act 1 and which in act 2? I specifically want: does the judge want the truth, is information access symmetric, is *standing* symmetric (a contractor's engineer arguing against the customer who signs his paychecks), and how cheap is the true claim to verify.
- Is the ice water the verification asymmetry that debate needs — a true claim a lay judge can check in thirty seconds — or is it something categorially different, i.e. not an argument but a demonstration that bypasses argument? Does that distinction matter for whether debate generalizes past lab-friendly claims?
- Here's what bugs me: in act 1 the truth was known, was stated by the honest side in plain language, and lost anyway; in act 2 the truth won, but through a lone actor with no institutional stake and no adversary at all. Neither looks like optimal adversarial play producing truth. Does that refute the debate program, or does it just show the guarantees are conditional and Challenger sits outside the conditions? If the latter, name the exact condition that broke.
- Act 3 is a two-experts-disagree-by-three-orders-of-magnitude case with judges who can't adjudicate. Would forcing them to argue have helped? Or is management's 1-in-100,000 not a belief state at all but an institutional posture, in which case no amount of debate resolves it because there's nothing there to track?
- Given all that, pick between debate and iterated amplification as the scheme this episode actually supports, and say what institutional feature would have to change for the other one to work here.

And be honest about where the mapping is just anachronistic — these are bureaucracies, not optimal game-tree players, and I know the ML version has guarantees about a game that a teleconference doesn't have. Tell me which parts of the analogy you'd throw out rather than stretching it.
