At a family dinner last month my sister and her husband argued for the better part of an hour about their four-year-old's speech, and I have been turning it over ever since. I have no background in linguistics or in machine learning, so I would like you to referee the exchange as it actually happened, move by move, rather than give me a general lecture on how children learn language.

The trigger was that the child had begun saying "goed" and "breaked," after months of saying "went" and "broke" correctly. My sister said this was a sign the child had abstracted a rule and was now applying it where it did not belong; she had read that children pass through a stage like this and come out the other side. Her husband, who builds and trains models for a living, said it was nothing of the kind: this is exactly what any statistical learner does once it has seen many regular forms and a handful of irregular ones, and you get the same "goed" out of a network that contains no rules whatsoever. So, he said, she had it backwards — the error was evidence for pattern-matching, not against it.

She replied that this could not be the whole story, because the child has never heard anyone say "goed"; nobody in that house talks that way. Producing something no one has ever produced in your hearing is what you would expect from a rule, and not what you would expect from a tally of what has been heard. He answered that novelty proves nothing at all: his models produce sentences that no one ever typed into them, and he would be the last person to claim those sentences were stored somewhere in advance.

Then she shifted ground. She said the real issue is not novelty but correction: nobody in a household disciplines a child's sentence structure. Parents correct lies, correct names for things, correct facts about the world, but they do not sit a child down and say which word orders are disallowed. So the child is never told how to choose among the enormous number of rule-systems compatible with what she has heard, and must be bringing something to the task from the start. He said this assumed the conclusion. If a child's own malformed utterances drew no reaction, she would never learn anything, which is absurd; the information is not in the corrections but in the input, because the child hears millions of well-formed sentences and effectively never hears a malformed one, and that distribution is itself evidence. She said that tells you only which sentences are frequent, never which ones are impossible.

At that point he conceded part of her ground, in a way I am not sure I followed correctly. He said he was happy to grant that any learner arriving at the child's competence must be tilted toward some systems and away from others, because the data does not pick one out by itself. But, he said, that tilt lives in the architecture and the training procedure — his models have one too, and nobody calls it knowledge. So on his account the disagreement was never about whether there is an innate contribution; it was about whether a tilt counts as innate knowledge, and if you say it does, then everything he builds has it, and the argument stops being about children. She said that was word games: a tilt toward the patterns a species actually needs is a different kind of thing from a tilt that falls out of however you happened to wire up a program, and collapsing the two is what let him escape her argument. Neither budged after that.

Here is what I would like. Take the five moves in order — (1) his opening claim that the error is just what statistics predicts, (2) her novelty reply, (3) his response that his own models are novel too, (4) her no-correction argument together with his reply that the input itself carries the information, and (5) the final exchange about whether an inductive bias is the same thing as innate knowledge — and for each one say who is right, and how much of the point survives contact. Be willing to split a move: I suspect several of them are half-right in ways that matter.

Begin your answer with a single sentence stating your overall verdict — which of the two comes out better, and on what — and then give the move-by-move assessment. No preamble and no restatement of my question. And where both of them are missing something, say so plainly, including anything about whether this is even the kind of dispute that could be settled by watching a four-year-old. I would rather be told that the argument is a stalemate for a specific reason than handed a winner I have no way of checking.
