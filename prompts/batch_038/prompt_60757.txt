I'm a systems/safety engineer at a company that builds semi-autonomous machines for commercial greenhouses — a robotic arm on a rail that weighs pots, waters them, shuttles trays between benches, that kind of thing. I write the behavior specs that our firmware team and an outside ML vendor both implement against. Lately we've had a run of incidents that all have the same shape: the machine did something nobody intended, and when we went back to the document, the document hadn't said anything wrong, it just hadn't said anything at all about the situation. Slot 3 was occupied so the arm waited forever. A sensor got replaced with a slightly differnt model and a rule that assumed a particular reading meant "door open" went sideways. A learned policy that was fine for six weeks started dropping trays after we changed the potting mix.

Two pieces of our process are probably the problem. Every spec template ends with a boilerplate line we call the still-life clause: all conditions not explicitly mentioned in this document are assumed unchanged. And our release process carries a number we call the change budget — how many unhandled environmental changes a spec is allowed to bring into production before somebody signs a waiver. I've become convinced the still-life clause is doing serious conceptual work that nobody on the team has thought about, and that the change budget is counting something that can't be counted that way.

Please act as a philosopher of AI who has actually sat through engineering design reviews: someone who knows the frame-problem literature — the original worry about what stays the same when an action happens, the follow-on worry about how many preconditions you'd have to list before an action can be said to happen at all, and the more radical claim that the real issue is relevance and context-sensitivity rather than representation — but who also knows that a spec writer with a deadline isnt going to run a nonmonotonic logic on anything. Think carefully before you start writing, and open with a short brainstorm of the failure modes a procedure like this has to catch, because I want to check that you're targeting the right ones before I trust the checklist.

The deliverable is a decision procedure my team can run on a draft spec before it goes to implementation review. It needs to:

- be numbered steps in the order we run them, each step a question asked of the document
- say for each step what a passing answer looks like and what a failing one looks like — not "consider whether X" but an actual standard
- flag which steps are mechanical and which are judgment calls wearing a checklist costume
- rule on the still-life clause: is it defensible, and if so under what conditions; if not, what replaces it in a document that has to be finite
- rule on the change budget: is it a meaningful quantity, and if not what goes in the release process instead
- mark every point where the procedure stops and hands the decision to a person, and say for each handoff why it's a values question rather than a facts question
- say which version of the frame problem the procedure genuinely addresses and which one it punts on. I'd rather have something honest about only fixing the representation-level problem than something that pretends to solve relevance.

Then run it on three drafts that you write yourself, roughly in the shape our template produces, still-life clause and all: (1) a weigh-and-return-tray routine for the rail arm, (2) a halt-on-open-door safety rule where the sensor can be dirty, occluded, or swapped for another part number, (3) a plain-language spec for a behavior that's actually implemented by a learned policy we didn't write. For at least one of the three, give me the verdict I don't want to hear.

Sections, in this order, with rough lengths:

1. Failure modes I'm targeting — about 200 words, this is the brainstorm
2. The procedure — about 1000 words, the main event
3. Still-life clause: verdict — about 200 words
4. Change budget: verdict — about 200 words
5. Worked runs — about 700 words total, three short spec fragments plus what the procedure says about each
6. Where a human has to decide — about 200 words
7. What this procedure cannot do — about 150 words

No bibliography and no reading list, and please don't put anything in quotation marks as though it were a verbatim line from a paper or a book — paraphrase in your own words instead. I want the usable procedure, not the references. And don't hedge into "it depends" at every step; where it really does depend, say on what.
