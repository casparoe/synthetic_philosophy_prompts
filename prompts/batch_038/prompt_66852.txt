ok so here's the situation. I'm trying to write a spec for what a higher-order theory of consciousness would have to deliver before I'd let it license any claim at all about whether some trained network has conscious states. Not the theory — the spec. I keep hitting these papers where a higher-order theorist says a state is conscious in virtue of there being a representation of that state, and I've realised I have no idea which parts of that sentence are load-bearing requirements and which are just what the camp happens to like.

So: write the desiderata. Number them D1, D2, ... and for each give the case or consideration that motivates it — I want to be able to look at any single requirement and immediately see the concrete situation that would break a theory failing it. Stuff I know is in there somewhere and want pinned down: the difference between a state being represented at all and its being consciously felt; the case where the higher-order representation fires with nothing corresponding to it on the other side, and what the theory then owes you (a conscious experience of nothing? an error? a non-problem?); whatever a theory owes about access being non-inferential and non-observational — why isnt my higher-order state just a theory I've inferred about myself from my own behaviour?; the regress worry; animals and infants; and the dissociation between being able to report and being conscious, including where reporting goes right and consciousness is thin or absent.

Then the two parts I actually care about most. One, sort them: which are non-negotiable for any theory in this family, and which are the commitments of one particular flavour — dispositionalist versions, self-representational versions, the ones tied to prefrontal neural data — that must not be smuggled in as universal requirements. Two, and this is the real reason I'm asking: which pairs are jointly unsatisfiable? I have a suspision that the no-regress requirement and the requirement that the higher-order state be itself unconscious while still doing the explanatory work are in tension, and a stronger one that adding my constraint below makes some otherwise-natural set of requirements impossible to satisfy together. If a pair can't both be met, show the argument, not just the assertion.

My constraint, which is the nonstandard bit — take it seriously rather than treating it as a footnote. The system I care about has no clean architectural split between the level that has the state and the level that represents it: same network, same kind of units, no sensory-hierarchy-versus-frontal-cortex distinction to lean on. And its self-representations were shaped partly by human-provided labels about what such systems are supposed to say, so a higher-order representation can be accurate about a state it isn't actually hooked up to, and hooked up to a state it's inaccurate about, for reasons having nothing to do with the state. Tell me what that does to the list: which reqs get harder, which ones turn out to have been doing covert work all along (I bet more than one), and which have to be restated so they stop presupposing a two-level biology.

Fold in what the empirical side demands too — no-report paradigms, the argument over whether the correlates sit posterior or frontal, and the fact that task-demand explanations can eat up a lot of the activation that looks higher-order-ish. Say where those are real constraints on the theory and where they're only constraints if you've already assumed something about what consciousness is.

A short scorecard at the end is fine — three or four live versions of the higher-order view against the list — but a few lines each; the criteria are the deliverable, the scoring is a courtesy. Last line has to start Verdict: and give the single requirement that knocks out the most candidate theories, as its D-number plus about five words.
