Six months ago I took over chairing the independent data monitoring committee for a trial I helped design, and in the same season I had to make a decision about predictive testing for a familial condition, so questions about whether to acquire evidence have stopped being abstract for me. I know the textbook result. Good's theorem, as I would state it: for a Bayesian who updates by conditionalization and whose utility depends only on the state of the world and the act she ends up choosing, free information can never be expected to make her worse off, because the expected weight of evidence in favor of the truth is a divergence between prior and posterior and so cannot be negative. What I lack is an account of why my own verdicts about looking and not looking come out the way they do.

1. Interim efficacy data at the halfway mark, produced by an independent statistician under a stopping rule we fixed in advance: look, obviously.
2. The same numbers, unblinded, handed to me directly with no agreed rule, when I know I over-weight small samples and know the sponsor would like me to see them: do not look. Same data, opposite verdict.
3. A competitor's report that I know was built from a hand-picked subset and whose selection procedure I cannot reconstruct: look anyway, then discount.
4. A result for an untreatable late-onset condition, generated years ago, sealed, that a former technician offers to open for me: I want it opened, and I do not think wanting that is a weakness.
5. Being offered that very same test prospectively, free, next week: I would decline, and I think declining is right.
6. The outcome of a coin toss that settled a bet a month ago, money long since moved, nothing revisable: I want to know. Zero instrumental value, and I do not think that is irrational.
7. My surgeon offering to tell me beforehand whether his model predicts the operation will succeed, when he also tells me that patients who hear a prediction of failure withdraw and do worse than they otherwise would have: do not tell me.
8. Whether a rejected grant application can be appealed: I would pay a good deal to know. Whether the rejection letter's tone was contemptuous: I would pay the same amount to avoid knowing.
9. My late mother's diaries, which my sister has read and will not discuss: I want to read them, and I also do not think my sister was wrong to read them, or wrong to withhold what she found.

Cases 4 and 5 are the pair I cannot reconcile: identical content, identical consequences, opposite verdicts, and I would defend both. Cases 2 and 3 bother me almost as much, since in both I expect the evidence to move me more than it should. The one I keep going back and forth on is whether to commission a two-hundred-thousand-dollar study of whether our program works in a subpopulation, when I already suspect the answer is no and a null result would end a program that is currently helping people.

What I want is the principle, or the small set of candidate principles, that generates these verdicts. State it precisely, clause by clause, and tell me which case motivates each clause; I would like to see exactly where the instrumental-value story runs out and something else has to take over. Then tell me whether what you have given me is a repair of Good's theorem — in which case name the assumption each troublesome case violates — or a replacement for it. Then apply it to three cases I have not listed: a juror who could learn a fact the judge would exclude, a researcher deciding whether to run an evaluation she expects to fail, and a person deciding whether to read a partner's messages. Finally, be honest about which of my verdicts the principle forces me to abandon, including whether it can keep 4 and 5 or must collapse them.

I would rather you commit than catalogue. Pick the account you think is correct, say plainly which of my verdicts it cannot save, and argue for paying that price; a menu of options with the choice left to me is not what I am after.
