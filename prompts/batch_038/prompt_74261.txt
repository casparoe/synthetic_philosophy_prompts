For my term paper in a senior seminar on decision-making under moral uncertainty, I have been working on invertebrate welfare, and specifically on the argument that the sheer number of insects ought to dominate our reasoning even if the probability that any given insect is sentient is very low. What has struck me is how thoroughly stuck the argument is. On one side are people I will call the weighters: they hold that if you assign even a one-in-a-thousand chance of sentience to an insect, and insects number in the trillions in the wild and in the billions on farms, the expected harm is large enough to swamp ordinary vertebrate concerns, and that treating a small probability as zero is an unargued refusal to do the arithmetic. On the other side are the thresholders: they hold that the same arithmetic, applied consistently, tells you that your single most urgent moral obligation is to reduce the insects killed by crop harvesting, that this is absurd on its face, and that an argument whose conclusions you have to hedge, defer, or bury is thereby telling you that something is wrong with its premises. Both camps read the same work on nociception and learning in insects, both accept the same order-of-magnitude population estimates, and as far as I can tell neither has shifted position at any point in the last decade.

Our seminar tried to pin the disagreement down with a spreadsheet, and it is the spreadsheet that puzzles me most, because it left everyone more entrenched than before. On the numbers in it, which nobody in the room disputes: producing a kilogram of edible meat takes something like 1,500 farmed crickets and about seven-tenths of a broiler chicken, and if you set a cricket's capacity for welfare at one hundredth of a chicken's, then cricket meat is worse than chicken meat whenever the probability that an individual cricket is sentient is above roughly 5 percent. One faction says this is a devastating result, since 5 percent is a large probability and nobody would tolerate a 5 percent risk of a comparable harm to a vertebrate. The other says it establishes nothing, because the one-hundredth was chosen by fiat, because a break-even figure is meaningless without a calibration for the scale it sits on, and because the whole exercise assumes from the start that welfare capacities are commensurable on a single ratio scale. There is also a constraint that neither side wants to confront: you cannot adjust the cricket's relative weight without adjusting the weights assigned to fish, crustaceans, and chickens, and the moment you do, your conclusions about wild-caught fish and about cage-free eggs move with them.

So I want a diagnosis of what kind of disagreement this actually is rather than a verdict about who is right. Is the hidden question factual, concerning the relative welfare weights that nobody can measure, or concerning whether insect nociceptive and learning data are evidence of anything at all? Is it a disagreement in decision theory, about whether expected-value maximization is the right rule when the probabilities are stipulated rather than observed, or about whether aggregation across very many small harms is legitimate in the first place? Is part of it verbal, in that the two sides mean different things by the probability that an insect is sentient, one a credence constrained by comparative neuroanatomy and the other a placeholder for complete ignorance? Or is it bedrock: a prior about how much cost morality is allowed to impose and how much absurdity a principle can absorb before it counts as refuted, which neither side is in a position to argue for? My working hypothesis, which I would like you to attack if it is mistaken, is that the real issue is the epistemic status of the ratio scale, that the weighters treat one-hundredth as a rough but genuine measurement while the thresholders treat it as an arbitrary normalization, and that once you see this the 5 percent figure is not evidence for either side. Given whatever diagnosis you arrive at, tell me what would count as progress: a distinction, a formal result about reasoning under moral uncertainty, some kind of empirical evidence that would actually bear on the weights, or a change of question. And if your conclusion is that no progress should be expected, explain why, and tell me what a term paper is supposed to do in that situation. I am not after a survey of insect cognition and I do not want a reading list; I want the structure of the dispute.
