The student's answer (anonymous, ~470 words):

> Both books are about Battalion 101, and that's what makes the quarrel worth having: same unit, same orders, much the same record, so the disagreement is interpretive rather than evidential. Browning's men are middle-aged reservists from Hamburg, working class, too old for the front, not SS true believers. He reads them through Milgram: they shot because the order was given to them face to face, because their comrades were shooting, and because stepping out would have marked them as weak in front of men they'd work with for years. At Józefów Trapp told them anyone who didn't feel up to it could step out; a handful of roughly five hundred did, and one man who asked to be released got other duties and was even promoted later. Goldhagen accepts those facts — the offer was real and nobody was seriously punished for refusing — but says Browning has the interpretation wrong, because obedience and conformity describe what the men did, not how they could do it so readily and sometimes with such satisfaction. His cause is "eliminationist antisemitism": a centuries-deep German cognitive model in which Jews were the source of Germany's ills and had to be removed, held so widely that ordinary Germans and not just the SS treated killing them as legitimate. He calls the explanation monocausal and thinks that's a virtue.
>
> The standard objection — that a belief everyone shared can't discriminate the shooter from the man who stepped out — is correct as far as it goes: eliminationist antisemitism can't handle contrast (A). But Browning is in the same boat, and this is where the two-contrast test does real work. Conformity, obedience and careerism were equally available to all five hundred men, so they predict uniform behavior, and the unit wasn't uniform. Browning has no answer to that, and since he also says nothing about why this behavior was German rather than general, he fails (B) as well.
>
> There's a deeper problem with framing the debate as situation versus disposition. Culture is shared and sits outside the individual head, so eliminationist antisemitism is a situational factor as much as peer pressure is; and Browning's account leans on a coercive backdrop anyway, since refusing an order in that state could cost you your life. On this reading the two books aren't really opposed — both say the killers acted on what their environment handed them and differ mainly in which part of the environment gets named. That would also explain how the argument got so bitter without anyone locating a factual disagreement.
>
> Browning is often said to be correcting the fundamental attribution error in the historiography. That's fair but incomplete: what he corrects is the assumption that mass killing requires monsters, not the assumption that it requires beliefs. He leaves ideology out of the explanation entirely, and that's the point — these men killed without believing much of anything about Jews. So if the two-contrast test is the right standard, Goldhagen answers (B) and fails (A), Browning answers neither, and whatever explanation survives has to be built out of materials neither book supplies.
