I write a monthly long-form column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and this spring the column has acquired a companion podcast in which I record the on-stage conversation behind each piece. My next guest is a cognitive scientist whose new trade book revives a version of linguistic relativity that most of her discipline considers settled in the negative — not the strong determinist claim that a language imprisons its speakers, but something she insists is nonetheless stronger than the modest, widely accepted "thinking for speaking" reading. Her position, as I understand it from the book and from a long email exchange, is that grammatically obligatory categories — a language that forces its speakers to mark the cardinal direction of an event rather than a left/right frame, or to mark how the speaker came to know what she is asserting, or to assign gender to inanimate nouns — do not merely shape what speakers say while they are saying it, but reshape the non-linguistic cognitive machinery underneath: attention, visual discrimination, source memory, spatial transformation. She thinks the mid-century dismissal of Whorf rests on a caricature of Whorf and on the wrong kind of experiment, and she offers four lines of evidence I want to press on: fieldwork with speakers of an absolute-frame language who track north even on tasks with no linguistic component and who arrange sequences of events on a fixed cardinal axis regardless of which way they happen to be facing; a color-discrimination study with speakers of a language that draws its basic color boundary where English does not, in which a categorical advantage appears when the stimuli fall in the right visual field and disappears when the left hemisphere's verbal resources are loaded with a secondary task; work with a young, emerging sign language whose later cohorts developed classifier constructions their predecessors lacked, and whose signers now outperform both the earlier cohorts and hearing non-signers on a spatial transformation task; and the old dispute over whether an Amazonian language without exact number words leaves its speakers without exact quantity concepts, together with the later finding that the relevant speakers can be trained to exact matching, which she reads as showing that the missing piece was a practice, not a grammar.

I have read enough of the skeptical literature to know that these four cases are precisely where the fight is, and I do not want to spend forty-five minutes on a stage asking questions a sophomore would ask. What I need from you is a working question set: ten to twelve questions, arranged as an arc that starts from her best material and ends somewhere she would rather not go. For each question give me a one-sentence note on what I am actually fishing for and how I would render a typical answer in prose for a general reader, followed by a single follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give. Mark the three questions I could cut if the clock runs short. Please build the set around three deflections she is very good at and which I have watched her use in other interviews: that nobody in her camp ever claimed determinism, so the strong-Whorf strawman is not her position; that the effects are small, but small effects measured carefully are what science is made of; and that the burden of proof lies with the universalists, who should produce a null result that replicates rather than demanding she produce an effect that does. I want at least two questions constructed so that each of those moves, once made, cannot end the exchange — supply a second and third version of each such question that tightens the screws rather than merely repeating it.

Two further requirements. First, include the substantive objections I expect her to be braced for and that she will handle too easily if I let her set the terms: the reading of the verbal-interference result as evidence that language is recruited online during encoding rather than as evidence that perception itself has been restructured; the possibility that in the fieldwork cases grammar and ecology are confounded, so that a seafaring or desert-dwelling way of life produces both the obligatory directional grammar and the navigational skill, with no arrow of causation running from one to the other; the null results and failed replications on grammatical gender and object properties; the demonstration, by a linguist working carefully through the Hopi material decades after the fact, that the language in question does have abundant temporal vocabulary, which pulls the rug out from under Whorf's flagship example and raises the question of how much of her inheritance she is willing to disown; and the language-of-thought reply, which asks her to say what a Whorfian effect looks like mechanistically — a change in representational format, in the allocation of attention, in the contents of working memory, or only in the way a task is construed once it is presented. Second, be fair to her: include one question that lets her state the single piece of evidence she considers strongest and least contaminated by these worries, and one that asks what experimental result would force her to abandon the thesis rather than reinterpret it. Flag, at the end, the one question I am hoping she raises before I do, because it is the most damaging thing in the literature and I would rather she answer it on her own terms than have it look like an ambush. Assume an audience of intelligent laypeople who will not tolerate jargon, so every question must be answerable aloud in a public conversation, and please do not include anything about what drew her to the field or how the book came to be written.
