I'm teaching a graduate seminar that pairs decision theory with literature, and next week's text is *Moby-Dick*, which I want to read against Shubik's dollar auction and the wider literature on escalation of commitment. The obvious mapping is Ahab: he has already paid — the leg, the years, the ship — and every further day of search raises what turning back would waste, so the pursuit continues long past the point where any sane accounting would end it, with Starbuck making the textbook objection that sunk costs cannot be reasons. But I want the reading to press hardest where the mapping breaks.

Three questions. First, who is the other bidder? The dollar auction's trap is generated by the second bidder, who pays even if he exits; Ahab looks like bidder and auctioneer at once — he sets the prize by nailing the doubloon to the mast, and the crew then bid with their lives without ever having been told the real terms. Is that a fatal disanalogy, or a variant the standard cases don't cover, one in which the escalator also controls the escalation of others?

Second, does the novel actually endorse the sunk-cost diagnosis, or does it show that Ahab's problem isn't a mis-weighting of past expenditure at all but an endogenous change in his ends — the whale's value rising precisely because the price has risen — so that Starbuck's argument isn't even addressed to Ahab's error? What would have to be true for that reading not to make every escalating agent exculpable?

Third, bring in the two control cases: Captain Boomer of the *Samuel Enderby*, who lost an arm to the same whale and cheerfully declines to hunt him again, and the *Rachel*'s captain, still combing the sea for his missing twelve-year-old son when Ahab refuses to join him. Boomer cuts his losses; the *Rachel*'s captain escalates; Ahab escalates. Only one of them is condemned by the plot, and the ship that pulls Ishmael out of the water at the end is the one that never gave up searching. Given that, does the book treat escalation as a bias, a vice, or sometimes a form of fidelity — and does the ending undercut a Williams-style defense of Ahab's hunt as a ground project, given that the losses fall on men who never placed a bid?
