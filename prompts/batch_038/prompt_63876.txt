So me and a few friends run a little online reading group — mostly people with day jobs, nobody with any training in archaeology or philosophy, we just read stuff and argue about it. For like four months two of our members have been going at it over whether inventing farming was a disaster for humanity, and it's gotten kind of exhausting.

One of them, who I'll call Dana, is firmly in the "agriculture was the worst mistake we ever made" camp. Her view: foragers worked way less, ate a more varied diet, were taller and healthier, and lived in bands with no bosses and no big gap between rich and poor — and farming traded all of that for backbreaking labor, a worse diet propped up by a couple of staple crops, crowded disease-ridden villages, and hierarchies that we're still stuck inside of. The other one, Wes, thinks that whole framing is a fantasy. His arguments: nobody sat down and decided to farm, it happened over thousands of years and seperately in a bunch of different places, so calling it a "mistake" is like calling the ice ages a mistake; the foragers that anthropologists actually got to study in the 20th century had mostly been pushed into marginal land nobody else wanted and were trading with farming neighbors anyway, so using them as a picture of Pleistocene life is cheating; the bad health signals in the skeletons come at least as much from living packed together permanently with livestock and waste as from growing crops, so the real culprit is sedentism and density, not agriculture as such; and the whole comparison is rigged because farming supports maybe a hundred times as many people — sure, the average life got somewhat worse, but vastly more lives got lived, and Dana apparently doesn't think that counts, which Wes says is just her refusing to do the arithmetic.

Dana's counter is that a trap is still a trap even if you wander into it slowly and can't wander back out, and that you can't add up wellbeing across populations of different sizes to cancel out making each individual life worse. Wes says that's a dodge. Neither of them has budged an inch and honestly I think they're both partly right, and I also think they're arguing about different things without noticing.

Here's what I want you to write: the joint statement they should have produced if they'd sat down and done a proper adversarial collaboration instead of dunking on each other in a group chat. Specifically I want

1. the claims both of them can actually sign — about what the archaeological and ethnographic evidence shows, about what the transition did and didn't involve, about what a fair comparison between the two ways of life would have to look like, and about what's genuinely uncertain. These need to be substantive, not the cowardly "both sides agree humans ate food" kind.
2. the disagreements that survive, stated precisely and worded so that each side would accept the wording of their own position. Wes shouldn't have to sign something that reads like a strawman of his view, and same for Dana.
3. the cruxes — the handful of sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one of them.
4. for each crux, some case, dataset, or argument both would agree bears on it, with each side's prediction written down in advance of the answer, so we can see who's on the hook if it comes out the other way.

Format it as a numbered agreed-claims section followed by a table for the disagreements.

A few things I suspect are cruxes, but tell me if I'm wrong about where the action is: whether it even makes sense to call something a mistake when no one chose it and it wasn't reversible; whether the right comparison is average wellbeing per life or total wellbeing across everyone who's ever lived, and whether the extra people who only exist because of farming can count as gains given that their alternative was never existing at all; whether teeth and bones can tell us anything about how good life was on average or only about the fraction of people who got sick or malnourished as kids; and whether inequality should be laid at farming's door or at storage-and-sedentism's door, since some settled foragers who never planted anything had slaves and big status gaps. Also, if some of their fighting turns out to be just about words — I have a sneaking suspicion "mistake" is doing two different jobs for them, one meaning things ended up worse and one meaning somebody screwed up — say which part is verbal and why.

You don't need to give me a reading list or cite any books, I'm not going to look them up anyway, I just want the document itself. And please write it in American English — we've got an Australian in the group and his spelling keeps setting off everybody's spellcheck, so we standardized.
