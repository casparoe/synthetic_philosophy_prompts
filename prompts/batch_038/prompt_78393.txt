So I need a referee. I got into a four-hour argument with a friend of mine last night — he writes firmware for industrial controllers and reads way more philosophy than he lets on — and neither of us budged.

His whole move was the digger wasp. The story he tells: a *Sphex* wasp stings a cricket, drags it to the mouth of her burrow by the antennae, drops it outside, goes in to inspect the nest, then comes back out and hauls the prey in. Now nudge the cricket a few inches while she's inside. She comes out, finds it displaced, drags it back to the entrance — and because the prey is once again "sitting outside," her routine resets and she re-inspects the nest before dragging it in. Round and round, endlessly, while the experimenter takes notes. He also said, and I checked this part is roughly right, that the version everyone repeats descends from Fabre through a 1963 pop book about the brain, and that Hofstadter's word for the phenomenon is "sphexishness." His conclusion: what looks like the wasp weighing things up is a routine she cannot step outside of, and human deliberation is that same routine with better PR. So sphexishness goes all the way down — no free will worth the name, agency is a user interface, compatibilists are just relabeling the loop.

My pushback was mostly that the ethology under it is soft. There's an argument (I think from someone named Fred Keijzer) that in the experiments that were actually repeated, the wasp adapts after enough iterations; that the example has survived in the literature because it's rhetorically handy rather than because it's accurate; and that re-inspecting a nest after something has moved your prey might just be sensible behavior, not a stuck program. Also the antennae detail cuts the other way for me: if you snip the antennae off and the wasp doesn't think to grab a leg instead, that's not "the machine cannot reason," that's the experiment removing the one handle her routine was keyed to. And personally I watched a great golden digger wasp in my driveway abandon a paralyzed cricket mid-drag and just fly off when my dog came around the corner, which doesn't look like an endless loop at all.

Can you go through this properly?

1. Give me the correspondence table explicitly — what in the wasp story is standing in for the goal, for deliberation, for the outside interference, for the antennae, for the loop — and mark which pairings are doing real argumentative work and which are just decoration.
2. Sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually threaten his conclusion and the ones that don't. I suspect "the wasp is dumb and we're smart" belongs in the useless pile, since he'd say intelligence was never the point, but I want to know if I'm right about that.
3. Tell me what the wasp case quietly imports that the human case lacks. My candidates: the routine was built for one narrow unchanging environment; the "failure" is only visible to an outside observer who already knows what success would look like; and the loop is pathological only relative to a task nobody built the animal for. Are those the right ones, or are there bigger smugglings I missed?
4. Does the no-free-will conclusion survive with the disanalogies on the table, or was the analogy carrying it the whole time? And if it doesn't survive, what would be a better analogy for what he's actually trying to say — is there any analogy that could carry that claim?

How much of the Fabre/loop story do people who actually know the insect behavior accept today, and do the philosophers who use the wasp example think it shows there's no free will, or is my friend reading more into it than its users do?
