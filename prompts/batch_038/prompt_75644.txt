I'm a lay member of our city council's behavioural insights panel and last night two of the others got into it over a pilot, and I couldn't work out which of them was on stronger ground. The proposal: from April, every household on a metered water supply gets defaulted onto a seasonal tariff — the normal rate most of the year, but roughly 40% more per unit between June and September — with a one-tap switch in the app back to the flat year-round rate.

Nadia said this isn't a nudge at all. Nudges are meant to work without significantly changing people's economic incentives, and this changes the price, so it's a tax wearing a cardigan; it should go to the budget committee in public rather than being signed off by a panel like ours.

Ivan said she'd hold of the wrong end of the trial. The price gap works out at about 40p a month for a typical household and barely shows up in anybody's bill, and the trial arm where households faced exactly the same prices but had to make an active choice only got 31% onto the seasonal rate, against 84% in the default arm — so the incentive change is trivial and the architecture is doing essentially all the work, which is what a nudge is.

Nadia said that makes it worse, not better. If the whole effect is inertia, the council is trading on the fact that people couldn't be bothered, and 84% is not 84% of people consenting to anything; lower peak demand and fewer hosepipe bans is a good outcome, but a good outcome reached by exploiting inattention is still exploitation.

Ivan said her objection proves too much, because it would sink every default going, including the pension auto-enrolment and the opt-out donor register Nadia campaigned for herself; and anyway the pilot writes to every household spelling out what the default is, why it's been chosen and how to leave it in one tap, so if a nudge survives being announced out loud, that's about as close to consent as administration ever gets.

Nadia said the letter isn't neutral — it also tells people what most of their neighbours picked, so they'd be testing whether disclosure kills the nudge with a second nudge bolted onto it — and that Ivan had slid from "people wouldn't object if they noticed" to "people have agreed", which aren't the same claim.

Can you go through it move by move and tell me who's right at each step? Exactly five numbered items, one per move in the order they came, so Nadia's opening is item 1: a rating for whoever made the move (clearly right, mostly right, mixed, mostly wrong, clearly wrong) plus the reason, two or three sentences each. No preamble and no summing up at the end — if there's something neither of them has got hold of, work it into whichever item it belongs in.
