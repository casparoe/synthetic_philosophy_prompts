I am running a six-week graduate reading group on de re modality, and for the opening session I want a diagnostic instrument rather than a survey: a decision tree that sorts participants into positions on the counterpart theory versus transworld identity question *before* they have read anything about that dispute, so we can locate where the room actually disagrees rather than which labels people like.

**Fixed starting point.** State at the top of the tree what everyone in the room is assumed to grant: that some singular modal claims are true and others false — this particular candidate might have won that race, though he could not have been an octave taller than himself — and that quantifying over possible worlds is a legitimate way of systematizing such facts. The tree divides up the space beyond that common ground, so nothing in it should re-litigate whether de re modality is respectable at all.

**Structure.** Seven to nine branch nodes, six to eight leaves. Every node must be a concrete scenario, or a claim about a described scenario, that a participant can answer without knowing which position any given answer leads to. No node may contain the words counterpart, transworld identity, haecceitism, thisness, essence, overlap, or rigid designation; if a node genuinely needs one technical term, allow exactly one and gloss it parenthetically. Nodes should each be answerable in a couple of minutes, aloud, in the room.

**Cases to build the nodes around** (add others if you think they do more work):

(a) A world in which the only occupant relevantly resembling you is a qualitative duplicate — same shape, same matter, same memories and fingerprints — but who is not you. Is a claim about what you could have done made true by what happens to that duplicate, and if so, what does the duplicate's being *not you* cost or save?

(b) A world containing exactly two qualitatively indiscernible iron spheres, and the scenario in which their roles are exchanged with no qualitative difference anywhere. Is there a further fact of the matter that makes the exchange a genuine alternative possibility?

(c) Multiplicity: a world containing two near-duplicates of you, one of whom gets what you wanted and one of whom does not. Does the truth of the claim that you could have had it depend on how many duplicates there are, on how similar the best of them is, or on neither?

(d) Degree: as similarity fades — a more distant relative, a mere structural analogue, something of a different kind entirely that shares your matter — does "could have" switch off at a boundary, or does it admit borderline cases?

(e) Salience: does the truth-value of a de re possibility claim shift when the conversation shifts which respect of similarity is at issue, for instance between a claim about a person and the parallel claim about the organism, the statue, or the ship that person was?

(f) Nesting: at a world containing your duplicate, does what that duplicate could have gone on to do depend on facts about you here, or only on facts at that world?

(g) Any axis you judge orthogonal to this dispute — the concreteness or abstractness of worlds, for instance, or actualism versus possibilism about individuals — should be identified as orthogonal and kept out of the tree, with a sentence on why it is not a branch.

**Leaves.** Name each leaf with whatever label is standard, and include at minimum: a counterpart-style view with a single contextually fixed similarity relation; a counterpart-style view on which the relation in force varies with the question asked; transworld identity with no further individuating machinery; a view on which individual essences or thisnesses ground recurrence across worlds; a view that accepts the swap possibility in (b) and one that denies it while keeping transworld identity; an actualist leaf on which worlds are abstract and the very same individuals recur in them; and a primitivist or modalist leaf on which de re modal claims are made true by the individuals themselves, with no relation of identity or resemblance doing the work. If a leaf you need has no established name, mark it "unnamed" and describe it.

**Key.** Supply the mapping from answer patterns to leaves. Then list the answer combinations the nodes permit that no leaf occupies, and for each say whether it is empty because the combination is incoherent, because it is coherent but nobody finds it attractive, or because it is simply unexplored — do not run those three diagnoses together. Finally, flag every pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, and either supply an extra question that would separate them or concede that they are one position under two descriptions.

**Epistemic housekeeping.** Mark, leaf by leaf, whether your characterization is the standard one or a contested reconstruction, and say plainly which of your claims are matters of live dispute among people who work on this — for instance, whether case (a) is fatal to the counterpart-style views or merely an awkwardness they can absorb, and whether the essence-grounded leaf collapses into the bare transworld-identity leaf. Do not present either side of such disputes as the consensus, and do not let a reading you happen to favor appear in the handout as neutral. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations; where you are unsure who defends a view or how a label arose, say that you are unsure rather than filling the gap.
