I am a novelist, and I find myself having to defend a finished book against a charge that my own chosen analogy has made worse rather than better.

The book is polyphonic. Nine chapters, each a first-person account of the same night in a small manufacturing town — the shift foreman, the widow, the factory inspector, the boy who was on the roof, the night porter, and so on. The accounts contradict one another on matters of fact, and nothing in the book reconciles them; the ninth chapter is the transcript of a coroner's finding that is itself incomplete. Critics have called the structure evasive and the book morally cowardly: a refusal to say what happened, dressed up as respect for the witnesses. My defence, in interviews and in an afterword I have not yet delivered, is that the book is a working-out of the Jain parable of the blind men and the elephant — the image the tradition knows as Andhgajanyāyah, as my consultant pointed out — and that what reads as evasion is really anekāntavāda in narrative form: every account is true from its own standpoint, no account is the whole, and the reader is left in exactly the position the doctrine considers correct.

My consultant, a scholar of Jain philosophy, says I have this backwards and that the analogy is now carrying a weight it cannot bear. Her objections, as I follow them: the parable is about different people touching different parts of one animal, whereas the doctrine concerns aspects of a single thing that are not spatially divisible among observers; the parable, in most of its tellings, ends with someone who sees, and the doctrine reserves that seeing for a kind of knowledge none of my characters possesses; and the popular moral — everyone is partly right, so be humble — owes more to a nineteenth-century American poem in which the elephant stands for God and the blind men stand for the religions, and more to modern readings of anekāntavāda as a species of intellectual non-violence, than it does to the medieval logic of conditioned predication.

I would like a proper audit rather than reassurance.

1. Set out the correspondence explicitly, element by element: the elephant, the blind men, the particular parts they touch, the touching itself, the quarrel, the sighted man who enters in some versions, and the closing moral. For each, say what in anekāntavāda, nayavāda, or syādvāda it is supposed to map onto, and how good the fit is.

2. Of the three things my defence leans on — that no account is simply wrong, that there is no further fact of the matter available to anyone inside the story, and that the withholding is therefore a virtue rather than a failure — which genuinely belong to the doctrine, and which have been imported by the parable and its later retellings?

3. Separate the disanalogies that matter for this use from the ones that are merely local. I am not troubled that my narrators can see and the parable's men cannot, unless that turns out to be doing work.

4. Tell me whether the defence survives. Can anekāntavāda license a book that declines to say what happened, or does the doctrine presuppose a determinate whole that is in principle fully knowable — and if so, by whom, and what follows for a novel containing no such knower?

5. The question that most frightens me: would syādvāda convict some of my narrators of being flatly wrong rather than partly right, on the grounds that unqualified assertion is precisely what it exists to discipline? If so, name the sort of narrator it would convict.

6. If the elephant fails as the book's key image, propose one or two better ones — from within the tradition if anything there does the work, otherwise from the vocabulary of my craft, where I am fluent — and state what each would license and what it would forbid me to claim.

7. Mark clearly where the tradition is internally divided, and where you are unsure, instead of filling gaps with confident invention. I cannot check your sources, and I would rather know the shape of your uncertainty than be given false precision.
