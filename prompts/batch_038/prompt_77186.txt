I spent thirty-four years in a basement lab keeping mammalian cell lines alive — passaging them, freezing down vials and thawing them a decade later, re-authenticating lines by their STR fingerprint after one of the cross-contamination scandals, tracing clonal descendants with dyes and later with sequence barcodes. Retired two years ago, and to keep the gears turning I joined a reading group at the public library, and we've just worked through the teletrasporter chapters of Parfit's Reasons and Persons. I follow the argument, more or less. But I keep wanting to say the whole thing in the language I actually think in, and I don't trust myself to do the translation without quietly smuggling my own conclusions into it. So do it for me, as faithfully as you can manage, and then tell me what broke on the way over.

Here's what I want rendered into cell-lineage vocabulary — clones, passages, isogenic lines, cryovials, single-cell bottlenecks, genetic drift in culture, provenance, authentication by fingerprint, chimeras, embryo splitting, a planarian regenerating from a fragment, that sort of thing: (1) the reductionist claim that a person isn't a further entity over and above the brain, the body and the chain of mental states, so there's no extra thing that could survive or fail to survive all of that; (2) the claim that what matters in survival is Relation R — psychological connectedness and/or continuity — rather than identity, and that R comes in degrees and can take a branching form; (3) the fission case, where one record is used to build two replicas, so identity can't hold with both even though everything that seemed to matter is present twice, undiminished; (4) the spectrum of cases shading from ordinary survival into gradual replacement of parts and then into outright copy-making, with no non-arbitrary place to draw a line, and the inference that "will this be me?" sometimes just has no yes-or-no answer; (5) the "right kind of cause" clause — that a match in state isn't enough unless it's hooked up to the earlier state in the right way. For each of the five, give me the translation, then say what you had to bend to make it fit, then what has no expression in my vocabulary at all. Don't go easy on the bending part. I know that biologists cheerfully call a vial thawed last spring "the same line" as one frozen down in the eighties, and equally cheerfully say that a sheep cloned from an adult cell is not the sheep it came from — which makes me suspect my field has already picked a side in this debate, treating identity as lineage and treating state-match as mere evidence of it. If that's right, say so plainly, and say whether Parfit is agreeing with lab practice or contradicting it.

Then the residue report, which is the bit I actually want, plus two questions of my own that I don't think are in the book. First: sort your residues into (a) ones where my vocabulary is simply missing something — no way to say "it matters to me," no term for a single subject persisting rather than a population of descendants sharing a marker — and (b) ones where the failure to translate suggests the original philosophical claim was less clear than it sounded. Give a verdict on each residue, and say whether the missing-piece residues are gaps a biologist could in principle fill or whether they're structural. Second, my own case: suppose the transporter works from a lossy record the way an STR profile stands in for a genome — a handful of loci doing duty for the whole thing — and there's a knob on the fidelity of the match. At full fidelity nobody disputes that R holds; way down the knob the replica is a stranger wearing my face. Where does R stop holding, and who sets that point? Because in my lab the answer to "how close is close enough to be the same line" is fixed by the assay we happen to run and by conventions about passage number and drift, and if Parfit's R is fixed the same way, then what matters in survival turns partly on measurement resolution, which seems a strange thing for a deep metaphysical thesis to depend on. Is that a real objection or am I confusing what we can assay with what there is? Third, and this is the one I can't settle: if a biologist's language has no term for numerical identity over time at all — just markers, lineage and convention — then "identity isn't what matters" is going to come out true the moment I translate into it, and I'll have proved nothing at all. Tell me whether that cheap victory is actually happening, and if it is, point to the exact sentence where the smuggling occurs. No reading list, no pointers to other people's papers, and don't tell me to go look something up — I want the work done here, in terms a retired cell biologist can check against his own intuitions.
