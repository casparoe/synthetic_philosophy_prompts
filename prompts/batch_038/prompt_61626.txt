Restate, as faithfully as you can, a debate internal to inverse reinforcement learning and its use in inferring human preferences, in that vocabulary of mine. The debate, in practitioners' own terms, runs roughly as follows. First, underdetermination: the reward recovered from demonstrations is pinned down only up to an equivalence class, and the class is larger than affine rescaling — adding a potential-based shaping term leaves the optimal policy set unchanged — so what the algorithm learns depends on the prior over rewards, on the discount factor, and on the assumed optimality model, typically an explicit noise model under which the demonstrator chooses softmax-optimally. Second, dynamic inconsistency: if human demonstrators discount hyperbolically, the reward is not a single stable object; the naive and the sophisticated versions of the same person select different policies, and the practitioner must decide whether to fit the behavior of the momentary self or some reflective standard, with no purely behavioral fact of the matter settling the choice. Third, belief-reward confounding: the same trajectory can be optimal under high reward with pessimistic beliefs or under low reward with optimistic beliefs, so a misspecified model of the demonstrator's beliefs yields systematically biased reward estimates, and the bias is not cured by more demonstrations of the same kind.
