Right, so this has been rattling around my head for about a year now and I'd genuinely love someone to tell me where it falls over. Bit of background: I'm not academic at all, I run a small community hub for a charity — food bank stuff, a warm space over winter, a couple of evening classes, the usual. Last autumn the council asked me to sit on a little panel about helping residents make healthier choices, which mostly turned out to mean four of us arguing for two hours about whether they should rearrange the queue in the leisure centre cafe so the jacket potatoes come before the chips. Someone on the panel lent me a copy of Nudge and I read it on the bus, and ever since I can't stop thinking about it, partly because my brother-in-law reckons the whole business is just the state treating grown adults like toddlers and I've never been able to come back with anything better than "well, it works". So I've tried to writen down what I actually believe, as plainly as I can, and I want you to check whether the list hangs together or whether I'm holding onto things that can't all be true at once.

Here's the list. (a) There's no such thing as not nudging — whatever way you set out a form, a queue, a shelf or a website, you're steering people, so the only real question is which direction, not whether. (b) A nudge is only legitimate if the person would sign off on it once they knew exactly what was being done to them and had a proper think about it; if they'd feel conned, it's out. (c) But most of the time people don't have a settled preference sitting there waiting to be uncovered — what they want gets made on the spot by how the thing is put in front of them. (d) The reason manipulation is wrong isn't mainly that it gets bad results, it's that it uses someone as a tool instead of addressing them as a person who can reason. (e) At the end of the day what matters is whether people's lives go better; being free to make a mess of your own life has no value in itself, only in so far as it tends to produce better outcomes. (f) All the same, for adults whose choices only affect themselves, bans and mandates are off the table — you should always be able to say no. (g) Defaults are the good kind of nudge, the model case; opt-out organ donation is fine because nobody's being forced, you can always leave the register. (h) Sludge is different though. Deliberately making something more annoying because you want fewer people to do it — extra forms, a phone line open only between ten and two, making people come in person — is not on, even though that isn't forcing anyone either and the option is still technically there. Getting rid of sludge is obviously good. (i) And I don't like it when the default is set for the convenience of the organisation rather than the person — auto-enrolling everyone into whatever pension provider the employer happened to pick, or a GP surgery defaulting you into a text-reminder service so they can hit a target.

What I want is an audit, not a lecture. Go through and tell me which of these genuinely pull against each other, worst first — rank them, don't just list them all as equally bad — and for each one say what I'd have to give up, what the cheapest repair looks like, and what it costs me to make that repair. Also tell me which of the pairs only look like problems and aren't, because my suspicion is that I've been worrying about the wrong ones at the pub for a year. I've got no idea which commitment I'd drop first, they all feel obviously right to me, so please don't assume I'm attached to any of them. No reading list or literature review, I couldn't read half of it anyway — refer to positions and arguments if that helps but skip the citations. And end with a last line that begins Verdict: followed by the letter of the single commitment I should change first and a short phrase saying roughly what I'd change it into.
