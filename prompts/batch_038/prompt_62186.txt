I co-run a reading group that's about half philosophy grad students and half CS people, and every single session somebody wheelbarrows Gödel in to settle a question that has nothing to do with arithmetic. Minds aren't machines. God's knowledge can't be a formal system. Legal codes can't decide their own hard cases. Language can never fully describe itself. I want to hand everyone a one-page decision tree at the start of term that sorts these moves before the argument starts, and I'd like you to write it.

What I need:

- Build it from the logical space, not from a list of famous names. Each node should be a two- or three-way question about the claim the person actually just made, phrased so they can answer it honestly without knowing where each answer leads. Assume your reader has had one intro logic course and nothing more. The nodes worth spending your budget on are the ones that actually do technical work: is the target system effectively axiomatized, and does it contain enough arithmetic for the theorem to bite at all; is the move about what a system can *prove* or about what it can *express and define* (a lot of these slide from one to the other and never come back); does the argument need the human — or God, or the judge — to be consistent, and to *know* that they are; is the conclusion the modest "the system doesn't prove X" or the much heavier "X is true and I can see it"; and what's happening to the consistency premise — granted, denied, or just waved at.
- Somewhere between nine and twelve nodes, eight to twelve leaves. Each leaf gets: a name if the position has one, "unnamed" if nobody really defends it, one sentence stating the strongest honest version of the move, and one sentence on the most awkward thing that version is committed to. No bibliography, no footnotes, no citation hunting — I just want the tree and the labels.
- Make sure there's room for two kinds of leaf people usually skip: moves that turn out to be perfectly fine but much weaker than advertised, and moves that never invoke a theorem at all and just like the word "incompleteness" as a vibe. Also give me somewhere to put the arguer who's basically restating Gödel's own extramathematical conclusions, seperate from the people who appropriated the results later and went further than he did.
- Flag any pair of leaves your tree can't really seperate, and say what extra question would seperate them if you had one more node to spend.

Then run the tree on these five things people have actually said at me, and for each give the path it takes, the leaf it lands on, and the one question I should ask back:

1. "No consistent formal system can prove its own consistency. So a legal code can never certify its own coherence from the inside — there always has to be a judge standing outside it."
2. "Take the Gödel sentence for arithmetic. We can see that it's true. The system can't prove it. So whatever we are, we arent a Turing machine."
3. "If God's knowledge were a formal system it would fall under incompleteness. So either God's knowledge is incomplete or God isn't formal, and either way classical theism is in trouble."
4. "Gödel showed every system of description is incomplete, which is why any theory of language has to stay open-ended, always pointing beyond itself."
5. "All I'm saying is that no single effectively axiomatized theory is going to capture arithmetic, so anyone who thinks mathematics gets finished by choosing an axiom system is wrong."

Last thing: list two or three combinations of answers that your tree treats as logically open but that no one actually occupies, and for each say whether that gap is there because the position is incoherent, because it's just unappealing, or because nobody's bothered to explore it yet.
