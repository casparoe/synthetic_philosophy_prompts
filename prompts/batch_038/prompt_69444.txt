I'm in the middle of a public-policy master's seminar on the justification of individual rights, and for my paper I have been leaning on a chapter from a trade-press book about fairness and institutions that a friend lent me. The chapter's social-choice section offers the gloss on Sen's liberal paradox reproduced below. Since it is evidently written by someone who knows the material, I had been treating it as reliable; the more times I read it, though, the less certain I am that its conclusions follow from its premises, and I have no way of checking the original paper this week. Here is the passage:

"Sen's result is a proof rather than a slogan: no method of turning individual preferences into a social ranking can be simultaneously democratic, efficient, and respectful of personal liberty. Consider his illustration. A single copy of *Lady Chatterley's Lover* must be handed to one of two men or destroyed unread; one finds the book obscene, the other cannot wait to read it. Let each man hold a right over his own reading — each decisive, against everyone else's opinion, over the pair 'I read it / nobody reads it' — and let the Pareto principle settle everything else. The resulting social ranking is cyclic: Lewd's reading outranks nobody reading, nobody reading outranks Prude's reading, and Prude's reading outranks Lewd's, so there is no best social state at all. The lesson is uncomfortable but inescapable: liberty and efficiency are logically incompatible, which is why the familiar assurance that voluntary exchange secures both is weaker than its partisans suppose, and why any political theory that promises the two together is confused. Sen's own remedy is to abandon the Pareto principle wherever it collides with a right. The economists who objected drew the opposite moral: if respecting someone's right to read a smutty novel leaves a society unable to rank its options coherently, then that right was never a real feature of the world — rights are not weights in a social ranking but constraints on what others may do to you."
