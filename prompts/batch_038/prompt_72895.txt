I've been reading the AI-alignment forums since the debate proposal was little more than a thought experiment plus a toy experiment on sparse MNIST features, and the argument I keep circling without settling is the complexity-theoretic one: that a bounded judge, given two adversarial debaters who exchange short statements in turn, can get traction on questions far outside the reach of direct elicitation, because checking a good argument is cheaper than finding one. I spent eleven years as a compiler engineer before moving into AI policy work, so my instinct is that verification asymmetries are real but load-bearing on details — static analysis fails constantly not because the check is unsound but because the artifact has been arranged so the check cannot see the relevant part. I run a small podcast (about 2,600 subscribers, twice a month, recorded remotely, forty-five minutes per episode), and next week's guest is a research scientist at a frontier lab who defends debate-style oversight in roughly that form: adversarial pressure, short per-turn statements, step-by-step decomposition of arguments, and obfuscation treated as a tractable engineering problem rather than a refutation. He is courteous, technically formidable, and will not be moved by anything that smells like a misreading of the literature.

Please draft ten to twelve interview questions in the order I would actually ask them, each short enough to be spoken aloud in a single breath, and for every one of them give me a line stating what it is designed to force out of him, the answer he is most likely to give, and a follow-up keyed to that specific answer. I want coverage of at least the following, and I want the questions to bite in sequence rather than in parallel: the base case of the argument — the assumption that a bounded judge can reliably settle the simple, atomic claims the debaters are reduced to, and what becomes of the whole edifice if the reduction bottoms out in steps that are short but not easy; the gap between the length of the shortest sound argument for a claim and the amount of argument a judge can actually check inside a fixed attention budget (I have been calling this the obfuscation margin, and I would like him made to treat it as a quantity that varies by domain rather than as a slogan to be waved away); lemma depth — the case where every individual step is checkable in isolation but the argument requires holding a dozen intermediate results in mind at once, so that decomposition into short statements may not restore checkability at all; the analogy with defenses that looked robust only because the attack had been misspecified (obfuscated gradients in adversarial machine learning come to mind, as does a computer-assisted proof no referee has read line by line, or a code diff whose correctness depends on invariants maintained four thousand lines away), and whether he takes these as warnings or as reassurances; the fact that in any real deployment the two debaters are not independent adversaries but products of overlapping training data, shared priors, and possibly a shared interest in keeping the judge comfortable — so what, concretely, supplies the zero-sum pressure the argument leans on; and finally an elicitation question about which measurement he would run and which result would make him abandon debate in favour of a different oversight approach.

Two further constraints. First, no softballs and no throat-clearing: nothing about what drew him to the field, nothing whose honest answer is a slogan, and nothing he can dispose of by saying that the theorem is an illustration rather than a proposal — indeed I would like the sequence arranged so that this particular deflection, which he has reached for in print and on panels, becomes progressively more expensive to use, with the final two questions closed off to it entirely. Second, do not include any question that asks him to name sources, recommend papers, or point listeners to further reading; we cut that segment two seasons ago, and I would rather the questions be answerable from his own understanding than from his reference manager. If the conversation is running long, tell me which three questions to drop and which of their follow-ups to promote into standalone questions.
