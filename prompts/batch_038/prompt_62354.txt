I'm TA-ing the philosophy of mind section this term and for the free-will-and-the-brain week I'm putting up a one-pager on where I actually stand, so the students have something concrete to go after. I'd much rather find the weak joints myself than have a sophomore find them in discussion. Here's the list, roughly in the order I'd defend them:

1. The Libet paradigm isn't broken. There's preparatory brain activity that ramps up well before people report the urge to move — the ramp starts on the order of half a second before the movement, the reported urge only a couple hundred ms before it — and I don't need those numbers to be sacred, but I don't think the whole thing is artifact.

2. That said, I don't think the ramp is a decision. The reinterpretation where the readiness potential is just stochastic neural noise drifting toward a threshold seems much better to me than the story where your brain decided 350 ms ago and you're only catching up. On the noise view there's no determinate decision sitting there in advance of the crossing, so there's nothing consciousness could have failed to make.

3. What I do think survives is the veto — Libet's own escape hatch. In the last stretch of time between the reported urge and the movement, a conscious no can still cancel the act, and I think that modest brake is enough to keep moral responsibility intact. This is the piece I lean on most, by far.

4. I also think the reported times of intention aren't clean readouts. The studies where people make a choice and then get shown an outcome, and their reported moment of choosing drifts toward whatever they were shown — that sort of result is persuasive to me. So I treat the clock report as a constructed judgment rather than a measurement of a mental event.

5. I'm a straight compatibilist: a deterministic universe wouldn't by itself threaten freedom. What matters is whether the action issues from the person's own reasons-responsive machinery, not whether it had prior causes.

6. The long-lead scanning results — predicting a left/right button press something like ten seconds before reported awareness, at maybe 60% — don't show a decision ten seconds early. At most they show which hand someone is leaning toward.

7. And I think the wrist-flick tasks are terrible models of the decisions that actually matter, like picking a lab or deciding to lie to a client. But I don't think that's an exit ramp, because whatever initiation mechanism the lab is exposing should be the same mechanism at work in the big decisions.

So: audit it. Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which pairs only look like they do? I'd rather you tell me two items are fine together and why than pad the list with everything that could conceivably be friction. Rank the real tensions by severity, and for each one say what has to give and what I actually lose by giving it up — including whether the loss breaks the rest of the list or just weakens it. Then tell me which single revision buys me the most consistency for the least cost. I'm not attached to any particular item, and please dont just tell me to be less specific about the timing.
