Two people in my Kant reading group had it out after last week's session on the Doctrine of Virtue's remarks about animals, and I got landed with refereeing. I couldn't do it. Neither would give ground and I've now lost track of who conceded what, so here's the exchange as best I remember it.

Nadia opened by saying Kant's indirect-duty account is structurally broken rather than merely unfashionable: if the wrongness of cruelty lies in its tendency to coarsen the agent's sympathy for human suffering, then in any case where that tendency is absent nothing at all is wrong. Her example was a man who is already thoroughly desensitised, who tortures a cat in complete isolation, will never be near another human being again, and dies a week later. On Kant's own reasoning, she said, he has violated no duty whatsoever, and a theory that lets that through isn't a theory of animal ethics with limits but one with a hole in it.

Ravi's reply was that she'd turned a deontic claim into a causal prediction. Kant isn't saying cruelty is wrong because it will measurably change your character; he's saying the act damages a predisposition in you that is itself morally serviceable, and the duty to oneself is breached in the doing. So the desensitised man escapes nothing — he's already in breach. He added that Kant's own bit about the old dog who has served his master faithfully, where he suggests the animal has as it were earned its keep and must be looked after to the end of its life, shows the constraint tracking something in the animal's own history rather than just the state of the agent's soul.

Nadia said that's exactly her point: if the wrong is entirely in the attitude expressed towards a suffering being, then the suffering is doing the normative work, which is precisely what the indirect view is meant to deny, so Ravi is quietly converting it into something close to a direct duty whilst pretending otherwise. Ravi said no, the suffering only matters because it's analogous to human suffering, and what's being respected is humanity in one's own person; the animal is the occasion, not the object.

Nadia then said the analogy can't carry that much weight, since the whole thing rests on Kant's claim that animals lack apperception — no awareness of themselves as continuing subjects — which is simply an empirical mistake, and a theory whose moral line is drawn by a refuted piece of eighteenth-century psychology isn't going to be rescued by exegesis. Ravi's last move was that this is beside the point: the criterion for standing on Kant's view is the capacity to legislate universal law, not self-consciousness or sentience, so even if you handed animals full apperception he would still refuse them direct duties, and Nadia was arguing against a premise that isnt load bearing.

Then tell me which of them is more right overall, and what both of them are missing. I have a suspicion they're sliding back and forth between what Kant actually believed and what his principles imply, but I coudn't get that across on the night.
