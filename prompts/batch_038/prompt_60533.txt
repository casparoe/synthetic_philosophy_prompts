Ninety minutes with the data and visuals desk of a regional newspaper — six reporters and two assignment editors, fluent in spreadsheets and per-capita rates, with no training in measurement. The aim is that they stop publishing headlines asserting the world is getting more violent, or less violent, without knowing what they have claimed. Plan the session for me; don't deliver the material to me.

Start with the dependency structure: what has to be settled before "is violence declining?" is even a well-posed question. My guess is that both the unit of counting and the boundary of the category precede any appeal to evidence, but tell me what genuinely depends on what, and where you would order it differently from me. Then name the single running case that carries the whole session, and specify what it must contain and what it must exclude — it has to fit on one slide, and it has to break under at least two of the errors below rather than merely illustrating the right answer.

Two errors I expect: that a decade of rising recorded domestic-assault charges shows rising violence rather than a widened definition and improved reporting; and that a long decline in realized violence is evidence about the capacity for a far larger war in the coming decade. Complete the map with the ones I've missed, mark the point in the sequence where each gets headed off, and tell me which one I should deliberately allow to surface before correcting it. End with a single question I can put to the room whose answers would show whether they can now state a trend claim precisely, plus what I should refuse to cover at this level and say out loud that I am refusing. No exact figures are needed: where a case turns on a number, tell me what shape the number must have and why.
