I am three years into a novel whose difficulties are structural rather than sentimental, and I have come to suspect that the structure keeps smuggling in a metaphysics I have never chosen deliberately. The book is organised as a set of nested returns: the same three days are narrated four times, each pass in a different grammatical tense, and the fourth pass is written entirely in the future perfect, so that the reader is continuously looking back at events the narrator has not yet reached. My editor's objection, in a long letter last autumn, was that the fourth section either presupposes those days are already fixed and complete or else it is a stylistic affectation with no cognitive content; I could not answer her, because I do not know which of the two I believe. In June I am leading a nine-day workshop for writers at a residency in the mountains — eleven novelists and two librettists, none of them trained in philosophy, all of them temperamentally resistant to jargon — and I want to hand them an instrument rather than a lecture.

What I would like you to build is a decision tree, of between nine and twelve nodes, that sorts a working novelist by the structural choices she has already made in her manuscript and delivers her at a named position on the reality and architecture of time. Every node must be phrased as a concrete question about craft or about the text itself — whether a chapter placed out of chronological order is a discovery or merely a rearrangement; whether my future-perfect section describes something that is there to be described; whether a character's death continues to be a fact about the world once no one remembers it; whether a scene can be told backwards without loss, and if the loss is in the telling or in the events; whether two of my four narrations of the same three days are descriptions of one thing or of two; whether a fully specified present moment would, in principle, settle what the next chapter says — and not as a question about metaphysical vocabulary. No node may ask anything a reader could answer by recognising a label; if a question could be settled by knowing what a word means, rewrite it. The leaves must include, at minimum: presentism; the growing block; the block universe or eternalism; the moving spotlight; the relational or Leibnizian view on which time is nothing over and above the ordering of events; and the idealist denial that succession is real at all. Treat the A-theory/B-theory distinction and the question of branching versus single-track time explicitly as axes, and say whether each is a node of the tree, a cross-cutting dimension, or an artefact of the questions I have chosen. Each leaf should carry the name of the position, one sentence on what it costs a novelist who adopts it — the commitment she will find most awkward in the fourth year of a draft — and one sentence on which of my four narrations she would be obliged to call the true one, if any. Where the tree cannot genuinely separate two leaves, flag the pair and say which further question would be needed, or concede that no craft question separates them.

Finally, I want the tree turned on Borges, since he is the writer my novel keeps arguing with. Locate, at the leaves you have defined, the position implied by the argument of the essay he wrote in two versions in the nineteen-forties extending Berkeley's and Hume's eliminations to time itself, together with his earlier essay on the history of eternity and his essay on the tortoise and the infinite; and separately locate the stories of the condemned man granted a year of inward duration inside the instant before the volley, of the man whose memory admits every moment without abstraction, and of the labyrinth whose book is a branching of times rather than of space. Do not quote him, and do not supply quotations, page numbers, editions, or a reading list: paraphrase, and where a text you would need to cite precisely is one you can only recall in outline, say so and mark that placement as provisional rather than inventing a passage. Note too which of these texts your own tree leaves undetermined, and why — whether the instrument is too coarse, whether the writer is deliberately inconsistent, or whether the story and the essay simply answer to different questions. Close with the unoccupied cells: taking the cross-product of your axes, identify the combinations of answers that no recognised position inhabits, and for each say briefly whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, because it is unattractive to anyone who has thought about it, or because nobody has yet thought of it — the last being, I confess, the answer I most want to find, since an unexplored cell is the only place a novel has any business standing.
