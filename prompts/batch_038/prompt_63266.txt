I am circulating a written statement of my settled positions ahead of a value-theory workshop whose opening session is devoted to attacking the circulated statements. Before I send it out, I would like a rigorous consistency audit of the position rather than a summary of the neighborhood it comes from. My commitments on incommensurable value and hard choice are these nine.

1. Value pluralism, in a strong sense: the goods at stake in serious deliberation are not measurable against one another on a single scale, and I mean this metaphysically rather than epistemically. The absence of a common measure is a fact about value, not a limit on our knowledge of it.

2. Genuine incomparability exists, and I accept the standard argument for it: if A is neither better nor worse nor exactly equal to B, and a small improvement in B fails to make B better than A, then the pair falls outside the trichotomy of better, worse, and equally good. The force of the argument, as I see it, lies in the contrast between what small improvements fail to do and what sufficiently large improvements would do.

3. Incomparability requires heterogeneity. On my view a pair can be incomparable only if the goods the two options realize belong to different evaluative frameworks. Within a single framework, differences of degree always yield comparability, however hard the choice may be to make.

4. Most hard choices are nevertheless not incomparability cases. For career-scale dilemmas my default diagnosis is parity: a fourth, positive comparative relation in which there is a determinate relation between the options, yet neither is better than the other and they are not exactly equal.

5. Hardness is never mere vagueness. I reject accounts on which a choice is hard only because the relevant comparative concepts are imprecise, since such accounts imply that the difficulty would evaporate under sufficient sharpening, and in the cases I care about it does not.

6. Voluntarism about reasons, unqualified: where comparison leaves the matter open, an agent can create a reason to choose one option by committing to it, and the commitment itself requires no antecedent reason. It is not made for a reason; it makes there be a reason.

7. Deliberative completeness: the exhaustion of reasons is never a legitimate resting place for a rational agent. Deliberation must terminate in a determinate choice; an agent who reports that she cannot rank two live options has not concluded deliberating but abandoned it; and an adequate theory of practical rationality must deliver a unique ranking for every pair of options an agent actually faces.

8. Tragic residue: when an agent permissibly chooses one option in a genuinely hard conflict, the unchosen good does not thereby cease to make claims on her. Regret, and sometimes reparative obligation, survive the choice, and their survival is evidence of the agent's perceptiveness rather than a refusal to stand behind her own decision.

9. A case verdict I intend to defend at the workshop. A conservator must choose between (a) full restoration of a badly damaged fresco, which returns the work to public view but destroys the surviving physical evidence of the painter's underdrawing technique, and (b) stabilization only, which preserves that evidence but confines the work permanently to a study collection where it will not be seen. My verdict: (a) and (b) are incomparable in the sense of (3), and the case stays hard at every scale — the verdict would not change if the restored fresco were to be seen by ten million people rather than ten thousand, nor if the evidence at stake bore on a single minor attribution rather than on the dating of an entire school.

What I want is an audit. Specifically:

(a) Identify the pairs and larger clusters that are in genuine tension, and for each specify the mechanism — which clause of which commitment is denied by which clause of the other, rather than a general observation that the two sit uneasily together.

(b) Rank those tensions by severity, most damaging first. Do not present them as a flat list of worries of equal weight.

(c) For each genuine tension, state the cheapest revision that removes it and what the revision costs me: which of the things I care about in the original claim I would no longer be entitled to say.

(d) Identify the pairs that only appear to conflict and explain why they do not. I expect a number of these, and an audit that flags everything is useless to me.

(e) Tell me whether the verdict in (9) can actually be delivered by my own principles as they stand, or whether the case must be redescribed or re-diagnosed — and if redescribed, what I lose by the redescription in the workshop setting, where the case is meant to be doing independent work.

(f) Identify the single revision that would resolve the most, and defend that choice against the obvious alternative candidates.

Two constraints. I am deliberately not telling you which of these I would be most willing to surrender; treat all nine as equally revisable and let the analysis decide. And please do not supply references or a survey of who holds which view — citations are not the deliverable. The deliverable is a point-by-point assessment of whether this package hangs together.
