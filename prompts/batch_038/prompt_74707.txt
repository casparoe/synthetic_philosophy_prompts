**Question 3 (25 marks). Answer all parts.**

(a) Say what it means to call a consequence relation paraconsistent, and identify the principle whose failure this amounts to. *(5 marks)*

(b) Dialetheism is the view that some contradictions are true. Explain why a dialetheist needs a paraconsistent logic, and what would go wrong if she retained classical logic. *(6 marks)*

(c) Priest's logic LP assigns sentences one of three semantic values. Describe the semantics briefly and explain how LP handles the Liar sentence *this sentence is false*. *(8 marks)*

(d) State which classical principles LP gives up. In particular, is LP's treatment of the Liar like or unlike that of a theory which assigns the Liar no truth value at all, such as Kripke's fixed-point construction? *(6 marks)*

**Candidate's answer:**

(a) A consequence relation is paraconsistent when it does not license the move from a contradiction to an arbitrary conclusion, the principle usually called explosion or *ex contradictione quodlibet*: A, not-A, therefore B. So in a paraconsistent logic one can reason from inconsistent premises without every sentence of the language becoming a theorem. Non-explosiveness is the whole of the definition here: a logic counts as paraconsistent just in case it is not explosive, which is a weaker requirement than being three-valued or having any particular semantics.

(b) If the dialetheist kept classical logic and conceded even a single true contradiction, explosion would make every sentence a theorem; her theory would be trivial and would carry no information at all. Since she wants to hold that certain specific contradictions, such as the Liar, are true while most contradictions are not, she needs a logic in which inconsistency does not entail triviality. That is exactly what paraconsistency supplies. So dialetheism and paraconsistent logic are really two sides of one project: a paraconsistent logic is the formal component of dialetheism, and anyone who adopts one is committed to the other. One cannot work in a paraconsistent logic without believing that some contradictions are true, and one cannot believe that some contradictions are true without abandoning classical logic.

(c) LP has three semantic values: true only, false only, and both true and false. A sentence is designated, and hence preserved by valid inference, if it is true — so both the first and the third values are designated. Negation flips true only and false only, and leaves both true and false unchanged; the other connectives behave in the natural way. The Liar sentence says of itself that it is false. If we suppose it is true, then what it says obtains and it is false; if we suppose it is false, then what it says obtains and it is true. Both routes lead to the same place, so LP assigns the sentence the third value, both true and false. The advantage is that the reasoning is carried out inside a single language: because LP has a truth predicate that applies to its own sentences, there is no need to climb a Tarskian hierarchy of object languages and metalanguages, which is the main cost of the orthodox treatments.

(d) What LP gives up is bivalence, understood as the claim that every sentence receives exactly one of true and false. Since the Liar has both values rather than one, the law of excluded middle, A or not-A, no longer comes out valid, and neither does the law of non-contradiction, not-(A and not-A). In this respect LP resembles intuitionistic logic, which also refuses excluded middle — the difference being that the intuitionist withholds truth from the Liar while the dialetheist hands it both values. The comparison with Kripke is similar: his fixed-point construction also declines to give the Liar a classical value, and the two approaches differ mainly in bookkeeping. This is why the third value is best understood as a way of containing the contradiction: the dialetheist does not have to endorse the Liar outright, only to recognise that it falls into a special category of sentence. It also follows that the revenge problem is not a serious difficulty for LP, since whatever strengthened version of the paradox the critic constructs will simply receive the third value again, and a sentence that has that value is already outside the range of classical principles.
