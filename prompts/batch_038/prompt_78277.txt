So my sister is starting a PhD in economic history in the fall and her statement of purpose got torn apart by one of the referees, who basically accused her of being a historicist of the kind Popper buried back in the fifties. She forwarded me the report and I've been reading The Poverty of Historicism to work out whether the guy actually has a point, and I've hit a wall I can't get past on my own.

Her project is conditional, or so she insists: roughly, given a certain bundle of property-rights institutions, some threshold of state capacity, and enough urban density, a region industrialises within a couple of generations. She says that's just law plus initial conditions, the same form a physicist uses when they say a bridge will fail under a given load, so Popper's complaint about prophecy can't touch her. The referee says it touches her anyway. Reading along, I can see two completely different complaints in there that seem to get mashed togther: one is about trends being mistaken for laws, his point being that a trend hangs on initial conditions and a single change can flip it, while a law has to hold unconditionally and independent of time and place. The other is about knowledge, the bit where he says we can't predict the future growth of our own knowledge because if we could predict it we'd already have it, and since the course of history is strongly influenced by the growth of knowledge, history itself can't be predicted. Is my sister's work vulnerable to both of these, or only one? What would she have to add to her conditional generalisations for Popper to count them as legitimate scientific prediction, and is there anything she could add that isn't just a redescription of the past?

Then there's the objection I keep hearing from people in her department, which is that reflexivity cuts both ways: if publishing a prediction changes the behaviour it predicts, that's not a reason to give up on laws, it's a sign the law was incomplete, and the right move is to build the effects of the prediction's own publication into the theory itself. That seems plausible to me for the trends stuff. But does it have any purchase on the knowledge argument? My gut says no, since there the problem isn't that a prediction would alter the outcome, it's that the prediction is impossible in the first place — but I can't articulate why, and I suspect I'm hand-waving. Related worry coming from the other direction: my housemate thinks the knowledge argument proves way too much. She says on the same logic we couldn't predict that an effective malaria vaccine shows up within a decade, or that some particular encryption scheme gets broken, and we clearly do make sensible bets about things like that all the time. Is she right that it over-generates, or is there a principled line between predicting that someone will come up with idea X and predicting that idea X will get arrived at? I mostly want the place where the two arguments bite differently, not a summary of the book.
