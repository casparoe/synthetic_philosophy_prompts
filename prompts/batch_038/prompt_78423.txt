I have no training in mathematics or philosophy — I read around these things for pleasure, slowly, and I have never had anyone check whether I was reading them correctly — so I would like a careful referee rather than an introduction to the subject. Last month two people I play cards with argued for the better part of an hour about an old puzzle, and I still cannot tell which of them was making sense. I will report the exchange as it went and try not to lean toward either side.

The puzzle, which I understand a French mathematician put into a probability textbook in 1889 precisely as a warning: draw an equilateral triangle inside a circle so that its three corners touch the circle, then choose a chord of the circle at random. What is the chance the chord is longer than a side of the triangle?

(1) Elliot said the answer is one third, and he gave his reasoning: hold one end of the chord fixed, and the other end is as likely to land anywhere on the rim as anywhere else; the chord beats the triangle only when that second end falls within a third of the way round. He said this is just the obvious rule of treating possibilities alike when you have nothing to distinguish them.

(2) Marisa said there are three procedures that are each about as obvious as his, and they disagree. Pick a radius and then a spot along it, and draw the chord through that spot at right angles: one half. Or pick any spot inside the circle and take the chord for which it is the middle: one quarter. So, she said, the words at random do not pick out any procedure at all, the puzzle has no answer as worded, and anyone who announces a number has quietly made a choice they have not admitted to.

(3) Elliot said that if one sentence yields three numbers, the sentence is what is broken, not the arithmetic — and then he went further, saying this convinced him there is no such thing as a probability belonging to a situation, only probabilities belonging to ways of describing situations.

(4) Marisa said she did not need to settle that, because she had run the thing. She works in a print shop and had paper to spare: she printed sheets of identical circles, cut drinking straws to roughly four times a circle's width, scattered them onto the sheets from standing height, and kept only the straws that lay right across a circle with both ends sticking out. Of 312 such straws, 150 cut a chord longer than the triangle's side — a shade under one half. She said that settles it: the answer is one half, and the other two numbers belong to procedures nobody can carry out with anything as ordinary as a straw.

(5) Elliot said the experiment is rigged by the straws. A longer straw lands across a circle more often than a shorter one, so the count must depend on how long she cut them; with straws ten times the circle's width, he said, she would have got a different proportion, and she had simply chosen the length that flattered her favourite number.

(6) Marisa said that is backwards: she made them long on purpose so that length would stop mattering. She added that you could see the result on the sheets — the middle points of her chords were crowded toward the centre of each circle, with bare patches near the rim — and that this is exactly why one quarter is wrong, since that number comes from pretending a middle point is as likely near the edge as near the middle.

(7) Elliot said the crowding is an artefact of throwing straws, and that her scatter is one physical process among indefinitely many while the puzzle never mentioned a process. So her experiment cannot be an answer to the puzzle; at most it is a description of her afternoon.

(8) Marisa said the puzzle does not say how big the circle is or where it sits, so an honest answer cannot be allowed to depend on either. Her test: draw a smaller circle inside the big one, off to one side. Her scatter gives the small circle the same one half it gives the big one, because a straw does not care which circle it happens to cross. But the even-spread-over-the-interior rule gives the off-centre small circle a different proportion of long chords than it gives the big one, and the fixed-endpoints rule goes wrong as soon as you change the circle's size at all. On her test, one third fails both ways, one quarter fails on position, and one half is the only one that survives.

(9) Elliot's last move was that she had chosen the test after seeing which number it favoured. He said there are endlessly many candidate rules with some claim to be natural — for instance, being indifferent about the length of the chord itself, so that every length from nothing to the full width is equally likely, which he worked out to a little over thirteen in a hundred — and that unless she can say why indifference about position and size outranks indifference about length, what they have is a stalemate between two tastes.

Neither budged, and I have been turning it over ever since. Please go through the exchange move by move, from (1) to (9), and for each one say whether it is clearly mistaken, partly right, or clearly right, with a sentence or two of justification. Then tell me who came out ahead, and on which points specifically. I would especially like to know: whether 150 out of 312 is actually enough to separate one half from one third and one quarter, or whether the count is too thin to do the work Marisa wants; whether her scattering of straws proves anything at all about the puzzle as it was originally worded; whether Elliot is right that the sentence itself is defective, and whether his larger claim in (3) follows from that; whether the requirement that the answer be indifferent to the circle's position and size is a legitimate constraint or a smuggled preference; and whether Elliot's thirteen-percent candidate really stands on a par with hers under that requirement, or whether he was entitled to assume it does. Finally, please say what you think the answer to the puzzle is, if it has one, and name anything important that both of them missed.
