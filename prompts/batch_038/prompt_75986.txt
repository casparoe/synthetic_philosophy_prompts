So — I'm giving a 40-minute talk at a medical school workshop on gamete donation and embryo policy, with about 25 minutes of Q&A after. The room is mostly clinical embryologists and fertility doctors, a couple of bioethicists who work on donor registries, one family lawyer who does parentage cases, and exactly one metaphysician, who is a four-dimensionalist and works on vagueness. I'm the token philosopher and I'm flying in the night before. My talk defends origin essentialism: the claim that you could not have come from a different egg and a different sperm, that the particular gametes you came from are necessary to you, its not just a contingent fact about which ones happened to make you. I know how that sounds to people who handle embryos for a living, and I know the panel chair has already said by email that he thinks metaphysics is word games, so I've deliberately built the case without the famous wooden-table example and I want the prep sheet to target the argument as I've actually built it, not the textbook version.

My argument has two legs. First, a slippery slope: if you think you could have come from a slightly different gamete pair — say a different sperm from the same ejaculate, same egg — on the grounds that the difference is negligible, then the same reasoning applies one step further out, and again, and after enough steps you've licensed the claim that you could have come from gametes with no connection at all to your actual ones, which is just giving up on origins explaining anything. So either you draw the line at zero deviation or you lose the whole idea. Second, a positive story about why: identity has to run along a non-branching causal-historical route, and gamete provenance is the only candidate that's actually non-branching — the zygote isn't, because it can split into two, and neither the zygote nor the body is a safe anchor for the same sort of reason. I then claim this isn't purely academic: it's what's doing the work in the intuition behind identity-release donation and the "right to know your origins," and I argue that the non-identity stuff doesn't dissolve that.

Give me the questions I'll actually get from this room. Not the ones a metaphysics seminar would raise — no counterpart-theory lecture unless the four-dimensionalist is the one asking, and if he is, tell me how he'd put it, because I can guess but I want it in his words. I want: seven or eight objections ordered by how much damage each one does to the talk; each phrased in the vocabulary of whoever's raising it (an embryologist is not going to say transworld identity, she's going to say something about ICSI, or spindle transfer, or vitrification, or mosaicism); a reply I can deliver in about 90 seconds for each; an honest note on which of my two legs each objection is aimed at and where my reply is softest; what I should concede up front in the talk itself so I'm not conceding under fire; and the one question I'm praying nobody asks, plus the least-bad thing I could say if it lands. Flag any objection where I'm better off just saying "that's a real problem and I don't have an answer" than bluffing, because this crowd will know immediately. And keep it self-contained — I need something I can read on the plane, so don't point me off to go read anything.
