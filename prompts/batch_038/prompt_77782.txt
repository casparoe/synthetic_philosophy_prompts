I spent thirty-eight years running a soil-and-crop physiology group at a land-grant university, most of it designing randomized block trials for extension agents, and since retiring I have taken up philosophy in a desultory but earnest way. Dewey has been the most arresting discovery, chiefly because he insists that democracy is not merely a machinery for aggregating preferences but a mode of inquiry — a claim I at first found maddeningly loose, since in my own working life inquiry was scrupulously apolitical and its politics, such as they were, concerned only who got the tractor. Last month I attended a public seminar at which a policy institute laid out a concrete institutional proposal, and I should like your assessment of how Dewey, were he alive and fully apprised of it, would judge it.

The proposal — call it the Adaptive Compact — runs as follows. A mid-sized state delegates roughly three percent of its education and housing budget to a standing panel of four hundred citizens selected by sortition, stratified to reproduce the demographic composition of the electorate, serving staggered eighteen-month terms. The panel enacts nothing. It designs and authorizes controlled policy trials in municipalities that consent to participate; where feasible, assignment is randomized, and a permanent non-partisan evaluation office reports back effect estimates with uncertainty intervals. Municipalities that decline may still adopt whatever proves effective, at their own expense. Two features complicate the picture. First, in the pilot jurisdictions, survey work indicated that most citizens encountered the panel through short algorithmically amplified clips of its more heated exchanges rather than through its published reasoning, and the institute proposes to treat this as an unavoidable condition of the informational environment rather than a defect to be engineered away. Second, a rival faction at the seminar would abolish sortition in favor of a stakeholder assembly whose seats are allocated to organized groups in proportion to their measured exposure to a given policy's consequences.

Taking Dewey's commitments as seriously as you can — his quarrel with Lippmann, his account of a public as something that comes into being only where the indirect consequences of transactions are perceived and organized, his insistence that ends-in-view are reconstructed in the course of inquiry rather than fixed in advance of it, his resistance to identifying democracy with the counting of heads, and his conviction that expert knowledge turns tyrannical the moment it is severed from the experience of the many — which parts of the Compact would he recognize as experimentalism properly conducted, and which would he regard as technocracy dressed in experimentalist clothing? Two points interest me most. The pre-registration clause seems to me a head-on collision with his fallibilism about ends: a panel that must declare in advance what result counts as success has foreclosed precisely the reconstruction of the problem that he thought inquiry was for, and I cannot tell whether he would treat that as a fatal objection or as a bookkeeping convenience. And the clipping problem seems to me graver than the institute admits, since on his account a public that cannot perceive what is being done in its name has not been misinformed so much as prevented from existing at all; the Compact may be generating excellent evidence for a public that the Compact simultaneously dissolves. I should also like to know whether he would prefer the sorted panel or the stakeholder assembly, or would reject the framing of that choice outright. Close with the single amendment you believe he would demand as a condition of his support, and then with the strongest objection a serious Dewey scholar would raise against your reconstruction — I want to see plainly where your extrapolation is least disciplined. Please do not trouble yourself with quotations or references; I have the texts at hand and am after your reasoning about the commitments, not apparatus.
