I've been going down a rabbit hole on population ethics for the case of infinitely many people — I'm auditing a grad seminar this semester and what started as a term paper topic has kind of metastasized into a personal research program, so bear with me while I state the thing carefully. Here's the view I've talked myself into; I'll call it the equidensity view. Take worlds containing countably many people, each with a well-defined welfare level. Compare two such worlds only by the density of lives above the neutral level: W1 is better than W2 just in case W1's density is strictly higher, where "density" is cashed out as coming out higher on *every* shift-invariant finitely additive measure over positions, so we never have to pretend the Cesaro limit is the uniquely privileged way of averaging. When two worlds tie on all such measures, I say they are exactly equally good — not incomparable, equally good — and I refuse to bolt on any tiebreaker, because every tiebreaker I've looked at (leximax, discounting by position, anything that resolves a stalemate by inspecting who shows up early in the ordering) is positional privilege dressed up as a principle. Two consequences I actually like rather than regret: adding a merely finite number of wonderful lives to a world that already has infinitely many changes nothing at all, and the heaven-and-hell style worlds where half the people are blissful and half are wretched come out equal no matter how they're arranged.

So: what would count against it? I want a genuine map of its exposure, not a list of standard complaints about infinite ethics. Specifically — (1) which formal results or axiomatizations would show it's incoherent, or would collapse it into something simpler; e.g. if unanimity across all shift-invariant measures turns out to be extensionally equivalent to just comparing Cesaro limits wherever those exist, then I've bought a lot of machinery for nothing, and I'd want to know whether that's true or false. (2) Which cases can it not describe or does it get badly wrong — including worlds where density isn't defined at all (blocks of increasing length, sets with no natural density) and worlds that tie on density but differ in how the good lives are spread versus clustered. (3) Which of these costs would a committed equidensity theorist *concede* as counting against them and which would they wave off as question-begging — I want that split made explicit, especially on the Pareto point, since I suspect I'm forced to call a strict finite improvement "equally good," and I can't tell whether that's a bullet I get to bite or a concession I have to make. (4) What does the view predict that rivals like leximax or an overtaking criterion don't, so I can see whether it's paying rent anywhere.

And then the honest bit: have I insulated it by qualification? I've got a fallback at every step — Cesaro where it's defined, unanimity where it isn't, incomparability where unanimity is silent — and I worry that makes the thing close to riskless, since any counterexample gets absorbed by whichever layer it lands on. If it is that well protected, is that a defect here the way it would be for an empirical theory, or is "risk" just the wrong frame for a normative proposal? I'd like you to say what the analog of a failed prediction even is for a view like this, if there is one. Rank the defeaters from fatal to merely awkward as you go. For what it's worth, what would actually move me is a proof rather than an intuition: show me the theorem and I'll change my mind, whereas one more thought experiment about spread-out versus clustered good lives and I'll probably just add a footnote.
