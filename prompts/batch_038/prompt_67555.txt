I work on the RL side of an LLM team, no philosophy background, so forgive the vocab. There's a thing about our own pipeline I keep coming back to and I can't tell whether it's just obvious to everyone else or whether nobody has actually written it down.

Roughly what we do: crowdworkers get shown a prompt plus two model outputs and asked which is better. We fit a Bradley-Terry style model to all those pairwise picks, which gives us one scalar per output, train a reward model on that scalar, then run RL against it with a KL penalty back toward the base model so the policy doesn't wander too far. "Better" is supposed to carry helpful, harmless and honest all at the same time, in one number. We reuse the same scalar to rerank samples at inference. The reward model gets frozen for the whole run even though the policy is drifting away from the distribution it was fitted on.

Half my job is defending against reward hacking, and everyone here will tell you plainly that optimizing a proxy too hard breaks the thing you actually wanted. We length-normalize or tell the raters to ignore length, because we know longer answers get picked more often. We log the cases where the model games the score and shrug.

So what theory of value is all this quietly running on? A few I can half see: that the goodness of a response is a single real number; that it's a property of the text alone, not of how it was produced or what the model was "up to"; that different people's judgments can be pooled into one ordering; that any two answers are comparable. Does it also assume the raters' mistakes are noise that averages out, when we know they're systematic and in a known direction? And what is the KL term doing normatively — it reads to me like "typical is safe," which feels like a strange thing to bake into an objective about goodness.

The part that bugs me most: is any of this consistent with what the same field says elsewhere — that human values are plural and don't reduce, that a model shouldn't just be agreeable, that sincerity matters? If the model outputs something it doesn't believe because that scores higher, our pipeline literally cannot see the difference. Are we commited to something like "what matters is satisfying the preferences people express when forced to choose," or is the practice compatible with several different views and we just never picked one?

What I want out of this isn't a label. Reconstruct the implicit theory as carefully as you can. Separate what the pipeline strictly requires from what it merely happens to fit well with. Tell me which rival views of value or of evidence it rules out without ever saying so. Point at where it's philosophically underdetermined — where two very different theories would license exactly the same code. Say which parts of it contradict each other, if any do, and whether the contradiction is in the objective or in how we talk about it. And if we swapped the scalar for something else — multi-objective rewards, rule-based checks, whatever — what changes in what we're committed to, not just in the implementation. Doesn't matter to me if you don't cite anything, I mostly want the reasoning.
