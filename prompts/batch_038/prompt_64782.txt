I do philosophy of law for a living and read Kant on the side, and I've somehow talked myself into opening our reading group's Kant term with twenty minutes on where I've landed with the universalisability tests. Half the room has never opened the Groundwork; the other half has read nothing else and will pounce. So before I say any of this out loud, I'd like someone to take the list apart.

Here's what I'd be committing to:

1. The test applies to maxims, not to bare actions or outcomes, and the maxim that gets tested is the one the agent herself would offer, in her own words. I don't think there's a God's-eye register of correct maxim descriptions sitting behind the agent.
2. But an agent can't wriggle out of a verdict just by tailoring her description finely enough — "lying to extract money from a gullible stranger on a wet Tuesday" — so there has to be some constraint on how specific the maxim may be before the test loses its bite.
3. I like the practical-contradiction reading best: a maxim fails the contradiction-in-conception test when its universalisation would defeat the very purpose the agent has in acting on it. The false promise is the paradigm case — if promising-what-you-don't-intend became universal law, promising itself would collapse, and the liar couldn't get the money she was after.
4. The CI is purely formal. It doesn't import values, ends, or claims about human flourishing, and that's precisely what makes it non-consequentialist and what makes it binding on any rational being whatsoever rather than only on creatures shaped like us.
5. Contradiction in the will does substantive work: it's how the imperfect duties get generated, and I think Kant's argument for beneficence there is broadly successful.
6. Perfect duties admit of no exceptions in the agent's favour, but imperfect duties carry latitude — I get to decide when, how far, and on whom to bestow help.
7. O'Neill is right that treating the CI as a decision procedure you consult on each occasion misreads what it's for; it tests the principles an agent lives by.
8. I accept Kant's own verdict in the murderer-at-the-door case. Lying is out, and I don't think the system should be quietly re-engineered to make that verdict disappear.
9. The Formula of Universal Law and the Formula of Humanity aren't just paraphrases of one another — a maxim can clear the first and still be ruled out by the second, and they do different kinds of work.

Which of these genuinely can't all stand together, and which only look like they clash until you push on them? I want the real conflicts ranked by severity rather than dumped in a pile, and for each one: what exactly has to give, what's the cheapest revision, and what I lose philosophically by making it. If one of them is load-bearing in a way I haven't noticed, say so. Please answer in British English, and skip the bibliography and the caveats about contested scholarship — I just want the audit.
