I run a small interview series attached to our department's workshop on historical method, and next month's guest is an environmental historian whose first book proposes a revision of Braudel that my officemates and I have been arguing about for weeks. Her claim, as I understand it, is that the longue durée is not a layer of the world but a property of the evidence. What Braudel treats as the slowest and most explanatory timescale — the geography of the Mediterranean, its mountains and coasts, its trade routes and harvest rhythms — is simply what a record converges on once documents have decayed, been destroyed, been copied only in summary, and been preserved chiefly where they were administratively durable. The archive behaves like a low-pass filter: the near-invariant survives, the ephemeral does not, and the historian mistakes the resolution of her instruments for the structure of reality. She thinks this rescues Braudel from the charge of geographical determinism, because on her reading a structure is not a force bearing down on actors but a limit on what can be reconstructed about them; and she thinks it also explains why the second of Braudel's three timescales — conjunctures, cycles, price series — feels shakier than the first, since conjunctures rest on series that are only partly durable. She is very good at retreating into the vocabulary of resolution, and at insisting that events are not less real, only less recoverable.

What I want from you is ten to twelve questions, in the order I should ask them, that go after the substance rather than inviting her to restate the thesis. Specific targets I need covered: the proxy problem, since tree rings, ice cores, lake sediments, and the long European grape-harvest series give us the slow layer at annual or better resolution and look like precisely the case where the slow is not filtered out of the record — I want to know what has to be true of her account for it to survive that; Sewell's argument in "Three Temporalities: Toward an Eventful Sociology" that a Braudelian structure cannot be transformed from within and that events reorganize structures — does making structure epistemic dissolve that objection, or merely relocate it; the middle timescale, and where she draws the line between a real cycle and a documentary one; Part III of The Mediterranean, the history of events and individuals, which I want her to say is either a failure, a concession, or the control condition that makes the other two parts intelligible; the political stakes, given that Braudel taught for years in Algiers and that the charge of presenting North Africa and the Ottoman world as timeless is a serious one — on her account the timelessness lives in the archive he inherited, which could defuse the charge or sharpen it; a direct question about what finding would actually refute her, which I want posed as a serious methodological point rather than a gotcha; and one question early on that lets her say what she thinks historians ought to do differently, because I do not want the whole hour to be adversarial.

For each question, give the follow-up keyed to the reply she is most likely to give — assume she reaches for resolution, for the recoverability-versus-reality distinction, and for the claim that every historian already grants that sources are uneven — and mark in a phrase what would count as her conceding ground rather than restating the thesis. Then tell me which single question is the hinge, the one where a bad answer costs the book its reason for existing, and which one she will try to slide past. Finally, I expect one of the two historians in the audience to object that she has dressed ordinary source criticism in the vocabulary of time, and that a claim about what survives in an archive has nothing to do with a claim about temporal scale; build me a question that forces the distinction instead of letting her nod along with the objection. Keep everything paraphrased — no invented quotations from Braudel or anyone else — and do not close with suggestions for further reading. I want the questions, not a bibliography.
