I go to a Tuesday night reading group where three of us work on Nyāya and one guy does Buddhist pramāṇa theory, and they have never once agreed about anything, which is most of the fun. A few weeks back we were on the opening list of four sources and I sketched an argument for why the list should collapse — half as a joke, half to annoy the Buddhist — and then I couldn't put it down, because the same argument seems to run twice and land in two different places, and both runs feel like they go through. Writing it out below as cleanly as I can.

(1) Call something a seperate pramāṇa only if it has work to do that no other pramāṇa can do. If everything M would deliver could equally be delivered by some other source doing the actual producing, then M isn't a source, it's a way of describing what came out.
(2) Now take śabda. A trustworthy person tells me there's water past the ridge. What actually generates my belief? An inference, apparently: this is the speech of an āpta, an āpta's speech about matters like this tracks how things are, therefore water past the ridge. The sounds are just the trigger; the epistemic work is done by my read on the speaker. This is the two-pramāṇa line the Buddhists press and I've never heard an answer to it I was happy with.
(3) Upamāna collapses even faster. The forester tells me a gavaya is like a cow, months later I see one standing in a field, and what produces the cognition "that's the thing he meant" is perception of the animal plus memory of the instruction. So it's śabda and pratyakṣa in a trenchcoat.
(4) But (2) and (3) only push testimony onto inference, and inference doesn't hold up either. Pervasion — no smoke anywhere without fire — is only ever known from cases I've actually run into: the hearth, the smithy, the burnt field where there's smoke and no fire. Strip out the perceptual acquaintance with instances and there's nothing for the inference to run on. I know Nyāya wants to say the karaṇa here is the parāmarśa, the third cognition where the pervaded reason is located in the subject — but that parāmarśa is just remembered pervasion applied to a perceived locus, so it doesn't introduce anything that isn't perception and memory already.
(5) So from (1)–(4): everything reduces to pratyakṣa, and there's exactly one pramāṇa. Which is where the materialists started, and I hate it, because I don't want my knowledge of the past, of other minds, or of a proof in arithmetic to be some species of seeing.
(6) Except the same premise runs backwards. Determinate perception — seeing that as water, that as smoke, that face as my sister's — requires concepts, and I didn't coin a single one of them; every concept I have reached me out of other people's mouths. And perception with all of that stripped out, the indeterminate kind, isn't a source of anything I'd call knowing — it's a registration that never becomes a claim about anything. So the operative producer of every cognition capable of being knowledge is instruction from others, and everything reduces to śabda. One pramāṇa again, only the other one.
(7) Steps that look equally good in both directions, driven by the same criterion, giving two incompatible answers. So either (1) isn't the criterion it pretends to be, or both conclusions hold, which is absurd. Something has to give and I can't find it.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look unobjectionable when I wrote it — I want the diagnosis, not just the verdict. I'll admit up front I'm most attached to (1) and to (2), and (5) and (6) obviously can't both stand. If you think the argument is actually valid and the question is which plausible premise is cheapest to abandon, fine, but then price them all for me, including what the loser gives up: what happens to knowledge of the past, or of dharma, on the surviving view. And I want the answer from inside the tradition's own machinery where it has any — its account of instruments, of what counts as the producing cause versus a mere condition, of how determinate perception is supposed to work — not just a modern gloss about two senses of "depends on," though if you think the modern gloss is genuinely the better answer, say that and say why the tradition's resources are worse. Finally, whatever you settle on has to face the hard case: statements about things wholly beyond the senses, where there are no observed instances to check pervasion against and no independently certified āpta to vouch for the speaker. If your fix handles that, show me how. If it leaves it a mystery, admit it.
