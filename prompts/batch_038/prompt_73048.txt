I sit on the Priority-Setting Panel of a regional health authority — eleven members: four clinicians, two health economists, a patient representative, three lay members, and me. For eleven years the panel has appraised proposals for the authority's £48m "Healthy Lives" fund in quality-adjusted life years, against a cost-per-QALY threshold of £30,000. A coalition of mental-health and social-care providers has been pressing the board to replace that metric with a wellbeing-based one, and the board has asked our panel to decide the question before the next appraisal round opens in six weeks. I have to circulate a memo that the panel can vote on.

The proposed metric works as follows. Each funded programme reports its effect in years of life-satisfaction gained, measured with the authority's annual population survey instrument: an 0–10 ladder question, plus three anchoring vignettes describing a person in full health, a person whose walking is limited to roughly 200 metres, and a person in the worst health state respondents can imagine. Respondents place the vignettes on the same ladder, and individual answers are then linearly re-anchored so that the vignettes come out at fixed values; the stated purpose is to put respondents who use the ladder differently onto a common scale. One ladder point sustained for one year is provisionally converted at 0.05 "wellbeing-adjusted life years" (WALYs), a ratio the economics unit fitted from a single cross-sectional regression of QALY weights on ladder scores in the 2019 wave. The board proposes to apply the same £30,000 threshold per WALY.

Two pilots are on the table for the coming round:

- Programme S — community companionship and social prescribing. Cost £1,200 per participant for a three-year course. Piloted in 2019. Ladder gain +0.42 points, sustained three years. QALY gain +0.006 per participant per year. On the QALY metric: £66,700 per QALY, fails. On the wellbeing metric: £19,000 per WALY, passes.
- Programme H — elective hip replacement. Cost £4,800 per patient, one-off. Piloted in 2024. Ladder gain +0.55 points, sustained two years. QALY gain +0.22 per patient. On the QALY metric: £21,800 per QALY, passes. On the wellbeing metric: £87,300 per WALY, fails.

So the two metrics do not merely disagree at the margin; they reverse the ranking of these two programmes outright.

Last month the authority's statisticians filed a note that has split the panel. Mean vignette placements have moved between waves. In 2019 the full-health vignette was placed at 8.6, limited mobility at 3.4, and worst imaginable health at 1.2. In 2024 full health is still at 8.6, but limited mobility is now placed at 4.3 and worst imaginable health at 0.9. Over the same period, a fixed clinical cohort — re-interviewed every wave, with no change in its recorded clinical status — has seen its mean re-anchored ladder score rise by 0.6 points. The statisticians offer two readings: either this cohort is genuinely faring better, or respondents now use the ladder differently and interpret the vignettes differently, in which case the re-anchoring procedure has failed at the exact point where it was supposed to do its work, since it assumes vignette interpretation is stable across waves and that assumption cannot be tested from inside the survey. They ran three defensible corrections for Programme H's 2024 figures: piecewise linear re-anchoring on all three vignettes gives +0.36; leaving raw scores untouched, on the grounds that it is the vignettes rather than the ladder that changed meaning, gives +0.55; re-anchoring on the invariant upper vignette alone, assuming proportional compression below it, gives +0.71. These correspond to £133,000, £87,300 and £67,600 per WALY. The note observes that none of the three changes the pass/fail verdicts for S and H in the coming round, because Programme S was measured in 2019 and needs no correction. It does say that if the first correction is the right one, then applying a conversion ratio fitted on 2019 data to ladder scores collected from 2024 onward understates the cost per WALY of programmes sitting in the lower half of the ladder by roughly a third — which is where nearly every future social-care and mental-health proposal will sit.

One lay member has said she does not see how the panel can compare one person's satisfaction with another's at all, under either metric, and would rather we allocate by rule of thumb and stop pretending to a scale. The patient representative points out that Programme H's modest ladder gain is exactly what you would expect from people who have lived with a bad hip for years and have already adjusted to it, and that a metric which rewards that adjustment is a metric that quietly defunds the sickest patients. The economics unit replies that the QALY has its own version of the same trouble, since its health-state weights come from members of the public imagining lives they have never led.

Please draft the memo I will circulate. It must begin with the memo itself — no preface, no covering note to me, no restating of the situation — and its first line must state the recommendation: adopt the wellbeing metric, do not adopt it, or adopt it under conditions you then spell out as operative rules the appraisal team could actually implement. The memo should also:

1. Say what happens to Programmes S and H in the coming round under your recommendation, and how the £48m is to be appraised meanwhile.
2. Explain to a lay member what the vignette drift does and does not undermine, and state plainly whether the spread across the three corrections changes your recommendation and why or why not.
3. Take a position on the status of the 0.05 conversion ratio, given that it was fitted on a wave whose scale may no longer exist, and given that it, rather than the ladder scores, is what determines whether anything clears the threshold.
4. Set out the strongest case for the position you are rejecting — the patient representative's adaptation point and the lay member's scepticism about comparing satisfactions across persons both deserve real engagement, not a paragraph of concession — and say why it nonetheless loses, or say precisely what would have to be shown for it to win.
5. Specify what you want the statistics unit to collect or run over the next twelve months, with the trigger and the date on which the panel would revisit this decision.

Argue from the figures and assumptions given above rather than from the wider literature; do not cite studies, name authorities, or supply a reading list. Aim for roughly 1,100 to 1,400 words.
