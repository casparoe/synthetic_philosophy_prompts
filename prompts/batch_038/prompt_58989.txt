hey — I write a science-and-ideas column for a general-interest magazine, mostly about how institutions decide things, and I'm stuck on a term. No philosophy training to speak of, so bear with me.

Here's the pattern I keep running into. Some body — a grant panel, a hospital trust, a company doing its annual impact review — wants to divide a fixed pot so that total wellbeing or total value comes out as high as possible. To do that it has to measure, and the measurement is always a proxy: self-reports, written applications, testimonials, dashboards, review scores. Fine, everybody knows that, and I'm not after the general point about proxies. What I keep noticing is narrower and I don't have a word for it.

Suppose two claimants are exactly alike in how much value they actually produce per pound spent. One of them is dramatically better at making their output show up in the proxy: vivid case narratives, well-organised applications, quotable beneficiaries, the right boxes filled in. The other is equally good at the work but inarticulate, or works on something whose gains are diffuse and awkward to write about, or comes from a setting where claiming credit is frowned on. The allocator — which is honest, and is not rewarding rhetoric on purpose — gives the first one the money. And here's the part I care about: total value doesn't go up. It may well go down, because the second claimant's project was just as productive and got starved. The system isn't failing to maximise the real target so much as it's maximising a record of the target, and the record is cheaper to produce for some people than for others.

Two instances I have in hand. One: a rare-disease charity that allocates using patient-reported measures ends up concentrated on the conditions with the most organised, media-savvy patient groups, and the suffering totals barely move — I've got the numbers, they're flat. Two: an internal promotion process where reviewers were asked to score demonstrated value and reliably promoted whoever narrated thier year best; the HR audit afterwards found the promoted group's output was statistically indistinguishable from the passed-over group's.

Now, I know the classic puzzle in this neighbourhood, the one where a single person can convert resources into so much more happiness than anyone else that a welfare-maximising scheme ought to pour everything into them and let everybody else go short. That is not my case, and I want to be clear about that. Nobody in my examples is a better converter. The whole advantage sits in the reporting channel, and it's a standing feature of any allocator that has to measure at all, not a freak of nature you can argue away by saying such people don't exist.

So: does this have an established name? And I'd rather you ruled my suspects in or out than just listed options at me:

- Is it a species of the familiar point that when a measure becomes a target it stops being a good measure? My instinct is no. In my cases the proxy doesn't get distorted by being aimed at — it stays faithful wherever it registers anything at all. The failure is about who gets registered, and how much it costs them to be.
- Is it the same thing as the old objection to levelling by welfare, the one about people who cultivate lavish tastes and then demand compensation for them? Doesn't feel right either. My claimants haven't cultivated anything; they've just got more bandwidth into the instrument.
- Is it the mirror image of the argument that people in bad circumstances learn to want less and so report less need than they actually have? That looks like the same channel problem pointed the other way, and I genuinely don't know whether the two are one concept with opposite signs or two different mechanisms that happen to rhyme.
- And is there anything in the work on who is believed when they speak, or on who turns up to be counted at all, that already swallows this whole thing? I have a vague sense I'm describing something someone has already named from a completely different direction.

What I want is a committed answer, not a menu. Pick the single best treatment — the established term if there really is one, or a name you coin and would defend — and define it tightly enough that I could drop it into a paragraph without a philosopher wincing. Then tell me which of my suspects it's most likely to get collapsed into by a sloppy writer, and what specifically is lost in that collapse; one sharp distinction beats five hedged ones. Give me two boundary cases: a situation that looks exactly like this but isn't, and one that is this but doesn't look like it.

And be definite. If you think there's an existing term and you're not certain which, say that once, plainly, and then go with your best judgement — I'd much rather read a position I can push back on than a survey of the terrain. No reference list needed, I'll chase sources down myself, so please don't invent any.
