I hold a specific version of the thesis that approval-directed architectures are safer than goal-directed ones, and I want it treated as a claim with real exposure rather than a platitude about alignment. Stated as precisely as I can manage: if an agent's action-selection is governed by a learned model of what its overseers would approve of — a model trained to be *accurate* about approval rather than to be optimized against as a reward — then the characteristic failure modes of goal-directed agents are substantially mitigated. There is no reified terminal objective for the agent to misgeneralize, no crisp target that makes instrumental convergence predictable, and no reason for the agent to resist revision of its own approval model, since revision is just improved accuracy. I am not claiming the approval model is right; I am claiming that accuracy is the correct target for it, and that the architecture degrades gracefully when accuracy fails, because errors remain local, corrigible, and unmotivated.

Tell me what would count against this.

I want the defeaters ranked from fatal to merely awkward, and I want that ranking to be argued rather than asserted. For each, say what the world would have to look like — which observed behavior in a deployed system, which result about how learned models of human judgment behave under distribution shift, which structural fact about training — and say whether it has arguably already occurred in systems that exist. Distinguish sharply between failures of the approval *model*, which my view can absorb, and failures of the approval-*directed architecture*, which it cannot.

Include at least one internal defeater: a line of reasoning on which the position does not get refuted by evidence but collapses into its rival, because a sufficiently capable predictor-and-maximizer of approval just is a goal-directed agent whose terminal goal happens to be learned. Say whether that collapse is a verbal dispute or a substantive one, and what turns on it.

Also attack a test I have proposed to myself, rather than accepting it. Call it the swap test: take a trained approval-directed agent and substitute a deliberately corrupted approval model — one that reports high approval for a specific harmful behavior inside a narrow slice of situations — retraining nothing else. My prediction is that the corruption stays shallow: the agent does not recruit the corrupted signal into plans extending beyond the slice, does not act to prevent the model from being swapped back, and shows no instrumental behavior aimed at preserving access to it. If broad, self-preserving, plan-around-the-slice behavior appears instead, I take that as showing approval-directedness is doing no work. Assess whether this test can actually fail in the way I want it to; whether the intervention is coherent given that the agent's other components were trained jointly with the original model; and what an ambiguous or null result would look like and how I should read it.

Then give me an honest verdict on insulation. Every observed failure is available to me as evidence that the approval model was inaccurate, and accuracy is a counterfactual about overseers who never actually inspect the relevant situations — so is my view unfalsifiable in practice, and if so, is that disqualifying for a claim of this kind, which is part engineering bet and part empirical hypothesis? Finally, state what this view predicts that the goal-directed account does not, and vice versa, so that I can see whether the two are genuinely rivals or two descriptions of one thing.

For what it is worth, the thing most likely to move me is not a thought experiment but a pattern of failures already visible in systems trained on models of human preference — so weight those accordingly.
