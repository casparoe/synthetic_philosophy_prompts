**The view I will defend.** I call it the ledger view. Its commitments:

1. The lexical entry of a slur is identical to that of its neutral counterpart. Nothing derogatory is encoded in the word — not in its truth conditions, not in a conventional implicature, not in a presupposition, and not in a separate expressive dimension.
2. What makes a word a slur is that a standing social rule prohibits tokens of it, and the rule exists because such a token, in an unlicensed context, is an act of entering a claim into a public record: the speaker publicly ranks the targeted group below themselves and thereby acquires a commitment that anyone present can hold them to, cite later, and treat as evidence of where they stand.
3. Force is therefore graded and audience-relative rather than binary and lexical. A token does more damage the more the audience takes it to reveal a settled commitment of an identifiable speaker. I claim this predicts that force attenuates under anonymity unless the anonymity itself reads as concealment; that force survives indirect report of a named speaker's utterance but not hypothetical or generic mention; and that in some cases the ranking-claim is void — not excused, void — because the speaker's own social position makes it impossible to enter, which is how I handle in-group use and reclamation.
4. Speculative coda: a word becomes eligible for this treatment when it first acquires a vocative use, a way of addressing a member of the group rather than describing them, with predicative uses inheriting the prohibition by association. This is my answer to why prohibitions attach to some words and not their near-synonyms.

The argument on the day runs from the observation that the posited derogatory content does no compositional work that a story about the act and the audience's evidence cannot do, through the graded-force predictions in (3), which I take to be unavailable to lexical-content accounts without ad hoc machinery, to the vocative conjecture in (4).
