I am a machine learning engineer at a mid-size applied research lab. In six weeks I am giving a twenty-five-minute talk, followed by fifteen minutes of questions, at a half-day interdisciplinary workshop on minds and machines hosted by the cognitive science department of a nearby university. I have no philosophy training of any kind. What I know of the literature I have picked up by reading around over the past year, which means I can use the vocabulary but cannot tell which terms are load-bearing in ways I would trip over in front of specialists.

The room will hold about twenty-five people: two philosophers of mind who work on mental content (one holds something close to an interpretationist view, on which attributing content to a system is a matter of what makes the best external sense of its behavior; the other works on content-indeterminacy arguments), a developmental psychologist who studies concept acquisition in infants, two machine learning researchers from the statistics department who have published methodological critiques of probing studies and of attribution methods, a philosopher of science whose work concerns scientific realism and the epistemic status of unobservable entities in physics, and then graduate students plus two or three people attached to a bioethics center. Nobody does mechanistic interpretability as their day job, and my impression is that the two philosophers have met the field only through popular write-ups.

The position I am defending is that when an interpretability paper says a model represents some property, the claim is literally true in the same sense in which it is literally true that a barometer represents atmospheric pressure, and that the deflationary reading, on which features are a lens the analyst imposes, is simply mistaken. My argument has four parts, and I would like the objections aimed at them specifically rather than at interpretability in general.

First, criteria. I propose that a direction in activation space is a genuine representation of a property when the property was statistically recoverable from the training distribution under the training objective, when the direction is linearly decodable from activations, and when clamping or ablating it changes downstream behavior in ways that track the property. Representation on my account is a causal-role notion with a statistical entry condition.

Second, the basis objection. People say that any rotation of activation space yields equally good candidate features, so realism about directions is empty. I want to reply that the analogy to a gauge choice in physics fails, because a gauge choice leaves every prediction invariant, whereas here the trained weights and the loss single out a sparse set of directions as doing the work, and alternatives to them are not equivalent under intervention.

Third, content. I want to say that content is fixed by the direction's counterfactual profile across the training distribution rather than by our labels, and that this licenses features corresponding to no human concept at all. My exhibit is the demonstration Anthropic nicknamed Golden Gate Claude, in which amplifying one direction made a large model insert references to a particular San Francisco bridge into nearly every reply. That direction responds to photographs, drawings, and text, and to some suspension bridges that are not that one, so its content is narrower than bridge and broader than the name; I want to claim there is a fact about what it is.

Fourth, and this is where I am most exposed: I deny that representation requires the possibility of misrepresentation in any normative sense, and I deny that it requires a subject, a goal, or a function in the biological or teleological sense. I restrict my claim to the subpersonal machinery and explicitly disclaim any inference to belief, understanding, or experience.

As supporting evidence I plan to cite convergence: independently trained models of different architectures end up with directions whose causal profiles line up, which I read as what one would expect if there were a fact of the matter rather than an artifact of one analyst's decoding choices.

Please prepare my question period. I want the eight or so objections this particular room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do if I handled it badly, and phrased in the vocabulary and register each objector would use so that I recognize them as they arrive. For each, give me a reply I can deliver in under two minutes, mark clearly where that reply is soft or where it quietly assumes something I have not argued for, and tell me what to concede in my opening so that the objection loses its force before it is raised. Where two objectors would press in different ways, tell me which one to answer first and why. Then give me the single question I should most dread from this audience, an account of why it is the dangerous one, and a fallback answer that does not require me to defend a view I do not hold.

Be committed rather than even-handed. Rank the objections, decide which of my four claims survive this room and which do not, and if one of them is indefensible as I have stated it, say so plainly and tell me what to replace it with. I would much rather be told by you, now, that my fourth point collapses under a normativity objection I have not properly understood than discover it from the interpretationist in the second row. Also flag anywhere I am using a philosophical term in a way a specialist would hear as a misuse, since that will cost me credibility on points that are otherwise sound.
