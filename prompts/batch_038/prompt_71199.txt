Ok, I'm trying to get better at writing speculative fiction that has actual philosophy in the gears instead of philosophy in a speech bubble, and I want you to write me a short story — somewhere around 1200 to 1800 words — that dramatizes Frege's puzzle about identity statements. The thing I want dramatised is why an identity claim can be a real discovery rather than a tautology: if the two names just pick out the same object, what exactly is the news?

Here's the setup, feel free to rename things and rearrange the furniture, but please keep the machinery:

Some far-future survey bureau maintains the master catalogue of named objects out past the heliopause — mostly things nobody will ever see, just light curves and drift data. Two senior archivists run it and they've been quietly feuding for years about what a catalogue entry actually *is*.

One of them is a pure-pointer person. To her a name is just a handle bolted onto an object; the record is a coordinate and a dossier and nothing more, and there is no such thing as "the way the object was picked out" as part of the entry. When two long-standing entries turn out to be one object, her reaction is that this is a filing error being corrected — a merge, two rows becoming one, zero information gained about the universe, only about their own sloppy bookkeeping. I don't want her to be a strawman: give her the strongest version of that reply, the one where what's new isn't in the sky but in the head of whoever didn't know the handles matched.

The other archivist insists the entry has to store the route from the name to the thing — how it was found, what it was taken to be, the trail of reasoning that got you there — because without that the catalogue literally cannot record that a discovery happened. And the incident that drives the plot: two entries, one a slow faint mover logged by an automated sweep, one a named object from a crewed flyby a century earlier, turn out to be identical. For the pointer archivist it's a deletion. For the other it's the most important event of her career, and she has to write it down, and she discovers her system has nowhere to put it — the fact that these two are one isn't in either row, and adding it seems to require a third kind of thing that is neither the object nor the handle.

Then have the pointer archivist land the counterpunch: the "route" she wants to store is itself going to be filed under some handle, so she's just added another name, and the whole problem comes back one level up. Make that objection sting and make it hard to answer.

Constraints I care about:

- No character says the words "sense" or "reference," and nobody mentions Frege. The distinction has to be felt through what the catalogue can and can't store.
- No exposition dumps, no epigraph, no dialogue that reads like a seminar. If a paragraph could be lifted out and used as a textbook gloss on the puzzle, cut it.
- Give the two archivists actual personalities and a history — the feud should feel like it predates the incident.
- End on the log entry she finally commits (or fails to commit), quoted as the last thing in the story. That entry should be short and should make clear, without explaining, what she thinks she's recorded.
