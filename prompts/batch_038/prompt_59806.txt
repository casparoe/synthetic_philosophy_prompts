---

**Never repent: illusion as a non-renewable resource in du Châtelet**

Du Châtelet's list of what happiness requires is mostly uncontroversial for its genre: health, freedom from prejudice, passions harnessed to one's own happiness, tastes that can take over from the passions once the passions burn out, illusions carefully preserved, virtue, the banishing of sad thoughts, and — the item I want to focus on — never repenting. Read as a list of tips, the repentance clause looks like the weakest entry on it, a bit of aristocratic self-indulgence, the sort of thing that sounds profound at Cirey and less so in print. I think it's actually the load-bearing item, and that the rest of the list is organized around protecting it.

The reason is an asymmetry she never states but seems to assume throughout. Disillusionment is one-directional. You can be argued out of an illusion, disabused of one by an event, cured of one by paying attention to it; but you cannot decide to be re-illusioned. The will has no purchase on the thing, because an illusion's content is exactly the kind of content that a deliberate act of endorsement would falsify. Believing the beloved more admirable than she is cannot be held at will, since holding it at will entails knowing that it is held for a reason other than its truth. What's left after an illusion is destroyed is not a blank slot waiting to be refilled but a scar: you now know that contents of that type are destroyable.

If that's right, illusions are a stock, not a flow. They're accumulated involuntarily, spent involuntarily, and not renewable by effort. Du Châtelet's only remedy for an exhausted passion is substitution — replace the passion with a taste, find a new object, take up study, or cards, or another love. She never suggests recovering the old one, and on the asymmetry she couldn't. Substitution is also a strictly worse deal: a taste is her own word for a diminished thing, a passion with the temperature turned down, saved for old age precisely because it no longer costs much. And each substitution teaches you something about substitution, which is itself disillusioning. The stock can only run down.

That gives "never repent" its real job. Repentance is the one operation by which a person destroys their own illusions from the inside, deliberately, and about the one object for which no substitute is available: your own past self. To repent of a passion is to reclassify it, retrospectively, as an error — and once it's an error the illusion that made it motivating is gone and cannot return, and you're left knowing that your enthusiasms are the sort of thing that can be reclassified as errors by you. On her own asymmetry, repentance isn't a moral cost paid for a moral benefit. It's self-inflicted bankruptcy.

The obvious tension is with her insistence that virtue is required for happiness and that vice makes happiness impossible. If you may never repent, how do you ever correct yourself? Her answer, I think, is that the field has already been sorted: prejudices are the false beliefs that produce vice, and those she wants destroyed by examination, ruthlessly, especially the religious ones; illusions are the false beliefs that produce agreeable sentiment, and those she wants hoarded. So the sorting is done by consequences rather than by truth. Prejudice is what makes you vicious; illusion is what makes you happy.

This is where the view gets genuinely interesting and genuinely unstable, and I want to press on it rather than tidy it up. Because the classification is consequentialist, it can't be made prospectively — you don't know whether a given false belief is a prejudice or an illusion until you've watched what it does to you. But the whole point of the asymmetry is that you can't undo the classification once made: an illusion examined is already dead. Du Châtelet therefore owes a way of sorting false beliefs into keep and destroy that doesn't require inspecting them closely. What she has available, as far as I can see, is a proxy: destroy the ones that concern other people's opinions of you, keep the ones that concern objects of your own engagement — love, study, play. That's not nothing. But it isn't a criterion, and the paper ends by arguing she'd have done better to admit that happiness on her account partly consists in never auditing your own ledger.
