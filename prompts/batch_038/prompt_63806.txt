I'm a second-year cog sci PhD student and I've been the note-taker for our qualia reading group all year. We spent a month on Dennett's coffee tasters — Chase and Sanborn, the two Maxwell House guys who both end up saying the brew isn't what it was, one blaming his own newly refined palate and the other blaming his worn-out receptors — and my advisor got fed up with how slippery the cases are, so she had us regiment everything we looked at into four columns. The group filled these in by stipulation for the two originals, and then we kept using the scheme on cases we made up ourselves. Columns: forced-choice discrimination against the subject's own earlier baseline (D); affective pull, i.e. whether the thing still moves them and they still go looking for it (A); whether their memory of how it used to seem is intact and probeable (M); and their own attribution (R). Then she rules on a fifth column — did the experience change, yes or no.

Her rulings so far, which are now the group's canonical set:

- Chase: D up (he now sorts roasts blind that he used to confuse), A flat (same four cups a day, same relish), M intact, R is "the coffee's changed, but so have I." Verdict: experience unchanged — his standards moved, not his cup.
- Sanborn: D down (can't sort the good batch from the cheap anymore), A down (flatly indifferent), M gone (he can't reproduce the old profile even with samples in front of him), R is "my receptors went." Verdict: changed — and she's explicit that his say-so isn't what earns it, because with M gone the panel data is doing all the work.
- The perfumer we invented: D flat (every pair she could sort at 25 she can still sort), A flat (jasmine still stops her mid-sentence), M intact and then some (she can rank current samples against her remembered profile), R is "jasmine is thinner than it was, less golden." Verdict: unchanged — vocabulary drift, not nose drift.
- The wine student we invented: two years of training, D up (now sorts two vintages she used to confuse), A up (wine has become an obsession), M for the pre-training period gone, R is "nothing changed, I just learned the labels." Verdict: changed, and again her denial gets zero weight because she's lost the comparison.
- The aphasic barista: post-stroke, can't produce or judge a single sentence about flavor, but D flat on nonverbal matching-to-sample against her own pre-stroke baseline and A flat (same preferences, same reach-for-it behavior), M intact nonverbally. Verdict: unchanged — and she added, dryly, that nothing was missing from the file.

Now I've got a real one, not a thought experiment. There's a cellist in our longitudinal emotional-blunting study, six months on an SSRI. He sails through every auditory battery he passed pre-drug, including the tuning and timbre ones, so D is flat as a board. A is unambiguously down: no more frisson, he's stopped putting records on, he describes listening as "taking in information now." M is intact — he kept listening diaries for years and can match old recordings to his remembered responses. And R is the strange part: he's adamant the sound itself hasn't changed at all, that it's exactly the same sound, it just doesn't reach him. He's drawing the very distinction the pump is supposed to make unavailable, and he's drawing it confidently.

I have to code him for thursday. What do you think she'll rule — changed, unchanged, or refuse to rule? Walk me through the candidate rules that are still alive after those five verdicts (I can see at least two that fit all of them and I can't break the tie), say which of those come apart on my cellist and what each one commits her to saying about him, give me your best guess with the reasons for preferring it, and then name the one further case that would settle which rule she's actually running. Bonus question I'm not going to ask her directly: she tells us every week that she's with Dennett on qualia-as-intrinsic-private-properties being an incoherent posit, and yet she hands down confident change/no-change rulings without flinching. Is the cellist the case where that tension finally bites, or can she hold both — and if she can hold both, what is the fifth column actually a column of?
