Here's the question exactly as it appeared on the exam:

**Question 3 (25 points).** Consider the following argument, which I'll call the Trust Argument.

(1) Human beings raised in communities where promises are generally kept develop fairly reliable expectations about how others will behave, and those expectations let them plan jointly — pooling labor, deferring consumption, taking on projects that span years.
(2) Beings who can form and act on joint plans of that kind achieve, on average, better health, longer lives, and more of their own settled aims than beings who cannot.
(3) A life that realizes more of a human being's characteristic capacities and settled aims is, to that extent, a flourishing life.
(4) Therefore there is reason for each of us to keep our promises, and a community that wants its members to flourish will treat promise-keeping as a virtue.

*(Note: premise (3) is contested. Some philosophers read "flourishing" as a purely biological or descriptive notion; others read it as already evaluative, so that (3) is not a neutral report of fact. Address both readings.)*

(a) [10 points] Where, if anywhere, does this argument move from descriptive premises to a normative conclusion? Is the argument valid as written?
(b) [10 points] Does the argument commit what Moore called the naturalistic fallacy? Explain what Moore meant by that charge, and say whether it is the same charge as the one at issue in (a).
(c) [5 points] Suppose we repair the argument by adding a premise. Say which premise you would add and what the need for it shows.

And here's the student's answer (I'll call her M.):

> For (a), Hume's point is a point about inference. In the Treatise he complains about authors who build up a long chain of claims about what is and is not the case and then, without any warning, start writing about what ought and ought not to be; the shift is imperceptible, he says, but the new relation has to be explained, because it doesn't seem to follow from the others. So the question here is just whether (4) is entailed by (1)-(3), and I think the crossing happens at (4). Premises (1) and (2) are empirical claims about planning, health and aim-satisfaction, and (3) is likewise a factual claim about human beings — all three could appear in a psychology or a biology paper without a word being changed. But (4) says there is reason for each of us to keep our promises, and "reason" there is normative: it is not a claim about what anyone believes, wants, or feels. Since nothing in (1)-(3) says that anything is to be done, the argument as written is invalid. It's a small gap but it's a real one. All that follows from the fact that promise-keeping supports joint planning is that promise-keeping supports joint planning.
>
> For (b) my answer is no, and the important thing is that (b) is not the same question as (a). Moore's charge isn't about an inference going wrong, it's about a definition going wrong. The naturalist says that good means the same as, say, pleasant, or desired, or conducive to survival, and Moore says that can't be right, because for any natural property N it remains perfectly meaningful to ask "yes, but is N good?" If good just meant N, that question would be as empty as asking whether a bachelor is unmarried. That's the open question argument, and I think it succeeds: it shows good is a simple property that isn't reducible to any natural one, because if the question stays open then the two expressions can't mean the same thing, and if they don't mean the same thing then they can't be picking out the same property in the world either. Now the Trust Argument never offers a definition of good. It doesn't say that promise-keeping *is* some natural property, it just reports some natural properties and then draws a conclusion. So strictly speaking it doesn't commit the naturalistic fallacy, it only makes an invalid inference. Ultimately, though, the naturalistic fallacy is just the is-ought gap showing up at the level of definitions instead of arguments: Moore's complaint is that the naturalist's definition would let you slide from an is to an ought for free, which is Hume's complaint exactly.
>
> Someone like Foot would say the whole difficulty in (a) dissolves, because on her view flourishing is a moral concept, so premise (3) already contains an ought and there's no gap left to cross. I don't think that works, since (3) is the kind of thing a biologist could assess.
>
> For (c) the repair is to insert something like "each of us has reason to promote our own flourishing" between (3) and (4). Then the argument is valid. But the fact that you have to add it is exactly Hume's point, and it also shows the point isn't very deep: any argument of this shape can be made valid by supplying whatever normative premise it needs, so the is-ought gap turns out to be more a matter of bookkeeping than a genuine limit on moral argument. The interesting question becomes whether the added premise is true, which is the question the author of the argument was hoping to dodge.
