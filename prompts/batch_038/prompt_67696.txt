Last night a friend and I spent three hours on the Norway–America comparison in punishment, and he left me unable to answer him. His case runs roughly as follows: Halden opened in 2010 with a capacity of about 250, Norway's reoffending rates were already the lowest in Europe well before it existed, so the humane facility cannot be credited with the national outcome; the two systems count recidivism over different windows and against populations that differ in sentence length and in the concentration of disadvantage; and the whole comparison therefore confounds prison design with social structure. I think he is overreaching, but I cannot wave him off.

Our department's public lecture series is bringing in a penologist who has spent two decades designing Scandinavian-style facilities and who maintains that the punishment-versus-rehabilitation framing is itself misframed — that custody organized around the normality principle is not leniency but simply correct practice, and that the retributive impulse rests on a category error about what imprisonment is for. Write the questions I will put to her. They must engage the substance of the objection above, not generalities about whether prisons ought to be comfortable. I want one that isolates the attribution problem — what observation would distinguish humane custody lowering reoffending from a polity that reoffends less choosing to build humane custody; one on the arithmetic of a single 250-bed maximum-security institution sitting inside a national system; one on proportionality, if release becomes conditional on demonstrated change, including what happens to the prisoner who never demonstrates any; and one on whether treatment that functions as a precondition of liberty is distinguishable in kind from coercion. For each, state the answer she is most likely to give, then a follow-up that makes that answer cost her something. Nothing about her career or how she came to the field.
