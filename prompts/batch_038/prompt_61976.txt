I run a 90-minute reading group on whether your fellow citizens have special claims on you, and I want to hand everyone a sorting quiz at the door — the kind that works out what they actually believe from cases, not from what they call themselves. Can you build it?

Nine questions, eight if nine is a stretch. Each one is a concrete scenario with two or three options, then a scoring key at the back that maps answer patterns onto positions.

Hard constraints:

- No question may name a philosopher, use a term of art (associative duties, cosmopolitanism, statism, agent-relative reasons), or ask anything as abstract as "does nationality have moral significance?" Someone with zero philosophy background should be able to answer each item in twenty seconds and feel like it was just a question about a situation.
- Every question has to be one where the positions genuinely come apart. If two of your leaves pick the same option, that item is wasting a slot — cut it and tell me you cut it.

The key has to reach at least these leaves, each one named in plain English rather than by reference to who defends it:

1. no compatriot priority, full stop
2. compatriots have claims on you because you're both caught up in shared practices and institutions
3. compatriots have claims only derivatively — the state is what owes them, and you owe the state
4. partiality is a permission, not a claim: nobody can demand you favor them, but you're allowed to
5. floor-then-partiality: everyone's basic needs come first globally, above the floor you may favor your own
6. whatever you think belongs in the sixth slot — I'm pretty sure there's a live position I haven't listed

Three of the cases are mine, so please use them roughly as written:

- Someone born in country A, left at age two, never voted, never paid taxes, never used a school or hospital there, but holds the passport — and A now drafts every passport holder. Meanwhile they've spent their whole adult life in country B, using B's roads and clinics. Does A have a claim on them?
- Your country started an unjust war. One dose of morphine left, one wounded soldier from your side, one wounded enemy civilian. Both will die of pain otherwise.
- A compatriot whose yacht is sinking next to a foreign family whose boat is sinking. The compatriot is a good swimmer who'll lose the boat but keep his life; the family won't.

I also want at least one item that separates agent-level from institution-level partiality — people who give identical answers about what *you* should do but split completely on what *the state* should do, and I want to catch them. And at least one item where a compatriot is just a bad person, because I suspect "shared identity" and "shared cooperative scheme" come apart there and nobody notices until you make them choose.

At the end, give me three things beyond the key: which cell of your answer-space no actual position occupies, and whether that's because the view is incoherent, because it's unattractive, or just because nobody's gotten around to it; which pair of leaves your quiz can't reliably seperate and why; and which single item is doing the most sorting work, so I know what to defend if someone says the thing is too long.
