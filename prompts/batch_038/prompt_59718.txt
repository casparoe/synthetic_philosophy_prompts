I write a science-and-ideas column and for the past six months I've been reporting a long feature about why biologists still can't agree on what selection actually acts on. The note my editor and I agreed on is that the piece doesn't take a side. That's a lie, obviously — I have a side, it's just an unstated one — and a population geneticist friend who read an early draft told me my "view" is incoherent and then, infuriatingly, wouldn't say where. Background you should have about me: I'm not a biologist, I did philosophy as an undergrad about twenty years ago and never got past intro evo, but I've read a fair amount in the popular and semi-popular literature and I've interviewed maybe fifteen people for this piece. So before I revise, can you audit the package for me? Here it is, stated as honestly as I can:

(1) Selection proper happens to replicators, and the only things that replicate with the fidelity selection needs are genes — organisms and groups are things genes build or belong to, they get sorted, they don't get copied.
(2) But I don't want to be a gene's-eye-view reductionist: organisms are real individuals, not just packaging, and the history of life is largely a history of things getting more integrated — the move to cells with nuclei, to multicellular bodies, to colonies that behave like a single creature.
(3) The mid-century group selectionism that Williams went after is dead and deservedly dead: the math showed that selection between groups can't hold out against selection within them once there's any migration, and that result hasn't been overturned, only shouted at.
(4) That said, I basically accept the major transitions picture — at each big transition, selection at a higher level suppressed the free-riding that would otherwise have torn the aggregate apart, and that's how new higher-level individuals got built.
(5) I never let "for the good of the species" survive into copy. If a trade press book says it, I cut it.
(6) I think Hamilton's rule handles altruism without needing groups at all, and I lean on the claim that inclusive-fitness and multilevel accounts are two ways of writing the same math, which makes me suspect the levels debate has been, at bottom, a notational quarrel dressed up as science — including that big public fight about whether inclusive fitness could handle eusociality, which struck me as mostly two camps scoring rhetorical points.
(7) And I think selection is a very general thing: any population with variation, some heritability and differential reproduction is undergoing selection, which is why I'm comfortable talking about selection on ideas, on firms, even on algorithms, in the same breath.

So: which of these genuinely pull against each other, which pairs only look like they do, and what is the cheapest revision that leaves me holding something I could defend in print to an actual theorist? Rank the tensions by how bad they are instead of listing seven of them as equally worrying, and for each one say specifically what would have to go and what I'd lose by giving it up — I'm not going to tell you which commitments I'd rather keep, partly because I've decided that would bias you and partly because I genuinely don't know. I also have this nagging sense that one of the terms in my list is doing double duty for me and I'm getting away with it in the draft because nobody reads closely, but I can't pin down which one or where the switch happens. Don't give me a tour of the debate, I've read plenty of that; I want the audit.
