In three weeks the Clinical Ethics Advisory Committee of the public hospital network where I serve as clinical informaticist will vote on whether to authorize a twelve-month pilot of a system I helped design. I have fifteen minutes to present and then the floor is open. I would like a preparation sheet for the question period.

The position I am defending is narrow and, as far as I can tell, unfashionable on both sides: that a machine can be given a legitimate ethical role in clinical care only if it is forbidden to weigh. Concretely, the system monitors the chart, the medication orders, and the transcripts of interdisciplinary rounds in intensive care unit cases that the team has flagged as possible transitions toward comfort-focused care. It produces no scores, no rankings, and no recommendations. Its sole permitted output is a flag naming a consideration that the record indicates the team has not entertained at all — for instance a statement the patient made months earlier to a home health aide that never reached the chart, the way the proposed schedule concentrates care burden on a single night-shift nurse, or an evident misunderstanding by the family of what survival after tracheostomy would actually involve. The design rests on a distinction between omission and weighting. The system may surface a consideration no one has raised; it is architecturally barred from commenting when a consideration has plainly been raised and the team has simply assigned it a different weight than the system would have. This is not a post-hoc filter on outputs but a constraint on what the model is trained and prompted to generate, and a flag fires only above a confidence threshold that the record is silent on the consideration rather than merely thin on it. A second feature is a dissent ledger: every flag the team dismisses is logged together with their stated reason for dismissal, and the ledger is reviewed by this committee quarterly, so that a pattern of dismissals becomes visible to the institution instead of evaporating into individual charts.

My argument, in brief: first, most documented failures of bedside ethics are failures of attention rather than of judgment — the decisive consideration never entered the room. Second, weighting disagreements between a machine and a care team are both irresolvable and corrosive, because the machine has neither the standing to weigh nor any accountability for the weight it assigns. Third, omission claims, unlike weighting claims, are checkable against a record, which makes them the only sort of moral output that can be audited by people who are not the machine's designers. Fourth, it follows that the honest design space is confined to omission detection coupled with a reviewable ledger of dismissals.

The committee has seven members, none of whom is a machine learning researcher. Two are attending intensivists; one of them spent eighteen months championing a sepsis early-warning alert that was retired after it produced hundreds of alerts per week at a dismal positive predictive value, and he has not forgiven the vendor or himself. One is the hospital ethicist, trained in casuistry, who has written publicly against what she calls the moral outsourcing of bedside judgment. One is a nurse educator whose constituency is the night shift. One is the network's risk-management attorney, whose first question about any new artifact is what it does to the standard of care and to discovery. One is a patient and family representative, a bereaved mother whose late husband required an interpreter for every encounter. The chair is a hospitalist broadly sympathetic to me but under pressure to reduce documentation burden.

Give me the eight or nine objections this committee will actually raise, in rough order of how much damage each would do to the vote if I handled it badly. Phrase each in the idiom that particular member would use, not in the idiom of a philosophy seminar — and keep out the objections I would get at an AI ethics conference; no one in this room will raise trolley problems, value alignment, or machine consciousness. For each objection, supply a reply I can deliver in under two minutes at a podium without notes, and a candid line on where that reply is soft, meaning where a competent questioner could press and make me look evasive. Tell me which two or three objections I should concede in my opening five minutes to buy credibility, and which one I must never concede. Identify the single reply that has to land for the pilot to survive, and explain why it is the load-bearing one. Finally, give me the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one that, if the ethicist or the attorney finds it, I have no good answer for — together with the most honest answer I could give if it does come.
