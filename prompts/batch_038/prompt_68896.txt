Give me your credence in the following proposition.

P: The translation performance of present-day large language models shows that Hofstadter was wrong in Le Ton beau de Marot — that producing genuinely good translations does not require the translator to grasp what a text is about in the way a person does, so that a system with no embodiment and no experience of the world can translate at parity with a skilled human across the full range of texts he cared about.

I want a decomposition, not a bare number. Assign a probability to each of at least these terms, with a line of justification apiece: (i) whether "understanding" here picks out a substantive property or is merely a relabeling of good output — if you think the dispute is largely verbal, make that a term in the calculation rather than an escape hatch; (ii) what fraction of the textual range Hofstadter cared about — plain expository prose, gendered and Winograd-style ambiguities resolvable only by world knowledge, jokes and idiom, and constrained verse of the sort built around Marot's little poem — current systems now handle at human parity; (iii) whether the residual failures look principled, marking a difference in kind, or look like engineering shortfalls that more data and scale will close; (iv) how much of the verdict shifts depending on whether evaluators judge outputs blind or know the system's provenance. Then combine them into a single figure for P.

Finally: state which term your number is most sensitive to, and what single observation would move it most. For calibration, a colleague who works on machine translation puts P at 0.15 while I put it at 0.7 — tell me which of the four terms most likely accounts for that gap.
