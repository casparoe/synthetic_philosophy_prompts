I spent most of last night arguing with a friend who trains models at one of the frontier labs. The subject was whether his team's continued work is defensible once you take the alignment tax seriously — the compute, the schedule, the forfeited capability gains that getting the safety story in order actually costs — and I kept pressing the question that seemed to me to decide it: does his lab's participation change anything, or does the same capability arrive regardless? Each time, he closed the exchange with the same sentence: if we don't build it, someone else will. It was doing real work. It let him treat restraint as pure loss and treat any safety expenditure as an indulgence that his less scrupulous competitors would simply decline to pay for, so that the competitiveness constraint binds his team and nobody else's.

What I could not say at the time, and what I now think was the crux, is that he slid without noticing between two versions of that line — "someone else will," and then, when I pushed him, "someone with fewer red lines will" — and the second does not follow from the first.

So I would like the sentence taken apart. Give me a menu of the distinct claims it could be making rather than a defense of whichever reading you find most plausible, and cover at least these: that no particular team is necessary for the capability to appear at all; that abstention would merely delay it, together with the further question of how much delay would be needed to alter the outcome rather than postpone it; that what turns on his team's participation is who holds the controls once the capability exists, which is a claim about the distribution of outcomes rather than about whether they occur; that unilateral restraint cannot change anyone else's incentives or the norms governing the race; that "we" slides between his team and the whole population of comparatively careful teams, each individually dispensable; and the separate move of treating whatever all this establishes as a permission to do what would otherwise be blameworthy, rather than as evidence that doing it is harmless. For each, say whether it is a platitude that nobody in our argument was disputing, a substantive and empirically contestable claim, or simply false — and whether it delivers the conclusion my friend wanted, or whether the gap between that premise and "so the tax is not worth paying" survives every reading.

Please answer as exactly six numbered items, one per reading in the order above, each no more than two or three sentences, with no preamble and nothing after the sixth.
