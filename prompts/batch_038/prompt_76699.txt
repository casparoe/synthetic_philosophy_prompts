I'm four years into a novel about a forger and the appendix is kicking my ass. I don't want you to write fiction, I've got the fiction. I want the philosophy document two of my characters agree to sit down and produce, and I want it good enough that a reader who actually knows this debate doesn't roll their eyes at me.

Setup. My forger, Vosmeer, spent thirty years copying a minor seventeenth century Haarlem painter nobody's heard of, and he was good at it in ways that matter for the argument: he bought up genuinely period canvas from junk shops and cut it down, he mixed pigments from period recipes including the lead ones, he aged panels in a damp shed. So technical examination settles nothing. Two characters have been at each other for four chapters.

- Ilse, a conservator. She thinks a picture's worth is bound up with its having been made — by a particular hand, at a particular moment, in the particular way it was made. The achievement reading, she calls it. For her an indistinguishable copy is worth close to nothing *as a picture*.
- Bram, a critic. He thinks if two things look exactly the same to any pair of eyes then the difference between them isn't in the pictures, it's in the paperwork, and if it isn't in the pictures it can't be a difference in what the picture is worth looking at.

What I need is the joint statement they sign at the end of a good-faith adversarial collaboration. The hard part is the wording: each of them has to be able to read the statement of their own side and say yes, that's what I mean. No straw men, no fudge.

And it has to handle the cases that are actually in the book, because those are the interesting ones:

1. Vosmeer copied a painting that burned in 1943. The forgery is now the only surviving witness to it apart from some bad black-and-white photos. Does the copy inherit the original's standing, or is it a document *of* the original rather than an artwork in its own right? Both characters have to take a line here and say where they expect the other to land.
2. Sometimes Vosmeer finished what the original painter left unfinished — invented the missing bottom third of a composition in period style. That's neither copying nor original work, and it breaks one of my character's stories more than the other's. Whose, and how.
3. There's a scene where a museum hangs a known Vosmeer in a show about deception, label and all, and a visitor is more moved by it than by the real thing across the room. Ilse says the visitor is moved by something they're mistaken about. Bram says she's just dressing up her theory as other people's error. What do they agree counts as evidence here, and what does each of them predict about, say, whether the visitors stay moved once they've been told twice?
4. Bram privately thinks some of the Vosmeers are simply better pictures than the originals — he had hindsight, he knew what art history was going to reward. Ilse thinks that move helps *her*: a superb copyist-inventor is making a real achievement, just not the original painter's. I genuinely can't tell whether she's right, and I'd rather the document lay out the structure of that question than settle it.

Then the cruxes proper — the sub-questions where settling the thing would actually move at least one of them — with the case or argument both accept as bearing on each, and each side's prediction written down beforehand. And a section on how much of this is just words. My hunch is that authentic, original, and genuine are doing at least three separate jobs in the argument and both characters have been trading on that, and I'd like the document to say which stretch of the dispute is verbal and why, in their own terms.

Format, in this order, because I'm pasting the sections into different chapters:

- **Parties and ground rules** — 5 or 6 lines: who's signing, what they've agreed counts as a reason, what they've agreed not to appeal to.
- **Claims both accept** — numbered, around 12. Some will be boring. Fine, the boring ones are load bearing.
- **Where they still differ** — a table: the disagreement stated neutrally, then Ilse's own phrasing of her side, then Bram's of his, then a column saying whether the row is factual, evaluative, or verbal.
- **Cruxes and predictions** — four or five of them, each with the agreed case and both predictions on the record.
- **The verbal part** — a couple hundred words.
- **What would change my mind** — one entry per character, and they have to be things that could plausibly happen to a real person.

No citations, no bibliography, no name-dropping philosophers; this goes in a novel and I don't want footnote apparatus, and I'd rather you reason it out than reach for authorities. Aim for under about 1,800 words all in. Oh and one last thing — if you think some piece of my setup is impossible, like that no real conservator would ever say the copy is worth nothing, tell me. I'd rather fix the character than the appendix.
