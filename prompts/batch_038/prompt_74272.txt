I hold a twelve-month research fellowship in practical ethics and must submit a project proposal of roughly 1,200 words to a review panel that includes both academic philosophers and people who actually administer grant-making programmes. My topic is the framework, sometimes abbreviated ITN, used in effective-altruism-adjacent circles to rank candidate cause areas along three dimensions: importance, tractability, and neglectedness.

My working suspicion, which I want the proposal to develop rather than merely assert, is that the three dimensions are not three estimates of a single quantity — the marginal value of one further unit of effort devoted to a cause — but claims about three different kinds of thing. Importance is a property of states of the world; tractability is a property of the world taken together with an agent's capabilities and information; neglectedness is a property of the distribution of other agents' attention. The third is the difficulty. It is measured by how much work and funding a cause has already attracted, which means that a framework which publishes its rankings lowers the score of precisely the causes it recommends, so the criterion is not stable under its own use.

The project I want funded asks whether the framework can be reconstructed as a decision procedure justified by the computational and informational constraints under which real allocators operate, rather than as a theory about how good cause areas are, and if so, what its systematic failure modes are. Please write the proposal. It should state the research question so that a reader can tell what would settle it and what would leave it open; explain why the two standard defences of neglectedness (as a proxy for diminishing marginal returns within a cause, and as a proxy for a cause being underpriced by everyone else) and the two standard objections (that it is a sociological artefact of what the community happens to have read, and that it double-counts with tractability) all leave that question unanswered; set out a method that says explicitly which components are conceptual analysis, which are formal, and how a worked application to two or three named cause areas would test the reconstruction rather than merely illustrate it; defend that method against the obvious alternatives, namely an applied audit of past allocation decisions and a purely formal model carrying no conceptual argument; describe what a successful result would look like and, separately, what would count as the project having failed; give a month-by-month plan for the twelve months; and close with an honest paragraph identifying where the scope is probably too ambitious for one person in one year.

Refer to positions, arguments, and examples rather than to the literature — I will supply the citations myself before submission.
