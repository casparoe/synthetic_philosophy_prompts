Much of the constitutional commentary on the British monarchy is organized around a single power: the sovereign's legal capacity to withhold royal assent. That power was last exercised in March 1708, when Queen Anne refused the Scottish Militia Bill on the advice of her ministers, and it now survives essentially as latent insurance — real in law, dormant in practice, justified by what it would do in an emergency rather than by anything it currently does.

There is a second and far less discussed mechanism, and I would like a careful account of its conceptual relationship to the first. Under Westminster procedure, where a bill touches the royal prerogative, the hereditary revenues of the Crown, the duchies, or the personal property and personal interests of the sovereign, King's Consent must be signified in each House; if it is not, the question on third reading cannot be put and the bill dies. Unlike the assent veto, this is not dormant. It operates continuously, and the government's own drafting guidance requires the sponsoring department to approach the Palace at an early stage, never fewer than fourteen days before introduction. Two further features matter. King's Consent is officially characterized not as a prerogative right of the Crown but as a matter of House procedure, and a House of Commons select committee concluded that Parliament could abolish it by resolution alone, with no legislation required. And the mid-nineteenth-century edition of Erskine May defended the practice precisely on the ground that it allowed the Crown to protect its interests in advance, so that it would never have to fall back on refusing assent at the end.

With that setup, please explain the following.

First, the two mechanisms were originally presented as functional substitutes, yet they have diverged sharply: one has hardened into pure formality while the other retains operative content — inquiries into the practice have concluded that it is almost certain some bills were altered before introduction in order to accommodate concerns about Crown consent. What explains the asymmetry? I am less interested in the history than in the underlying structure. What is it about a power exercised in advance, through routine procedure, and in the register of the monarch's private interests, that keeps it live, when a power exercised after the fact, dramatically, and in the register of the public constitution decays into a formality? Is there a general principle here about how the exercise-frequency of a power determines whether it retains determinate content?

Second, there is a puzzle about the direction of advice. The convention holds that the sovereign consents on ministerial advice; yet in practice ministers appear to reshape a bill in response to concerns raised by the Palace before that advice is ever tendered. Does the acting-on-advice convention neutralize this power or merely relocate it? Please explain what normative work the convention is actually doing, and whether it is coherent to maintain both that the monarch acts only on advice and that the monarch's private concerns influence the content of legislation.

Third, explain the difference in kind between a power the sovereign holds and a power the sovereign's own subjects extend to him — that is, between the legal power to refuse assent and a procedural courtesy Parliament maintains about itself and could withdraw by resolution. Which of the two is more robust over time, and why? Does a self-imposed constraint of this kind behave differently from a legally entrenched one, and does the answer change depending on whether the constrained party is the legislature or the executive?

Finally, and briefly: is this asymmetry between a dramatic unused power and a quiet constantly used one peculiar to monarchy, or does it recur in other constitutional settings?

I am not looking for references or a reading list, and please do not pad the answer with background I have already supplied. I want the conceptual analysis. Please write your answer in British English.
