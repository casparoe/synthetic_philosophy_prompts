I am drafting the term paper for my seminar on the ethics of speech, and before I circulate a draft I would like the underlying position audited for internal consistency. My supervisor has already remarked that the paper "wants to have it both ways in at least one place," but declined to say where, so I have written the view out in full rather than trusting my own sense of it. The paper defends a sharp distinction between lying and misleading, together with an account of why the former is the graver wrong. My commitments are these:

1. To lie is to assert what one believes to be false. Nothing further is required: no intention to deceive, no deception achieved, and no falsehood in what is said — a speaker who asserts what she believes false and happens to be right has lied all the same.
2. Whether a speaker has lied is settled by two things only: her own beliefs, and what her words mean in the language she shares with her audience. It is not settled by what the audience takes her to have said, nor by what they were entitled to expect of her.
3. Misleading is a distinct act rather than a covert species of lying: the speaker asserts what she believes true, believes some further implication to be false, intends her audience to draw it, and so never asserts the false content.
4. Lying is pro tanto more seriously wrong than misleading, and this survives the equalisation of consequences: where a lie and a piece of misleading leave an audience with the same false belief, held with the same confidence, producing the same decisions, the lie is still the graver wrong.
5. That gravity is not, at bottom, a matter of harm or of the falsehood believed. In asserting, a speaker offers her word and thereby invites her audience to believe on her say-so; they hold that entitlement whether or not they exercise it. Lying wrongs them by extending an offer one does not intend to honour. I call this the vouching account.
6. Symmetry of Vouching — my own principle, and the hinge of the paper: the entitlement to believe on a speaker's say-so is not confined to what she asserts. Where she deliberately communicates p and her audience is entitled to rely on the inference to p, she has vouched for p no less than if she had asserted it. Vouching admits of degrees of openness, but not of degrees of force: an implication is vouched for less visibly, not less fully.
7. What a speaker is answerable for having conveyed is not limited to what she asserted. If the context makes p the inference a reasonable audience will draw, she is answerable for p even where she sincerely disclaims having intended it.
8. The entitlement to take a speaker's word can be withdrawn by the setting. Where the parties have expressly disclaimed reliance, or where an activity is understood by all participants to involve insincere representation, the audience has no standing to complain that it was invited to trust.
9. The wrong of lying is not exhausted by what it does to the person addressed. Taking one another's word for our beliefs is a shared resource; a lie degrades it for people who were nowhere near the exchange, and they are among those wronged.
10. Several classificatory questions here are genuinely unresolved, and I intend to leave them so — in particular, whether a heavy hedge ("to my knowledge," "as far as the records show") converts an assertion into something weaker, and hence whether the hedged speaker asserts anything at all.

Three cases carry the argument, with the verdicts I want from them.

*The briefing.* An agency spokesperson, on the record, is asked whether a proposed disposal site will exceed the regulatory threshold. She says that every scenario her agency has modelled keeps the site inside the threshold. She knows that the scenario her own staff regard as most likely is precisely the one she has declined to model, and she intends the reporter to leave reassured. My verdicts: she has not lied; she is answerable for the reassurance exactly as a liar would be; and her wrong is of the same kind and, I want to say, of the same weight.

*The letter.* A department chair is asked for a reference and writes that everything she knows about the candidate's teaching is favourable. Over two terms she has declined to sit in on the candidate's classes and has left the student evaluations unopened; she has no belief at all about whether the candidate teaches well. My verdict: on my own definitions she has neither lied nor misled, since she holds no belief that the reassuring implication is false — and yet she is the worst of my three agents, worse than a chair who simply writes a sentence she believes to be false.

*The table.* In a commercial negotiation, both sides have signed a term sheet stating that neither relies on the other's projections. The seller says that they project strong growth next fiscal year, believing it false. My verdicts: this is a lie by (1) and (2); the buyer has no complaint, given (8); and the seller is guilty of, at most, poor taste.

Please audit the set rather than reconstruct it charitably into something I did not say. Specifically: (i) the genuine tensions, ranked by severity rather than listed as equally bad, each stated as a particular collision between numbered commitments or between a commitment and one of my case verdicts, with the derivation of why the collision is real; (ii) for each, which item must be surrendered or restricted, and what the repair costs elsewhere in the set — I am most interested in repairs that look local and turn out to propagate; (iii) the pairs that only appear to conflict, together with the distinction that dissolves the appearance, since I expect some of my commitments to be flagged by seminar-mates that I should in fact refuse to give up; and (iv) your judgement on which single revision would purchase the most consistency overall, and what the paper would lose by making it. I have deliberately not indicated which commitments I would be most willing to abandon. If one of my case verdicts is simply unavailable to me on my own principles, I would rather be shown that than have the verdict quietly adjusted to fit.
