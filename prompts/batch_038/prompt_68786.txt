Slightly unusual request, and it's for my own benefit rather than anything I'm assigning. I sit on our faculty senate's committee looking at wether we should replace the way we currently elect senate members — eleven single-member constituencies, one winner each — with a single university-wide list elected proportionally. Last week the phrase 'one person, one vote' turned up on both sides of the table, and I've come away genuinely unsure it's even the same phrase.

The chair argued that the single-member system is what respects it, since under a party list you can't point to anyone who's answerable to you in particular, so your vote gets dissolved into an undifferentiated mass. Then a colleague from maths argued the single-member system flouts it, because our constituencies are equal in headcount but wildly unequal in competitiveness, so a vote in one of the four seats that always goes the same way is worth much less than a vote in a seat decided by a dozen ballots — and he clearly took himself to be closing the matter rather than making a good point. Somebody else put in that the phrase is really about malapportionment, equal-sized districts, and has nothing at all to do with how votes get converted into seats; I suspect she's right about how it works in American constitutional law, but I wouldn't want to say so from the front of the room without checking.

Could you take the sentence apart for me? I want the distinct precise claims it could be making — what exactly is supposed to be equalised, and equal for whom, measured when and by what standard: equal weight of the ballot as cast, equal probability of affecting the outcome, equal share of the seats, equal chance of ending up represented by somebody who shares your views, equal consideration of interests, whatever else is hiding in there. For each candidate reading tell me whether it's a platitude everyone at that table already accepts, whether it's substantive and arguable, whether it's simply false, and whether the single-member system satisfies it, the proportional list satisfies it, both do, or neither does — flagging anywhere the verdict turns on further details like district magnitude, how many seats are at stake, or whether you're comparing prospects before the election or outcomes after it. Then tell me which reading each of my three colleagues actually needs in order for the slogan to do the work they were making it do, and whether that's a reading they're entitled to; and finally whether the chair and the mathematician are contradicting each other at all, or just talking past one another with the same noise.

One formatting thing: no headings, no bullets or numbered lists, no bold, no tables. I want it as continuous prose, the way you'd write a long email to a colleague, one paragraph per reading if that helps you keep yourself honest. Don't pad it — what I'm after is the discriminations, not a survey of electoral systems.
