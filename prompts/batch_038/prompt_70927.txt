I hold a position I will call the sedimentation thesis, which I take to be the defensible core of the Braudelian and more broadly Annales programme. It has four commitments:

(S1) Depth follows slowness. An explanation of a historical outcome is deep in proportion to how much longer the timescale of the factors it invokes is than that of the outcome itself — geography, climate, soils and yields, disease ecology, demography, transport costs, land tenure, kinship and settlement patterns.

(S2) Structure fixes the reachable set. Those slow variables determine which outcomes are reachable by any sequence of events within a given period. Agents and events select within the set; they do not define it.

(S3) Substitutability. For any individual whose decisions look decisive, there is a wide class of counterfactually available occupants of the same position who would have landed the outcome in the same region of the set. Individual agency supplies variance, not location.

(S4) Anti-eventualism as a scale claim. Narrative history of battles, treaties, assassinations, and rulers' characters is not simply false. It describes surface variation, and its appearance of decisiveness is an artifact of the temporal resolution at which it is told.

I want a map of this thesis's exposure — not a defence of it, and not a generic caution about determinism.

First, rank the defeaters. Sort them into fatal, seriously damaging, and merely awkward, and say for each what the world or the documentary record would have to look like for it to bite.

Second, say which defeaters a committed structuralist in this tradition would themselves concede as counting against the thesis, and which they would absorb by redescribing. For each redescription, judge whether it is a legitimate appeal to a difference in explanatory level or an evasion.

Third, take the insulation worry head on. S2 and S4 look jointly able to convert any event-centred finding into a confirmation: an apparently decisive individual becomes someone the structure selected, and a genuinely surprising turn becomes a shift in the reachable set. Is the thesis therefore unfalsifiable, and is that a defect for a framework of this kind? If it is, say precisely where the thesis must stick its neck out — what it has to forbid, what it should be able to say before the fact, and what test would discriminate it from its rivals: narrative political history, rational-choice institutionalism, leadership studies, world-systems analysis, and strong geographic determinism of the Diamond variety. If it can be insulated without loss, argue that instead.

Fourth, work through the pressure points I have identified and add any I have missed. Path dependence is the one I find most destabilizing: the logic of lock-in — positive feedback, multiple equilibria, small early contingencies selecting among them — looks like a structuralist's friend but may make tiny events decisive exactly where structure underdetermines which equilibrium obtains. Can S2 be repaired by restricting structure to the equilibrium set rather than the outcome? Relatedly, the comparative-historical literature that borrowed structural vocabulary ends up locating contingency at critical junctures; does the thesis survive being softened into structure, punctuated? Then the Black Death: onset on event-time, consequences structural for centuries in wages, tenure, and labor bargaining — does that show the distinction of durée is one of description rather than of causal kind, or can I absorb it by classifying plague ecology as itself a slow variable? Then the Great Divergence: the much-discussed argument for rough parity of living standards between England and the Yangzi Delta around 1750, contested as it now is, together with the observation that standard structuralist explanations — coal endowment, a resource-rich New World periphery — are usually identified only after the outcome is known. Tell me whether the hindsight objection is decisive here or merely a general methodological worry, and whether the subsequent revision toward a larger and earlier gap helps or hurts me. Then predictive failure: the structural frameworks of the mid-twentieth century did not anticipate the collapse of the Soviet bloc or the speed of East Asian industrialization. Does that count against a framework that never claimed predictive reach, and if it does not, what is the framework worth? Then ideas: an argument once articulated changes what agents can want, so tell me what the timescale of conceptual change is and whether the thesis needs one. Finally the Anthropocene: if human activity now moves climate, hydrology, and coastlines on decadal scales, is S1's claim about the slowest-moving protagonist falsified or merely dated?

Fifth, address methodology. The thesis trades in counterfactuals about a single, unrepeatable past. What standards of evidence are actually available — matched comparisons of neighbouring regions with similar structures and different events, formal models, sensitivity to initial conditions — and are they sufficient, or am I committed to counterfactuals that cannot be tested even in principle?

Conclude with your honest verdict: should the thesis be weakened, domain-split (say, true for agrarian demography and disease ecology, false for war onset and constitutional design), or defended as it stands? And name the single objection I should worry about most.

One note on my own test. I currently think the decisive counterexample would be a case in which deep structure was held as fixed as it can be held, and surface events produced durably divergent outcomes. Tell me whether that is the right test, or whether I have set it too easily or impossibly.

You need not supply references or a reading list; where naming a debate or an argument helps me locate it, do so, but I would rather have depth on the three or four most serious defeaters than coverage of everything.
