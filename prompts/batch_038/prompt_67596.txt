My friend and I spent most of yesterday evening arguing about structural realism, and we finished roughly where we began, so I would like a disinterested opinion before round two. He has no formal training in philosophy of science but reads a good deal of it, and after the argument he sent me his account of the position in writing. I reproduce it as faithfully as I can:

"Structural realism is the sensible middle position in the realism debate. The pessimistic meta-induction has a point: science keeps discarding its pictures of what unobservable things are made of — the ether, caloric, phlogiston — so a realist committed to those pictures has no real answer to the history. But the no-miracles argument has a point too: it would be extraordinary if theories that repeatedly predict novel phenomena were getting nothing right about the world. Structural realism keeps both by attending to what actually survives theory change. Fresnel accounted for diffraction by treating light as a transverse wave in an elastic solid ether, and his theory implied something his opponents considered absurd — a bright spot at the very centre of the shadow cast by a circular disc — which was duly observed. Maxwell then kept all of Fresnel's equations and simply dropped the ether. That is the pattern: the structural content is retained, the ontological content is not. So we can be realists about the structure while remaining agnostic about the objects that occupy it — or rather, the objects are nothing over and above positions in the structure, relations without relata. This is why structural realism is immune to the pessimistic meta-induction: no empirically successful theory has ever had its mathematical structure thrown away. And it is why I think the realism debate is largely verbal. Realists and antirealists agree on every equation, and once you strip away the pictures of the furniture, 'structure' is just what the instrumentalist was prepared to accept all along."

My instinct, which I could not defend at the time, is that he has made the position either trivially true or empty, and that the two halves of his message are not really the same view. The one place I did manage to press him: he begins by saying we should stay agnostic about the objects and ends by saying the objects are nothing but positions in a structure, and those did not strike me as one claim said twice. He said I was splitting hairs, and I have no way of telling which of us is right.

Could you go through his message claim by claim and tell me which sentences a working structural realist would accept as a fair statement of the view, which would make them wince, and which are simply wrong? Specifically:

1. Does the historical case carry the weight he puts on it? I know the outline of the bright-spot story, but not whether the way he tells it — including who discarded what, and when — is what a historian of physics would recognise, or whether the inference from that case to his conclusion is one that critics of the position have already disputed.

2. Is the assertion that no empirically successful theory has ever had its mathematical structure discarded something structural realists actually hold, or does it quietly strengthen the position? If there are counterexamples, what kind are they, and what would a structural realist say in reply?

3. On agnosticism about objects versus objects as nothing but structural positions: is there a genuine distinction here, and if so, which claim is the more cautious and standard form of the view and which is the more speculative one? Can one coherently hold both, and what would a critic say to the second?

4. Does his final conclusion — that the debate is verbal, because agreement on the equations plus the word "structure" is all the instrumentalist ever granted — follow from anything true in his summary, or is it precisely what a structural realist would deny? Is there a question about what "structure" must mean for the position to say anything at all, and does his account answer it?

Finally, tell me what I ought to concede to him, and give me the two or three questions I should put to him next time that he cannot answer by repeating the summary. I am not after a reading list or a map of the literature; I want to know which of his sentences survive scrutiny and where I should have pushed harder.
