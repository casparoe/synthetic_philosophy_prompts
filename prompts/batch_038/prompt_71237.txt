I am circulating a statement of my decision-theoretic commitments in advance of a workshop at which each participant's list is to be attacked, and I would rather find the cracks myself first. Here is the set.

(1) For me, the rationality of a set of choices is a matter of coherence, understood concretely as unexploitability. An agent is irrational to the extent that a clever counterparty can offer her a sequence of trades, each of which she finds acceptable by her own lights, and thereby leave her strictly worse off than if she had never traded at all, whatever she goes on to learn. I take this criterion to govern dynamic situations and not only one-shot choices, and I appeal to no standard of correctness beyond it.

(2) In the single-urn case — ninety balls, thirty of them publicly announced to be red, the other sixty an unannounced mixture of black and yellow — I strictly prefer a ticket that pays on red to a ticket that pays on black. I also strictly prefer a ticket that pays on black-or-yellow to a ticket that pays on red-or-yellow. Neither preference budges if the swap is offered to me at a small fee.

(3) My explanation of (2) is what I call the draughtsman's edge: whoever assembled the problem may know more about it than I do, and ambiguity aversion is my way of discounting for that. Two riders. First, the edge must be modelled honestly — as a probability distribution over possible compositions of the urn, not as a licence to evaluate the same state of the world differently according to which ticket I happen to be holding. Second, the explanation must not depend on facts about who built the problem: had the urn been filled by a physical process that nobody observed and nobody could have influenced, I would make exactly the same choices, and I think I should.

(4) In the two-urn case — one urn with a publicly known fifty red and fifty black, one with a hundred red and black balls in unknown proportion — I am exactly indifferent between betting on red from the unknown urn and betting on black from the unknown urn, and I prefer the known urn to the unknown urn whichever colour I bet on.

(5) I hold precise credences wherever I have credences at all. For the unknown mixture in (2) I would say one third red, one third black, one third yellow, on a principle of indifference over the possible compositions. I nonetheless deny that this single number should settle what I choose, on the grounds that one number cannot carry the whole of my view about a situation of that kind.

(6) I reject the claim that ambiguity aversion is a bias to be engineered out of people. It is a response to something real about the situation — uncertainty that cannot be reduced to a frequency or to a well-supported model — and a decision theory that treats it as an error is describing the wrong phenomenon.

(7) Methodologically, I accept an axiom only where it can be motivated independently of its role in making a representation theorem go through.

What I want from you is an audit, not a defence and not a lecture: which of these pull against each other, how badly, and what the cheapest repair is in each case. I genuinely do not know where the problem lies, and I am deliberately not telling you which of these I would be most reluctant to give up. Where a pair is genuinely at odds in one respect and only apparently at odds in another, say which is which rather than reporting the pair once.

Please answer in British English throughout, and organise your reply under exactly these headings, in this order:

**Overview** (about 120 words). The headline verdict: is this package tenable as it stands, and which single commitment decides the answer?

**Genuine tensions, worst first** (about 700 words). For each real inconsistency, give the argument that establishes it — with explicit arithmetic over the tickets wherever arithmetic rather than intuition is doing the work — and then say what would have to give and what giving it up would cost me. Rank them by severity and justify the ranking.

**Tensions that dissolve** (about 250 words). Commitments that look incompatible on a first reading but are not, with a sentence or two on why the appearance arises and on exactly what has to be added before a contradiction appears.

**The cheapest single revision** (about 200 words). Which one abandonment or weakening would clean up the most, what survives it, and what I would lose that (6) and (7) suggest I care about.

**Residual worries** (about 100 words). What that revision leaves unfixed, and where I should expect to be pressed at the workshop.
