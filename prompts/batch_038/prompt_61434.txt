You are a philosopher of mind who also keeps current with the empirical literature on sleep and dreaming. You are being asked to referee an argument as it was actually made, not to survey a debate or to give your own view of it.

Two members of a reading group I belong to — Iris, a philosophy student, and Wren, who works in computational cognitive science — were assigned an old exchange in which both authors end up denying that dreams are experiences, though for very different kinds of reason. At the following session the two of them turned the discussion into an argument about which of those denials is worth holding. I was taking notes, and here is the exchange in five moves.

1. Iris: We agree that dreams aren't experiences, but only one of us has a reason that can't be argued away. On my reading the denial is conceptual. 'Experience' gets its sense from the criteria we apply in waking life — you can ask what someone was doing at a given moment, when it began, how long it went on, whether anyone else could have noticed. None of those questions has an answer for a dream, since the only thing to go on is the impression reported after waking. So no experiment could ever show a dream to be an experience, and any amount of sleep-lab data is beside the point.

2. Wren: Then your denial is worthless, because nothing could count against it. Treat it as an empirical hypothesis instead. Either the dream happens during sleep and is remembered afterwards, or it doesn't happen during sleep at all and the narrative is manufactured by the process of waking up and laying down a memory. The second option predicts things the first struggles with: recall that is immediate and complete at the instant of waking and gone within seconds; narratives that show up reliably only when the sleeper is roused from one particular stage; reports that grow longer and more detailed the more you question the dreamer. That is an inference to the best explanation, not a piece of grammar.

3. Iris: It isn't an explanation, it's a redescription. You never say what the manufacturing consists of or what it is answerable to — and the moment you make it answerable to the sleeper's ongoing activity, whatever it is working on is something happening during the night, which is experience under a new label. Nor can you have it both ways: you keep referring to the contents of the dream narrative, as though there were contents that could be gotten wrong.

4. Wren: A confabulating patient has contents too, and nobody supposes his story reports an experience he was having. As for the mechanism being a black box, it is the ordinary narrative-and-memory system running with no external anchor to correct it — a system we already know produces confident, detailed, false reports. And your claim that nothing could count against me is simply false; here is something that would. Lucid dreamers can be briefed in advance to signal by moving their eyes in an agreed pattern once they realize they are dreaming; the signal lands on the recording when they say they made it, and afterwards they can carry out pre-arranged timed tasks — counting, further signals — in step with the clock in the lab. If a sleeper can execute a scheduled sequence on schedule, something experience-like was running during the night, and my rival hypothesis loses those cases.

5. Iris: That is a retreat dressed as a victory. You have made the question answerable only for a handful of trained sleepers attached to a machine and hovering near the surface of sleep; for everyone else, and for last night, there is still no answer at all. If whether I dreamt is settled by what an electrode can pick up, then 'experience' has stopped meaning what it meant, and that is precisely the confusion I said your picture was hiding.

Neither would give ground and I could not tell who was ahead. Think carefully about each move on its own terms before answering, and consider for each one what it would take for it to fail, what it silently assumes, and whether the two of them are using 'experience' in the same way — but keep all of that working out of the response.

Please adjudicate move by move. Give exactly five numbered items, one per move, in the order above. Do not write an introduction, a summary of the two positions, an overall winner, or a conclusion; the response must consist of the five numbered items and nothing else.
