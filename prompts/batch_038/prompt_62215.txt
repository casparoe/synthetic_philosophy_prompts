Three years ago I retired from a career in developmental biology and have since taken up philosophy in earnest. Kripke's *Naming and Necessity* has absorbed me, and of its arguments the necessity of origin strikes me as by far the most consequential for a natural scientist: that no organism could have developed from a different sperm and a different egg. As I read it, this is a modal claim in exactly the same style as the thesis that water is H2O — an essence picked out by empirical inquiry, known only a posteriori, and so answerable to how reproduction in fact works. Two puzzles follow for me.

First, if the thesis rests on the biology, what laboratory finding would count against it? Somatic-cell nuclear transfer seems the obvious test case: a clone arises from an egg stripped of its nucleus together with a differentiated body cell, neither of them the gametes of the donor, and yet the resulting animal is treated as a new individual rather than a copy of that donor.

Second, since gametes contribute nothing but genetic material, does the thesis not simply reduce to the claim that my genome was fixed at fertilization? If it does, what are we to make of monozygotic twins, whose cells accumulate divergent mutations after the split, and of the chimerism found in ordinary development?

I would like the analogy with the a posteriori necessities examined rather than assumed, and the two puzzles answered on that basis.
