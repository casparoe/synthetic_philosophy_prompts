I have no formal training in mathematics or economics, so I will have to ask you to supply what I cannot. For about fifteen years I have staked money on outcomes I believe I can estimate better than the people offering the odds, and I arrived at my rule for sizing a stake the expensive way, long before I learned that it goes by Kelly's name: stake the fraction of your bankroll that maximizes the rate at which it grows over the long run.

Since then I have read two camps of commentators telling me two incompatible stories about that rule. One camp says the rule is no discovery at all, that it is simply the maximization of expected utility with a logarithmic utility function, and that the talk of growth rates is decoration. The other camp says the rule is a revolution, that the whole expected-utility tradition rests on a confusion between averaging across possibilities and averaging across time, and that once the confusion is exposed expected utility must be abandoned.

I think both stories are wrong in the same direction, and I would like you to argue my view as strongly as it can be argued. My view is this. The growth-rate rule is not a utility function and not a rival of one, because it does not rank outcomes at all; it ranks policies, that is, whole ways of behaving across a long series of stakes. Its authority rests on a single fact that can be stated without mentioning utility, preference, or time averages: if I stake the growth-maximizing fraction and you stake any different fraction, on the same repeatable bets, from bankrolls of the same size, then as the number of bets increases the chance that I finish ahead of you approaches certainty. Everything else in either camp's story is either a method for computing that fraction or a manner of talking about this fact.

Please build the case in four parts.

First, establish the fact in a form I can check at my kitchen table. Take the situation I know best: a bet offered at even money that I take to win fifty-five times in a hundred, staked at a fixed fraction of the bankroll each round, every round independent of the last and at the same odds. Show me why the growth-maximizing fraction finishes ahead and why every other fraction finishes behind — not merely behind on average, but behind with a probability that goes to one. Logs, sums, and limits I can follow; measure theory I cannot.

Second, tell me honestly whether this fact amounts to anything more than the ordinary law of large numbers applied to the logarithms of returns. If it does not, then say where the real content of my position lies: what must be granted about what a bettor ought to want before the fact carries any normative force at all. I suspect this is where my position is most exposed, and I would rather see the exposure described than papered over.

Third, take up the objection I hear most often: that almost nobody faces an endless run of identical independent bets; that people stake money in order to reach particular sums by particular dates rather than to grow without end; and that for such a person the right stake may be far larger or far smaller than the growth-maximizing one. Concede whatever in this is true, and then show why it limits the reach of my position rather than refuting it.

Fourth, tell me whether someone who stakes half the growth-maximizing fraction, because they doubt their own estimate of the fifty-five in a hundred, is a counterexample to my view or an application of it.

I am not looking for a bibliography or a list of things to read; I have no means of chasing either. I want the reasoning. And if you believe my view is simply false, say so in a short paragraph at the very end and say what should replace it — but do not let that belief weaken the argument you were asked to make.
