I write a fortnightly column on science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and my next piece concerns whether large language models have settled a long-running argument in cognitive science. My principal source, a researcher who builds word-embedding models, reaches constantly for a cartographic analogy: concepts are territories in a high-dimensional space, individual words are coordinates within them, and analogy-making is nothing more than measuring distance and direction between territories. On his account, the familiar result that the displacement from man to woman runs parallel to the displacement from king to queen shows that analogy is geometry, and he says this vindicates the thesis of Douglas Hofstadter and Emmanuel Sander's *Surfaces and Essences* — that analogy is not a rare talent exercised by poets and physicists but the ordinary, continuous operation of thought, such that perceiving an object as a chair already involves analogizing it to chairs encountered before.

Before I let that claim stand in print, I would like the cartographic analogy audited properly. Specifically:

1. The correspondence table. What plays the role of the map, of the scale, of the territory, of the act of measuring, and of the person doing the measuring — and which of those roles, if any, has no counterpart in the human case the analogy is meant to illuminate.

2. The disanalogies, sorted by whether they matter. A map's metric is fixed in advance, its distances are symmetric, and a territory does not rearrange itself when someone consults it; human analogy, on Hofstadter and Sander's telling, involves fluid concepts and what they call slippage, whereby a feature migrates from one concept into another as two situations are brought together. Tell me which disanalogies of this kind are fatal to my source's conclusion and which are merely decorative — the sort a clever reader raises to feel sharp without anything changing.

3. What the map quietly imports. I suspect the analogy smuggles in the premises that similarity is something found rather than made, that there is a single settled answer to how alike two things are, and that the relevant respects of resemblance are handed to us by the space rather than chosen by the analogizer for a purpose. Assess whether that suspicion is correct, and if so whether it is a defect in the analogy or a genuine difference between embeddings and minds that the analogy conceals.

4. A complicating fact. A second researcher told me that the king-and-queen result comes out cleanly only if the query word itself is excluded from the candidate list, which suggests the measured distance is not a property of the map but a property of how the question was posed. Fold this in: does it strengthen or weaken the cartographic picture, and why?

5. The verdict. Does embedding geometry actually implement the mechanism Hofstadter and Sander describe, or is it a different operation mistaken for that mechanism because the two share a vocabulary? I want a straight answer rather than a hedge, though it must acknowledge the strongest consideration on the other side and say what would have to be true for that consideration to win.

6. A replacement. If the map fails, what analogy should a columnist use instead to convey to a bright non-specialist both what embeddings genuinely do and where the comparison to human analogy-making stops?

Please write for an intelligent reader who has never opened either book or run a line of model code. I would rather have the reasoning made explicit than be handed the standard talking points from either side, and I would rather be told where the analogy is load-bearing than be reassured that it is only an illustration.
