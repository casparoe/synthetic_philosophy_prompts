I am in the fourth year of a dissertation in international relations on how policymakers use historical analogies, and I have reached the point where I no longer trust the framework I came in with, because it does not reproduce my own considered judgments. So I want to do this the honest way: state the verdicts first, then ask what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here is the apparatus I have been working with. An inference from a source case S to a target case T is admissible in policy argument only if it satisfies five clauses: (i) a mechanism clause — the inference runs on a shared causal process, not on a shared surface description; (ii) an enabling-conditions clause — the background conditions that made the mechanism operate in S are present in T; (iii) a symmetry clause — disanalogies are weighed with the same diligence the arguer applies to analogies; (iv) a defeasibility clause — the arguer can state what would have to differ in T for the mechanism not to fire, so the analogy carries its own defeat conditions; and (v) a proponent clause, which I know is unorthodox — the same argument carries more or less weight depending on whether the person offering it has previously made a comparable judgment and been right.

My verdicts:

1. Appeasement reasoning used to justify the Suez intervention: inadmissible. It satisfied the surface match and nothing else.
2. The same appeasement reasoning, transferred to the domino theory in Indochina: also inadmissible, and for a different reason — the mechanism it invoked had no counterpart in an internal insurgency.
3. Appeasement reasoning applied to the invasion of Kuwait in 1990: admissible. I still think it was good inference at the time, not merely lucky. This is the pair I cannot reconcile with 1 and 2, and I suspect the difference is contaminated by my knowing how the Gulf War ended.
4. The post-Vietnam criteria for committing troops — clear objectives, sustainable public support, an exit strategy — used to argue against intervention in the Balkans: admissible as a demand for planning.
5. The same criteria invoked against intervening in Rwanda in 1994: inadmissible. They were used to convert a planning test into a permission to stand aside, and I want the principle to name what changed.
6. The Thucydides-style claim that a rising power and a ruling power are structurally prone to war, applied to the present great-power case: inadmissible as a prediction, yet I think it remains legitimate as a caution that changes how one weighs evidence. I worry this is a dodge, and I want to be told whether "legitimate" is splitting into two notions here.
7. In the run-up to the 2003 Iraq war, both the Munich analogy and the Vietnam analogy were live at once, pointing in opposite directions; I judged both rhetorically powerful and epistemically empty. But a third analogy, drawn from the postwar occupations of Germany and Japan, I took seriously. I cannot say why the third survived.
8. Reasoning from the 1918 influenza to non-pharmaceutical interventions a century later: admissible. Reasoning from the Cuban Missile Crisis to brinkmanship dynamics between rival AI developers: inadmissible — despite being offered by people with better formal models and a cleaner mechanism story. This is the second pair I cannot reconcile.
9. The proponent clause: when a former official who was wrong about a past intervention now invokes it, I discount the argument; when a former official who was right invokes it, I upgrade the argument, and I suspect that is double-counting the same evidence.

What I want from you: a principle, or a small number of candidate principles, stated precisely, that delivers these verdicts — or that tells me which of them must be abandoned. For each clause, say which case motivated it and what work it is doing, and flag any clause that turns out to be idle or reducible to another. Then say what the repaired principle rules on three cases I have not listed: analogies to interwar protectionism in current trade policy, analogies to past financial crises in central-bank emergency response, and analogies to earlier small-war interventions in debates about arming a state under invasion. Finally, tell me whether clause (v) can be preserved in any non-circular form, and whether verdict 6 survives the split between epistemic and deliberative legitimacy that it seems to presuppose.
