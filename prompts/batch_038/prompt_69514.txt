I'm in a monthly reading group that's been slowly grinding through Malthus, and there's this one guy — call him Dan, a semi-retired actuary and easily the most numerate person in the room — who ends basically every discussion by saying "Malthus was right, he was just early." It's become his trump card. Last week I brought up what I thought was the obvious problem: English real wages didn't sink toward subsistence after 1800, and world population went from about a billion to eight billion without the famine check firing globally. Dan said sure, there was a stall around mid-century, but by 1900 they'd multiplied, and anyway the mechanism is real — the escape was a one-off historical accident (New World grain, guano, coal, the nitrogen fertiliser thing) — and if you'd asked Malthus in 1798 to predict the next three centuries instead of the next fifty he'd have been right. Then someone else piled on with the same move applied to the 1972 Club of Rome computer model, claiming that when people later checked its base-case run against actual data it tracked surprisingly well, which supposedly shows these long-run doom models are "right but early" rather than wrong. I want to push back on both, but every time I try I end up sounding like I'm just saying "but it didn't happen," which he can absorb into the slogan effortlessly.

I'm auditing a philosophy of science course at the same time and I've half-learned the vocabulary — auxilary hypotheses, something about research programmes being progressive or degenerating, the idea that you can always save a theory by tinkering at the edges — but none of it seems to bite on this particular case, and I can't tell whether that's my fault or the case's. So here's what I want: lay out the different precise claims that "Malthus was right, he was just early" could be making, as a menu I can bring to next month's session. Full range, from the readings so weak that they'd be true of any failed forecast ever issued, up to the readings that would be genuinely substantive and would actually earn Dan his point. For each one, say what it commits you to, whether it leaves the theory falsifiable for anyone alive now, and whether the "early" is doing real work or is just goalpost-moving that doesn't have to announce itself. A few specific confusions I want sorted, because the group slides between them without noticing: (a) "right about the past" versus "early about the future" — these feel like the same complaint but I think they're different claims and I'm fairly sure Dan switches between them mid-sentence; (b) right about the mechanism but wrong about the outcome, versus right about the outcome but wrong about the timing, and whether the first one can ever be checked; (c) whether a theory that never says *how* early it is can meaningfully be called early at all, since Dan has never once committed to a date or a probability; and (d) whether it matters that Malthus himself revised the book across six editions and added a "moral restraint" check that let people dodge the misery — is Dan's rescue just Malthus's own rescue, and does the fact that it's the author's rescue make it more legitimate or less?

And then the practical bit: tell me which of these readings Dan is most plausibly deploying given what he actually said, and give me one question I can ask him that would distinguish his reading from the theory simply being false — something he can't answer with a shrug and another restatement. Same for the Club of Rome guy, if the answer differs. Please don't give me a life of Malthus or a summary of the Essay (I've read it), and no reading list or bibliography, I just want the analysis. Two pages at most, organised as the menu.
