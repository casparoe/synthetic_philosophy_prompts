I'm a novelist and this year I'm one of three judges for a mid-list fiction prize — small money, big egos, you know the type. Our charter says a book can only take the prize if a majority of the jury finds it does all three of these things: (1) it does something genuinely new with form, (2) it has a voice that couldn't belong to anybody else, (3) it's morally serious without sermonizing. So we scored each criterion separately, wrote our reasons into the minutes, and only then voted on the overall question of who wins.

We'd narrowed it to two. For the one everyone's fighting over — call it the Kessler book — here's how it came out. Me: yes, yes, yes. Second judge: yes, yes, no (he thinks it's brilliant but cold). Third judge: yes, no, yes (she loves the seriousness, thinks the voice is derivative). So on each of the three criteria, two of us out of three said yes. But when we got to "does the Kessler book win," it went 1–2 against, me being the lone yes.

The chair wants the minutes written so the overall vote decides and the money goes to the other book. I keep feeling like that's a kind of cheating — that the jury's actual position, taken criterion by criterion, is that Kessler deserves it, and that two of us voted against our own recorded reasons. But then the third judge says she voted no overall because she doesn't think our three criteria are jointly enough, that a book can pass all three and still not be the winner, which seems to blow up the charters logic entirely.

One more thing that has me suspicious: the chair told us beforehand, cheerfully, that he'd rather we took the overall vote first and treat the criterion votes as commentary, and that if we did it the other way round he'd argue the criterion votes bind us. So the order of the ballots seems to be doing some of the work, and somebody's steering.

You're someone who thinks professionally about how groups reach verdicts — social choice, collective judgment, whatever you want to call it — and I'm a smart layperson, not a specialist. Think it through carefully before you answer: work out what the vote patterns actually commit the group to, whether the minutes should record a winner, and whether the third judge's move is legitimate or a dodge. Then put your bottom line in one or two sentences up front, followed by what I should actually say at Thursday's meeting. Hard cap of 300 words for the whole reply, please, and no reading list — I'm not writing a paper, I'm trying to survive a commitee.
