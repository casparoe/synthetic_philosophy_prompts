You're a moral philosopher who specializes in contractualism and has actually taught this material. Think it through step by step instead of giving me a survey of the literature. Here's an argument I built for a paper and now think proves way too much, because it only uses premises Scanlon himself wants.

(1) Contractualism compares principles by the strength of individual complaints taken one at a time: what matters is how strong the strongest individual objection to a principle is, not how many objections there are.
(2) That's the whole point of the view — it's why a million mild headaches can't outweigh one person's death. Letting numbers count would let my claim be diluted by the existence of other people's claims, and justification is supposed to run to each person separately.
(3) Equal case: one stranger drowns unless you rescue him, or five others drown unless you rescue them. By (2) numbers are inert, so the six complaints are pairwise identical and neither side has the stronger objection.
(4) And (1) supplies no tie-breaker that doesn't smuggle the numbers back in. What's left over is a coin, or an equal chance for each of the six.
(5) So contractualism is committed to Taurek's coin flip: you may not save the five.
(6) Fine, suppose you bite that bullet. Now slide the case. Instead of five deaths it's five people each losing an arm, and the one still dies — nobody's complaint on the five side is as strong as his, so save the one. Same verdict with a hundred arms, a thousand lost fingers, a billion headaches.
(7) But between "five people each lose their life" and "five people each lose an arm" there's a continuum, and (1)-(2) never say where along it the verdict flips. The strength of the strongest individual complaint changes by imperceptible degrees while the answer is supposed to jump.
(8) A rule with no determinate flip point has no flip point. So either the coin flip generalizes all the way up and numbers never count for anything, or it generalizes all the way down and numbers count whenever the harms get close enough — which is aggregation.
(9) Contractualism is therefore either Taurek or a species of consequentialism; it isn't a third position.

I'd rather keep (1) and (2) — they're what makes the view interesting — and I can't accept (9). So which step do you reject, and why did it look fine when I wrote it? Then price the options concretely: what does it cost to give up (4) vs. (7) vs. (2)? In particular, does the idea of matching complaints off one-for-one so that an unmatched remainder decides the case actually survive, and if so what makes exact equality morally load bearing rather then just a bright line drawn at an arbitrary spot in the continuum?
