I am a novelist and one of five directors of the Ferrand Press, an independent literary house now in its ninety-third year. Every book we have published since 1936 has been set in Corbie, the face Etelka Vaszary cut for us in three optical sizes between 1932 and 1937: six point for the pocket editions, ten point for trade, eighteen point for titles and jacket copy. She never cut a bold; by the account of her granddaughter, who holds the surviving drawings in Budapest, Vaszary wanted one and the press could not afford a second set of matrices. Of the three sizes, only the ten-point punches survive complete. The eighteen-point exists as drawings, the six-point only in printed proofs.

This autumn we launch the Corvina Series, translations from Hungarian, Czech, Polish, Romanian, and Slovene. Corbie carries no diacritics beyond the Western set — no comma-below s and t, no ő, ű, or ř — and no bold for the running heads the series requires. We must extend the face. The board holds three proposals.

First, a large digital foundry offers to build a nine-weight family by interpolating from the ten-point punches and generating the new diacritics programmatically from existing components: modest fee, nine months, on condition that the result be licensed under the name Corbie. Second, a two-person studio offers to redraw the text size by hand from the drawings and proofs, deriving the diacritics and a single bold from a fresh reading of Vaszary's habits: four times the fee, twenty months, and they will not permit the name Corbie on the specimen, only a new title with a credit line placing it after her. Third, our senior trustee proposes neither. He wants the board first to write a specification — measurable features: stroke contrast, the x-height to cap-height ratio, the flat side of the o, the axis of the stress, the form of the Q's tail — and then tender it competitively, so that compliance can be verified rather than argued about.

There is one further fact the memo must confront rather than merely cite. Each bidder set the same paragraph for us, and we inserted, unannounced, a photographic copy of a 1934 trade proof. Shown the sheets without labels, the board split four to three on which was really Corbie. The majority preferred the interpolated digital sheet. Two of us preferred the 1934 proof, which is Corbie and was not chosen.

Draft the memo I will circulate before Thursday's meeting. It must recommend one course — a bid, the specification, or a fourth option you think we have missed — with a budget, a timeline, and the credit line we would print on the title page. It must give the board a working test we can apply to the glyphs still to come, and it must say plainly which part of this decision no test can capture and who is to hold that judgment in the years after this board has turned over. Take the trustee's objection at full strength: we are spending money we do not have on a distinction we cannot state, and if we let the second studio rename the face we forfeit the only thing ninety years of continuity is worth. Do not write me an essay about style. I have a professional interest in that subject and no authority on this one; my own books are set in Corbie, and the memo should not lean on anything I would say about voice. Nine hundred words at most.
