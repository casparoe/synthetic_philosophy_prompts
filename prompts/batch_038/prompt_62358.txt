I'm assembling materials for the disagreement week of a second-year epistemology seminar, and I've decided to screen Sidney Lumet's 1957 film *12 Angry Men*, adapted from Reginald Rose's teleplay. My students know the story well enough that neither I nor you need to summarize it; what I want from you is a critical reading of the film through the conciliationism–steadfastness debate — roughly 1,500 words, written as something I could hand out as a discussion piece. Please don't quote the screenplay (I'd rather you not guess at dialogue; paraphrase scenes instead), and don't append a bibliography or a list of articles my students should read. I'm also deliberately steering away from the film's usual framing as a civics parable about the jury system and deliberative democracy; I want the epistemology of disagreement proper.

The obvious hook is Juror 8's opening position: eleven votes to convict, one to hold out, in a room where everyone has heard exactly the same trial testimony and where the film gives us little reason to think the lone dissenter is smarter or better informed than the majority. On the surface that is a textbook case of peer disagreement, and if the equal-weight prescription applied, Juror 8 ought to have slashed his confidence toward the crowd's. His proposed secret ballot — he agrees to go along with conviction if the other eleven remain unanimous without him — looks almost like a pre-committed conciliationist concession. Yet he never actually lowers his view in proportion to the numbers, and the film plainly treats that as heroic rather than vicious. I'd like you to say whether the film endorses steadfastness, endorses conciliationism, or quietly shows that the question is malformed in this setting.

Three complications are worth pressing, and a good reading should not paper over them. First, peerhood: the film is full of asymmetries that seem to license unequal weighting — Juror 5's familiarity with how a switchblade is actually used, Juror 9's habit of noticing what others miss (the marks on the nose of a witness, and of Juror 4), Juror 3's displaced anger at his son, Juror 10's prejudice, Juror 7's indifference about a ballgame. Does the film think a bare head count is evidentially insignificant, and does it favor assessing the reasoning behind an opponent's belief over weighting the belief itself? Second, evidence accumulation: once the duplicate knife, the paced-out walk to the witness's door, the angle of the wound and the eyeglass impressions enter the common ground, the jurors are no longer disagreeing on a shared total evidence, which arguably dissolves the disagreement problem rather than answering it. Is there a residual disagreement that survives the sharing — Juror 4 and Juror 3 remain unmoved by arguments that move everyone else — and what does the film do with it? Third, the aim of the deliberation isn't simply truth but a binary verdict under an asymmetric default: the burden of proof means "split the difference" has no meaning here. Does that structural feature save Juror 8's stubbornness from being epistemic vice, and would the move generalize outside the courtroom?

I'd especially like your view on Juror 11's rebuke of Juror 7 for switching his vote simply because continuing the discussion has become tedious. That scene is the film's most explicit statement about how votes ought to change, and it seems to insist that revision must answer to reasons rather than to pressure or convenience — which sits uneasily beside any view that makes another person's disagreement itself a reason to change your mind. Is Juror 7's switch different in kind from the early switches of Jurors 1 and 6, and what does the film's distinction imply about the objection that conciliationism makes believers absurdly spineless? Finally, be honest about where the film resists the reading and where it may get something wrong: it never tells us whether the boy was in fact guilty, so we cannot use Juror 8's eventual vindication as evidence that steadfastness pays off; and Juror 3's surrender arrives through personal collapse over a torn photograph rather than through argument. Does the film thereby concede that the deepest disagreements are not settled by reasons at all — and does that support or undercut the project of an epistemology of disagreement? Point me to the three or four scenes you would screen for the seminar, say what each is doing in the argument, and close with what you think the film adds to this debate that argument alone doesn't.
