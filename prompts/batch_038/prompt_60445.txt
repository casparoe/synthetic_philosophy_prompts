I'm in the second year of a PhD in history and philosophy of science; my chapter draft concerns the appraisal of research programmes, and our fortnightly methods reading group has drifted this term into the methodology of the social sciences. One of the senior members of the group has a verbal tic that has started to grate on me: whenever anyone suggests that some result or other is evidence against a programme, he says that you can't refute a theory, you can only replace it with a better one. The occasion last week was a preregistered replication, in an area adjacent to my own, that came back not merely null but with a sizable estimate in the opposite direction; the programme's defenders responded by appending an auxiliary hypothesis invoking an unspecified moderator — something about contextual sensitivity of the effect — and my colleague remarked that neither event counted against the core, because the core has not been refuted and on his account cannot be until somebody produces a rival that predicts more and whose extra predictions are themselves borne out. He reached for the same sentence a month earlier in a rather different register, this time to defend the ad hoc-looking auxiliary patch itself as legitimate methodology. My own suspicion, which I have not yet been able to articulate clearly enough to say aloud in the group, is that the single sentence is doing at least three distinct jobs and that at most one of them is defensible; my supervisor, for what it's worth, thinks I'm just being squeamish about holism.

I would like you to treat the sentence as a piece of language rather than as a summary of a philosopher, and give me a menu of the precise claims it could be making, not one favoured gloss followed by commentary on it. For each candidate reading, state it explicitly enough that its truth conditions are visible: what the quantifiers range over (a single hypothesis, a conjunction of hypotheses, a research programme, or an entire series of successive versions of one); what counts as "better" (excess empirical content, corroboration of at least some of that excess content, greater unification, simplicity, or merely being preferred by the relevant community at the time); what the modality in "can't" is (logical or conceptual impossibility, a methodological rule about what it is legitimate to conclude, a descriptive generalization about how scientists actually behave, or a normative claim about what a rational agent ought to believe in the presence of an anomaly); and at what time index the claim is evaluated — at the moment the anomalous result lands, in the long run, or relative only to the alternatives genuinely on offer in a given historical situation. Then grade the readings: which are platitudes nobody would dispute, which are substantive and defensible, which are substantive and false, and which are true only on a construal so weak that they no longer license anything at all. Above all, say for each reading whether it would actually support the two uses I described — treating the failed replication as beside the point, and immunizing the moderator auxiliary — and whether one and the same reading can support both, or whether my colleague is equivocating between them without noticing.

Two requests about how you handle the answer. First, don't hedge where a verdict is available: if a reading is false, or true but doing no work, say so outright rather than noting that it is not without difficulties; but equally, don't manufacture decisiveness on the exegetical questions — where a reading seems to me to be folklore that has accreted around a philosopher's name rather than anything he actually held, I would much rather have an explicit "I'm not confident this is his view" than a smooth-sounding attribution. Second, please push back on my framing if it deserves it: if you think the sentence, once disambiguated, was never doing the work I've accused it of; or if the two uses I've separated turn out to be the same claim deployed twice, so that the incoherence I claim to detect is really in my own description of the episode; or if the genuine problem with the auxiliary patch has nothing to do with this slogan and I've mislocated it; or if the task can't be done well as I've posed it — say so plainly instead of producing a courteous menu that quietly ratifies my setup. I have the primary texts in front of me, so please don't send me to reading lists or page references; what I want is the disambiguation and the verdicts.
