I'm a third-year PhD student and I run our department's little podcast — one decent mic, a lot of Zoom calls, me doing all the editing at 1am. Next month's guest is a visiting moral philosopher with a pretty specific account of lying, and I want to prep properly, because my usual questions ("so, how did you get interested in this?") reliably produce ninety seconds of nothing. Can you act as an experienced philosophy interviewer — someone who's moderated a lot of colloquium Q&As that were hard but fair — think carefully about the actual structure of her view before writing, and then give me eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up attached?

Her view, as I understand it, so you can key the questions to it rather than to a generic "lying is bad" position: the wrong-making feature of a lie isn't the intention to deceive and isn't the falsity of what's said, but something like usurpation — the liar seizes a decision that belongs to the hearer, namely which beliefs to form about the world, and thereby converts the hearer's own deliberation into an instrument of the speaker's purposes. Two things fall out. First, a transparent lie — one the hearer spots as a lie, or would gladly endorse if she knew about it — doesn't wrong anyone on her account, since no deliberation was hijacked. Second, bluntness is the mirror image: a refusal to usurp that can still dump real costs on the person who has to hear it. She also thinks this explains why Dutch-style directness and Japanese-style indirection feel like different moral cultures rather than just different table manners: in the second, a lot of ordinary speech is what she'd call benevolent usurpation — you quietly take charge of someone's beliefs for their own sake — which on her view isn't automatically a wrong, but it does shift who is doing the deliberative labour in the relationship.

I want questions that turn on that machinery, not on lying in general. Please include at least one on the residue problem: if transparent white lies wrong nobody, why does so much of our ordinary unease about them survive, and is she really entitled to write off the entire phenomenology as error? One on whose endorsement is doing the work — actual, hypothetical, or idealized — and the squeeze that comes with each: if it's hypothetical the view licenses exactly the paternalism we'd want to block, if it's actual she can't explain why lying to someone who would never endorse it is worse than lying to someone who might. One on whether her cultural contrast is a claim about norms or about the distribution of inferential burden, and how you'd tell those apart from inside either culture without just redescribing the stereotype you started with. One on non-paradigmatic hearers — children, people with advanced dementia — where the thing being usurped arguably isn't there yet or isn't there anymore. One on whether bluntness can itself become a kind of reverse usurpation, and whether there's ever a right not to be told. One on Kant's reply to Benjamin Constant about the murderer at the door: does she owe the Kantian verdict, and if not, what in her account is playing the role the categorical imperative played in his? And one closing question on what argument or evidence would make her abandon the usurpation story for a plain deception-based one.

Two more constraints. She's very prone to answering "well, it depends on the case," so at least one question should be built so that answer isn't available. And keep everything speakable — short sentences, no clause nests I'd fumble reading aloud. For each follow-up, actually predict the answer she's most likely to give and write the second question so that the predicted answer becomes uncomfortable; don't just tack on "and why is that?" Finally, when you're done, put a single last line in the exact form `\answer{...}` containing just the number of the one question you think she is least likely to have a good answer to.
