ok so I need help with a speech and I'm kind of out of my depth. I'm the director of a small regional museum — 900 members, one wing, the town's idea of a big night — and Saturday we're unveiling a landscape panel that's been in our own storage since 1961, catalogued the whole time as by a well-known Dutch painter. Six weeks ago our conservator sent me her technical report: the panel and the pigments are right for the period, but the underdrawing has a habit of hatching that doesn't match the master's other panels, and she thinks it's much more likely a good workshop copy than the thing itself. She hasn't gone public. The two art historians we've invited to say a few words have both seen the report and they disagree completely about whether it even matters. One thinks it changes nothing aesthetically — a picture that looks exactly the same to a competent viewer is as good a picture, and the only real loss is money and a bit of romance. The other thinks that's naive: you can't see a painting except as belonging to some category, and categories are fixed by who made it and how, so the moment you believe it's a copy you'll be seeing different features — what looked like boldness starts looking like derivativeness — and there's no going back to the old way of seeing it. I've read enough of both to know I don't know who's right, and I don't want to settle it in five minutes at a wine reception.

It gets worse. The local paper ran a full page feature last month off the old attribution and a photograph, calling it one of the finest things in the county, and a good chunk of our members have read that and formed an opinion without ever having stood in front of it. The historians say that's harmless, that the review just pointed at something already there. The conservator says its exactly backwards and they'll be looking for what they were told to find. I think she's right and I don't know what to do about it, since I'm the one who sent them the photograph.

What I have to give is the opening remarks, four or five minutes, before the cloth comes off. In the room: donors and the town council, a few families with kids who came for the free cider, both historians in the front row, our conservator who is dreading being blamed for ruining the evening, and one trustee who is quietly furious because the insurance valuation and the climate-control grant were both based on the attribution. You can leave the painter unnamed — "our landscape", "the panel" — I'll say the real names myself.

The speech has to:
- tell people about the report without turning the evening into a retraction, and without lying about what we know. I can't call it a masterwork and I can't call it a copy either, and I won't say "scholars will continue to debate".
- not pick a side between the two historians, but also not paper over the fact that they're actually disagreeing. The front row will smell a dodge in two sentences.
- give the room something concrete to do with their eyes in the ninety seconds after the unveiling — an instruction about how to look at a picture when you're not sure of its history, and what to do about the review they've already read. That's the only place I can see the two views being held in one breath, so please make that the hinge of the thing rather than an afterthought near the end.
- say something to the conservator that lets her feel her report was an act of honesty and not sabotage, without it turning into an HR moment or naming her at all.
- be speakable by a non-actor to people holding glasses.

Please write the whole speech in the words I'd actually say, not an outline or stage directions, roughly 600–750 words. Warm and plain-spoken, a little dry humour allowed in maybe two places. No "tonight we gather", no courage, no journeys, no applause lines that would embarrass me in front of the historians — if a sentence only works because it's vague, cut it.
