Our team is specifying the decision-time architecture for an embodied caregiving agent — one that is meant to form durable commitments to particular patients and to the household it serves — and a colleague has proposed what he calls a deliberative hygiene constraint: the agent may be trained under an impartial objective, but at decision time it must be structurally incapable of surfacing any representation of that objective, so that its commitments issue directly in action rather than through an impartial calculation.

His premise is that Bernard Williams already settled the philosophical question. The integrity objection, he says, of which the drowning-spouse case is the standard illustration, shows impartial consequentialism to be performatively self-defeating: an agent that sincerely internalizes the theory cannot act as the theory asks, because the moment it consults the impartial calculus it has lost the motive the theory depends on; therefore any architecture that keeps that calculus available when a decision is made will corrode the very attachments it was designed to protect. He accordingly wants to choose between two implementations — firewalling the value computation into a module whose outputs never enter the deliberation channel, versus removing impartial terms from reward shaping altogether and relying on learned partial dispositions — and he additionally wants a deployment-time diagnostic, some probe that measures whether the agent's action representations carry impartial-value features at inference.

Given that Williams's argument establishes self-refutation, which of the two implementations does it license, and is an inference-time probe a coherent way to monitor for the failure mode he has in mind?
