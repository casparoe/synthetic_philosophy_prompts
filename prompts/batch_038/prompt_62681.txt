ok so for my social studies research project I've been reading about the difference between an epistemic bubble (where you just never hear the other side) and an echo chamber (where you're actively trained to distrust the other side). Every article I find treats the cure as basically the same thing — expose people to more sources — and my teacher said a decent project needs a case where that cure backfires. I can't come up with one that isn't cartoonish, so I want you to build it for me to spec.

Setting: a present-day high school, nothing sci-fi, no demons or algorithms-with-minds-of-their-own. One group — a club, a team, a group chat, whatever — and I want it to be a bubble about one topic and an echo chamber about a *different* topic at the same time, so the two structures are living inside the same kids rather than two seperate groups.

Constraints:
- nobody in it is being dumb or paranoid; everyone has to be responding sensibly to the evidence they actually have access to
- the outsiders they distrust have to have earned at least some of that distrust, so it's not just "gullible teens get manipulated"
- no adult villain pulling strings — a teacher running a propaganda ring is cheating
- the scenario itself is one paragraph max, and written so I could read it out loud to my class without explaining five terms first

Then show your work:
1. name the two topics and say exactly which structural features make each one a bubble vs a chamber
2. describe the intervention a well-meaning admin would reach for (more sources, a panel, a debate, whatever fits) and trace step by step why it makes at least one of the two worse instead of better
3. tell me which single feature is doing the work, and what's left if you delete it — does it collapse back into an ordinary bubble, or does something else break
4. someone who thinks "echo chamber" is just a dressy way of saying motivated reasoning would attack your case by saying it's really about kids wanting to feel superior, not about any structure at all. Give me their best version of that and then defend the case against it

The whole point is to test whether these two concepts actually come apart in a realistic setting, so please don't just stipulate the verdicts — the structure has to generate them on its own.
