Please answer this as a professional philosopher who also has a serious working command of formal decision theory — someone who has taught the material to advanced students and can keep the technical questions cleanly separated from the normative ones. Before you commit to any view, I would like you to begin with a short brainstorming section: lay out the candidate diagnoses of my situation, including at least one that would vindicate me and one that would vindicate my granddaughter, and only then work through them. Think carefully, and do not simply hand my own hunches back to me in better words.

Let me describe the situation. It is a practical one, though I suspect it has a shape you will recognize more readily than I do. I am a retired analytical chemist; I spent thirty-five years at a national measurement laboratory, where nothing was ever reported without a documented uncertainty and a traceable record behind it. Since retiring I have served as one of five trustees of a small charitable foundation in my county, and I chair its grants committee. This autumn we had to choose between two applications for our single large multi-year award, both concerning water quality in our watershed. The first came from a long-established council proposing a riparian planting programme of a familiar kind. Between our own files and published evaluations, we have followed thirty comparable projects over two decades: ten met their stated five-year targets, twenty did not, and the reasons for those failures are well understood by people who were present for them. The second application was of a type with no precedent here — paying farmers directly against nitrogen loads measured by a newly deployed sensor network. Our two outside reviewers judged the design more ingenious and the need more urgent than anything else in the pile, and one of them wrote that there is no comparable case anywhere, so the honest answer about its prospects runs from total waste to a model everyone copies. We voted four to one for the first proposal. The dissenting trustee, a retired schoolteacher, said that we had chosen the project we could describe rather than the project we actually wanted.

A few weeks later my granddaughter, who is finishing a doctorate in statistics, heard about the vote and tested me over dinner with two wagers. First: a thousand dollars if the planting programme hits its five-year target, against a thousand dollars if the sensor programme would have hit its target had we funded it. I took the planting programme without hesitating. Second: a thousand dollars if the planting programme misses its target, against a thousand dollars if the sensor programme would have missed. I took the planting programme again, again without hesitating. She said that my two answers flatly contradict one another, that they cannot both be explained by any single set of numbers I hold about the two programmes, and that a person with preferences like mine can be bled dry by a counterparty willing to trade with him repeatedly. I asked her to write out that sequence of trades for the two wagers exactly as I had answered them, and she could not; she said the trick requires a longer game. I have been turning that over ever since.

Three further things I have noticed, which I mention because I do not know whether they matter. First, when the committee discussed the sensor proposal on its own, weeks before the other file reached us, two trustees spoke warmly of it; the unanimity emerged only once the two applications were lying side by side on the table. Second, my reluctance is far stronger here than in cases where my ignorance comes from somewhere else: I am perfectly calm about not being able to predict whether next June will be wet, and I would bet on the weather without a second thought, yet the same blank in the record feels entirely different when it is produced by applicants who chose what to show us. Third, although the money is committed and nothing can now be undone, I find that I would gladly pay a consultant to go looking for comparable cases elsewhere — I cannot explain to myself what I would be buying. And behind all of this sits a hunch I have not been able to state properly to the board: that the absence of a track record is itself a piece of evidence, because organisations that have good records advertise them.

So, concretely: is my granddaughter right that my two answers cannot both stand, and can you show me precisely why, in terms I could repeat to her? Does her claim about being bled dry actually land on the two wagers as I answered them, or only on something else she may have had in mind? If my preferences admit of no coherent numerical description, what follows from that — am I exposed to some real harm, or only to a bookkeeper's disapproval? Does the fact that my preference for the planting programme weakened when the two files were considered apart show that I never had the preference at all? Is my hunch about disclosure worth anything, and if so does it account for everything I have reported to you or only part of it? And finally, as a matter of practice: should I ask the board to reopen the vote, and what should we do differently when the same choice comes round next year? In answering, please keep four questions distinct — whether my preferences are formally coherent, whether any incoherence in them makes me practically irrational, whether they are nonetheless epistemically defensible given how my ignorance arose, and what an institution like ours should do — and tell me where the theory is genuinely contested rather than implying a consensus that does not exist.
