So — background, briefly. I ran a physical chemistry lab for 35 years, mostly reaction kinetics, retired four years back, and now I read philosophy because my knees dont do the hiking anymore. Lately I've been going round and round on whether the world is actually getting less violent, and I want a number from you plus the working. Not a shrug, not "it depends how you define violence" — I already know it depends, I want to see the dependence laid out where I can inspect it.

Here's the proposition, as precisely as I can state it:

(P) A human born in 2025 faces a lower lifetime probability of being killed by violence — homicide, war, genocide, state execution — than a human born in 1900 did, and this is a real, stable feature of the world rather than an artifact of how we've chosen to measure and slice it.

Give me your credence in P, then break it into the terms you think it's actually built out of, with a rough probability for each and one line of justification. The terms I'd expect to see — add or cut as you like, you're the one doing the arithmetic:

1. Definitional drift. In my lab, if the reference material changes you cant report a trend, you report a calibration artifact. What counted as violence expanded enormously across the century: colonial "pacification," wife-beating, the beating of children and apprentices, most industrial deaths. So give me P(the series is measuring the same quantity at both ends).

2. The denominator. Roughly 1.6 billion people in 1900, about 8 billion now. Per-capita rates can fall while absolute body counts rise. Which of those is the morally relevant number, and does your answer change your credence in P, or only in some other proposition you think people are confusing with P?

3. Documentation. Pre-1950 homicide counts are patchy and war deaths on the periphery were often never counted at all. Which direction does that measurement error bias the trend — up or down?

4. Tail risk. The distribution is fat-tailed: nuclear exchange, engineered pathogens, great-power war, whatever the AI people are worried about this decade. Does a low mean rate mean anything when the variance is dominated by rare enormous events? So: P(the last eighty years is a genuine regime change rather than a lucky gap).

5. Widening the net. If you fold in non-lethal violence — incarceration, displacement, chronic fear, and animals in industrial farming if you count them — does the trend survive at all?

Now the part I actually care about, and here I want you to behave like a scientist rather than a commentator: run the stipulation twice. Once with the definition frozen at 1900 standards — count only what a 1900 coroner or a 1900 war office would have counted. Once at today's expanded standards. Tell me whether P survives both, or only one, or neither. That's the test that separates "the world changed" from "our bookkeeping changed," and I've never seen it done cleanly in the stuff I've been reading.

Then combine and give me the final number, and say which single term it's most sensitive to. Also tell me, for each of the five, whether you think it's a measurement problem or a metaphysical one — in my field those get conflated constantly and it wrecks arguments, and I suspect the same thing is happening here. Related: I've been reading Popper on whether there can be laws of history, and I find him pretty persuasive that there can't be big predictive ones, which leaves me wondering what kind of claim P even is — a law, a trend, or just a description of one very long sample. Say which you think it is, because that seems to bear on whether 4 should be weighted the way you weighted it.
