I write a science-and-ideas column and I'm trying to do a piece on a question that sounds trivial and turns out not to be: what do pleasures actually have in common? Warm bath, the last step of an argument clicking into place, a joke landing, the taste of something very cold when you're overheated and it's almost painful — everyone insists these are all "pleasures" and I can't find the shared ingredient anywhere. Before I interview a single person I want the spec, so: write me the desiderata. What does any account of what makes a pleasure a pleasure have to deliver — which cases it must get right (I'm thinking the person who enjoys something throughout and then sincerely says afterwards it was horrible, the person who doesn't want an intense pleasure to carry on, pleasures that are supposed to be about something unpleasant), which distinctions it can't collapse, and which results it can't contradict, including the clinical finding that anticipating a pleasure comes apart from having one and the neuroscience line that liking and wanting are seperate systems. Give me the case or the consideration behind each requirement, not just the requirement.

The bar for adequacy, so you know what you're aiming at: good enough to tell me whether "pleasure" names one thing or is a grab bag we've been mistaking for a natural kind. Then say which of your demands are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just one camp's prejudices dressed up as criteria, and whether some subset of them can't all be met at once — if the list is itself a no-go result, that's the story and I want it stated plainly. One neuroscientist pitched me the view that pleasure isn't a quality of an experience at all but a marking the system applies to it; treat that as a serious candidate rather than a curiosity and say whether it survives your list. Short scorecard on the main existing options at the end. British English, please.
