I am a junior, and I help coordinate my school's Thursday philosophy discussion group. Last week our topic was whether dreams are experiences. Two members, Elena and Marcus, had each read a little of the Malcolm–Dennett argument beforehand, but as far as I could tell the moves they actually made were their own, built on cases I do not remember from anything either of them cited. I took notes. Below is their exchange, condensed but faithful to the order in which things were said. I did not take a side at the time and I do not have one now; I would like a referee who was not in the room.

1. **Elena.** For something to be an experience there must be a determinate stretch of time during which it is going on, such that it makes sense to ask what part of it the subject is in. Dreams fail that test. No outside observer can catch someone in the middle of a dream in a way that reveals how long the dream has been running; dreamers' estimates of dream length bear no reliable relation to elapsed sleep time, so that an hour-long adventure seems to occupy a few minutes of rapid-eye-movement sleep; and if a person is woken with no impression of having dreamt, there is nothing they were doing. The only item with a determinate time of occurrence is the impression on waking. So "I dreamt that p" reports an impression, not an overnight episode, and a dream is not something that happens to you while you sleep.

2. **Marcus.** That first premise is the whole argument, and it is exactly what needs defending. Requiring a determinate time and place assumes an experience is a presentation — one unified show running at a moment in a stream. Deny that assumption and the conclusion does not follow. Mental processes can be distributed, with their content settled only as they are recruited into reporting. Compare digestion or an immune response: real, causally efficacious, and nobody thinks they need a timestamp inside consciousness in order to occur. And the sleep data point the other way: how dense and vivid a report is tracks sleep stage, and the pattern of eye movements during a period of sleep tracks features of what gets reported about that period. If literally nothing happened during the night, those correlations would be inexplicable.

3. **Elena.** Both halves of that reply fail. On the data: a stage-of-sleep correlation is equally consistent with my view, because the waking impression is itself an event tied to arousal, so the correlation could be between sleep stage and the availability of an impression rather than between sleep stage and an episode. The data is neutral, and my argument was conceptual anyway: even perfect imaging could not show a dream's temporal boundaries, because there is nothing for the boundaries to be boundaries of. On the analogy: it proves too much. Memory consolidation and thermoregulation are also real nocturnal processes and nobody calls them experiences. If a dream is a process in that sense, then you have conceded the substantive point — that it is not something the subject undergoes — while keeping the word. On your view a person woken with no impression has nevertheless dreamt, and then "dreamt" means nothing more than "his brain was in stage R."

4. **Marcus.** I am not reducing dreams to stage R, and I agree most sleep processes are not experiences. But your inference from "no impression" to "no dream" smuggles in a retention requirement: you are making the reality of an experience depend on its being kept. Two cases. My younger brother often wakes announcing with total confidence that he dreamt, produces no content at all, and then in the afternoon a fragment surfaces that he describes as belonging to that night; on your account there was no dream when he woke and a dream later in the day, which is backwards. And in confabulation experiments subjects given a plausible prompt will generate dream reports they take to be memories of their night. If impressions are constitutive of dreams, then being led to produce a story counts as having had the dream. So impressions are neither necessary nor trustworthy, which means there has to be something that they are impressions of.

5. **Elena.** The brother case is describable just as well on my side, without the extra entity: at waking there is an inchoate impression of having dreamt, and its content fills in as retrieval proceeds. Nothing requires that a finished episode be stored somewhere awaiting access. And your confabulation point cuts against you, not me. It shows that impressions are an unreliable marker; unreliability of a marker is not evidence for an unmarked thing. If the only evidence for a nocturnal episode is a report that can be produced on demand, your "something else" is posited precisely where evidence is impossible, which is the classic sign of an empty posit. My account keeps dreaming where it can be checked: in the one thing we actually have.

6. **Marcus.** Notice that "checked" keeps changing meaning. You began with interruptibility, which is supposed to be a third-personal test, but now an interruption only counts if the subject notices it. Processes can be interrupted without anyone noticing — you can suppress an immune response and feel nothing — so requiring a *noticed* interruption to establish that something was occurring defines the phenomenon in terms of the available evidence for it. And your view has costs you have not priced. If there are only waking impressions, then ordinary thoughts — that a noise interrupted my dream, that two people once dreamt much the same dream, that I was halfway through it when the alarm went — do not come out false, they come out unintelligible. A theory that turns that much everyday talk into nonsense rather than error is paying a very high price for its tidiness.
