My sister Maya and her husband Dev got into an argument over dinner that lasted the better part of two hours, and I was stuck in the middle of it with no way to break it up. Here's roughly how it went, in order.

It started because Maya did one of those mail-in DNA kits and got a report saying she's 18% something-or-other, and she announced it to the table as a fact. Dev said he didn't think she was entitled to that. His reason wasn't the usual one about percentages shifting between versions of the algorithm — he said the report is downstream of what people *say*. The company builds its reference groups out of customers who claim all four grandparents came from the same place, so the labels on the comparison populations come from self-reported family stories. Which means, he said, the number she's treating as a measurement is really a machine reading built on top of other people's word about their own grandmothers.

Maya said that's just confusion. A lab isn't testifying to her, it's measuring her. Instruments don't tell you things, they show you things, so whatever she's supposed to be suspicious of, it isn't testimony.

Dev said it is testimony, because what actually arrived was a written report in English, addressed to her by name, with a stated confidence level — a company asserting something to her, which she accepted on the spot with zero independent checking.

Maya said everyone does that all the time and it would be absurd not to. Practically everything she knows about the world beyond what she can see out her window came to her on someone's word: the dates of historical events, the existence of Antarctica, her own grandfather's name. If you had to have positive evidence in hand before accepting any of it, you'd never get started, since the evidence you'd appeal to is itself more of the same. Trust first is the only door in.

Dev said that proves too much. A guy knocks on your door, says he's a licensed roofer, and you hand him your savings — same structure, obviously not okay. Trust has to be earned source by source, claim by claim. He added that if default trust needed no backing whatsoever, gullibility wouldn't be an epistemic failing at all, and it plainly is.

Maya said his "earn it case by case" procedure is made of the same stuff he's trying to avoid. How does she know the company is reliable? Consumer reviews, a magazine article, her friend Priya who did the same kit and says it matched her known family history, the fact that they're accredited by somebody. All of that is further testimony. So he's vetting testimony with testimony, which is exactly her point: there's no non-testimonial bedrock anywhere, and his local check either collapses into the global default he's denying or collapses into skepticism.

Dev said no collapse is needed. You don't require bedrock, you require a working filter: track each source over time, check for internal consistency, look at what the source has to gain — a company selling kits has an obvious incentive to make results feel personal and definitive — and ask whether the source was in a position to know. That's burden-shifting, not burden-carrying. And he said the incentive test bites right here, since the framing of her report is exactly what a seller would produce.

Maya said that's not a justification for any single belief, it's a heuristic for choosing which testimonial communities to plug into. And she said his own filter undercuts him: run it and the thing that fails is the marketing language around the result, not the chemistry, so his complaint was never really about testimony in the first place.

Somewhere around here Dev said she was reinventing Reid and she said he was doing a bad Hume impression, and I honestly couldn't tell whether either of them knew what they meant.

Then, separately, tell me what both of them are missing — some distinction or some piece of the debate that, if they'd brought it up, would have settled the thing or dissolved it.
