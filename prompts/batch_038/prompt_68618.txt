So I run a monthly disagreement workshop — eight of us, we pick a contested question and try to actually settle it. Two of our regulars have spent the whole year fighting about Aumann's agreeing-to-disagree result, and it's stopped being about the theorem and started being about who's allowed to use the theorem. I want to hand them a joint statement to sign, drafted by somebody who isn't in the fight.

Party A: thinks the result is a live constraint, not a curiosity. Two people who have genuinely exchanged posteriors, both know they've done it, both know the other is a coherent Bayesian, and who still disagree — at least one of them isn't doing what they claim to be doing. So a standing disagreement is always evidence of a defect somewhere (different priors, undisclosed evidence, a break in the common-knowledge chain), and the right response is to go hunting for the defect, not to shrug and call it a difference of worldview. Party A also leans hard on the back-and-forth annoucement result — you state your posterior, I state mine, repeat — and takes it to show that in any finite setup the exchange itself manufactures the common knowledge the theorem needs.

Party B: thinks the theorem is correct and empty exactly where it matters. The common prior does all the work; any pattern of posteriors can be rationalized by backing out to some suitable prior, so the theorem can't be violated in principle, only re-described. And the common-knowledge condition isn't a technicality that a workshop protocol discharges — it requires agents to condition on infinitely nested claims about each other's reasoning and each other's claims about their reasoning, which no actual person does. Party B's stock case: two perfect Bayesians with an identical prior, one of which wipes its memory of the evidence right after using it, so nothing past round one is common knowledge. They insist that's not a counterexample to the theorem, it's a model of us.

The artifact both accept as data: over 30 rounds of our "state a number, read theirs, restate" protocol, 26 converged inside three rounds. In the four that didn't, going back through the transcripts showed the estimand had quietly shifted mid-exchange — they were answering different questions and neither noticed at the time. Party A calls that the theorem working. Party B calls it a protocol that rigs the assumptions in from the start, since both sides are handed one shared question and told to produce a number.
