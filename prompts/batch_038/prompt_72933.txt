THE EXAM QUESTION

Question 2 (25% of the exam; closed book; 60 minutes recommended; approximately 800 words maximum).

Knobe's chairman cases are standardly said to reveal an asymmetry in ordinary attributions of intentional action.

(a) State the two versions of the case and the pattern of responses they elicit.
(b) Explain why that pattern is surprising in light of the orthodox analysis of intentional action.
(c) Explain and evaluate one attempt to account for the asymmetry without appeal to moral considerations.
(d) What, if anything, does the resulting debate show about the relationship between normative evaluation and the concepts of folk psychology?

THE STUDENT'S ANSWER

(1) Knobe's study of 2003 presents participants with two versions of the same story. In both, the vice-president of a company tells the chairman that a proposed program will increase profits but will also affect the environment: in one version the program will harm the environment, in the other it will help the environment. In both, the chairman says that he cares only about making as much profit as possible and does not care at all about the environmental effect, and in both he instructs the vice-president to go ahead, and the effect duly occurs. Asked whether the chairman harmed the environment intentionally, the great majority of participants said yes. Asked whether the chairman helped the environment intentionally, the great majority said no. That asymmetry is the Knobe effect, also called the side-effect effect.

(2) The result is philosophically interesting because it cuts against the standard picture of intentional action in the analytic tradition. On a belief–desire analysis, an agent acts intentionally when she acts out of a pro-attitude toward some goal together with a belief that her action will realize that goal; intentionality is a matter of how the agent's mind stands to the action, not of how the action fares morally. Applied to the chairman, the analysis should return the same verdict in both versions. His beliefs are identical, since he is told of the side effect and accepts that it will occur; his desires are structurally identical too, since in each version he wants the profit and is indifferent to the environmental consequence. If participants nonetheless divide their judgments along moral lines, then either they are not applying the belief–desire analysis or the analysis fails to describe the concept they are actually deploying. The more radical conclusion drawn in the literature is that the concept of intentional action has a normative component, such that whether a foreseen side effect counts as intentional depends in part on whether it is a bad one.

(3) A deflationary response tries to rescue the orthodox analysis. Knobe himself defends the skill hypothesis. On this view intentional action requires the exercise of a relevant skill, and the asymmetry follows from participants' differing assessments of the chairman's competence rather than from the moral status of the outcome. In the harm case the chairman must steer his plan so as to secure the profit while carrying the environmental damage along with it: bringing the damage about as the price of the profit is something he has to do skillfully, and participants therefore count it as intentional. In the help case nothing comparable is required of him, since the environmental benefit arrives for free, as a windfall he neither aimed at nor had to bring about, and participants accordingly withhold the judgment. If this is right, ordinary judgments have been tracking control and competence all along, and no appeal to moral evaluation is needed; the asymmetry is a fact about the concept of skill rather than evidence that folk psychology is morally loaded.

(4) A related thought is that participants are implicitly reasoning with the doctrine of double effect. That doctrine holds that it may be permissible to bring about a bad effect one foresees but does not intend, whereas it is impermissible to bring about the same effect intentionally; it further requires that the bad effect not be a means to the good end and that the good be proportionate. The chairman case looks like a textbook application of it. Participants see that the harm is something the chairman knowingly brings about in the course of pursuing a profitable end, so they treat it as intended, and hence as impermissible; the help, by contrast, is treated as merely foreseen. On this reading the Knobe effect does not show that the folk concept of intentional action is morally loaded. It shows something more reassuring: that ordinary people are competent moral reasoners who apply a long-standing principle of casuistry to a novel case, and apply it correctly.

(5) Two conclusions follow. First, because the effect is robust and does not depend on the precise wording of the vignettes, the concept of intentional action as philosophers have analyzed it must be internally inconsistent: it licenses both the judgment that the chairman intentionally harmed the environment and the judgment that he did not intentionally bring about an environmental effect he knew he would bring about, in cases that are alike in every respect the analysis treats as relevant. Second, the remedy is to stop using intentionality attributions as a guide to moral assessment and to replace them with a purely mental-state notion of acting for a reason, which can be specified without reference to outcomes and therefore without the normative contamination the data reveal.
