so, quick background: 35 years in microbial oceanography, mostly chemosynthetic symbioses down at the vents, retired four years ago, and I've fallen into the philosophy hole headfirst. physics and molecular biology are home turf for me, philosophical vocabulary is not, so please go slow on the terms but don't soften the argument.

here's what I can't get straight in my head. everybody wants a definition of life. there's the NASA-ish one about a self-sustaining chemical system capable of Darwinian evolution, and a dozen others. I follow the usual objections to it — mules and sterile people are alive but can't evolve, viruses do some of the work but not obviously all of it, a beaker of self-replicating RNA is awkward. fine, those are quibbles at the level of the list. but underneath there seems to be a much deeper disagreement about what kind of thing an answer would even be, and I can't tell whether the people on each side are arguing with each other or just past each other.

three camps as I read them:
1. checklist people — life has necessary and sufficient conditions and we just haven't nailed them yet.
2. water people — the analogy is water: pre-Lavoisier you could have listed all the macroscopic stuff (wet, clear, boils near 100, dissolves salt, fills the lakes) and picked out the right stuff most of the time, but the real answer was never a definition, it was a discovery about what the stuff is, and until you have that a definition can only be a placeholder. on this view life might be a single underlying natural kind we haven't identified.
3. family-resemblance people — "alive" is like "game", the instances share overlapping similarities with no common core, and hunting for an essence is a catagory mistake.

what I want: an explanation of what's actually at stake between these, and whether they're answers to the same question at all. some sub-questions that are bugging me:

- if the water analogy is right, what discovery would play the role of H2O? is there any live candidate, or does the analogy quietly assume the thing it needs to establish — namely that there's one underlying kind to find, rather than a grab bag of processes that got going separately more than once?
- camps 2 and 3 seem to give the same practical advice (you can't know yet, stop demanding conditions) for opposite reasons. does the difference show up anywhere? e.g. do they disagree about whether we'd recognize life if a probe brought back something weird from Europa or Enceladus?
- darwinian evolvability is a property of populations and lineages, never of an individual, but being alive feels like a property of individuals. that mismatch looks structural to me, not a nitpick. which of the three handles it best, or do all three inherit it?

to make it concrete, my own critters: the big vestimentiferan tube worms we worked on hatch symbiont-free and pick up their internal sulfur bacteria from the environment afterward, so what's alive and what gets passed down the generations don't line up in any simple way. whatever account you favor, I'd like to hear what it says about whether one of those worms, plus its bacteria, is one living thing or two, or whether that question is malformed.

no reading lists and no name-dropping please, and don't hedge at the end — I want you to actually pick which of the three is most coherent and argue for it against the other two.
