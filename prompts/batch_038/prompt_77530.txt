Assess the following claim. Consequentialism is undermined by the problem of cluelessness: because the long-run consequences of our acts ramify indefinitely, and because many of our acts influence which people will ever exist, we are in no position to determine whether any available act makes things better or worse overall; and a theory that cannot tell us what to do, even in principle, fails as a moral theory. The student's answer, reproduced in full and within the word limit:

The cluelessness objection begins from the scale of our ignorance. Any act we perform sets in motion consequences that extend indefinitely far into the future, and each of these has consequences of its own. A consequentialist asks us to compare the total value of the futures available to us, but we can survey only a vanishingly small initial segment of any of them, and we have no principled way of extrapolating the rest. The difficulty is compounded by the fact that many of our choices affect which people will later exist: as Parfit's non-identity problem shows, a choice that alters who is born alters the very population whose welfare is to be summed, so there is no fixed set of persons against which the alternatives can be compared. On Lenman's diagnosis this leaves us not merely uncertain but clueless — uncertain, in the ordinary sense, about matters we might come to know, but clueless about matters on which we have no basis for forming even a rough estimate.

It matters that this is not simply the complaint that consequentialist calculation takes too long. The impracticability objection says that a finite agent with limited time and information cannot complete a computation that is in principle well defined. The cluelessness objection is meant to be stronger: it says the computation is not well defined for us even in principle, because there is nothing to compute from.

The consequentialist's natural reply is to work with expected utility. What matters for rightness, on this reply, is not the value of the actual future but the value we can reasonably expect it to have; where evidence is thin the probabilities are correspondingly flat, and flat probabilities will in most cases reproduce the ranking common sense already endorses. I think this reply succeeds, though not for the reason usually given. The point is not that the expected-utility framework formally accommodates ignorance — of course it does, since it accommodates any probability assignment whatsoever. The point is that our ignorance of the far future is not as total as the objection assumes. The claim that we cannot estimate the long-run effects of, say, having a child or donating to an effective charity rests on the empirical assumption that no reliable generalizations about such effects are available. That is an empirical claim, and it is contestable. Demography, epidemiology and economic history do yield generalizations about the long-run effects of particular kinds of intervention, and the more of this work is done, the better our estimates become. Cluelessness of the kind Lenman describes is therefore a temporary condition of a young science rather than a permanent feature of the moral situation. Once the objection is seen to depend on a claim about the state of our knowledge, it ceases to be an objection to consequentialism specifically and becomes the familiar problem of acting on incomplete evidence, which every moral theory faces.

A related retreat is available within consequentialism itself. Rule-consequentialism evaluates rules rather than acts, and rules are assessed over whole classes of situations; the noise that swamps the long-run effects of any single act may average out across a class. This does not make the problem disappear — the choice of which rules to adopt is itself a choice with indefinite consequences, so the extrapolation difficulty reappears at the level of rule-selection — but it shifts the question from one we cannot answer to one where we have at least some statistical purchase.

The problem is also not peculiar to consequentialism. A threshold deontologist must know how bad a consequence has to be before a constraint is overridden, and that is a comparative judgement about states of affairs. A virtue ethicist who holds that the virtues are those traits that promote human flourishing owes an account of what actually promotes flourishing over time. Any theory that makes rightness depend in part on how things turn out inherits some version of the difficulty.

So the claim in the question is half right. Consequentialism is self-defeating: a theory that asks us to maximize the good while leaving us no way to determine which acts do so will, if conscientiously followed, lead us to act against the good it aims at. But this does not make it false. It may be that an act's rightness is fixed by facts we cannot know. What is fatal is the further assumption, implicit in the question, that a moral theory must be capable of guiding conduct. A theory of rightness and a decision procedure are different things, and our inability to use consequentialism reliably to decide what to do is a fact about us rather than about the theory.
