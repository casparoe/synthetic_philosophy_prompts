ok so i run the podcast for my school's philosophy club and next month we've got a visiting prof coming on who defends the idea that your beliefs shouldn't always be single numbers — sometimes the rational thing is to have a whole set of probability functions instead of one credence. Our audience is high schoolers, some of whom have had a stats class and none of whom have heard of formal epistemology.

Can you write me 8-10 questions, each with a follow-up keyed to the answer he's most likely to give? The follow-ups matter as much as the questions because i want to be able to say them out loud without sounding like im reading off a printout.

stuff i defintely want covered, at least one question apiece:

- dilation, where you conditionalize on some evidence and your credences get *less* sharp instead of more. he's going to say that's a feature not a bug, and i want a follow-up that doesn't just let that slide — something like asking whether a framework where new information makes you more confused can really be tracking your evidence.
- how does an imprecise state actually tell you what to do. there's a whole menu of decision rules people propose (maximality, E-admissibility, some maximin thing) and picking one seems to sneak the precise stuff back in through the side door.
- whether being imprecise is rationally required or only allowed. both answers look bad to me and i want him to pick: if it's required then im irrational right now about a coin i've watched land heads ten thousand times, and if it's only allowed then the framework isn't doing any normative work at all.
- the Ellsberg setup. does ambiguity aversion actually support sets of probabilities, or does it just show people dislike bets where they don't know the odds, which is a different and weaker claim?
- whether the whole thing is a notational trick for saying i'm uncertain about which precise credence function is mine, i.e. second-order uncertainty wearing a costume.
- one question where he has to explain a technical term in plain english without losing the content, aimed at a 16 year old.

also: his stock move when he's cornered is to say some version of it's not a thesis, it's a modeling framework, so build the sequence so that reply gets harder and harder to use as the episode goes on. and close with a question about whether any of this bites outside thought experiments — like climate sensitivity or pandemic modeling, where people already have to act under deep uncertainty — asking whether the imprecise probability answer gives you anything you couldn't get by just saying we don't know.

no generic questions (nothing like what got you into philosophy), no softballs, and please keep each question short enough that i don't run out of breath saying it.
