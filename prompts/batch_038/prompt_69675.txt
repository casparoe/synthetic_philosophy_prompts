(1) A consideration is a reason for me to φ only if I could φ on the basis of it — something that could never move me in φ-ing isn't a reason, it's just an incentive.
(2) The demon case (the one people use for this, Parfit's I think): a demon will torment me unless I admire him. I can't admire the demon on the basis of the threat. Admiration isn't the kind of thing you can do for a payoff. I could maybe bring it about — self-deception, hypnosis, spending more time with his fans — but that's a reason to cause admiration in myself, not a reason to admire.
(3) So the threat isn't a reason to admire him, and there's no such thing as a wrong-kind reason; the phrase just labels an incentive.
(4) Same goes for belief. A million dollars for believing that p can't be followed in believing that p — I can only believe p on the basis of considerations that bear on whether p is true.
(5) So the only candidates for reasons to believe are truth-related considerations.
(6) But those don't clear (1) either. Take some trivial fact, like the number of books on my shelf. Even if I could settle it, there's nothing to be said in favor of my forming that belief — no point I'd be serving, nothing I'd be missing — and forming beliefs isn't something I can do at will anyway.
(7) So there's no reason to believe that the number of books on my shelf is even, and likewise for most ordinary p.
(8) Which means evidence and truth aren't reason-giving for belief after all; (5)'s candidates fail too.
(9) But by the fitting-attitude / buck-passing schema, p is worthy of belief iff there's reason to believe p, and X is admirable, fearsome, or desirable iff there's reason to admire, fear, or desire X.
(10) Since there are never reasons to believe, and since admiration, fear and desire are equally not-under-my-direct-control — you can't fear at will either, and (2)–(3) already handled admiration — nothing is worthy of belief, admirable, fearsome, or desirable.
(11) Reasons to act survive, because acting is voluntary. But every normative property that reasons were supposed to explain comes out empty. So the wrong-kind-reasons problem isn't a problem: it dissolves, and it takes the reasons-first program plus epistemic normativity down with it — there is literally nothing wrong, reason-wise, with someone believing the earth is flat.
