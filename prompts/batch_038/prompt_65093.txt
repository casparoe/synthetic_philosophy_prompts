I am setting the take-home examination for an upper-level seminar in political philosophy on the justification of institutions, and I need one essay question, a model answer, and a marking scheme.

The question must be self-contained. A candidate who has never read Burke or Chesterton, and who brings only the conceptual vocabulary we developed in seminar, should be able to answer it from what is printed on the page. So the question has to embed a concrete case with all the relevant facts supplied. Please use the following invented case, or something materially equivalent to it:

*A county ordinance adopted in 1937 requires that any newly drilled residential well be sited at least one hundred feet from any adjoining property line. The ordinance's preamble was destroyed in a 1972 courthouse fire, and no statement of legislative reasons survives; the county's own archivists report that no living official can say why the distance was set at one hundred feet rather than fifty. The planning staff now propose repeal, relying on hydrogeological modeling which indicates that fifty feet is adequate for the county's soils. A well-water users' association objects, arguing that the burden lies on those who would remove a rule whose purpose has been lost. The staff's alternative proposal is a five-year pilot: the setback is waived on a defined set of lots, with quarterly groundwater sampling and automatic reinstatement of the rule if any sample exceeds a contamination threshold. Separately, the setback has made infill construction infeasible on the small lots concentrated in one unincorporated part of the county, and the association supporting repeal notes that the resulting housing scarcity falls mainly on renters, none of whom had any voice in 1937 and none of whom are represented in the current proceedings.*

The question should be answerable in about two hours of writing and worth 25 marks. Its difficulty must not come from exegesis. It should turn on a distinction my candidates kept collapsing in discussion: between (i) a procedural allocation of the burden of proof — a default presumption against dismantling an arrangement whose rationale cannot be recovered — and (ii) a substantive thesis about inherited arrangements, namely that they tend to encode functional wisdom that no individual reformer is in a position to reconstruct. These come apart in both directions: one can accept the default while rejecting the substantive thesis, and one can accept the thesis without endorsing the default. The case is constructed so that the two components have different fortunes, and the question must require candidates to see that.

Specifically, the question should require them to do all four of the following:

1. Say which of (i) and (ii) the objectors actually need for their conclusion, and what happens to the argument if only one is available.
2. Identify the premises each component requires to be true *of this ordinance* — not true of traditions in general — for the objection to hold, and say which of those premises the stated facts support, which they leave open, and which they undercut.
3. Confront the objection that persistence is not evidence of function. This has two edges I want both drawn: the ordinance's eight-odd decades without a documented contamination event cannot distinguish the hypothesis that the rule prevented harm from the hypothesis that the harm was unlikely in any case, and a rule may survive for reasons — inertia, the absence of organized challengers, the interests of those it favors — that have nothing to do with its working.
4. Reach a verdict on the repeal proposal, including the pilot, and defend it rather than assert it. The pilot is there to force the question whether a monitored, reversible, partial removal discharges the burden the fence principle assigns, or whether the monitoring is itself a removal that the principle forbids.

After the question, give a model answer of roughly 700 words. Then give the marking scheme: allocate all 25 marks across the parts, and separate an A from a B not by general praise but by naming the specific moves each must contain. Follow that with four or five characteristic errors and what each costs, and be as concrete as you can about the shape of them. Candidates in past years have, for instance, read the fence principle as a claim about the wisdom of traditions when it is doing procedural work; conflated it with the precautionary principle, which looks forward to the risks of new interventions rather than backward at the rationale of existing arrangements; credited Burke with the fence slogan, when his argument runs through prejudice, prescription, and the limits of abstract reason rather than through the justification of individual rules; and treated the hydrological model as reason on one side and the ordinance as mere tradition on the other, when both are evidential sources of different kinds and neither certifies itself. If you see other errors that the case as constructed is likely to provoke, list them instead or in addition.

Two further requirements. First, on epistemic footing: please separate what is settled from what is genuinely contested among people who know this literature. Where your model answer offers an interpretation of Burke, or claims that a distinction is standard rather than your own, mark it as such, and do not present one side of a live interpretive dispute as the consensus reading. Where the model answer takes a controversial line on the verdict, the marking scheme must say explicitly that the contrary verdict earns full credit provided the candidate does the work that would justify it. Second, do not include a bibliography, reading list, or citations; candidates sit this without library access, and everything they need must be in the question itself.

Please write the whole thing in American English.
