I audit an interdisciplinary seminar on animal communication at my university, and at the last session two of the regulars — Nadia, a behavioral ecologist, and Owen, a philosopher who works on models and explanation — got into an argument that went on for most of the hour. I took notes but was out of my depth and couldn't tell who was winning. Here is the exchange as I reconstructed it; the moves are numbered for reference.

1. Nadia opened with the peacock's tail as the paradigm case of Zahavi's handicap principle. Growing and carrying such a structure is genuinely wasteful, and that waste is precisely what buys reliability: a male in poor condition cannot afford a full display, so a female reading the tail can trust it. Take the cost away and the signal collapses into bluffing.

2. Owen said this mislocates the mechanism. On Grafen's reconstruction, what does the work is not waste but the shape of the cost function: a given increment of display must cost a low-condition signaler proportionately more than a high-condition one. Given that, honesty is an equilibrium outcome even if the highest-condition males end up with greater net fitness than they would have had without displaying at all. The tail need not be a net burden on anybody.

3. Nadia answered that he had simply drained the label of its teeth. On that reading, an index — a threat vocalization whose lowest attainable pitch is set by the size of the caller's vocal tract — is honest without any cost, and so-called cheap talk between parties whose interests largely coincide is honest at zero cost. If the definition of a handicap is stretched until those cases can be folded in, there is no possible observation that could violate it.

4. Owen conceded part of this: the label does get attached post hoc to any conspicuous, expensive-looking trait, and indices and cheap talk should be excluded because they rest on different honesty mechanisms — physical constraint and interest-alignment, respectively. But he maintained his version is testable, because it predicts an interaction rather than a level. To function as a handicap, adding a fixed amount of ornament must reduce survival more for a low-condition male than for a high-condition male. He then said that most of the field evidence people cite is correlational — the showiest males doing best on every axis, survival included — and that this pattern is not merely compatible with the theory but expected under it, since condition and ornament are confounded in observational data.

5. Nadia called that insulation: a theory that reads apparently contrary data as itself predicted has no way to lose. She said the honest conclusion is that observational studies cannot test handicaps at all, and that only manipulation can — artificially elongating or clipping tail feathers and then tracking who dies — adding that such experiments have yielded small and inconsistent effects, which is itself informative.

6. Owen agreed that most manipulations are inadequate, but for a reason she had not given: they vary ornament alone, not ornament crossed with condition, so they cannot estimate the interaction either. He then raised a second, theoretical objection of his own, drawn from Getty's critiques. Grafen's proof assumes that costs and benefits trade off additively in a single currency, the way money spent trades off against income earned; but fitness is not additive, since an animal that does not survive cannot mate, and rebuilding the model with multiplicative payoffs can make the handicap equilibrium disappear. If the result is an artifact of a payoff assumption nobody can measure, he said, arguing about the empirical evidence is premature.

7. Nadia replied that this proves too much. If the conclusion flips on a currency stipulation, the principle is a modeling convenience rather than a hypothesis about animals, and the correct summary is that once indices, conventional signals policed by receiver retaliation, and cheap talk among allies have been peeled away, there is no distinct residue left that deserves the name.

8. Owen turned to the human cases to make a broader point. Costly religious observances, engagement diamonds, and conspicuous leisure are routinely described as costly signals of commitment or wealth, yet in each the signaler expects to be repaid — in trust, cooperation, or a partner — so none is a handicap in the strict sense either. What maintains honesty there, he said, is the audience's ability to detect and punish bluffs, not the price of the display.

Please adjudicate this exchange rather than summarize the literature. Specifically:

- Go through moves 1–8 and say of each whether it succeeds against what it was responding to — clearly right, partly right, or clearly mistaken — with a brief justification. Where a move concedes something, say whether the concession was required or gratuitous.
- Is Owen's level-versus-interaction distinction a genuine rescue of the handicap principle, or the empty redescription Nadia takes it to be? State an observation that would be incompatible with his version, if one exists, and say what it shows about the principle's content.
- Settle the dispute in moves 4 and 5 over the correlational data. Does the finding that the most ornamented males survive best refute the handicap account, support it, or fail to bear on it? Lay out the inference explicitly, including what would have to be true for Nadia's insulation charge to bite.
- Evaluate Getty-style objection in move 6. Does it undermine the handicap principle as a claim about animals, only one particular proof of it, or neither? Say what would have to be settled to know which.
- Is Nadia right in move 7 that nothing distinctively handicapping remains once the other mechanisms are separated out? In answering, say what would have to be true for cost itself, rather than constraint or retaliation, to be the only available guarantor of honesty.
- Does Owen's move 8 actually bear on the biological question, or does it switch subject matter without argument? If the human cases do bear on it, say how.
- Finally: identify what both of them are missing. Is there a question neither raises — about the receiver's interests, about what counts as a cost, about the role of the models themselves — whose inclusion would change the shape of the dispute? And state plainly which of the two is more right overall, and on which points the other is more right.
