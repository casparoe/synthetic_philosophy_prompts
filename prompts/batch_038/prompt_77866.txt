I'm a software engineer at a company that makes an in-home voice assistant used mostly by people over 75 and by their adult children: medication prompts, appointment scheduling, a daily check-in call, and escalation to a human when something looks wrong. I own the conversational layer, which means that in practice I decide what the system is allowed to say about itself. We have no framework for that, no one with any philosophy training on staff, and I have been adjudicating case by case until I stopped believing I was being consistent.

The decisions are frequent and not hypothetical. Every release adds capabilities and invites new framing from marketing, and users ask the assistant about itself constantly — are you real, are you a person, do you get lonely, will you be here tomorrow, do you like me. One user asked it to stay on the line during a fall. Another asks it to say goodnight to her late husband. The stakes are practical: an overclaim gets us a consumer-protection complaint and may cost a lonely person something real, while our own telemetry suggests flat denials reduce adherence. Product counsel signs off, but only on whether a statement is deceptive as a matter of law; they have no view about minds and will approve "understands" without blinking. Cadence is two weeks. Whatever you produce must be runnable by me in about forty minutes per feature, and readable by a dozen PMs and designers who have never read any philosophy and will not start.

I want a decision procedure grounded in what the imitation-game tradition actually establishes — the proposal that indistinguishability in conversation is a criterion for attributing thought, and the standard reasons given for thinking behavioral equivalence is or is not enough. Translate that into gates I can run on any proposed wording or feature. Specifically I need:

- The criterion being operationalized, stated in one or two plain sentences, plus an explicit list of what the translation drops and why it is safe to drop.
- An ordered sequence of questions, what counts as a sufficient answer at each, where the sequence terminates in a hard no, and where it hands the decision to a named human because what remains is a question of values rather than facts.
- A rule for the system's self-reports. Its answers about its own inner life are produced by the same machinery we would be testing, so interrogating it does not look like evidence; say what the procedure does with the question anyway, given that users will keep asking it.
- Treatment of eval numbers. We can run a transcript study with naive raters and a fixed turn budget and get something like "61 percent could not tell at twelve turns." State precisely what such a figure licenses and what it does not, including why the score partly measures the raters and the modality rather than the system.
- The steps that are judgment calls dressed as measurements. Mark them as such.

I also have two home-grown rules I want you to attack before folding them in, and to say where each misfires:

1. Contractor substitution — rewrite the copy as though a human being were doing the same work behind the interface; if the sentence would be false there, or would legally require disclosure there, it requires disclosure here.
2. A panel number is not a mind-meter — an indistinguishability score may support comparative performance claims only, never claims about what the system is.

Then run the procedure on three cases, showing the reasoning at each gate:

(a) The assistant's standing reply to "are you real / do you have feelings." My draft is a flat denial that it is a program without feelings; our UX research says it alarms people.
(b) A campaign line for the next release: "It understands your mother's routine and knows when something's wrong." The underlying detector flags deviations from a learned baseline with 78 percent sensitivity and roughly one false alert in five.
(c) A line in our internal release notes describing a triage tool that summarized an outage: "it reasoned its way to the root cause."

At least one of these should come out against what I want. If they all come out in my favor, say so, and identify which one a reasonable reviewer would decide differently.

Finally, what the procedure cannot do. I expect it cannot tell me whether the system has experiences, but be specific about the other things it will systematically get wrong, what a determined marketing team could do to clear every gate while still misleading people, and whether the philosophical disagreement over behavioral criteria — a system indistinguishable in conversation yet arguably with nothing it is like to be it, versus the argument that a gradual functional match preserves experience — changes any actual step, or only the confidence with which we hold it. Say plainly if it changes nothing. No bibliography, reading list, or citations: assume nobody reading this has read any of it.
