I'm two thirds through a novel that's basically a two-hander: an older man who built a company from nothing and the much younger man he handed the day-to-day of it to, and the whole book is about how each of them is now stuck needing the other and refusing to admit it. The older one needs to be needed, the younger one needs to not be seen needing.

Midway through, the younger one says to him, flat out: you can't be recognised by someone you despise.

It's the line my readers keep quoting back at me, which is exactly the problem — I think they're hearing about four different claims in it and I'm honestly not sure which one I meant when I wrote it, and it matters because he throws the line back at the younger man in the last chapter and I need to know whether the book is agreeing with him or catching him out.

So: what precise claims could that sentence actually be making? Give me them as a menu, weakest to most radical. Start with whatever reading is just a platitude about gratitude or self-respect, run up through readings that make it a claim about what recognition *is* — whether despising someone makes you incapable of the attitude rather than merely unwilling, whether the contempt has to be reciprocated for the sentence to bite, whether it's about the desirer or the despised or both — and end at whatever version would be a strong metaphysical claim rather than psychology.

For each reading, tell me if it's true, and if it isn't, what near-miss version of it is.

Then the Hegel bit, because I half-stole the shape of it from the master–slave argument and I want to know how much licence that actually gives me. Which readings does the 1807 Phenomenology section genuinely support, and which would be me smuggling something in under his name? Specifically: the standard gloss is that the master ends up with a recognition that's worth nothing because it comes from someone he's reduced to a thing rather than an independent other — does that entail my line, or only something adjacent? And can recognition fail in the other direction too, where the despised one is disqualified as a source, which is closer to what the younger man is actually doing in that scene.

Last thing, and this is the one I can't work out myself: is there a reading where the sentence isn't a truth-claim at all but a move — a way of foreclosing the argument, or a moralising cover for wanting out of a relationship you're complicit in? I suspect that's the reading the last chapter needs, and if it is I'd like to know what would make a reader land on it rather than on the sincere one.
