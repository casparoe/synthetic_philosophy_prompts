In 1952 the American Psychiatric Association's first DSM included a diagnosis of gross stress reaction, intended for transient reactions to overwhelming events; as I understand it, DSM-II in 1968 dropped it, so that for more than a decade there was no official category for combat trauma in American psychiatry. Then DSM-III in 1980 created post-traumatic stress disorder, largely on the strength of a campaign by Vietnam veterans' organizations together with a few clinicians who had run discussion groups with veterans and argued for a distinct post-war syndrome of their own. Over the following decades the diagnosis widened substantially: the DSM-III requirement that the stressor fall outside the range of ordinary human experience was dropped in DSM-IV, the category came to cover sexual assault, accidents, disasters and civilian violence, US lifetime prevalence estimates settled around a tenth of adults, and a large veterans' compensation, disability-rating and litigation apparatus was built around the label.

Take Hacking's looping effects of human kinds and analyze this episode through it, as a case study rather than a slogan. Begin with the mapping: who classifies whom, what exactly is the new description, and where is the loop located? Does a veteran who learns the term flashback and thereafter organizes and reports his intrusive memories under it count as the kind having changed the people classified, or only as a change in reporting about unchanged people — and what would settle the difference?

Then do the allocation, which I think is where the theory is usually fudged. Separate looping proper from three rival explanations of what happened after 1980: detection artifact, where a new checklist simply finds cases that were always there; contagion, where symptom shapes spread through a community by learning and imitation; and strategic response to incentives, where compensation, disability ratings and courtroom credibility change what is reported and even what is felt. Say which of the observed changes belong to which, and whether Hacking's framework has the resources to tell these apart, or whether looping effects functions as an atmosphere that any of them would satisfy.

Two features of the case I want pressed on. First, the people to be classified organized the classification: veterans' groups read the clinical literature, testified to the task force, and supplied the phenomenology that became Criterion A. Does an account whose loop typically runs from experts downward to classified subjects have room for a kind that lobbies for its own creation, and does that make the loop tighter here or reveal that it is doing no explanatory work? Second, after 1980 clinicians and historians began redescribing shell shock, combat fatigue and even nineteenth-century railway spine as proto-PTSD. Retroactive reclassification appears to loop with the dead, who cannot reorganize themselves under a new description. Is that a genuine limit case for the thesis, or a different phenomenon that the vocabulary cannot name?

Close by saying what the episode does to the theory — confirmation, anomaly, or a case it was never intended to cover — and in particular whether it supports or undercuts Hacking's insistence that being made up by a classification is no reason to deny that the classification picks out something real.
