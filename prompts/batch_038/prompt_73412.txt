ok, I need to write something for Thursday and my head is still ringing from a two-hour argument about it last night, so bear with me. I'm one of five members on the Safety Review Board of a regional light-rail agency — the other four are two mechanical engineers, a retired airline accident investigator, and a county lawyer. An anonymous tip came thru our ethics line on Sunday about the hairline cracks we found in the yaw brackets on cars 118 through 141. Maintenance records for that period are garbage, so before the tip we'd been carrying about a 55% credence that the cracked brackets came from supplier Lot 7 rather than Lot 12, and separately we reckoned that if it is Lot 7, there's maybe a 35% chance those parts skipped the stress-relief step in March, which is the failure mode that would actually explain the cracks.

The tip doesn't say any of that. It says, in effect: if the cracked brackets are from Lot 7, then they're the ones that missed stress-relief in March. That's it. Our Safety Assurance Manual §4.2 tells analysts to treat reports as evidence for the proposition stated, which is useless here, because what's stated is a conditional, and the board has now split three ways about what it does to our numbers. The engineers say you just rule out the combination the tip excludes and recompute — which drops us to roughly 30% on Lot 7 and means inspect-only. The retired investigator says the tip says nothing whatsoever about which lot, so 55% stands and we ground the Lot-7 cars, which is about $180k/day in replacement bus service, and our policy threshold for grounding is anything above 50%. And my friend, who did formal epistemology in grad school and who I was arguing with until about 1am, says both are wrong, that the right answer depends entirely on the tipster's own reporting rule — what they would have written had it been Lot 12 — and then he produced a line from the actual tip I'd skimmed past: the person adds that they wouldn't have named Lot 12 even if it were Lot 12, because the Lot 12 vendor is his brother-in-law's shop. He says that one sentence changes everything. I say he's doing philosophy at me instead of helping me decide whether 24 cars run on Monday.

So: draft the memo I'll circulate Wednesday night. It needs a bottom line the board can actually vote on — ground, inspect-only, or buy time by doing something specific first, with a timeframe — plus a short section proposing what §4.2 should say going forward about conditional tips, since we're going to recieve more of these. Assume two of the five readers have never heard of the Judy Benjamin problem and shouldn't have to: explain the disagreement in plain language, in about a paragraph, no jargon, no citations, no bibliography, nothing I'd need to look up. And give me the strongest version of whichever position the memo ends up rejecting, stated fairly enough that the person holding it can say "yeah, that's my view, and here's where you've gone wrong" — if I have to eat crow at the meeting I'd rather do it holding a document that saw it coming. Roughly 800 words. Also tell me, separately from the memo, what piece of information arriving before Thursday would flip your recommendation.
