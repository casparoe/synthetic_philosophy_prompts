I write a fortnightly ideas column for a science mag — mostly neuroscience and AI, with occasional detour's into philosophy, which is where I'm about to get myself in trouble. I'm working on a piece about how the people building value-aligned models keep rediscovering metaethics badly, and I wanted to build the middle of it around the Frege-Geach argument. Here's the paragraph I've got drafted:

"Suppose Geach's point was that if 'murder is wrong' just expresses the speaker's disapproval, then in a conditional — if murder is wrong, it's wrong to pay your little brother to murder — that same sentence has to be expressing something different, since you aren't disapproving of anything when you're merely supposing. So the expressivist equivocates in every moral argument there is. The only repair is to admit the sentence has truth conditions after all, at which point expressivism stops being a rival to moral realism and becomes realism with an embarrassed face."

My editor loves it. But a philosopher I've interviewed before read it over and texted back that I'd got it exactly backwards, that the equivocation charge is the last thing Geach was saying, and then went dark on me. So before I file: what precisely is backwards there, and what is the problem Geach was actually posing? Relatedly, I keep reading the whole argument as an empirical prediction about how speakers deploy moral language, which makes me want to say it could be settled in a afternoon with a corpus and four hundred subjects and that nobody's bothered — is that a stupid reaction or is there something to it? And if the expressivist doesn't have to concede truth conditions, what do they say instead: is there a live dispute left, or has the quasi-realist program quietly dissolved into the realism it was supposed to be an alternative to? Last one, and be blunt — does any of this actually bear on my real subject, whether a language model can be said to have moral attitudes, or am I about to commit a category error in print?

Answer in ordinary continuous prose, please: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just paragraphs the way you'd write an email. I'm going to be lifting sentences out of it, so write it so that any single paragraph can stand on its own.
