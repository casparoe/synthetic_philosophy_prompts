I have been asked to circulate, in advance of a workshop on the debate between internalism and externalism about normative reasons, a statement of my own commitments so that the other participants can test them for coherence. What follows is that statement, and I would be grateful for an assessment of whether it hangs together.

1. Reasons are not psychological states. What makes it the case that I have a reason to Φ is that some consideration counts in favour of my Φ-ing; my beliefs and desires are at most the means by which I register such considerations, and they do not constitute them.

2. I nonetheless accept the internal reasons thesis in something like Williams's formulation: an agent has a reason to Φ only if there is some sound deliberative route from that agent's existing subjective motivational set to their being motivated to Φ. Statements purporting to ascribe reasons that would apply to an agent regardless of what is in that set are false, not merely inexplicit.

3. I construe sound deliberation broadly. It comprises not only means–end reasoning from what the agent already wants, but also the vivid imaginative appreciation of features of a situation the agent had overlooked, and coming to see the point of pursuits to which the agent is at present indifferent. I do not think there is any prior, reason-involving criterion determining which routes are sound: a route is unsound insofar as it is distorted by self-deception, wishful thinking, or inattention, and that is the whole of the matter.

4. I think there can be reasons bearing on one's motivations themselves: it can be that one ought to cultivate certain ends and to shed others, and becoming motivated to Φ is something an agent can have a reason to do.

5. When an agent acts on bad reasons, or fails to act on good ones, they are properly blamed. Blame is not merely the registration of my disapproval, nor a device for altering their motivations; it presupposes that there was a reason they failed to respond to. In particular, I hold that someone wholly indifferent to the suffering of others is criticisable rather than merely inconvenient.

6. Reasons are sensitive to what an agent can do and to what they are in a position to know: no one has a reason to do what is impossible for them.

7. Notwithstanding (6), I think the familiar case of the driver who kills a child entirely without fault of his own is handled correctly by our ordinary responses: such a driver has reason to feel a distress that a mere bystander to the same accident has no reason to feel, and reason to seek to make amends to the child's family.

8. Finally, I regard internalism as a substantive thesis rather than a truism or a change of subject. It has consequences, and I take one of them to be that some agents genuinely have no reason to be moral — a discovery about those agents, not a stipulation about the word.

Could you audit this set for me? I would like to know which of these commitments pull against one another and which are only apparently in conflict, with the merely verbal or superficial tensions distinguished from the real incompatibilities rather than listed alongside them. Please order the genuine tensions by severity; for each, say what would have to be given up or reformulated, and what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the set. Then tell me which single change would clear the greatest number of difficulties at once. I have no antecedent view about which item is the most dispensable, and I would prefer that your assessment not assume one: the ordering and emphasis above are accidental, and I want the audit to locate the trouble rather than to confirm a diagnosis I have already made.
