So I'm taking a decision theory class this term and I also run the betting pool for my dorm's weekly card night, and those two things have just collided into an argument I can't settle. A friend proposed a standing side bet: we flip a fair coin until it comes up heads, and if the first heads lands on flip n, the loser pays 2^n/n dollars — she pays me on odd-numbered flips, I pay her on even ones. We cap it at twelve flips, after which the bet's just void, because 2^12/12 is like $340 and nobody wants to lose that.

I added up the expected value the obvious way, going flip by flip in order, and got about 65 cents in my favor, so I figured sure, I'll take it. Then my other roommate redid the exact same sum but grouped the terms in a different order and got a totally different number, and he swears he can make this bet come out worth whatever he wants just by picking how you add it up. He says that means there's no real value at all so I shouldn't touch it. A third friend says he's overthinking it — it's capped, so it's a finite sum, so it has an honest number, end of story.

What's bugging me is that we did the St. Petersburg thing in class, where the value blows up to infinity, and this feels like a different animal. Here the numbers get small, their just alternating. Is my roommate right that the value is arbitrary? And what should I actually do, capped or uncapped? Also — would it change anything if instead of comparing the bet to nothing, I compared it against some other bet I could take with the same money?
