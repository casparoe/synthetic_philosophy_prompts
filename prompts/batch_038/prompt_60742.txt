Three hours at a whiteboard last night left me unable to say what is wrong with a friend's argument, though I am confident something is. The setting matters. I am drafting comments on a proposal to restate a body of financial-regulation rulemaking as a machine-readable code — rules encoded so that compliance determinations follow by decision procedure — and the sponsors attach to it what the safety-engineering world calls an assurance case: a structured argument, backed by evidence, that the artefact will behave adequately in operation. My friend, who works on formal verification, thinks Gödel's second incompleteness theorem shows the entire enterprise is misconceived, and he generalises the point in a way that I believe proves far too much. Here is his argument as he stated it.

1. Any rule system that is recursively specified, consistent, and strong enough to encode statements about its own expressions and derivations falls under the second incompleteness theorem: it cannot prove the arithmetical sentence asserting its own consistency.
2. A machine-readable regulatory code with a decision procedure is such a system, and so is the verification regime that certifies it — each is recursively specified, and each is easily rich enough to do arithmetic over its own records, versions, obligations, and derivations.
3. Therefore neither can establish from within that it is free of contradiction, and any demonstration of adequacy must be mounted from a standpoint outside the system being certified.
4. But the certifying standpoint is itself a recursively specified rule system — an audit framework, a meta-specification, a higher-level verifier — to which (1) applies in turn.
5. Therefore the demand for assurance generates a regress with no terminating level: every certifier requires a certifier, and none can be finally certified.
6. Hence no non-question-begging assurance case is possible for any such artefact. The assurance case is rhetoric: it presents as demonstration what is in truth an unargued assumption of trustworthiness somewhere in the stack.
7. The rational response is accordingly to stop seeking assurance and to design instead for its absence — reversibility, redundancy, containment, monitoring, and the ability to withdraw the system from service.

I raised what seemed to me the obvious objection: that in mathematics the regress looks benign, because stronger systems routinely certify weaker ones — ZFC proves the consistency of Peano arithmetic, and Gentzen established that same consistency by means of transfinite induction up to ε₀, resources unavailable within arithmetic itself. His reply, which I could not answer, was that the certifying resources are no better founded than what they certify. Gentzen's induction principle is not itself demonstrated; we accept it because it seems evident, and then congratulate ourselves on having proved something. So the hierarchy of consistency proofs does not defeat (5) — it illustrates it, in the one domain where everyone agrees the practice works.

He also pressed a self-application point that I would like addressed rather than waved away. If (1) through (6) hold, his own argument is a recursively specified inferential system making claims about the adequacy of inferential systems, and by its own lights it cannot be certified either; if the right response to that is a shrug, then (6) has licensed precisely the move it condemns.

For my part, I would happily keep (7), and I suspect I would keep a deflated (6) — assurance as defeasible, evidential, and comparative rather than demonstrative. But I already believe both for reasons that have nothing to do with diagonalisation, which is why I suspect this argument is a bad route to a destination I like. What I want from you is a diagnosis, not a survey.

(a) Identify the step at which the argument first goes wrong — or, if you judge it valid, the premise that is least costly to surrender. Say which, and say why that step looked perfectly innocent: what makes it feel as though the technical content of the theorem transfers intact to the engineering setting.
(b) Be explicit about what the second incompleteness theorem does and does not license here — the conditions under which it bites, what exactly the unprovability claim is a claim about, and whether the property it concerns is the property an assurance case is concerned with.
(c) Price the alternatives. For each of the two or three steps a defender might abandon instead, state what the argument thereby loses and whether the residue remains interesting.
(d) Say whether my friend's reply about Gentzen succeeds, and what distinguishes a vicious regress of certification from a benign one, if the difference is not the presence of an unproved top level.
(e) Say whether (6) and (7) survive on grounds that owe nothing to incompleteness, and if they do, whether invoking the theorem in this debate is doing any work at all or is merely decorative.

Open with a single sentence stating your diagnosis, then give the analysis. Close with a line in exactly this form:

Verdict: Step N — the flaw, in eight words or fewer

where N is the number of the step you reject, or, if you take the argument to be valid, the number of the premise you would give up.
