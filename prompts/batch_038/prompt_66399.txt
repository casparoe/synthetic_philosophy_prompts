Our giving group ran an importance/tractability/neglectedness exercise on Saturday and one member walked out convinced the whole framework refutes itself. His argument, roughly cleaned up:

(1) Neglect can only matter instrumentally: the sole reason it's good to be one of few people working on X is that your marginal contribution to X is larger than it would be in a crowded field.
(2) But "how much progress an extra unit of effort produces" is precisely what tractability is supposed to measure.
(3) So neglectedness isn't a third factor sitting alongside importance and tractability — it's a component of tractability, and any ranking that treats the three as independent inputs double counts.
(4) Worse: if tractability is read marginally, as in (2), then the better you estimate it the more of the neglectedness information you've already absorbed, so the neglectedness term adds nothing but noise.
(5) And the noise points the wrong way. The causes scoring highest on neglect are disproportionately ones that well-informed people examined and passed on, so a high neglect score is partly evidence against a cause being worth doing.
(6) Hence a group that gets more rigorous about measuring neglectedness gets worse recommendations: it becomes increasingly confident in causes that are merely unexamined rather than undervalued.
(7) Therefore ITN isn't just a crude heuristic, it's self-undermining.

I suspect the break is somewhere in the middle steps, but I couldn't defend that if pushed, and if I had to choose I'd rather keep (1) than (7). So: which step would you reject, why did it look unimpeachable on a first read, and what does the person who keeps it have to pay — including whether there's any reason to want to be one of few people on a cause that isn't just a claim about marginal returns.
