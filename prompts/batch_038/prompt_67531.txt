An appellate panel of three judges must decide whether a defendant is liable, where liability obtains just in case both that the defendant breached a duty of care (P1) and that the breach caused the plaintiff's loss (P2). Judge A affirms both; Judge B affirms P1 but denies P2; Judge C denies P1 but affirms P2. Premise-by-premise voting therefore returns a finding of liability, while a direct vote on the outcome returns none.

Write a dialogue between Judge A, who contends the panel should vote premise by premise, and Judge B, who contends it should vote the conclusion. Let B open by naming the awkward fact that A's preferred rule is the one that vindicates A's own judgment in this very case, and require A to answer at the level of the rule rather than the case — across a docket on which each judge's first-order view will sometimes be on the losing side. Neither may lean on the bare observation that one procedure delivers a verdict no member of the panel holds, or that the other discards the reasoning behind the votes; both already know this, and each should say why it is not decisive. Have them work through what the panel's opinion can coherently report as decided, given that a majority affirms each premise while no majority affirms their conjunction; whether a judge may vote a premise against her own belief in order to block a worse outcome, and whether the two rules differ in how much room that leaves; and what happens a decade on, when premise-level holdings laid down by differently composed panels constrain one another. Both judges should be equally formidable, each answering the other's actual point rather than a convenient version of it, and the dialogue should close without either simply capitulating.
