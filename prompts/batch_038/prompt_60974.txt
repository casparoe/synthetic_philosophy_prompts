I chair a standing faculty committee at a large public university, charged with reviewing administrative proposals that would apply behavioral methods to student affairs. The proposal now before us comes from the Office of Student Success. A vendor model, trained on our own retention records, ranks incoming first-year students by their estimated likelihood of not returning for a second year. At present, students in the top quintile of that ranking receive an email inviting them to a one-credit transition seminar; roughly one in five enrolls. The office proposes instead to place those students into the seminar by default before they build their timetables, with removal available online at any time during the first two weeks. It projects participation will rise to something like three-quarters, and cites a pilot at a comparable institution in which both seminar completion and second-year return improved.

That pilot also produced a finding which has split my committee. When the reason for the placement was disclosed to students at the moment of enrollment — that is, when they were told a model had identified them as likely to struggle — the removal rate roughly doubled and most of the gain disappeared. In the undisclosed condition the gain held. For several years I have worked with a principle that I wrote into this committee's own charge and that I will call the announcability condition: an intervention of this kind is permissible only if it could be announced, in plain terms, to the people subjected to it, without the announcement either defeating the intervention or rendering it indefensible to them. The vendor's data appear to describe precisely the case the condition was drafted to exclude. The office's counter-proposal is that disclosure occur at the end of term, in aggregate, rather than at placement.

Please draft the memorandum I will circulate to the committee and, should we approve anything, forward to the Provost. It must reach a bottom line — approve, reject, or approve under specified conditions — together with a concrete design and a timeline for the coming academic year. Beyond that, I want it to do three things.

First, determine whether this arrangement is properly called a nudge at all. Choice is formally preserved, but the default is set by an institution that controls the student's record, her financial aid standing, and her schedule, and the opt-out requires a student to notice something, understand it, and act against a timetable already built around it. Say whether the classification carries normative weight here or is merely taxonomic, and do not treat that question as rhetorical.

Second, take up the announcability condition directly rather than around. Is it a condition on legitimacy, on effectiveness, or on both, and are those separable in a case like this one? Does end-of-term aggregate disclosure satisfy it? And what should the committee do if the honest answer is that the condition, as I drafted it, forbids the version of the program that works and permits only the version that does not? If you conclude the condition should be amended or abandoned, say so and say what would replace it.

Third, give the strongest case against approval its full weight, including the fact that the flagged quintile is disproportionately first-generation and Pell-eligible, so that the students most likely to be sorted into a visibly remedial section are those least able to absorb being marked. I want the memo to be explicit about whether that is a distributive harm or an expressive one, since the answer changes which conditions could actually repair it.

The audience is twelve colleagues, three of whom are philosophers, the rest of whom work in registrar, student affairs, and institutional research and will not tolerate vagueness about what would be implemented on Monday. Write for them. I would rather receive a conclusion I disagree with and can argue against than a balanced survey of considerations I already know.
