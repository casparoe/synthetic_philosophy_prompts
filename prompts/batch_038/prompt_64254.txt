I run a volunteer depot that redistributes surplus food and household goods across a mid-sized European city — about forty of us, four years in, two vans and a rented unit. For most of that time our allocation has been a clipboard list, a messaging group, and two people's judgment about who needs what this week, and it has worked far better than anyone expected. Last autumn the membership voted to pilot a proper allocation system instead: a request form, a points formula weighting household size and urgency, and a dashboard that would produce each week's distribution plan. I was asked to lead the technical review, and reviewing it has put me in an awkward position, because the thing that keeps going wrong with the pilot is not a bug I can point to and fix. Separately, and not entirely coincidentally, I have facilitated a monthly reading group for three years; we spent two months on the socialist calculation debate — Mises's original article, Hayek's two essays on knowledge and on competition as a procedure, and Lange's reply — and finished that block six weeks ago. Several of the members who argued hardest for the platform are in that group. Since finishing the readings, the argument has gone in circles: our two trained economists insist that Lange answered Mises at the level of formal principle and that everything else is implementation detail, and the rest of us insist that four years of standing in the depot on a Thursday evening says otherwise. Neither side is budging, and I have come to think both are right about different things, which is precisely the thing I want them to feel rather than be told.

I want to reconvene the group for one session of two and a half hours — eighteen people, a back room with movable tables, a whiteboard, a flip-chart, marker pens, plenty of paper and scissors, and about forty euros I could convert into small tokens or chocolate coins if a scoring currency helps — and I would like you to design the activity we run. What I want them to come away seeing is the gap between what can be true of a planning procedure in principle and what it is like to operate one: that the planning side can be given exactly the theoretical apparatus Lange described and still fail, and that its failure will not be located anywhere the apparatus can see it. They can recite the debate competently; they have not inhabited it. Two design constraints matter more than the rest. First, the participants have to run into the problem themselves before anyone names it: no introductory lecture, no framing statement that gives the game away, and no use of my own vocabulary of tacit knowledge or of competition as a discovery procedure until we are well into the debrief. The rules, the roles, and the scoring have to be arranged so that the planning side looks as though it is about to succeed — ideally it genuinely does succeed in an early round, which is Lange's point — so that what happens later reads to them as a discovery about the system rather than a trick I have set up. Second, and just as important, the exercise cannot be a Hayekian sermon with the market assigned to win. There must be a real round in which the planning side visibly outperforms the market, whether on equity, on some case where unaided exchange produces an outcome everyone in the room finds perverse, or on a good whose value no participant can state privately in advance. If the game is loaded, the two members who most need this session will smell it in five minutes and we will have learned nothing.

Please give me the full apparatus: roles and how to divide eighteen people between them, the round structure, exactly what each participant is handed in each round and in what form (the format of the information matters as much as its content, so if you are relying on cards or slips I want the wording on them), a scoring scheme that needs no real money but produces genuine stakes, a facilitator's script for the minimal amount of instruction, and a minute-by-minute timeline for the hundred and fifty minutes. Then tell me how the debrief maps particular moments we can expect in the room onto particular claims in the debate — ideally specifying which of our two economists' positions each moment vindicates and which it undercuts. Tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run this and how to handle it when it does, including the failure mode in which a competent, cooperative group simply solves the game and everyone goes home agreeing with the economists. And be frank about the one feature of the debate the exercise cannot reproduce, the thing I will have to supply verbally at the end because the simulation is structurally incapable of delivering it.
