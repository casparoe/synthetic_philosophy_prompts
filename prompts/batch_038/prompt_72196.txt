I'm a third-year PhD student in historical demography — my thesis is basically 18th- and 19th-century parish registers plus a set of colonial poll-tax rolls — so I live inside the missing-data literature and it's pretty much my native language at this point: Rubin's taxonomy, MCAR/MAR/MNAR, ignorability, selection models, inverse probability weighting, bounds, multiple imputation, sensitivity analysis, the whole total-survey-error decomposition.

My department's history-and-theory reading group has spent the last year pushing the archival silences stuff at me — Trouillot mainly, and then the critical fabulation end of it, Hartman and people working in that vein — and I want to know what any of it amounts to in the vocabulary I actually think in. So: restate the debate as faithfully as you can into missing-data terms, taking these claims one at a time.

One. Trouillot's staged account, where silence gets built in at several seperate moments — in the making of the sources, in the assembling of them into an archive, in the pulling of narratives out of them, and in the retrospective significance the whole thing is later given. Is that just the total survey error decomposition with better prose? Coverage and frame error, nonresponse, processing error, interpretation error?

Two. The stronger claim, the one I cant translate: that the archive doesn't merely fail to record certain people, it constitutes the categories in which anyone could have been recorded at all, so the missing aren't a well-defined set of units with unknown values. My instinct is coverage error — the frame doesn't cover the population — but they'd say that already grants there's a population sitting there independently of the frame, and that's the thing under dispute.

Three. The refusal to impute. Hartman-style critical fabulation writes alongside the gap and flags the invention rather than filling it in. Is that anything other than a badly behaved multiple imputation, or a bounding exercise with extra steps? Or is refusal a category error in my framework, which always either imputes, bounds, or says insufficient data?

Four. The claim that describing the record-generating process as a neutral 'missingness mechanism' is itself part of the exercise of power. Don't just point me at Desrosières and the history-of-statistics literature, I've read some of that. I want to know whether the formal vocabulary can carry it.

Then the residue report, which is the bit I actually care about: which of these four translate cleanly, which only translate if you bend them out of shape — and if so say exactly where you bent them — and which have no expression at all in the missing-data framework. Be honest about the direction of the cheat, too. The obvious way to do this whole exercise is to quietly assume there's a complete dataset with holes in it, which is precisely what claim two denies, and then make the archivists look confused by comparison. If you catch yourself doing that, stop and say so rather than papering over it.

Last thing: is the residue a defect of my vocabulary — i.e. is statistics genuinely missing a way to represent a unit that was never constituted in the first place — or is it evidence that the strong version of the silence argument is doing rhetorical work rather than saying anything? I've been arguing for the second in the reading group for months and I'm starting to worry I'm wrong. I want your actual view with reasons, not a survey of the two camps.
