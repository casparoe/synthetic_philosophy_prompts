I sit on the standards comittee of a 40-unit housing co-op — we're elected by the members and we rule on complaints about how people use their units and the common spaces. Our bylaws are hopelessly vague: they say a member can't use their unit in a way "inconsistent with the standards of the community" or in a way that "unreasonably interferes with other members' enjoyment," and we've basically been making it up as we go along. Our record over the last few years:

- Member with a backyard chicken coop that stank and drew rats: ordered to shut it down.
- Member who put a big sign in their front window backing a party most of us find genuinely hateful, after 20-something furious letters: allowed to keep it up. Minutes record the committee as unanimous that being upset is not itself a reason.
- Member who swam nude in the rooftop pool around 6am, when hardly anyone is up: told to cover up — but the minutes frame it as the people coming up for coffee being unable to avoid it, not as nudity being wrong in itself.
- Member who rented out their deeded parking spot to an outsider at market rate: formally censured, even though nobody could name an actual injury and three members said it looked harmless. The minutes say it "corrodes the idea that we own this place together."
- Member who ran a long loud Bible study that hogged the common room: told to move it into their own unit and cap it at ninety minutes, framed entirely as a room-scheduling problem.
- Member who used the shared workshop tools to build furniture for sale outside: not stopped, just charged a fee. Concern recorded as wear on the tools and fairness to everyone else.
- Member who posted a long argument on the internal forum that the co-op should stop admitting families with children: post stayed up, no action taken, tons of angry replies.
- Elderly member whose unit had become a fire hazard from hoarding: we intervened over her objections.

Now the case that's splitting us, and I want an honest read rather than a diplomatic one. Two members have started hosting a swingers party in their own unit twice a month. Soundproofed door, no noise complaints in eight months, guests park two blocks away and come in singly, nothing visible from any common area. A petition signed by 24 of the 40 households asks us to order them to stop. Two households claim two prospective buyers pulled out after hearing about it, but that's secondhand and nobody's verified it. Everyone who signed admits they have never seen or heard anything from these parties and that the objection is entirely to knowing it goes on in the building. The couple say they'll stop if we tell them to, but would rather not, and they point out — correctly — that they've broken no specific rule.

So: how should we rule if we want to be consistent with our own record, and how do you predict we'll actually vote? Those aren't the same question and I want both answered. Which precedent is the closest analog, and which one do people on the committee keep reaching for that doesn't actually fit? Lay out the candidate rules our record is jointly consistent with — there's definitely more than one, and I want to know exactly which of them come apart on this case and what each says about it. One of them will be something like "harm, broadly understood, including damage to the shared understandings that make living here possible," because that's how we justified the parking censure. Tell me whether that rule is actually any different from "whatever a majority of us finds repugnant" — how do the two come apart in practice on these facts, or do they not? And if our record genuinely underdetermines this one, name a further complaint we'd plausibly receive in the next year that would settle which rule we've committed ourselves to.
