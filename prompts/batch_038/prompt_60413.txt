I'm a PhD student in my second year and Monday I have to run a 90-minute methods seminar for second-year archaeology undergrads on how much you can actually infer about what people believed from the stuff they left behind. They've had no philosophy, they've just spent a term on excavation recording, and they all did an essay on Stonehenge last year, so they think they already know what the problem is. I've got a whiteboard and a tray of casts they can pass around.

The case I want to carry the whole session is invented — I built it out of bits of three different sites and I want it to stay invented, so nobody walks in having read about it. A pit cut into the floor of a house, filled in one go and sealed under a fresh clay floor that was itself relaid within a season: about three hundred sherds, all from vessels of a single shape, nothing refitting into a usable pot, every break fresh and clean; the burnt bones of one juvenile goat; a handful of charred grain. No other house on the site has one. For contrast I'll set the village midden beside it — same period, mixed species, rolled worn edges, sherds from eleven different forms, some mended and reused.

I don't want the explanation from you, I want the plan. What order do the pieces have to go in, and what makes each one depend on the one before it? What does that pit-case need to have in it to do the job, and what must it not have? My supervisor has told me the word "ritual" isn't allowed out of my mouth for the first forty minutes — work with that constraint rather than around it, because I think it's actually load-bearing and I can't say why. Then the misconceptions: I'm braced for "so it was religious then" and for "if we can't know, it's all just made-up stories," but tell me the ones I haven't thought of and the exact point in the sequence where each should get killed. And a closing question that separates the students who got it from the ones who've just learned to say "we can't know" — I'd like them to have to commit to something, not abstain. Finally, what should I openly refuse to cover; I'm guessing Bayesian machinery and the whole procesual/post-processual argument, but tell me what to cut and how to say I'm cutting it without sounding like I'm dodging.

For reference my draft order is: open with what underdetermination means in general, then show the pit, then list the readings it could bear, then the midden, then land on "we can know a lot, just not what it meant." I suspect this is backwards and that going abstract first will lose them, but I can't articulate why.

You've taught this to non-philosophers often enough to know where it fails every year, so write from that. Think hard about what genuinely depends on what before you commit — sketch two or three candidate sequences first, say which one you'd actually run and why. No headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables: continuous prose, like an email back to me, as long as it needs to be.
