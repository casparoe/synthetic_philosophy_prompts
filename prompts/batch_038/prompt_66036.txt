Okay so before you write anything I want you to actually sit with this, don't just start producing text.

I'm 44, I write payroll software for a living, no history degree, never been within a mile of one. For six years I've been in a reading group at our town library, eleven of us, average age somewhere around 68. Every September the group picks a theme for the year and Dale, who founded it in 1991 and runs it like a benevolent dictator, announces it at the kickoff social with index cards and everything. This year he's announced From Darkness Into Light: How the Middle Ages Ended and the Modern Mind Was Born. Ten books, Rome falls, monks copy things, plague, Petrarch gets excited, Copernicus and Galileo, Newton, and then us. Nice clean line.

Here's my problem. Somewhere in the last two years I went down a rabbit hole entirely on my own and now I can't un-see it: those three boxes feel to me like they were built backwards, from where we're standing. I read that the actual Latin phrase for the middle ages first shows up in 1469, which would mean that for close to a thousand years nobody alive thought they were in the middle of anything, they thought they were in the present. And Renaissance sounds to me like a word chosen by people who needed the thing before it to be bad. And the Scientific Revolution one is the shakiest of all for me, because I can't find a single year where anybody at the time noticed the furniture of what counts as knowing had been swapped out. My own half-baked version of the worry, which I'd like you to take seriously and sharpen rather than just tolerate, is that period names work like the names a weather service gives a storm. Nobody standing in the flood is calling it Hurricane anything. The name gets assigned later, from a desk, so the paperwork is manageable, and then quietly the name decides which claims get paid, because two towns get hit and only the one inside the named storm gets help. Same with periods, I think: put the line at 1453 or 1500 or 1543 and whole populations land on the wrong side of a name somebody picked for their own filing system.

So. Dale has asked me, me, to give the opening remarks Thursday, because apparently I'm the thoughtful one. Seven minutes, call it eight hundred words. The room will be the eleven plus spouses, plus a woman from the town paper, plus two high school history teachers who mostly come for the wine. Dale is 79 and this theme is his baby, and he chose a year about things getting better because his wife died in the spring. Nobody in that room is a professional historian and I'd bet two of them would be offended on Dale's behalf if I got cute.

I need you to write the remarks. The constraints are the hard part. It has to actually work as opening remarks, welcome people, thank the library, get them to sign up, name the first book, it cannot read like a seminar paper. It cannot endorse the darkness-to-light arc as literally true, I'm not standing up and saying a thousand years were a dark intermission while everybody eats cookies. But it also cannot do the thing where I wink at the room and announce that of course we all know periodization is a construct, because that humiliates Dale in front of his own group and it's also false in the other direction, the periods do real work, they're how anybody finds anything at all. The two teachers will hear exactly what's going on and the 79-year-olds will not, and it has to land for both at once; specifically I want the storm-name idea in there in a form where a layperson hears a nice piece of imagery and a teacher hears an argument. And it has to end so that Dale feels honored and the year feels like it's going somewhere, even though going somewhere is one of the things under question. Land that honestly, don't cheat me with a vague uplift.

Take the role of a working speechwriter who has also spent twenty years in historiography, someone who knows what the live arguments about the Middle Ages and Renaissance and Scientific Revolution labels actually are right now, including the fights about whether there was a twelfth-century renaissance too and about who the periodization flatters, and who can still write a sentence a tired person can follow after two glasses of wine. Before the speech give me a short paragraph or two of your reasoning: what you think the single load-bearing move is, what you cut, and where you think my rabbit hole has gone wrong. Be blunt in that part, I'd rather hear it from you than in front of eleven people.

Format, and please respect this: no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no italics, no tables, no numbered lists, no bracketed stage directions, anywhere in your reply. Continuous prose only, the way you'd write an email or a letter to a friend, including the reasoning part and including the speech itself. Write the speech the way it should come out of my mouth, and if you want a pause or a laugh, build it into the rhythm of the sentences rather than telling me about it.
