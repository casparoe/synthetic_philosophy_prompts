I teach an upper-level epistemology seminar — fourteen students, one eighty-minute block — that has just finished a unit on the classical Indian theories of knowledge, and I want to build a session in which the students have to operate inside the pramāṇa framework rather than watch me summarize it from the front. Some context on why I am the wrong person to improvise this on the fly: I came to Nyāya by way of the rationalist blogosphere, where the default epistemology is roughly observe, update, and treat what other people tell you as evidence discounted by your model of the source — that is, a picture on which testimony has already been reduced to inference plus a reliability prior. My students hold something like that picture without knowing they hold it, and I suspect it also shapes how they read the reduction debates between the schools, because on their view the question of how many independent sources there are looks like a merely verbal one. I want them to feel what it costs.

Here is the activity I am after. Teams are assigned a school and a correspondingly fixed budget of admissible pramāṇas: one team restricted to perception alone, one to perception and inference, one that also has comparison available, one that also has the testimony of reliable speakers, and, if it is not too much to stage, one that additionally has postulation and non-apprehension. Points are earned by licensing claim cards using only the means one's school admits, and the design constraint that matters most to me is that the reductionist teams should discover through play — not through my telling them — that certain cards cannot be reached with their admitted means, or can be reached only by smuggling in something they have disallowed. Good candidate cards would include the meaning of a word no one present has ever had ostensively taught, a fact about a town nobody in the room has visited, the reliability of the very informants one would have to induct over, and an absence (that there is no jug on the table, known without surveying anything). Please also include a card built on the familiar case of being told by an informant that some unfamiliar forest animal resembles a cow and then later recognizing one: that card should be contested by the comparison team and the testimony team at the same moment, and the argument over who actually licensed it should be the pivot of the session.

What I need from you, concretely: the full card list, with for each card which admissible means can license it, which cannot, and why; a scoring or token economy that requires no real money; a challenge mechanic by which a team can demand that an opponent show the absence of a defeater for their warrant, because I want the extrinsic-validity view to surface as a felt cost of doing business rather than as a footnote I have to recite; a minute-by-minute timeline for eighty minutes including setup; and a debrief that maps specific in-game moments onto the live questions — how many independent sources there are, which of them collapse into which, and what the collapsing school has to give up. Also tell me plainly what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it. My own guesses are that the perception-only team will either hoard the easy cards or get accused of smuggling inference into every observation report, and that the testimony team will freeze once challenged on circularity, but I expect you can name better failure modes and say how to referee each. Finally, be honest about which feature of the actual debate the game structurally cannot reproduce and that I will have to supply by hand afterward. Please do not attach a bibliography or a list of primary texts to assign — I have my reading list — but do make the internals precise enough that a graduate TA could run the session in my absence without asking me anything.
