I'm in a decision-analysis reading group that meets Thursdays — mostly stats people, plus me and one other philosophy student. There's a guy in it (I'll call him Dev, he did his PhD on elicitation) who calls every toy case before anyone else gets a word in, and in two years I've never actually caught him contradicting himself, which is deeply annoying. We're doing three weeks on the value of information starting next month and I want to be able to push back properly, so I've been loging his calls to figure out what rule he's actually running. Here they are:

- Two fertilizers, one better for your soil, a $20 kit tells you which. Buy the kit. Same setup with the kit at $500 and the difference between the brands worth $400 to you: don't buy.
- A free, accurate prediction of the day you'll die, nothing you can do to change it. He said looking and refusing are both fine, the number doesn't buy you anything. Someone said "but it might let you get the will done," and he said then it buys you something and you should look.
- A free, accurate test for a treatable infection, and the two weeks waiting on the result will be horrible. He said obviously test, and he was impatient about it — feeling awful isn't a reason not to look, it's just something you put up with because there's a decision riding on it.
- Paying to find out whether your kid's college application got in ahead of the official date, when knowing early changes nothing you'll do. News 70% likely to be good: pay. News 30% likely to be good: don't. His explanation was that you're buying the savoring, not the information, which I thought was a weird thing for him to say given the test case above.
- A free, true test result that you already know you'll misread — the group's version was a guy who hears "positive screening" and jumps straight to certainty of disease regardless of base rates. Don't take it. He was emphatic: free and true doesn't matter if you're going to mangle it.
- You've already pre-commited to a surgery, deposit paid, and now a friend offers to show you the surgeon's outcome stats for free. Looking is fine, but it can't change anything, and if you're the kind of person who'll get cold feet, don't look.
- Your landlord will raise the rent if she finds out you're testing the water for lead, whether or not you find anything. Don't test — and if you must, test somewhere she can't see.
- A free test the night before surgery where you'll be sedated when the decision gets made and the result won't be passed to the surgeon. Skip it.

Here's the case we get next week and I want to place a bet on it before I walk in. A woman can get a free genetic test that tells her whether a preventive drug will help her or hurt her — it's a big help for most people and actively bad for the variant carriers, and she has to decide about starting it this month. Because her mother had the complication, she puts her own odds at roughly half, so the bad-news branch is very live. Two wrinkles: the lab reports to her insurer that she was tested (they never see the result, just the fact that she looked), and being on that list raises her premiums by a fixed amount every year regardless of what it says. And she knows herself — if it comes back positive she'll lose a year to it, she says she'd pay a lot to not spend a year that way — but she also says, soberly, right now, that she'd rather know. Nobody doubts she'd make whatever decision the result calls for; that part isn't in question.

So: what's Dev running? I want the two or three candidate rules that fit all eight of his earlier calls, what each one says about this woman, and your best guess about which way he comes out with the reasons for preferring it over the others. Then — the part I actually care about — give me one more toy case in the same style that would split whichever rules survive, so I can spring it on him the week after. If his calls genuinely don't pin the new case down, say that plainly and tell me what principle he'd be adopting under each possible answer, becuase I want to know which version of him I'd be arguing with. And while you're at it: is the fertilizer case just Good's theorem about free information, and are the rest of these the standard ways that theorem is supposed to break? I keep trying to tell him his whole list is one theorem plus footnotes and he says it isn't, and I can't tell whether he's right or whether we're arguing past each other.
