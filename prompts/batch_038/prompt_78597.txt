A friend of mine is deep into collapse theory — Diamond, Tainter, that whole shelf — and at dinner he killed my objection with "civilizations die from suicide, not murder." My objection was the Late Bronze Age: interlocking palace economies coming apart within a couple of generations around 1200 BCE, with drought evidence, raids from the sea, quake damage, trade links snapping. That looks like murder, or at least manslaughter. His comeback was that no outside shock drops a society that wasnt already hollowed out, so it's still suicide.

Can you take that slogan apart and tell me what it could actually be claiming? I want a menu of the distinct readings, from the near-tautological to the strong empirical thesis to whatever responsibility claim is hiding inside the metaphor — and for each, whether it's trivially true, testable (how?), probably false, or just too vague to evaluate. Say which readings are really the same claim in different clothes, and what "suicide" has to presuppose about a society being the kind of thing that can intend or choose at all. Then tell me which reading he needed to block my argument and whether that one holds up. Does any of it depend on what "collapse" is being made to mean — loss of complexity vs. disaster vs. reorganization? The Rapa Nui and Maya cases seem to turn on that, since in one the story is now rats and resilience and in the other the south empties out while the north keeps building.
