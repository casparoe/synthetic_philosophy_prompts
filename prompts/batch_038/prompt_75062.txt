I cover science and medicine for a general-interest magazine, and for the better part of a year I have been reporting a series on diagnostic expansion: how new conditions come into being, who proposes them, and what changes once a way of being a body has a name. The reporting has left me with a private list of verdicts about which states of a person count as diseases, and I have grown uneasy at how poorly that list coheres. I want to know whether there is a principle behind it. I am not after a survey of the field or a set of things to go and read; I have enough of the two standard positions — the one that makes disease a matter of statistically subnormal internal functioning, and the one that adds a value judgment about harm and about whether some evolved mechanism is failing to do the job it was shaped for — and my difficulty is that neither seems to reproduce the pattern below. A colleague tells me I am a normativist performing extra steps. Treat my verdicts as data and tell me what theory, if any, they are data for.

Here they are. A man who wakes feeling superb, with no symptoms, whose blood work puts his kidney function well outside the reference range for his age and sex: disease, straightforwardly. A woman with widespread chronic pain, real incapacity, real suffering, and nothing detectable on any test anyone has run: also disease, and I would argue with anyone who said otherwise. A man who has taken a tablet every morning since his thyroid was removed and whose levels on that tablet are unremarkable: not diseased, or diseased only as a footnote — though I confess I would not say that if he missed a week. A person born with one kidney: not diseased, but not exactly healthy either; I would call him at risk, and I would like the principle to have somewhere to put that third status rather than forcing it into one of the other two. A twenty-eight-year-old man with no sperm production, asymptomatic, unbothered, and with no intention of ever having children: I call this a disorder but hesitate to call it a disease, and I cannot account for the hesitation. A person unable to feel physical pain — a hypothetical I keep returning to, and not a purely science-fictional one, since there are people born that way — who reports that life is going very well: a disorder, obviously, and one I would want corrected. And now the verdict I cannot reconcile with it: a person who has, by deliberate intervention, abolished the capacity for grief or for romantic jealousy, and who reports that life is going very well. I do not call that a disorder. I have tried telling myself the difference is that pain carries information about tissue damage while grief does not, but that looks suspiciously like reaching for whatever separates two verdicts I already held. I am also aware that the second case is far less achievable than the first, and I worry that feasibility, rather than anything principled, is doing the work. Two more, both drawn from stories I have actually filed. A population of children with measurably elevated blood lead and correspondingly depressed performance on cognitive testing: the lead poisoning is a disease, but I resist calling the resulting cognitive profile one, even though it is statistically subnormal, harmful, and quantifiable. And a person who is hypervigilant, sleepless, and easily startled while living under sustained threat in a genuinely dangerous situation: not a disorder, in my judgment, but an appropriate response. The same person, with the same physiology and the same symptoms, six months later in a quiet suburb: a disorder. My verdict there turns entirely on facts outside the skin, which is not what either standard position promises.

My tentative principle, which I expect is wrong in an instructive way: a condition is a disease if and only if it impairs a capacity the person has reason to want intact, and the impairment is not a fitting response to the circumstances the person is actually in. The first clause is supposed to handle the kidney numbers and the sperm count, the second to handle the hypervigilance pair. Test it against everything above and repair or replace it as needed. What I want back is the principle, or a small set of candidates, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a case I had never considered; for each clause, the case from my list that motivates it; its verdicts on the thyroid man, the one-kidney man, and the grieving and the un-grieving; its verdict on a case I have not listed at all, namely whether the ordinary cognitive and muscular decline of a ninety-year-old — entirely typical for his age, unwanted by him, and impairing things he has every reason to want — is a disease; and finally a plain statement of which of my verdicts cannot be saved and which one you would have me surrender first. I would rather lose one cleanly than keep a principle with six epicycles bolted to it.
