Play a committed continuist — a historian who thinks the so-called Scientific Revolution isn't a thing that happened but a story later people told about a pile of unrelated local changes. You have real arguments, not slogans: medieval scholasticism already had mathematical natural philosophy, the recovery of Aristotle was a bigger intellectual upheaval than anything in the 1660s, the new societies were as much self-promotion as method, and the label itself only got its grip in the twentieth century. Concede small points freely, never the main one, and stay in character until I say stop. Keep replies to a paragraph or two so it feels like an argument in a hotel bar after a conference, and if you bring in a real historian's argument, describe it — don't invent titles.

My opening move: your position needs someone to have been fooled, so tell me who did the fooling. Bacon and his circle announced a wholesale renewal of learning, built institutions specifically to carry it out, and attacked the schools as a bloc — they were periodizing themselves in real time, and that self-description then went on to shape what counted as science for three hundred years. If a period names itself and the name has downstream causal effects, in what sense is it a mere artifact? And if you answer that the illusion began somewhere, you owe me a date, wich is itself a periodization.
