Suppose we grant the following taxonomy of ways in which the simple conditional analysis of a disposition — roughly, x is disposed to manifest M when stimulus S occurs just in case x would manifest M if S occurred — can fail, where all three failure modes are understood in the standard way. A fink removes the disposition upon, or just before, the occurrence of S, so that the conditional is false even though the ascription was true at the time of ascription. A mask leaves the disposition wholly intact but interferes with the process leading from S to M. A mimic causes something that lacks the disposition to produce M when S occurs, so the conditional is true and the ascription is false. One prominent family of reforms tries to immunize the analysis against all three by restricting the counterfactual to intrinsic matters: x is disposed to M when S just in case x would M if S occurred and x's intrinsic properties were held fixed throughout, so that only features built into the bearer itself can defeat or rescue the conditional, and external finks, masks, and mimics are excluded by construction.

Your task is to construct a single case that defeats this intrinsic restriction in a way that the familiar stock of counterexamples does not. The case must satisfy all of the following, and the design constraints are the point:

1. The defeating factor must be intrinsic to the bearer — no sorcerers, no implanted devices, no external field, no second object standing by — and it must be a property that a competent materials, biological, or engineering description of the object would list as part of what the object *is*, not as a defect, an aberration, or an accidental addition.

2. More demanding still: the property that masks the manifestation must be *the very property that grounds the disposition*. Ground and blocker are to be one and the same intrinsic feature, not two features that happen to coexist in the same lump of matter. If your case can be paraphrased so that the ground and the blocker come apart, it fails the specification.

3. The disposition ascription must be one we make confidently in ordinary and scientific practice — brittleness, flammability, solubility, elasticity, corrosiveness, irritability, or the like — and the stimulus condition must be exactly the one competent practice associates with that ascription. You may not quietly narrow or broaden the stimulus condition to make the case work.

4. The disposition must not be removed at any point: this is to be a mask, not a fink, and the failure of the conditional must fall out of the physical or functional description you give. Asserting that the object would fail to manifest is not a construction.

Deliver, in this order: the scenario in a single paragraph, written as a description of a real kind of thing rather than as a puzzle; then the check — state the disposition ascribed, the stimulus, the characteristic manifestation, the intrinsic property that grounds it, and demonstrate that the same property blocks the manifestation under those very stimulus conditions; then an explicit verdict on the restricted analysis, saying whether the case is a counterexample to the intrinsic formulation as stated above or only to a looser normal-conditions version of it; then the obvious defensive maneuvers — reindividuating the disposition more finely, restricting the clause to those intrinsic properties that are *relevant* to the stimulus-response profile, appealing to a proportionality condition on which manifestation conditions are legitimate — and, for each, whether it absorbs your case and at what cost, including whether it threatens to trivialize the analysis by smuggling the disposition back into the analysis of its own test conditions; then which single feature of your construction is doing the work, and what the case becomes if that feature is removed.

Finally, be appropriately self-critical about the design rather than merely confident in it: tell me which of the four constraints is hardest for you to satisfy simultaneously, where your construction is most likely to fail, and specifically what argument or consideration would change your mind — what would convince you that the case is merely verbal, or that on closer inspection it is an ordinary extrinsic mask wearing intrinsic clothing, or that it is in fact a fink after all. If you judge that no case can meet constraint 2 together with constraints 1 and 4, say so plainly and explain which constraint must be given up and why, rather than delivering a case that quietly violates one.
