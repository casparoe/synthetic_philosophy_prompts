I'm a third-year history student in a methods seminar on causation and I've hit a wall on my paper about the July Crisis, mostly because I don't have a word for something I keep running into. Week two, a classmate argued the alliance system made a general war basically unavoidable once the crisis started. Someone shot back that the same alliances had produced no war in the Moroccan crises or the Balkan wars, so that can't be it. And he just... agreed, and said fine, what really did it was the mobilization timetables, they left no room for decision once Austria declared. Then a third person said no, timetables are an instrument of policy, the real cause was German war aims, and the timetable guy rolled with that too. Nobody lost anything. By the end of the hour all three claims were still on the table and the multi-causal picture of the war's origins was exactly as strong as it had started, even though each individual claim had been knocked down in turn. Same shape in something I read for a different course, a post-mortem of a bank collapse: any one of three problems — liquidity, fraud, permissive regulation — would have sunk it on its own, and I remember thinking that sentence makes the explanation impossible to get wrong, because whichever problem you disqualify, the other two are still sitting there doing the job.

So does this have a name? I know about overdetermination, but that seems to be about the world actually having spare causes, not about an account becoming bulletproof — and the thing I'm describing happens even when nobody has established that there were spare causes, it's more of a move than a finding. The readings mention INUS conditions, and I think someone named Ragin talks about multiple conjunctural causation, and I'm fairly sure Althusser used "overdetermination" for something completely different and Marxist, plus there's equifinality from systems theory. Isn't it also just falsificationism in causal drag — an immunizing maneuver, where you rescue the claim by pointing at a second cause instead of bolting on an auxiliary assumption? I need to know which of these actually fits and how they differ, because they can't all be the same idea. But a label won't be enough, I need the carving. There's an obviously legitimate version — redundant causes are real, two sufficient causes can genuinely both be present, and invoking the second one isn't cheating. And there's the illegitimate version where "it was overdetemined anyway" is doing nothing but absorbing the counterexample. What's the test? What would have to be true for a redundancy claim to be worth something rather than act as a shield — can it ever be checked, and if so how, given that the whole point is the counterfactual seems to come out the same either way? If there's no established term for the illegitimate version specifically, please just coin one, define it tightly, and give me boundary cases, because I have to tell my supervisor what I mean and "that thing where they keep swapping causes" isn't going to cut it.

Last bit: I also keep needing the mirror image and I have even less language for it. The case where an outcome required ten separate conditions to all line up, so every one of them can claim to be a cause, none can be isolated by any single-factor comparison, and every monocausal account turns out to be technically true and completely useless — I feel like I've seen this used to make an argument unattackable in the opposite direction, since any counterexample just shows the other nine were doing the work. Is that the other pole of the same phenomenon, or a genuinely different thing with its own name? My instinct is that they're not opposites at all, and that the distinction between them is exactly what my paper needs to turn on.
