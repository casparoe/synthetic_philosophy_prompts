I teach a graduate seminar on practical rationality and I want to assign *Groundhog Day* alongside the money-pump literature, on the following reading. The loop is a natural experiment on the money-pump argument for transitivity, because it deletes the feature that does most of the argumentative work: Phil Connors's cycling among ends — hedonism, then despair and repeated suicide, then a long program of self-improvement — never accumulates a net loss, since the world resets and no one can charge him for the round trips. If the force of the pump argument lies wholly in exploitability, the film ought to invite us to call his cycling harmless; instead it presents it as so much time wasted, and makes the way out a settled set of ends pursued day after day. That seems to point toward a non-pragmatic ground for preferring stable, transitive preferences.

Is that too quick? A pump requires intransitive preferences held at a single time, whereas Phil's look more like changing tastes, which would put the film in the resoluteness-versus-sophistication debate rather than the transitivity one. And it is not obvious the loop really deletes cost, given that his skills and his standing with the townspeople persist, and that some outcomes — the old man who dies whatever Phil attempts — are fixed regardless of what he chooses.

Give me a reading that takes both the film's actual sequence of choices and the decision-theoretic debate seriously in both directions: where the pump framing illuminates the film, where it distorts it, and whether the film contributes anything to the debate that argument alone does not. Tell me plainly which parts of my reading are a stretch. And where the literature bears on your answer, keep what is settled among people who work on this clearly separate from what is genuinely contested; do not hand me a contested position as though it were the consensus.
