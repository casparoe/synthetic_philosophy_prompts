I am preparing to lead the final discussion section of an advanced seminar on formal epistemology, and the session is meant to step back from the technical results and ask what kind of normative argument the accuracy-first program actually offers. I am comfortable with the machinery: I can reconstruct the accuracy-dominance argument for probabilism myself, I understand why the coherent credence functions over a finite set of propositions are exactly the convex hull of the truth-value assignments, and I see how the Brier score turns total inaccuracy into squared Euclidean distance, so that an incoherent credence function sits outside a closed convex set and can be projected back onto it at a point closer to every possible world than it was. What I lack is a clean answer to a conceptual question about the structure of the resulting justification, and I would like a careful explanation rather than a survey of positions.

Here is the tension. On one hand, the dominance argument is advertised as an improvement on the Dutch book argument precisely because it is not goal-directed: it never tells the agent to maximize anything, but only observes that, whichever way the world turns out, some coherent alternative to her credences would have been more accurate. That is a sure-thing defect, and it is supposed to be immune to the familiar complaint that maximizing a quantity licenses trade-offs. On the other hand, Selim Berker's critique of accuracy-first epistemology is that it is a species of epistemic consequentialism: it aggregates accuracy across propositions, thereby treating propositions as separate in a way that permits sacrificing accuracy on some for greater accuracy on others, and it can, in cases of misleading evidence, license believing against one's evidence. It is not obvious to me how much of that charge reaches the dominance argument as such, as opposed to the expected-accuracy arguments the same program uses to establish updating norms. The matter is complicated by a technical detail I keep returning to: the standard dominance theorem is proved for inaccuracy measures that are additive, continuous, and strictly proper, but it has since been shown that additivity can be dropped, with the theorem going through on continuity and strict propriety alone. If the summing across propositions was not doing the essential work, then where is the aggregation that the objection targets actually located? My suspicion is that it migrates into strict propriety itself, since that condition is defined in terms of expected inaccuracy, and expectation is already a weighted sum across worlds; but I cannot tell whether that is an insight or a confusion, nor whether cross-world aggregation is the thing that makes a normative argument consequentialist in the relevant sense.

Could you explain the issue as you see it? In particular, I would like: (i) a clear account of the difference between aggregating inaccuracy across propositions and aggregating it across possible worlds, and of which of the two a dominance argument genuinely relies on; (ii) a worked illustration of why the distinction matters, ideally a small case in which the incoherence is not internal to any single credence but lies in the relations among several of them — for instance, credences close to one in two contingent propositions together with a credence in their conjunction low enough to violate the corresponding coherence constraint — since in a one-proposition algebra there is nothing to aggregate across and the objection seems to lose its purchase entirely; (iii) an assessment of whether the sure-thing structure of dominance really insulates the argument from the consequentialism charge, or whether that charge, if it lands at all, lands just as hard here; and (iv) some indication of what follows for the unity of the accuracy-first program if the static coherence argument turns out to be non-teleological while the dynamic arguments are teleological. I am not after references or a reading list, but the conceptual anatomy: which assumptions are substantive and which inessential, where each notion does its work, and whether the dispute with Berker is ultimately a disagreement about the logic of normative argument rather than about credences. If some part of this is genuinely unsettled, or if you think my framing rests on a false dichotomy, say so plainly and explain why.
