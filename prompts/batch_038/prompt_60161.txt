Our meetup is doing a film night on 12 Angry Men (the 1957 Lumet one, not the remakes) and somebody in the group chat has already written up a long post arguing the movie is a clean refutation of the Condorcet jury theorem: eleven jurors look at the same evidence and land on guilty, one guy with better priors talks them out of it, so the crowd is just a mob and wisdom-of-crowds reasoning is dead. I want to push back but I'm not sure I can, so — read the film seriously through the jury theorem and tell me who's actually right.

Things I think that post skips, and please tell me if I'm wrong about them:

- The theorem wants votes independent given the truth. These jurors are about as correlated as votes get: same two witnesses, same slum-kid stereotype, same hot room, same sight of each other's faces. So the 11-1 opening isn't a sample of eleven, it's a sample of maybe two or three with nine echoes attached.
- When Juror 8 calls for a secret ballot instead of a show of hands, that reads to me like a deliberate decorrelation move — the film showing you the independence assumption being manufactured rather than assumed. But I've also seen the opposite argument, that deliberation is exactly what destroys independence, and the movie arguably supports that too, since nearly every flip happens only after someone else talks that juror into it. Which one is the film making?
- The verdict has to be unanimous, which isn't the rule the theorem is about at all. Under plain majority rule they convict in ninety seconds and the theorem says that's the correct answer. Unanimity converts the jury from an aggregator into a debating society on purpose. Is that anti-Condorcet, or is it Condorcet — he was mainly worried about criminal tribunals and asymmetric error costs, and a mandatory death sentence is the case where you'd pay a lot in hung juries to push the false-conviction rate down.
- Biggest one: are they even answering the question the theorem needs? The charge is guilt beyond reasonable doubt, not "is he guilty," and for most of these men thier competence on the second question might be under a half.

What I want is criticism, not a restatement of the theorem. Which scenes do real work for it — the duplicate switchblade, timing the old man's walk to the door, the eyeglass marks on the witness's nose, the baseball fan who flips early and gets called out for not believing his own vote — where does the film genuinely undercut the crowd, and where does the honest critic have to admit it's also a story about one persuasive person moving a group hard in either direction, which is a worse result than the theorem simply failing? Does the movie vindicate a corrected jury theorem or burn down the whole project?

Mark your confidence as you go and flag which plot details you're sure of versus reconstructing. Don't hand me the standard one-rational-man-against-prejudice reading unless you think it survives contact with the math. And if you think the setup itself is confused — that a story about twelve particular men with particular grudges isn't the kind of thing that can confirm or disconfirm a limit theorem about independent voters, and I'm about to commit a category error in front of thirty people — say that plainly rather than being polite about it.
