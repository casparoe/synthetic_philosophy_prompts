In work on scalable oversight I keep running into the "obfuscated arguments" worry: if checking a case is much harder than constructing one, an expert who wants to mislead can always produce an argument the judge cannot evaluate, and debate's hope that truth is easier to defend than to attack collapses. I tried to state the worry precisely, and what came out proves far too much.

(1) A judge may rationally accept a claim on the strength of an argument only if she can reliably tell that argument apart from the best opposing case she could be given.
(2) Getting reliably better at telling arguments apart is itself an evaluative task, and arguments are the only tool for improving at it.
(3) So a judge's reliability at any level rests on her reliability one level up.
(4) Hence the chain either terminates in a capacity no argument conferred, is circular, or runs back infinitely.
(5) A capacity no argument conferred is a mere disposition and cannot supply warrant; circularity supplies none; and an infinite chain is never completed.
(6) Therefore no weaker judge ever rationally accepts anything on the strength of a stronger arguer's reasoning — not in a debate protocol, not in peer review, not in a graduate seminar.
(7) Yet graduate students plainly do learn from their teachers to spot bad arguments, so (6) is false.
(8) So one of (1)–(5) is false.

I would most like to keep (1) and (2), since surrendering either looks like conceding that oversight cannot be scaled by argument at all. Which premise or step should I give up, why did it look unobjectionable when I wrote it, and what does giving it up cost? And does whatever escape you find still cover the case that started this — two experts with opposite incentives, no patient teacher, and a judge who cannot check what she is being handed?
