I'm working on a piece for the ideas desk about the Plato of the Laws — the same guy who wants to censor the poets ends up legislating state-supervised drinking clubs, on the theory that wine is a cheap and safe way to test and train character: get the citizens a little drunk in company under a sober presider, see who holds up, treat the whole business like checking gold against a touchstone. He gates it by age too — nothing at all under eighteen, since the young are already fire and pouring more fire on them does harm, and only in moderation until thirty.

Can you write me a dialogue between two people arguing over whether that scheme works as moral psychology? One side defends it: exposure under guidance is what builds temperance, and someone who's never been tested in the presence of pleasure has a virtue that's merely untried rather than real — the drinking club is the training ground, and abstention leaves people unproven. The other side attacks it: practicing self-control while tipsy produces tolerance, not temperance; the sober presider is doing all the actual work, so the disposition never gets exercised on its own; and the age gate quietly assumes the settled character its supposed to produce. Both of them need to be genuinely sharp — no strawmen — and they should be answering each others actual moves rather than delivering parallel speeches.

I'd especially like to see the point where the defender has to give something up, and the point where the critic overshoots. A couple pages, and skip the footnotes and reading list — I just want the argument. But don't let it land in a bland tie where they agree to disagree.
