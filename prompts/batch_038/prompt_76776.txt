I advise a small philanthropic fund — call it $1,000,000. Its benefactor has a friend who runs a standing weekly bet: she may stake any fraction of the fund, and a coin that comes up heads about 55% of the time (an estimate drawn from several hundred practice flips) either doubles the stake or forfeits it. She expects the offer to continue indefinitely, needs no income from the fund, and cannot raise fresh capital. Two of my graduate students were asked what fraction she should stake; they produced the exchange below and then asked me to referee it. I was out of the room for most of it and want an independent adjudication before I weigh in.

1. **Nadia:** The Kelly fraction is the edge, 0.55 − 0.45 = 10%. Stake more and the fund is ruined with probability one; stake less and it is overtaken with probability approaching one. So 10% is the unique rational stake, not a matter of temperament.

2. **Owen:** Both halves are too strong. Overstaking destroys the fund only above roughly twice the edge — about 20% here — so 15% still grows without bound while carrying higher expected wealth than 10% at every finite date; and "overtaken with probability approaching one" is a claim about a limit no mortal bettor ever reaches.

3. **Nadia:** Expected wealth is the wrong object for a multiplicative process. At 15% the mean is inflated by fantastically lucky paths while the typical path does worse than at 10%, because the time average of one bettor's life is not the ensemble average across imaginary parallel bettors.

4. **Owen:** That is log utility in a new hat. Kelly maximizes E[ln W], which is just expected utility with u = ln, so your non-ergodicity argument is not a rival norm but a contested utility assumption — one that is arbitrarily risk-averse in absolute terms and enjoys no privileged status among the power utilities.

5. **Nadia:** Log does have privileged status. It is the only utility for which the optimal fraction staked is independent of how much wealth one happens to hold, and the only one whose maximization guarantees against being wiped out; those are conditions on a norm for recurring bets, not preferences.

6. **Owen:** Then even your own criterion does not yield 10%. Her 55% is an estimate, and the Bayesian who maximizes expected log wealth under a posterior over the true bias stakes less than the plug-in Kelly fraction — so the rule generating your number is expected-utility maximization with honest beliefs, which is exactly my point in move 4.

7. **Owen** *(pressing a variant)*: Suppose a matching grant requires the fund to reach $2,000,000 within ten offers, after which the offers stop. At 10% she doubles only by winning nine or ten of the ten — a couple of percent — whereas staking everything on the first offer doubles it with probability 55%. Growth-rate maximization is answering a question she is not asking.

8. **Nadia:** Then she is gambling, not investing, and the deadline is a reason to find other money. But grant the variant: no positive probability of total ruin can be rational, because ln 0 = −∞, so any strategy carrying such a probability has infinitely negative expected utility. The all-in bet is the one option ruled out absolutely.

Please adjudicate this exchange move by move rather than in the abstract, and check the mathematical claims rather than taking them as stated. For each move, identify what is correct, what is wrong or overstated, and assign a verdict. Then tell me which student is more right overall, what considerations neither of them raises that should change the advice we give her, and — for the case as originally described, before Owen's variant — what fraction you would recommend and why.

Give the move-by-move adjudication as a Markdown table with exactly these columns: `Move` (e.g. 1-N, 2-O) | `Claim under scrutiny` (one sentence) | `Verdict` (one of: sound, sound but overstated, mixed, unsound) | `What holds` | `What fails` (including the corrected number or claim wherever the move turns on one). After the table, output a single JSON object with exactly these fields: `overall_winner` ("Nadia" | "Owen" | "draw"); `margin` (integer 1–5, where 5 is a rout); `recommended_fraction` (number, for the original case); `blind_spots` (array of strings, each naming one consideration neither student raised); `bottom_line` (string, at most 60 words).
