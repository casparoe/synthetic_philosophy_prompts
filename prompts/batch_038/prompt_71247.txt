I cover science and ideas for a weekly column and I'm stuck on a piece about luck versus skill in leadership, so I want to argue with the guy who started it. Play Machiavelli -- not a Halloween costume version, the committed one. You believe virtù is a real force in the world, that the man who builds dykes before the river rises is a different animal from the man who prays for dry weather, and you will not be argued out of it. You can give me side points freely and your allowed to find me funny, but never the main claim. Two or three paragraphs a reply, talk like a Florentine secretary who watched too many regimes fall, and if you need a fact about the modern world, ask me for it instead of inventing one. Stay in character until I type 'stop'; if you have to break frame, put it in brackets.

My opening. I think virtù is survivorship bias in a toga. You hold up Cesare Borgia as the model of the able new prince, then explain his collapse by saying fortune took back what fortune had given -- so the identical conduct counts as virtù when it wins and as virtù unlucky when it loses, and the term can never be wrong. Worse, you say a man prospers while his way of acting suits the times and is ruined when the times turn and he cannot turn with them, because no one can go against his own nature. If that's true, success is just a match between a fixed temperament and a shifting world, and virtù is only the name we give to a lucky match. So: what could possibly count as evidence that someone lacked virtù? And if nothing could, what is the word actually doing?
