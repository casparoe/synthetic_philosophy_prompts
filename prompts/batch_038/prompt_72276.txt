I am a junior in high school, and for the past year I have been running my school's service club, which means that every spring I am the one who decides where roughly four thousand dollars of bake-sale and car-wash money goes. Over winter break I read a great deal about effective altruism — the argument that donations should be compared the way purchases are compared, by what a dollar actually buys — and then a great deal written by the people who attack it, especially those who say the whole enterprise is systematically tilted toward whatever happens to be easy to count. The problem I have run into is that my opinions about particular cases feel quite firm while my opinions about the underlying rule feel nonexistent, and I would like to find out whether the cases can all be saved at once. Please answer as a professional philosopher who works on practical ethics and the ethics of giving, and think the problem through before responding; I would much rather you begin by brainstorming several candidate principles, including ones you end up rejecting and saying why, than hand me the first tidy formula that happens to fit. I cannot look anything up right now, so please argue from reasoning rather than pointing me toward sources or a reading list.

Here are my verdicts, in roughly the order they occurred to me.

1. A charity that distributes insecticide-treated bed nets, with randomized trials behind its numbers: clearly worth funding, and if the club could only give to one thing, this is what I would pick.
2. A charity that trains guide dogs for blind people, which by my recollection costs on the order of fifty thousand dollars per working dog: not worth the club's money, even though I consider the work admirable and would defend it against anyone who sneered at it.
3. My town's food bank: I volunteer there every other Saturday and think that is time well spent, but I would not send the club's money there. I feel no contradiction in this, and I want to be told whether there is one.
4. An organization that campaigns to change trade and agricultural subsidy rules that hurt small farmers in poor countries: no measurable output, no way anyone could check the counterfactual, and yet I suspect it does more good than the bed nets. I do not know what to do with that suspicion.
5. A charity that builds schools in a region where the schools stand half empty because teachers do not show up: perfectly measurable, and in my view a bad donation.
6. An organization working on risks that could affect people centuries from now: I think this could be the most important thing anyone does and simultaneously that no one could possibly know that it is. I would not fund it, but I would not call a person who funds it foolish.
7. My grandmother's annual gift to her church's mission fund: not the most good she could do with that sum, and yet I do not think she is doing anything wrong by giving it. But I do think I would be doing something wrong if I spent the same sum on a concert ticket for myself. I cannot explain the difference between these two judgments.

My own tentative principle is this: give only where you can estimate what a dollar buys in lives saved or healthy years gained, and be willing to act on that estimate even when the numbers are rough. I would like you to test it against the seven cases, repair it if it can be repaired, and tell me plainly if it cannot be. Notice that cases 4 and 5 together seem to break it from both directions — measurement looks neither necessary nor sufficient — and yet I keep wanting to say something like "only give where you can measure," so I suspect I am being pulled by something I have not identified. Cases 2, 3, and 7 are the ones I am least able to reconcile with one another: they seem to involve three different standards for money, time, and other people's money, and I do not know whether a single principle can cover all three or whether one of my verdicts has to be surrendered.

Once you have your principle or your small set of candidates, please state each one precisely, clause by clause, and say which of my cases motivated each clause. Then apply it to three cases I have no verdict on at all, and tell me what it says rather than what feels nice: a program that pays to remove lead pipes and lead paint from housing in poor American cities, where the effects on children's health and behavior are real but arrive decades later and are hard to attribute; an organization that makes unconditional cash transfers to very poor households, where I can see exactly what the dollar buys but not what it produces; and a scholarship for one unusually talented student from my own town, which would cost about the same as forty bed nets. Finally, and this matters to me: tell me how confident you are in your main claims, claim by claim rather than in some general reassurance at the end, and single out the one point in your whole answer that you are least sure of, explaining what makes it shaky. If you conclude that one of my verdicts has to go, say so directly instead of bending the principle to keep it. I would rather be overturned than flattered.
