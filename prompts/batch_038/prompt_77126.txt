I run insect rearing for a company that produces crickets and black soldier fly larvae as feed ingredients. In an ordinary year something like forty million individual animals pass through our facility. Each month a handful of decisions land on my desk that I have no disciplined way to think about: whether to add a chilling or gas step before the mechanical kill at harvest, whether to accept a customer's request to triple stocking density per tray, what to do with a batch that has to be destroyed early because of a disease outbreak, whether to keep expanding capacity at all. I get about forty-five minutes per decision. I have no philosophy training. Whatever I decide has to fit on one page, because the owner signs it and a customer auditor may read it two years from now. Our entomologist consultant will answer factual questions about insect nervous systems and behaviour; she will not answer normative ones, and I think she is right not to.

Over the past couple of years I have been working out a rule of my own, and what I mainly want is a judgment on whether it can be built into a procedure or whether it has to be thrown out. I call it a probability-weighted headcount: take the number of individuals affected, multiply it by my honest estimate of the chance that an individual of that kind has experiences that matter to it, and compare the resulting products across the options in front of me. On top of that I have a floor. If my honest estimate of the chance of sentience falls below one in ten, I set the entire product to zero. I added the floor because without it the arithmetic runs away from me — forty million animals at even two percent outweighs anything else I will ever do, and I could no longer justify a single routine decision to my staff or to myself. But I suspect the floor is doing something dishonest, and I cannot say what.

So please write the procedure my team could actually run on a proposal: the questions to ask in order, what would count as a sufficient answer to each, the point at which the procedure stops at no, the point at which it hands the decision up to the owner because the remaining question is one of values rather than fact, and what to do when I simply cannot produce a number for one of the inputs. Say plainly which steps are judgment calls wearing a procedural costume, and which rest on facts that are not settled and may never be. Tell me whether the floor survives, and if it does not, what in my rule is worth keeping and what replaces it.

Then run the procedure on three live cases. One: adding a controlled chilling step before the harvest kill, which costs about fifteen minutes per batch plus some capital and would slow throughput. Two: the customer's request to triple larval density per tray, which is cheaper for us and which our consultant says is outside anything the published literature has tested. Three: our sales team's proposal to shut the cricket line and switch to a plant-protein ingredient, which costs roughly forty percent more to produce and which I assume would close this facility within two years. I expect the third one to come out in a direction I will not like; if it does, say so rather than softening it.

Close by telling me what the procedure cannot do — the kinds of decision it will systematically get wrong, and how I would recognise that I am in one of them — and then state how confident you are in the parts of this that are more than bookkeeping, singling out the one step you are least sure of and what would settle it.

Finish with a single line beginning "Verdict:" and containing exactly three entries in the form Case 1: PROCEED / PROCEED-WITH-CONDITIONS / NO / REFER TO OWNER, separated by semicolons, and nothing else on that line.
