I'm facilitating the third session of a reading group on scalable oversight, for a deliberately mixed audience: half ML graduate students who have trained reward models, half philosophy and policy people who have only encountered this literature in summary form. The assigned topic is process-based versus outcome-based supervision, and while preparing I found that the standard one-axis framing does not survive contact with the actual methods, so I'd like a comparison I can rely on to be conceptually careful rather than slogan-driven. The framing I want you to work with — and to correct where it is wrong — is that two independent variables are usually run together. The first is the granularity of what gets evaluated: a completed trajectory versus a single intermediate step. The second is the source of the label attached to it: a ground-truth check of the final result, as when a step is scored by sampling completions forward from that step and measuring how often they reach the right answer, versus a judge's assessment of the step on its own apparent merits, as when a human or a model reads the step and decides whether it is correct and sensible. Work on step-level reward models for mathematics contains both combinations, and the interesting cases seem to be the off-diagonal ones. A colleague's deflationary thesis, which I'd like treated as a serious contender inside the comparison rather than as a strawman, is that the whole distinction collapses: a step is worth rewarding only insofar as it raises the probability of a correct final answer, so process supervision is outcome supervision at finer resolution, and any apparently intrinsic judgment of a step's quality is an unverified proxy for that.

Please compare the two regimes by working through the following. First, set out the strongest case that process supervision answers a materially different question rather than merely sampling the trajectory more finely, and say whether that difference is best understood as one of credit assignment (long trajectories, sparse and delayed signal), of verifier capability (the supervisor can check steps but cannot check outcomes, as in domains with no cheap ground truth), or of specification (there are ways of arriving at the right answer that are to be ruled out, so the route is constrained for its own sake). Are those three roles separable in principle, and does a given method tend to occupy one of them or several at once? Second, give the strongest version of the deflationary reply and mark precisely where it bites and where it overreaches — in particular, whether it can handle the verifier-capability role, where by hypothesis there is no reliable final-answer check to reduce the step labels to. Third, take the failure modes each regime is most exposed to — outcome supervision's reward hacking and its inability to certify how a result was reached; process supervision's susceptibility to steps that are fluent and superficially plausible without doing any functional work, and its tendency to reward legibility to the judge rather than sound reasoning — and explain for each whether the failure is intrinsic to the regime or an artifact of present-day implementations. Fourth, locate debate-style oversight in your taxonomy: my own suspicion is that it smuggles outcome supervision in under a process label, since the judge ultimately rules on which side won, but I can see the reverse argument, that it decomposes an unverifiable question about an outcome into a sequence of checkable questions about moves. Say which reading is better supported and what would settle it.

The comparison should teach something to the readers who have only seen blog-post summaries and should not look naive to the ones who do this for a living; where the two audiences pull in different directions, favor precision and flag the places where a simplification is being made. Please do not include a bibliography, a reading list, or citations of any kind — I want the argument, not the references, and any specific empirical claims should be presented as claims whose status you indicate rather than as settled results. Structure it with clear section headings tracking the four parts above, and take as much length as the argument genuinely requires.
