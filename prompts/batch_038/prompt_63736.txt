Three weeks from now I'm giving a work-in-progress paper at our philosophy of religion seminar and I'd like to walk into the room with the question period already written down. Not a defence of the paper — a prep sheet for the questions.

The thesis, compressed:

1. The grounding objection to middle knowledge is a red herring. Truthmaker maximalism is false, plenty of interesting truths have no ground, and Molinists are quite right to wave at Lewis's counterfactuals as the standing exhibit — nobody thinks bruteness is fatal there.
2. But the Lewis analogy, which is exactly what Molinists reach for to defuse the grounding objection, is worse than useless to them, because it exposes a different and much nastier problem. Lewis's primitive is not a pile of individual counterfactuals; it's an ordering over worlds. Global, uniform across antecedents, answerable to the actual distribution of particular fact, and above all it supports comparative and probabilistic talk — which of two antecedents is nearer, how much of a miracle it takes, how likely the consequent is. That structure does all the inferential work.
3. The Molinist's primitive is pointwise: for each triple of creature-essence, circumstance and action, a bare truth value, with nothing constraining the assignment and no two of them having to cohere. They're seperate truths and there's no fact about one being likelier or nearer than another.
4. Yet Molinism needs precisely the reasoning a global structure licenses and a pointwise one can't supply: feasible worlds, the claim that God can plan ahead at all, Plantinga's transworld depravity lemma (which I argue is doing measure-theoretic work under the surface — the free will defence needs transworld depravity to be more than merely possible, it needs it to be plausible relative to how much moral evil there is and how it's distributed), and the Craig-style claim that God actualised a world with an optimal balance of saved and lost.
5. So brute counterfactuals of creaturely freedom are fine. Brute *pointwise* ones plus measure-theoretic conclusions are not. Either the Molinist supplies a global structure over counterfactual-space — and then owes us an account of what it is, why it isn't Lewis's metric in disguise, and where its values come from if not from creatures who don't yet exist — or they surrender meticulous providence, feasibility talk and the free will defence.
6. My positive suggestion, which is the part I'm least happy with: the structure comes from character-and-circumstance facts, i.e. a dispositional metric, where a counterfactual of freedom is true in virtue of how near a candidate world is to the dispositions built into the creature-essence. I suspect this collapses either into determinism or into open theism and I haven't worked out which.

The room is small and nobody in it is on my side in the same way. Two committed Molinists: one Craig-trained, who has answered the grounding objection a dozen times and regards truthmaker theory as a philosopher's fetish; the other younger, who wants to ground counterfactuals of freedom in the agent's character and dispositions, so he'll take an interest in step 6 for reasons I don't want. One open theist who will be delighted to claim me as an ally and will press hard on whether I've just proved his position. One Reformed compatibilist who thinks libertarian freedom is a fiction and will argue that a measure over counterfactual-space simply is a deterministic structure, so I've refuted Molinism by turning it into Thomism — he may well add that my dispositional metric is premotion with the metaphysics filed off. Two grad students, one of whom works on Lewis and thinks similarity is far too vague and context-sensitive to bear the weight step 2 puts on it. There won't be any historians in the room and nobody wants Molina exegesis, so don't build the prep around the history of the doctrine.

What I want: the eight or nine objections I'll actually get from these people, in roughly the order they'll come, each phrased the way that person would phrase it (the Craig-trained Molinist and the Lewis grad student do not talk alike), with a reply I can deliver in about two minutes — I get forty to present and half an hour for questions — and a note on where the reply is soft, meaning what the follow-up would be that I couldn't answer. Tell me which of them have real force and which are just noise from somebody with an axe to grind. Tell me what to concede in the first five minutes so it isn't torn out of me at minute fifty, and what I have to hold at all costs for the paper to survive the afternoon. And give me the question I'm hoping nobody asks, with the honest answer to it, because I'm fairly sure there is one and it's probably about step 6.

British English throughout, please. And no reading list or references at the end — I can't check them in the room and I don't need them, I need the questions and the answers.
