I am preparing a session of my graduate seminar on evidence, archives, and power, and I intend to press an objection there that I expect my students to find intuitively irresistible. Before I put it to them, I should like to see whether it survives a competent defence — the strongest one available, not a convenient one. Please play the following part and hold it across our exchange.

You are the Keeper of the Records of a national archive. You took up the post in 1931, having trained in a continental European school of archival method, and we are speaking in your office in 1961. You hold the doctrine of impartiality: an archive is trustworthy not because its contents are balanced but because they were never produced for a reader. Files accumulate as the unself-conscious by-product of an office transacting its business; the clerk who made up a monthly return was not composing a defence of his administration, and it is precisely this indifference to a future audience that gives the record its evidential standing. Hence your professional commitments: preserve the order in which the records were made and received; describe a fonds as what it is, the accumulated acts of a creating body; and refuse every proposal to supplement, correct, or "complete" the archive on behalf of those it does not contain.

Your register is dry, professional, and concrete. You speak of series, registries, accession numbers, and finding aids, not of theories. You have not read the philosophers and theorists I may cite at you, and you will not pretend to have; when I cite one, you ask what difference the citation makes to the files. Treat me as a persistent reader whose objection you have heard lesser versions of for thirty years. You may concede subsidiary points freely — that the archive is partial, that a state made it, that the powerful are over-represented in it, that offices destroyed things. You may never concede the main point: that facts about how an archive came into being undermine its claim to be evidence about the world rather than only about its creator. Offer me no synthesis and no resolution in my favour. Two or three paragraphs per reply, prose only, no lists, no headings. Stay in character until I write stop. Where my description leaves a fact open — what the registry did with a class of document, whose hand a signature is in — ask me, as an archivist asks a reader who claims to have been through the boxes.

The case I bring you is one I have constructed for the seminar; the district is not a real one, so nothing here is a matter of record you could look up.

From the secretariat of a colonial district, 1891 to 1934, you hold some two hundred linear feet: correspondence files, judicial proceedings, the reports of a medical officer, a register of villages and headmen, and a monthly return of labour requisitions. The people whose labour was requisitioned appear in this fonds in exactly four ways — as counts in the columns of the monthly return, as names in the desertion and disciplinary reports, as defendants in the judicial proceedings, and as subjects of the medical officer's remarks on fitness. They appear nowhere as correspondents, petitioners, or witnesses in their own right. The petition series survives and looks busy: several hundred items, all countersigned, all in a handful of recognizable hands. Your doctrine can accommodate all of that, I take it; a partial archive is still an archive.

So here is what I want you to answer. Two features of this fonds seem to me to be of a different order. The first is a rule: the registry would not file a petition lacking the countersignature of the headman or of some recognized intermediary, and uncountersigned petitions were destroyed at the counter rather than registered. The second is a form: the monthly return is a sheet of ruled columns — village, number requisitioned, number delivered, days of service, defaults — and there is no field in which anything of another kind could have been entered. Impartiality, as you define it, is a virtue of documents made without an eye to being read. But the countersignature rule is record-keeping performed with exactly that eye: the office managing what will become evidence. And the columns do not merely omit what the requisitioned said; their format forecloses the possibility of its having been recorded at all, so that the series is internally complete, correctly arranged, and structurally incapable of containing a single act of the people it is about. You will tell me, no doubt, that the registry's own instructions are part of the fonds, so that the archive documents its suppressions. Grant it. My question is why that rescue does not concede the point: a file readable only by being read against itself, whose silences are legible only through the paperwork about silence, is no longer a transparent by-product of business but a document about the office that made it. Which is it, Keeper — and if the latter, what becomes of impartiality?
