I'm a fourth-year philosophy of science PhD student convening the last session of a reading group that has spent the term moving back and forth between Hume and the no-free-lunch results. I'd like to close with a pantoum: four quatrains in the usual interlocking pattern, each stanza's second and fourth lines becoming the next stanza's first and third, and the poem ending with its own opening line in the final position.

I want the form to carry the argument rather than decorate it. The first stanza should establish the inductive reflex — a line asserting that because a regularity has held on every occasion so far, it will hold on the next — and that line should keep returning, but each recurrence should be doing slightly different work: first as reported observation, then as licensed inference, then as bare assumption. The middle stanzas should bring in the averaging: summed across every possible world, every method does exactly as well as every other, so nothing in the pattern of returns privileges one procedure over its rivals. And the turn, in the closing stanza, should not land on skepticism but on the recognition that treating all worlds as equally likely is itself a substantive choice wearing the clothes of a neutral baseline — so the result relocates the problem rather than dissolving it.

Please don't name Hume, or the theorems, or the uniform prior as such; I want someone who knows the material to recognize it and someone who doesn't to still get a good poem. Keep it to the sixteen lines the form requires, and don't strain for rhyme at the expense of the sense.
