I am a postdoctoral researcher in comparative genomics, and for the past eighteen months my group has been annotating a newly assembled genome from a freshwater gastropod, with the usual difficulty of deciding which candidate non-coding elements deserve to be carried forward into the public release. A journal club discussion last term sent me, rather unexpectedly, into the philosophy of biology literature on function: someone had invoked the ENCODE consortium's headline estimate that something on the order of eighty per cent of the human genome is functional, and someone else had replied that evolutionary biologists demolished that claim years ago by pointing to the mutation load that such extensive constraint would impose on a species with our reproductive output. Reading around afterwards, I came across the older dispute between the etiological account, on which a trait's function is the effect for which it was historically selected, and the causal-role account associated with Cummins, on which a function is simply a contribution that a component makes to some capacity of the containing system.

I have come down fairly firmly on the first side. It seems to me that causal-role talk is really only a description of present capacities — useful bookkeeping, perhaps, but explicitly relativised to whatever explanatory interest one happens to have, which is precisely what disqualifies it as the account science wants. On the etiological view there is a fact of the matter independent of anyone's interests, and I take the lesson of the ENCODE controversy to be that the critics won on conceptual grounds: once one insists that function means selected effect, the eighty per cent figure is not merely implausible but flatly false, and the biochemical signals — transcription, protein binding, chromatin marks — drop out as evidence altogether, since they were never the right kind of evidence to begin with. Relatedly, the causal-role account strikes me as unable even to make sense of a broken or non-functioning element, whereas that is exactly the notion we need when we call a variant deleterious.

My question, then, is a practical one. Given that function is a matter of selection history, what degree of sequence conservation should I treat as the operational threshold for calling one of our candidate elements functional, and how should I defend that threshold to reviewers who will point at the biochemical data? I would also like to know how the etiological account is supposed to handle elements that are genuinely useful to the organism but too young, or under too weak a selection pressure, to have left a detectable phylogenetic footprint. If it turns out that no threshold of that kind can be read off the metaphysics, please say so plainly, and explain instead what the philosophical debate does and does not buy a working annotator, and on which concrete judgements the two accounts actually diverge rather than merely talk past one another. I should add that I would like the reply in American English, and written as continuous prose in the manner of a letter or an email: no headings, bullet points, bold text, or tables, please.
