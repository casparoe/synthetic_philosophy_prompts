A friend and I argued for most of the evening about whether the economics of present bias actually delivers what philosophers want from an account of weakness of will, and we reached a stalemate I would like broken. Her position: once exponential discounting is replaced by a quasi-hyperbolic function — an extra weight on the immediately available on top of a longer-run rate — the akratic pattern falls straight out, because the agent's ranking of options genuinely reverses as the reward approaches. My position: that model does not so much explain weakness of will as explain it away, though I could not say precisely why.

Please explain the conceptual issue, attending to three points. First, a *sophisticated* agent, one who correctly predicts how each of her future selves will choose, never appears to act against anything she judges: at every moment she does what her then-current discounting ranks best, and she knew in advance that she would. Is such an agent simply incapable of akrasia, or is the incontinence located somewhere I am not looking? Second, the standard analysis requires a judgment about what to do *now* that the concurrent action contradicts, whereas hyperbolic discounting supplies reversed *plans* — resolutions about tomorrow that today's self discards. Does substituting a diachronic for a synchronic better judgment cost the account its subject matter? Third, if the discounter is in truth a chain of temporal stages with divergent utility functions, the judgment violated at a given time belongs to an earlier stage rather than to the agent who acts; is this the same dissolution that threatens self-deception, where no single self both deceives and is deceived, or is the analogy misleading?

Close with your own verdict: which of these pictures, if any, leaves room for genuine weakness of will, and what would have to be added to the others to recover it.
