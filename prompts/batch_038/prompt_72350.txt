So I run a tiny podcast that lives in the gap between cognitive science and typography — we record in a library basement, my co-host handles all the audio, and our audience is basically a hundred type nerds plus a handful of cog sci grad students. Next month's guest is a researcher working in the tradition of the old Letter Spirit project, the one about what makes every 'a' still an 'a' no matter how far you bend it. She's landed somewhere pretty distinctive though and I want the questions to actually engage with that instead of letting her give the standard tour of style versus skeleton, which is a talk anyone in the audience has already heard twice.

Her view, roughly, is that letter identity is path-relative. A glyph counts as an 'a' if it can be reached from some canonical starting 'a' by a chain of small, style-level tweaks — slant, weight, openness, perportion, how big the bowl is, whether the stem terminates in a flare — without crossing a recognition cliff at any single step. No feature or bundle of features does the work; the history of the deformation does. And she's upfront about the consequence: the relation isn't transitive. You can walk from a plain roman 'a' to something quite exotic in steps that each look like the same letter as the one before, and end up with two glyphs that both qualify as 'a's but that a reader shown them side by side would not see as the same letter at all. She also holds that the cliff isn't a fixed threshhold — it's set by context, so an identical glyph embedded in a word with a consistent style is more robustly an 'a' than that same glyph sitting alone on a page. When you press her on any of this she tends to say "run the model and see what falls out," which is a real answer some of the time and a dodge the rest.

Can you write me eight to ten questions, each with a follow-up aimed at the answer she's most likely to give? I'd like at least one on the transitivity business — I want to know whether she'll accept that her theory licenses pairs of 'a's with nothing in common beyond their ancestry, and what that costs her. One on bootstrapping: what makes the starting 'a' canonical, and isn't the whole account parasitic on a paradigm case she was trying to explain away? One on how a context-set cliff is more than a feature detector with extra steps. And one on the elephant in the room: systems trained on enormous sets of rendered glyphs end up with style-invariant letter categories without any path structure whatsoever, so what is the path doing for her — is it an empirical claim about human readers, or an engineering trick for getting machines to be creative with type? I'd also want something on double-story versus single-story 'a' (one letter or two, and does her answer change for a child learning to read versus a type designer), and something on handwriting, where a single person's 'a' drifts across the page without any cliff at all. Then close with a question asking what observation would count against path-relativity, phrased so that "run the model" isn't an escape hatch. No softballs about what drew her to the topic, please.
