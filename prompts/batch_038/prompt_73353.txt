I spent forty years reading past climates out of lake sediments and ice — inferring causes nobody observed from traces nobody made on purpose. Since retiring I have been auditing a seminar in the philosophy of archaeology, where the inference runs the other way: from made objects to the beliefs of the people who made them. I keep a log of the verdicts of the archaeologist who leads it, because her standards are stricter than anything I enforced in my own field, and I cannot yet see their shape.

Her verdicts so far:

1. A sealed deposit beneath a house floor — complete vessels, articulated animal skeletons with no butchery marks, a disarticulated human skull: she accepts that this was a deliberate, patterned, non-utilitarian deposit made at the closing of the house, and refuses every claim about what it was for.
2. An assemblage of the same character recovered from plough-disturbed soil: she refuses even the claim of deliberate patterning.
3. A midden holding an enormous quantity of cattle bone, prime-age profiles, systematic butchery: she rejects ritual feasting, accepting only large-scale consumption where many people gathered.
4. A large building with no domestic debris, benches around its interior, and repeated deposits of the right forelimb of one species: she accepts a communal structure used for patterned gatherings, and rejects temple.
5. Graves in which the richest furnishings belong to infants: she accepts ascribed, inherited rank — an institution, she says, not a belief.
6. A distinctive pottery style spreading across a region with no accompanying change in technology or settlement: she rejects both migration and shared ethnicity, accepting only shared practices of making and exchange.
7. A rite witnessed and written down by an outsider in the same valley at a much later date, producing deposits closely resembling prehistoric ones: she accepts the analogy as evidence that such deposits can be produced by rite, and refuses to let it name the rite.
8. Small clay figurines, deliberately broken before deposition, present in nearly every house: she accepts a repeated domestic practice of breaking, and rejects mother goddess and children's toy in the same breath, on the ground that the record is equally silent on both.

Now the case before her. A cave with intact stratigraphy holding some two hundred figurines of a single form, each broken at the same point, deposited in a fissure alongside the articulated remains of a bird nowhere eaten in the region and charcoal of a wood nowhere used as fuel; no domestic refuse of any kind; and a group recorded in the same valley two thousand years later — the practice being absent from the intervening record — described as breaking images and offering that same bird.

Tell me what rule she is running: that a claim is licensed only where the association of finds is demonstrably primary; only where practical alternatives have been positively excluded; only up to the level of patterned behavior, stopping before meaning; only where a single hypothesis accounts for several independent features at once; or some weighting of these that her verdicts pin down. Then predict her verdict on the cave, say which of her eight cases she will treat as its closest analog and whether that analog decides anything, and state what she commits herself to either way. Finally, name the one further case that would separate whichever of these rules happen to agree here.
