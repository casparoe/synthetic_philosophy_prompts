I'm TA-ing a third-year ethics seminar this term and I've got a stack of take-homes to mark tonight. The fortnight was on moral progress, set reading was Singer's *The Expanding Circle*, and the convenor's lectures hammered on the difference between explaining why the circle widened and showing that it widened for reasons that would hold up for someone who didn't already feel like widening it. The question I set was:

---

**QUESTION** (max 1000 words)

Distinguish carefully between three claims that are routinely run together in the moral progress literature: (i) the claim that the scope of moral concern has in fact widened over historical time; (ii) the claim that some particular mechanism is responsible for that widening; and (iii) the claim that the widening counts as progress. Explain what Singer's "escalator of reason" is supposed to contribute here — which of the three claims it is doing work for, and how the work is meant to go. Then set out the objection that the widening is reversible, that the circle can contract again under the right conditions, and assess how much damage that objection really does to Singer's account.

STUDENT ANSWER (947 words):

---

The question asks us to keep apart three claims that get collapsed into each other almost every time moral progress is discussed. (i) is descriptive: the set of beings whose interests count for us has, as a matter of historical fact, gotten larger — kin and band first, then co-nationals and co-religionists, then all humans, and now, contestably, non-human animals, foetuses, the far future, maybe artificial agents. (ii) is explanatory: something in particular caused the widening, and rival accounts compete over what it was — trade, print and literacy, rising prosperity, the state's monopoly on violence, or Singer's own candidate, the operation of reason. (iii) is evaluative: the widening was not merely a change but an improvement. Keeping these apart matters because (i) on its own settles nothing. A circle that widened for no reason, or for bad reasons, would still be a widened circle. And (iii) needs a standpoint, since if "progress" just means movement in the direction the present consensus happens to approve of, then every generation is making progress by its own lights, including generations that narrowed their circles. So (iii) has to be measured against something that is not identical to the opinion of whoever is doing the measuring.

Singer's escalator of reason is meant to do work on (ii) and, through (ii), on (iii). The raw material is an evolved and narrow altruism — kin selection plus reciprocity inside the band — which on his view is not really moral at all yet, because it discriminates. What widens it is that we are creatures who can represent other people's situations to ourselves, and our sympathetic responses track those representations. So the escalator is largely a story about information and imagination: as we come to know more vividly what the lives of strangers, foreigners and animals are actually like, sympathy generalises to cover them, and reason's job is to sort and stabilise the result — to notice that if I care about this person's suffering I can hardly pretend that the identical suffering of that one doesn't matter. On this account (ii) supports (iii) fairly directly. A concern that widens because we have stopped being ignorant about who suffers is a concern that is tracking something about the sufferers rather than expressing a local prejudice, and that is precisely what makes the widening progress instead of drift.

There is a cost to locating the mechanism there, and it is worth pressing. Sympathy as a psychological engine has well-documented biases: it responds far more strongly to the single identifiable victim than to statistical thousands, to the cute and the young, to the nearby and the recent. If the widening is driven by the generalisation of sympathetic response, then the future shape of the circle is hostage to those biases, and the account predicts — wrongly, or at least embarrassingly — that our sense of obligation will always be sharpest where victims are vivid and will stall exactly where they are abstract: the far future, climate change, industrial animal farming at scale, wild animal suffering. Singer's own practical conclusions, most of which concern beings who are neither vivid nor near nor attractive, sit badly with the mechanism he is offered.

Now the reversibility objection. The record is not a monotonic ascent. Expansions have been followed by contractions: the dehumanisation of neighbours in wartime, labour systems that were slavery in all but name revived after formal abolition, rights withdrawn from groups that had held them, the ease with which whole populations get placed outside the circle once a political entrepreneur finds it useful. Two readings are available. The weak one says (i) was never a law but a trend with local reversals, and that Singer's mechanism is a tendency claim: other things equal, reasoning pushes outward, and other things are frequently not equal. That reply is open to him and it isn't cheap — a tendency claim is not refuted by failing to manifest, any more than a disposition is refuted by being masked. But it comes at a price. If the escalator can be stalled, redirected or run backwards by the right social conditions, then the mechanism stops looking like an independent force and starts looking like a redescription of whatever direction concern happens to be travelling in, and a mechanism that cannot turn out to be wrong cannot carry any weight in an argument for (iii). The stronger reading is that the contractions are not interruptions but evidence that the widening was never caused by what Singer says caused it: what actually tracks the size of the circle is surplus, stability and low threat, so the circle widens when generosity is cheap and contracts when it isn't, and reason merely drafts the justification afterwards. On that version reversibility is not a puzzle for the theory but its refutation, and the escalator is epiphenomenal.

The deeper problem, though, is that (iii) is being asked to do more work than Singer can pay for. To say the widening was progress is to say it was progress toward something, and that something cannot be the current consensus or the claim goes empty. Singer accepts this and is an objectivist: there are ethical truths, and the expanding circle is our getting closer to them. Fine. But then the whole package is available only to realists. An expressivist, or anyone who thinks moral judgement is a dressed-up expression of socially shaped attitudes, cannot say that the abolition of slavery was moral progress at all; the most they can manage is that we now disapprove of what our ancestors approved, which is a change of taste and not an improvement. So the expanding circle, insofar as it is progress, already presupposes the metaethic Singer wants to argue for, and the historical record is not neutral evidence that realists and anti-realists examine from their own angles. It is evidence for realism. The widening looks like improvement rather than drift, and only realism can make sense of that looking. Singer's account is therefore self-supporting in a way that won't move anyone who wasn't already moved, but it also means anti-realists owe us an explanation of an appearance they aren't entitled to have.

Conclusion: (i) is probably true; (ii) is contested and, as Singer deploys it, possibly unfalsifiable; (iii) is only intelligible inside a realist metaphysic, and that is where the argument actually has to be had.
