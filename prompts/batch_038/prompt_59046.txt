Been doing the Kelly thing for a few years now, mostly sports betting at small stakes plus a couple of side hustles, and I've made peace with the standard story: estimate your edge, bet the fraction that maximizes long-run growth, and shade down if you're unsure about the edge. Totally fine. But there's a *third* reason I keep finding myself shading down, and I've never seen it discussed anywhere, and I can't tell whether it's a real pattern with a name or whether I've just dressed up my cowardice in math.

Here's what I mean. A bad outcome in a repeated bet isn't only a loss of money — sometimes it's a loss of the right to keep betting, and that loss is imposed from outside. Three cases I've actually watched. (1) A mate of mine used to stake a poker player whose edge was genuinely there; on paper the guy should have sat in the biggest game his roll supported, but my friend cut the stake after any bad month, so the player's real horizon wasn't the long run, it was however long it took to trip a reviewer who was looking at a sample far too small to tell skill from noise. (2) A small fund I follow, where the manager compounds nicely over a decade but has stretches of two or three quarters that look awful on a chart, and the people who can pull their money out don't wait for the decade. (3) Me: if I take a big swing and it goes wrong, the damage isn't just the stake, it's that my wife — who has, quite reasonably, a veto over anything touching the joint account — reclassifies me as someone who can't be trusted with the betting bank, and the whole project ends, edge or no edge. In all three the binding constraint isn't my utility function and it isn't uncertainty about my edge. It's that someone else is forming an estimate of me from a path much shorter than my edge needs in order to show itself, and that estimate decides whether I get another hand. So what you're really maximizing isn't growth rate, it's growth rate subject to not dropping below a threshold that lives in somebody else's head — and that threshold moves around depending on how patient they are and how noisy your results look to them.

So, is there a name for this? I can guess at neighbors — career risk, drawdown limits, the old complaint that you can be right and still get wiped out before being right pays — but none of them seem to nail the specific structure, which is that the absorbing barrier isn't at zero dollars, it's inside another person's judgment, and its height depends on their patience and on how much of your track record they can actually see. What I want is the carving, not just a label, so please be concrete about what this is *not*: how does it differ from (a) betting half Kelly because you're unsure of your edge, (b) plain risk aversion, (c) the ergodicity point that a bet good for a crowd can be bad for one person over time — I get that one, and this isn't it, because here the termination comes from outside rather than from the arithmetic of compounding — and (d) the opposite behaviour I've also seen, where someone close to the barrier starts taking *more* risk instead of less, which feels like the same setup producing the reverse answer and bugs me a lot. And if there's no established term, coin one, define it tightly enough that I could tell whether a given case falls under it or not, and tell me what it does to the sizing math: is there a version of the growth-optimal fraction that's a function of the length of the window your evaluator gets to observe? Bonus points for saying when the constraint should make you quit the game entirely rather than just bet smaller, becuase that's the decision I'm actually procrastinating on.
