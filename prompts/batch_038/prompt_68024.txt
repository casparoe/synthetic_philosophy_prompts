So I'm giving a workshop talk in a few weeks defending epiphenomenalism — qualia are real, non-physical, and don't push anything around — and defending it specifically against the paradox of phenomenal judgment: the charge that if my pain can't cause my wincing, or the sentence I write about it, then my pain talk is either a miracle of coincidence or wasn't about pain in the first place. My move is that acquaintance does the job causation was supposedly needed for. Phenomenal concepts are partly constituted by the very experiences they pick out, so a judgment can be appropriately about the quale without the quale causing it — constitutive relation instead of causal work.

The room: mostly physicalist philosophers of mind, two fairly combative illusionists who think my whole setup is confused rather than false, and a couple of experimental psychologists who work on metacognition and confidence ratings and won't be budged by anything that isnt a task design.

Build me a prep sheet, not a defense: the seven or eight objections I'll actually field from these people, roughly in the order they'll land, each phrased the way that subgroup would phrase it (the illusionists and the psychologists are going to say very different things about the same worry), a two-minute reply for each, and a blunt note on where the reply is soft. Mark which ones I should just concede in the first sixty seconds to buy goodwill. And tell me the question I'm praying nobody asks.
