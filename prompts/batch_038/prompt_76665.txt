I work on probabilistic forecasting and model calibration for a national hydrology service, and my professional vocabulary is credences as model outputs, proper scoring rules, reliability diagrams, prequential evaluation, and the thresholding step that turns a forecast into an operational decision. I have essentially no training in philosophy of language. I want to understand Moore's paradox, and I want to understand it in those terms rather than in the ones the literature uses.

Here is what I want translated. Take the sentence-schema formed by conjoining a plain empirical claim with a first-person present-tense denial of belief in that same claim — for concreteness, a hydrologist who says that the levee will hold through the season and, in the same breath, that she does not believe it will hold. Philosophers treat such a conjunction as defective in a distinctive way while insisting that it is not self-contradictory and can perfectly well be true. There is also a variant in which the second conjunct is not an absence of belief but a belief in the negation: that the levee will hold, and that she believes it will not.

Restate that situation, and the main diagnoses of it, in my vocabulary, as faithfully as you can. In particular, I want the counterpart in that vocabulary for each of the following:

(a) the absurdity itself — is it a coherence failure, a calibration failure, a decision-theoretic failure, or something the framework has no slot for;
(b) the fact that the conjunction can be true and that its conjuncts are jointly consistent;
(c) the family of accounts that locate the trouble in assertion rather than in belief, on the grounds that in saying that something is so one commits oneself to it, so the second conjunct retracts what the first does — and what assertion amounts to for a forecaster who publishes a number into a permanent log;
(d) the claim that belief is transparent, so that from the inside the question whether the levee holds and the question whether she believes it holds are not two questions;
(e) any difference in severity between the two variants above.

Then give me the residue report. Go back through (a) to (e) and say which translate cleanly, which you had to bend to fit, and which have no expression at all in scoring rules and reliability diagrams. Two cautions. First, do not smuggle: if a translation works only because you have already read my framework as containing a notion of flat-out commitment, say so plainly rather than presenting it as a result of the exercise. Second, confront the deflationary reading directly. In my vocabulary the Moorean conjunction has an utterly mundane counterpart — the event occurs and the model had assigned it 0.3 — and a well-calibrated system produces precisely that outcome three seasons in ten. Nothing is absurd about it; that is what calibration is for. Likewise, coherence binds a forecaster who assigns credence zero to a truth, but 0.3 binds no one. So it looks as though the paradox requires all-or-nothing belief and evaporates once belief is replaced by a graded forecast plus a decision threshold. Tell me whether that is right, or whether the residue shows that my vocabulary is missing something the philosophers are tracking — perhaps something about what it is to settle a question rather than to price one — or whether the original claim was less clear than it appeared.

Argue from the structure of the positions rather than from authority. I cannot check references, and I do not want a bibliography or a survey of who held which view.
