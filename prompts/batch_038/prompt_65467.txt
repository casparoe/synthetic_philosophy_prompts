I am preparing a short seminar presentation on the socialist calculation debate for a philosophy-of-economics reading group. The edited volume on twentieth-century economic thought I have been working from opens its chapter on the debate with the following paragraph, which I had planned to use as my framing:

"The socialist calculation debate was settled by Oskar Lange. In 1920 Ludwig von Mises had announced that socialism is impossible: with public ownership of the means of production there are no market prices for capital goods, so a planning board cannot know whether it is squandering steel or using it well, and even if it somehow had the data it could never solve the millions of simultaneous equations that describe an economy. Lange's reply, published in the 1930s, was that Mises had confused a practical difficulty with a theoretical impossibility. The equations do have a solution, and a socialist board can find it by trial and error: announce a set of accounting prices, observe the resulting surpluses and shortages, and revise until supply matches demand in every market. Managers are instructed to produce where marginal cost equals the announced price and to minimize average cost, so the whole system behaves exactly as a competitive market would, with the bonus that the board, unlike private capitalists, can also suppress monopoly and unemployment. Hayek's celebrated response in 'The Use of Knowledge in Society' did not dispute any of this. He conceded the theory and argued instead that the relevant knowledge is dispersed and largely tacit, so no board could ever gather it in time to be useful. What was left of the Austrian case was therefore an objection about incentives: planners and managers have no stake in the outcome, and the system decays through laziness and concealment. History vindicated that practical conclusion but not the argument, since nothing in Lange's scheme was theoretically incoherent, and the failures of the actually existing planned economies, which became impossible to dismiss after 1989, were failures of implementation."

My instinct is that this is broadly fair, and I am sympathetic enough to the market-socialist side that the paragraph gives me a story I would like to tell. But two things nag at me. I associate the phrase "tacit knowledge" with Michael Polanyi rather than with Hayek, and I cannot tell whether the chapter is borrowing another author's concept and passing it off as Hayek's or whether that is my misremembering. And the confident verdict that the debate was settled on theory and lost only on practice does not square with the fact that people whose judgment I trust still argue about it.

Could you audit the paragraph claim by claim? For each substantive assertion, tell me whether it is accurate, whether it is a defensible simplification, or whether it is wrong or misleading, and where it is wrong, tell me both what a careful defender of Mises or Hayek would object to and what a careful defender of Lange and Lerner would object to, since I do not want to replace an Austrian caricature with a socialist one. I am especially interested in these questions:

- Does the paragraph state Mises's thesis at the right level of generality, and does it capture the scope conditions Mises himself attached to the argument, including what he conceded about a socialist commonwealth embedded in a wider world market?
- Are the missing-prices point and the cannot-solve-the-equations point the same argument or two different ones, and does the paragraph's compression of them into a single claim matter?
- Is the "practical difficulty versus theoretical impossibility" move correctly assigned to Lange as his answer to Mises, or does the paragraph relocate a concession that belongs to a different author at a different date?
- Does it describe Lange's mechanism accurately, and does it omit anything whose absence changes the story? I have a dim memory that a formal solution for a collectivist ministry of production predates Mises's article, and that the marginal-cost rule is often credited to two people rather than one.
- Is the attribution of "tacit" knowledge to Hayek fair? More importantly, does rendering the knowledge argument as information that exists but cannot be gathered in time preserve what Hayek was claiming, or does it downgrade a claim about the character of the knowledge and the process that uses it into a claim about administrative latency?
- Is it fair to say the residue of the Austrian case was an objection about laziness and concealment? What work do residual claimancy, rivalry, and entrepreneurial discovery do here, and would an Austrian accept the incentive framing as a statement of their point?
- Once the paragraph is corrected, does the closing verdict survive? Is the split between conclusion and argument even coherent in this case, or does the correction collapse it?

I am not asking for citations, a bibliography, or a reading list, and you do not need to reproduce exact wording from any of the texts involved; I want your judgment about the substance.
