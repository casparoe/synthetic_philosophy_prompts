The case is a genuine dispute at the museum where I do some cataloguing, and we cant settle it.

Our prize object is a Roman marble torso, a dancing figure, about a metre high, acquired in the sixties and on the same plinth ever since. In 2011 a crate came off a trolley during a gallery move and roughly 30 per cent of the marble was shattered beyond salvage — the head, most of the left arm, a slab of the plinth. The conservator, who is very good, re-carved the missing parts in fresh marble from the same Tuscan quarry the ancient workshop used, fixed them with reversible stainless pins, and retouched the joins. What stands on the plinth now is the original 70 per cent plus new 30 per cent.

Meanwhile, the recoverable fragments were swept up and crated. Last summer a student spent a few weeks reassembling them — head, arm pieces, drapery — into a rough but recognisable version of the dancer, which now sits in the study room on a foam support. That's about 30 per cent of the original marble in something like the original arrangement, held together with modern filler and a carbon-fibre armature.

Three positions in the staff room:

1. The conservator says the statue never went anywhere. Statues survive losing bits of themselves — a torso with its head knocked off is the same statue, only damaged — and adding new marble is no different in kind from the consolidation work we do all the time. Her clincher: if she had done the repair one small chip at a time over twenty years, nobody would deny that the statue survived, so the repair can't have failed just because she did it in a fortnight.

2. The registrar says the 2011 accident destroyed the original. A statue can lose a head, not 30 per cent of its matter all at once. What's on the plinth is a new statue, made in 2012 by someone on our payroll; the study-room object is a ruin and a teaching aid, nothing more. She's already given the plinth object a seperate accession number, which is why the conservator is cross.

3. A philosopher friend of the director, brought in to arbitrate, says both of them are making a mistake, because there is no fact of the matter. 'Statue' and 'same statue' are labels our practices impose, and the practices simply haven't decided a case like this one; both descriptions are equally acceptable, and arguing about which is correct is like arguing over whether the crate of fragments is a heap.

So here's what I want the essay to do. Controversy, verdict, defence — don't just map the terrain.

- Is the thing on the plinth the statue catalogued in 1963? Is the study-room reconstruction it? Can both be? Must exactly one be?
- Take the conservator's one-chip-at-a-time argument seriously, because I think it's the best thing said in the staff room. Does it work? Does it matter that the replacement happened in a fortnight rather than over decades, and if it does, why on earth would the timescale make a difference? Our trustee circulated the old passage from Plutarch's Life of Theseus about the ship the Athenians kept in the harbour — in Perrin's translation, "They took away the old timbers from time to time, and put new and sound ones in their places, so that the vessel became standing illustration for the philosophers in the mooted question of growth, some declaring that it remained the same, others that it was not the same vessel." Tell me whether our case is that problem in fancy dress or a structurally different one, given the gradualism.
- Now think about the marble as stuff rather than as statue. The original lump is split between two rooms, roughly 70/30. Does tracking the stuff settle which object is the dancer, or does it just relocate the dispute? If you want to bring in the familiar worry about a statue and the lump it's made of occupying the same place at the same time, do bring it in, but don't let it eat the essay — I want the museum case decided.
- Address the deflationary option head on. Is there really nothing to be right about? Consider the worry that identity can't be indeterminate between two precise designators: if 'the statue on the plinth' and 'the statue catalogued in 1963' each pick out a definite object, then it seems there has to be a determinate answer about whether they're identical, however hard it is for us to know. Does that sink the philosopher's arbitration, or can vagueness get back in somewhere else — through the terms rather than the world, say?
- End with a couple of sentences on what the museum should actually write on the label and the valuation, since we do have to write both. Keep that short; I mainly want the metaphysics.
