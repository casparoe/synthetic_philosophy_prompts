I serve on the review panel of a small foundation whose only program funds work on collective-action problems. Before any grant is considered, the panel must settle a threshold question about the nominated situation: is it a genuine multipolar trap — what our program officer calls a Moloch case, in which the structure of the interaction drives the participants somewhere none of them want to go — or is it instead a mere coordination problem, an ignorance problem, or an ordinary conflict of interest that no institutional ingenuity should be spent on? Only the first kind is fundable. Our rulings so far, all unanimous:

1. Four national fleets working a single fish stock in waters beyond any regulator's reach: catch data is published and every captain can see the decline, each boat lands more by fishing harder whatever the others do, no quota regime or treaty mechanism is available to them, and the collapse would ruin the shore towns that live off the fleets and raise prices for consumers who take no part in the fishing. Ruling: a genuine trap.

2. Two consumer-goods giants that have escalated advertising spend for a decade: market shares are unchanged, margins are thinner, prices are higher, the two boards meet annually and know the arithmetic perfectly, both would prefer a truce, but an explicit truce would be illegal collusion and no lawful device lets them bind one another. Ruling: a genuine trap.

3. Two rival states building missiles neither wants, under mutual satellite surveillance and a permanent hotline: each gains by pulling ahead whatever the other does, no verifiable treaty is available to them, and the cost falls on taxpayers and on populations who bear the rising risk of accident without any say in it. Ruling: a genuine trap.

4. An industry split between two equally good technical formats: any firm can adopt the emerging convention at trivial cost, a joint announcement would settle the matter at once, customers are indifferent between the two, and the only losers are the firms themselves. Ruling: not a trap, but a coordination problem.

5. Two universities whose admissions offices had been shortening each other's reply deadlines: a common reply date was available, costless, known to both, and one each school had every reason to keep once the other adopted it, and neither the schools nor their applicants lost anything from the earlier scramble but administrative annoyance. Ruling: not a trap.

6. An open ascending auction for a painting between two collectors with private valuations and no intention to resell: each stops when the price passes their own valuation, no one is driven past their own limit, and what the loser forgoes the seller gains. Ruling: not a trap, but an ordinary conflict of interest.

Next month's nomination concerns a closed-end credit vehicle that owes two billion to forty institutional creditors against assets that are sound but illiquid. Creditors may withdraw on a day's notice; if enough withdraw at once, the vehicle must sell into a thin market and all of them take a haircut. Every creditor sees the same balance sheet, receives real-time notice of every other withdrawal, and is in daily contact with the rest; each would rather stay in if the others stay in, and rather be first out if the others might leave. No insurance covers these creditors, no legal mechanism exists for a binding standstill, and the vehicle's charter forbids suspending withdrawals; its only counterparties are the creditors, so its failure would cost no third party anything.

Predict the panel's ruling and identify the principle or principles it would be applying. Answer as a numbered list of exactly five items, each of one or two sentences, with no introduction and no conclusion: (1) your predicted ruling; (2) the principle or principles that fit all six precedents and yield that ruling; (3) the principle or principles that fit all six precedents just as well and yield the opposite ruling; (4) which of these you would bet the panel is actually running, and what in the record tips you that way; (5) the single further nomination you would put to the panel next in order to separate the leading candidates, with the ruling each of them would give on it.
