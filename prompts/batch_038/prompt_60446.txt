I have spent eleven years on a novel about a decade that begins with an eruption. The setting is a composite and the mountain is invented, but the sequence is not: the dust veil and the two ruined growing seasons, the hunger, then the typhus that arrives after the hunger and kills more than the hunger did. In April I give a forty-minute invited talk at an institute colloquium on climate and history, followed by an hour of discussion, and I expect that hour to be harder than the talk.

The room will be a dozen people or so. Preindustrial economic historians who work from harvest prices, parish registers, tithe accounts and tax rolls; two of them build or use the proxy series — tree rings, ice cores, lake sediments — and are accustomed to being asked to date things more precisely than the record permits. One paleoclimatologist who supplies such data and who objects, audibly, to the loose use of the word "anomaly." Two environmental historians whose formation is postcolonial and for whom the claim that the weather decided the outcome has functioned, in their reading of the literature, as an alibi that lets the creditor, the encloser and the conquering administration out of the account. A couple of literary scholars who regard the climate novel as an exhausted genre with a fixed set of tics, and who will have read my book as an instance of the genre rather than as an argument. No philosophers. No other novelists, so far as I know. The moderator is one of the economic historians: sympathetic, exacting, and fond of asking what evidence would change my mind.

My thesis is deliberately narrow, and I want the objections aimed at the narrow version, not at the vaguer version people will hear in the first ten minutes. Here is the sketch I intend to give them, so you know what to attack.

First: determinism is a claim about the distribution of causal responsibility among the elements of an explanation; what I am calling agency is a claim about the distribution of attention among the elements of a narration. The charge of environmental determinism is so often levelled at novels that make no causal claim at all, and I want to argue that this is because the two distributions are routinely confused.

Second: a non-human force can be treated as an actor in fiction without being given a mind, provided it is granted three things a novel otherwise reserves for persons — persistence across scenes; differential responsiveness, meaning it acts differently on different people and the differences are legible to the reader; and irreversibility, meaning what it does cannot be walked back by later scenes. I intend to call a treatment with all three an actor, and one with only the first a setting, and to argue that most climate fiction fails by giving the weather persistence but no differential responsiveness, which is why it reads as backdrop no matter how many pages the ash gets.

Third: historians who refuse agency to non-human forces pay a real price. They cannot represent constraint as such — only constraint already resolved into somebody's choices — and their prose consequently smuggles the weather back in through the passive voice, through adverbs, and through the timing of chapters.

Fourth: I will defend, in detail, the passage my hosts already have in excerpt. It is the chapter in which the aerosol layer is addressed in the second person, and the sentence near its end in which a rising in the third year is said to have been settled, as a matter of fact and not of metaphor, by the failure of the second harvest. I know exactly how that sentence sounds to the two environmental historians. I have chosen to keep it.

What I need from you is a prep sheet, not a defense of me. Give me the eight or so objections this room will actually raise, ordered by how much damage each would do if I answered it badly — likelihood weighted against severity, not severity alone. Phrase each in the idiom of the person most likely to raise it, complete with the disciplinary tic: the paleoclimatologist's demand that I name a magnitude rather than a mood, the economic historian's question about what my claim predicts that could be checked against the price series, the postcolonial objection in the vocabulary of alibi and exoneration, the literary scholar's in the vocabulary of genre convention. For each: a reply I can deliver in about two minutes standing up, without notes, without jargon I do not own; and a frank note on where that reply is softest, including the places where my second and third points may undercut each other.

Then tell me what I should concede inside the first five minutes, before I am made to concede it; what I must not concede under any circumstances, because the argument collapses without it; and which single reply has to land for the talk to be judged a success rather than a curiosity. Finish with the question I am hoping nobody asks — and with advice on whether to answer it honestly if it comes, given that I am a guest in their discipline and not a member of it.

No bibliography, no reading list, no named scholars: I am not going to cite anyone's field back at them, and a citation I have not verified is worse than none. What I want is the room's own vocabulary, accurately rendered.

One last thing, and I mean it. Rank your objections with real confidence rather than averaging everything toward equal seriousness, and tell me where my thesis is merely rhetorical. If the distinction between the distribution of causal responsibility and the distribution of attention turns out to be a distinction without a difference — if what I have called an actor is a stylistic mannerism wearing an argument's clothes, and the talk is therefore built on a confusion rather than a claim — say so in your first paragraph and tell me what I should defend instead. I would much rather hear it now than in April, and a polite working-around of the problem would be no use to me at all.
