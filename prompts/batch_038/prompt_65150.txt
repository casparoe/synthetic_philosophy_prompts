You are a professional philosopher whose research is in metaethics and in the theory of practical and theoretical rationality, and you have worked closely through the literature on the wrong kind of reasons problem. Please think carefully before answering, and treat this as a diagnostic task rather than a survey: I know the territory, and what I need is your judgment about its structure.

Background, briefly, so you know what I am working with. The problem begins from cases in which an agent has an incentive to adopt an attitude that the attitude's own object does not support: the demon who will reward you for admiring, loving, or fearing him though he is not admirable, lovable, or fearsome; the prize offered for believing something you have no evidence for. One camp holds that such incentives are genuine reasons, but of the wrong kind — considerations that count in favor of being in the state while bearing on nothing that the attitude is about, and so cannot be followed in deliberation, since the attitude answers a question to which they are not answers. A second camp holds that the orthodox sorting of reasons into object-given and state-given cannot be made to work, because there are right-kind reasons against belief and against intention that the theory is forced to misclassify; on this view the distinction is either empty or does none of the work assigned to it. A third camp goes further and denies there is anything to explain: incentives are simply reasons whose weight is normally swamped, and the talk of kinds is a useful fiction about what deliberation feels like from the inside. Running alongside all of this is the separate worry that the wrong-kind cases threaten analyses of value in terms of fitting attitudes or in terms of reasons to favor, since if a demon can make it fitting to admire him, fittingness is not what value consists in.

Two decades on, nothing has moved. Each side produces a case, the other says the case is mis-described, and every candidate criterion — constitutive aim, correctness standard, the question an attitude answers, the transparency of deliberation, a reasons-first account of normative force — attracts a counterexample and a rescue in roughly equal measure. I have a hypothesis, which I want you to test rather than endorse: that this is not one debate but at least four that share a name. There is a taxonomic question about whether the reason-relation admits a principled sortal at all; an existence question about whether incentives can be reasons for belief in their own right; a grounding question about whether any criterion can be stated without presupposing the standard of correctness it is meant to explain; and a question in value theory about whether fitting-attitude analyses survive the demon. And my further suspicion is that the stalemate is manufactured by a bridging assumption nobody states out loud — that a successful account must classify all the cases extensionally, rather than explain a deliberative practice — so that arguments aimed at the grounding question get deployed as though they settled the taxonomic one and vice versa. Drop that assumption, I suspect, and half the arguments stop applying to the other half of the questions.
