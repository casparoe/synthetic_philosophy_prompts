I head the Classifications and Definitions unit at a national statistical agency. Roughly fifty times a year a term in one of our official classifications comes into dispute — whether a person counts as unemployed, whether a settlement counts as rural, whether a death counts as a stillbirth or a miscarriage, whether a worker counts as employed in the informal sector — and my unit has to produce a recommendation that goes to a standing committee of statisticians, lawyers, and ministry officials, who either sign off or send it back. The recommendations are consequential: they set programme eligibility, they get incorporated by reference into legislation, and once published they define a time series that analysts and the press will treat as continuous for decades. My case officers are able but have no training in philosophy of language, we typically have a day or two per case, and the deliverable has to be a form someone can fill in rather than an essay. The pressure on the unit currently runs from both directions at once. From outside, we are accused of moving definitions to make the numbers look better; a columnist wrote last month that our unemployment rate is simply whatever we decide it is. From inside, a senior analyst who read a précis of Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" has begun arguing the mirror image of that charge: since the analytic–synthetic distinction does not survive scrutiny, there is no fact of the matter about what our terms mean, every definitional choice is a free pragmatic decision, and the outside criticism is therefore confused rather than substantive. Both positions are doing damage in our meetings. I am fairly confident neither is correct, but I cannot articulate the middle position, and "it's complicated" is not a usable answer in a committee paper.

Please write that middle position as a decision procedure my officers can actually run on a disputed term. I want the questions in order, what counts as a sufficient answer to each, what sort of evidence is admissible at each stage, and where the procedure terminates — in one of three outcomes: the term is to be settled by stipulation and published explicitly as a stipulation; the term is to be settled by investigation, because something about the world bears on the choice; or the procedure cannot settle it and the case is escalated to the committee as a question of purpose, values, or who bears the costs. Ground it in what Quine's argument actually establishes and in whatever has survived it. My understanding is that his conclusion was that the boundary is not sharp rather than that there is nothing worth marking, and that there are later attempts to salvage a workable notion — I have been told one turns on separating the idea of a sentence's being true in virtue of meaning from the idea of its being justifiable merely in virtue of understanding that meaning, and that another line of reply points to the way disagreement persists across an entire family of related belief pairs. Use what is usable, discard what is not, and tell me plainly which philosophical distinctions you are preserving, which you are dropping as no help to a non-specialist, and at which steps the procedure is a judgment call wearing the costume of a rule. Give me the small working vocabulary the procedure needs — my officers should not have to use the word "analytic" correctly in order to apply it — and a section on failure modes: what kind of case the procedure will systematically get wrong, in which direction, and how I would detect that it has.

Then run it on three cases we are actually carrying. First, a proposal to widen our count of the unemployed to include people who have stopped looking for work but who tell interviewers they want a job and would start one; our series has excluded them for forty years, and my instinct is to reject the proposal as tampering with comparability, so if the procedure reaches a different verdict I need to be shown exactly which step produces it and what weight comparability is allowed to carry against it. Second, a ministry that has asked us to identify the scientifically correct gestational-age threshold separating miscarriage from stillbirth, noting helpfully that neighbouring jurisdictions use different ones; I need the procedure to say plainly, in language a minister will accept, that the request as framed contains a mistake, while also refusing the conclusion that any threshold is as good as any other and specifying what would make one better than another for a stated purpose. Third, a standing argument between two regional offices over whether a particular kind of peri-urban settlement counts as rural, where I suspect but cannot demonstrate that the two offices agree on every fact about the settlements in question and are only attaching different labels to them; if there is a cheap diagnostic for that suspicion — some way of substituting the disputed term out and seeing whether the disagreement survives — build it in as a stage and apply it. Finally, tell me what the procedure cannot do for me: what it looks like when it produces a properly completed form and a bad recommendation, and why a clean run through the stages should not be treated as evidence that the recommendation will survive the committee.
