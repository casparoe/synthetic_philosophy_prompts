I am in the fourth year of a PhD in moral psychology and am, for the first time, the instructor of record for an upper-level undergraduate seminar, "The Psychology of Moral Disagreement." Enrollment is eighteen; every student has completed one statistics course and one course in either ethics or cognitive science. I need to set a single short-answer question for the in-class midterm: forty minutes, twenty marks, closed book, no materials of any kind permitted. The topic is moral foundations theory, and more precisely the question of whether the much-rehearsed differences in how political liberals and conservatives distribute their moral concerns across the various foundations carry any normative weight — whether, that is, they tell us anything at all about which side is right.

Two kinds of student performance must fail. First, a fluent recitation of the theory — naming the foundations, reporting that liberals lean mainly on care and fairness while conservatives draw on all of them more evenly, perhaps noting that the framework was later extended to accommodate liberty and oppression — should earn almost nothing, because it does not engage the question. Second, an answer that invokes the is/ought distinction, declares that no descriptive finding can bear on a normative conclusion, and then stops should also earn almost nothing. The crux I want the question to isolate is the territory between those two: what bridge premise, if any, could license an inference from a distribution of moral psychologies to a verdict about which morality is more accurate, and whether such a premise can be defended without already presupposing the verdict it is supposed to deliver.

To make sure students cannot answer from memory, the stem should present three stipulated studies and require them to reason about the evidence in front of them rather than retrieve findings. Please build the following into the stem, adjusting the wording as needed but keeping the substance intact.

Study 1: A survey administered in twenty-four countries finds that in every one of them, harm-based and fairness-based considerations predict condemnation of a described act more strongly than considerations of loyalty, authority, or purity. In every one of them, respondents placing themselves on the political right endorse all five kinds of consideration more evenly than respondents on the left, who concentrate on harm and fairness. The pattern holds both within societies and across them.

Study 2: The same instrument is re-administered with only the fairness items rewritten. When fairness is operationalized as proportionality of reward to contribution, the left–right difference on fairness is at its largest; when it is operationalized as equality of outcome, that difference disappears entirely. Nothing else in the instrument is altered.

Study 3: Participants are presented with purity violations stipulated to involve no identifiable victim and no plausible harm to anyone. A substantial proportion nevertheless condemn the acts as morally wrong. Asked to justify the condemnation, they can offer nothing beyond the act's being degrading, revolting, or unnatural; a minority, having exhausted every reason they can think of and been shown that the reason does not apply, refuse to withdraw the judgment. Disgust-sensitivity scores predict condemnation here even after controlling for religiosity and political orientation.

The question proper should then ask students to do something like the following: taking Studies 1–3 as given, state exactly what further premise or premises would be required for the asymmetry in Study 1 to count as evidence that one moral profile is more accurate than the other; formulate the most defensible such premise you can; and say what consideration, empirical or philosophical, would undermine it. You are welcome to reformulate my phrasing, so long as the demand remains that specific.

Please deliver three things. (a) The question stem as students will see it, including the three studies, sized to be read in about five minutes of the forty. (b) A model answer of roughly 700 words. (c) A marking scheme out of 20 organized around five to seven must-hit moves, with the marks each carries and a one-line note on how students in a course like mine typically botch it. The scheme should say explicitly what separates an A answer from a B answer; my expectation is that B answers here will identify a needed bridge premise but leave it undefended, or will observe that the genetic fallacy blocks the debunking reading of Study 3 without noticing that it blocks the vindicating reading of Study 1 just as surely.

At least three entries in the marking scheme should concern mistakes specific to the stipulated studies — for instance, treating the disappearance of the fairness gap in Study 2 as a mere measurement nuisance rather than as a threat to the claim that the foundations are genuinely distinct constructs rather than facets of a single disposition plus item content; treating the failure to articulate reasons in Study 3 as showing that purity judgments are not moral judgments, rather than as showing only that such judgments need not issue from articulable inference; and reading Study 1's cross-national regularity as either establishing that the foundations track real moral properties or establishing that they are evolutionary artifacts, when both readings require precisely the premise the question is after.

One constraint on everything you write: no citations, no references, no named researchers, in either the stem or the model answer. My students will have nothing in front of them, and I want no part of the assessment to turn on remembering who said what.
