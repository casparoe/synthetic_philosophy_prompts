I am in my fourth year of a philosophy PhD, writing on the ethics of assertion, and I have been asked to contribute a short review essay to our department's graduate journal on the lying/misleading distinction. A friend who works on legal theory sent me, as background reading, a piece from a general-interest magazine that he thought was unusually good on the topic, and I now cannot decide whether to cite it approvingly, cite it as a foil, or ignore it. The relevant passage runs roughly as follows; I am reconstructing it, so please treat the wording as mine and the substance as his.

"Philosophers distinguish lying from misleading. You lie when you assert something you believe to be false; you merely mislead when you say something you believe to be true while arranging matters so that your hearer draws a false conclusion from it. The distinction is real, and it marks a difference in kind rather than of degree, since in the second case you have said nothing false at all. It is also of no moral use whatever, and the reason is built into the account just given. What you have asserted is not fixed by your words but by the pragmatics of the exchange — by what a reasonable hearer takes you to be committing yourself to. So a sufficiently articulate speaker can always arrange his assertions to be true and his implicatures to be false, which means that lying, on this account, turns out to be a matter of grammar: an occupational hazard for the inarticulate and a loophole for everyone else. If the wrong of lying is that you get your hearer to believe something false, misleading gets him to believe the same thing by the same route, and the distinction collapses. The law has quietly conceded the point, since perjury and fraud alike turn on whether the hearer was left with a false impression rather than on whether the sentence uttered was literally accurate. Even Kant, who banned lying absolutely, is usually taken to have conceded it, because what he prohibits is untruthful declaration, and a literally true answer that sends the murderer to the wrong house is not one."

The case the piece has in mind is the familiar one: someone hiding a persecuted friend is asked by an officer at the door where the friend is, and either says that the friend is not in the house, which is false, or says that he watched the friend leave by the back gate twenty minutes earlier, which is true, since the friend did leave that way and then came back inside. The author concludes that because both answers leave the officer with the same false belief and are delivered with the same intention, the second is as blameworthy as the first, and that anyone who thinks otherwise is supplying cover for the eloquent. He also remarks, without citing anything, that when psychologists put such pairs to ordinary people the two are rated about equally harshly once the consequences are held fixed.
