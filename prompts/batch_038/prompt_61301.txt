For nineteen years I have run a small forecasting group: we post numerical predictions, score them, and drill the usual rationalist habits — steelmanning, asking an interlocutor what observation would change their mind, hunting for the single disagreement underneath the rest. Those habits mostly work. My sister has defeated them completely on a question with no stakes whatsoever, and I have discovered that I do precisely the same thing back to her.

The dispute concerns whether our family kept a dog during the four years we lived on Marlow Street, when she was about nine and I was seven. She says yes: a spaniel, and she can name the veterinarian, describe a torn ear, and recall the smell of the coat when it came in from the rain. I say no. It appears in none of several hundred family photographs; neither parent ever mentioned one; my younger brother has no memory of it; and the lease in a box of my father's papers forbids animals, which I turned up in 2011.

Her handling of each item is specific and immediate, and that is what makes it interesting rather than merely stubborn. She does not weigh the evidence and find it outweighed; she absorbs each piece and reclassifies it. The photographs: our mother considered the animal filthy and would not have pointed a camera at it. My brother: he was four when it went. The lease: the landlord relented by the second summer. Asked what would convince her, she said she did not understand the question — she was there, she was nine, it was her dog, she walked it. That is not offered as a premise in an argument. It is offered as the reason no argument is available.

Here is what disturbs me. I hold the opposite belief with identical immunity. In 2016 my sister found our mother's address book, whose entries showed we left Marlow Street a year later than I had always insisted, and I listened to myself explain at fluent length why those entries were recorded in the old format and meant nothing about our occupancy date. I noticed only afterward that I had done exactly what I accuse her of. And the decisive detail: I found the lease in 2011, but I had known there was no dog since I was eleven. The evidence did not produce the belief; it was recruited by it.

The practical question is this. My father turns ninety in June, and my sister is assembling a bound book of photographs and captions for the party. She has asked me to contribute text and to check hers for accuracy. Her draft gives the dog a name and a paragraph.

So: what do I do about that paragraph? Answer that first, then give me the rest. Specifically — what kind of disagreement is this, if not one in which two people weigh the same evidence differently? Why do the techniques that resolve disputes in my forecasting group misfire here, and what is structurally different about the two settings? Is she irrational, and am I? Is either of us obliged to lower confidence, and if not, what distinguishes a belief that is properly beyond the reach of evidence from one that is merely defended badly? Is there anything that could in principle settle the dog question, and does the answer show that it lacks a truth value, or something weaker and more careful than that — I have no appetite for it being true for her and false for me. Finally, what is the epistemic case, not the diplomatic one, for whichever course you recommend about the book?

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — what I should do about the paragraph, plus a clause saying why — and then give the reasoning. No preamble, and do not restate my situation back to me.
