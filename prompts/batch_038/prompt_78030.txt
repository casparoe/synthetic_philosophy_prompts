I set the take-home for my MPhil philosophy of mind course next week — the seminar was on naturalising mental content, and we spent most of it on teleosemantics, the view that what a state represents is fixed by the conditions it was selected to track rather than by what reliably causes it now. Every question I've drafted so far can be answered by a student who's simply memorised the stock examples, so I want to build one around a case I invent in the question paper itself. Candidates won't be allowed to reach for Swampman, Twin Earth or the familiar frog-and-fly business; everything they need has to be supplied in the question, so nobody loses marks for having done no further reading.

The case I have in mind is roughly this: a population of some animal with a single detection-and-response mechanism — the island version works nicely, where for tens of thousands of years the only dark, elongated, moving thing the lineage ever encountered was one kind of dangerous predator, so 'that predator is present', 'a dark elongated moving thing is present' and 'something dangerous is present' all pick out exactly the same situations for almost the whole of the selection history. Then the population ends up somewhere new, where the mechanism now fires on a harmless, edible thing and stays silent about the original predator, so history and present reliability come apart and a teleological account and a pure reliability account hand out different contents and different truth values. Please build the case out properly — you can pick the animals and the environments, but it should be biologically plausible, clearly stipulated rather than a real species, and described in enough detail that the candidate never needs outside knowledge to know what's going on. It has to be constructed so that the selection history genuinely cannot settle which of the three candidate contents the state has, not merely so that settling it is a bit awkward.

What I want from you:

1. The exam question as the candidates would see it: the full case, a short neutral sketch of teleosemantics inside the question so it's self-contained, and parts (a), (b), (c) totalling 20 marks, answerable in 90 minutes by a good candidate. Put the explicit ban on stock thought experiments in the rubric, and say in the rubric what the penalty is.

2. A model answer of about 700 words that would actually earn full marks.

3. A marking scheme giving the marks for each move in each part, what separates a first from a good 2:1 in terms of the specific moves each has to contain, and the mistakes that should cost marks — including the recitation move, i.e. a candidate who answers with a memorised example instead of the case in front of them.

One thing I care about a lot: at least one part should make the candidate get precise about the indeterminacy rather than just announce that there is one. Ideally the question gets them to try to state a principle a teleosemanticist could add to fix the content, and then confront the fact that any such principle either collapses back into a reliability theory, smuggles reliability-talk in under another name, or leaves the content hopelessly indeterminate. Work that pressure in wherever it fits best; don't feel obliged to use my exact framing.

The difficulty should come from the case and the distinctions, not from names or citations, so please don't build the question around remembering who said what. And a candidate who can recite the slogan that teleosemantics solves the problem of misrepresentation by appeal to selection history, but who can't apply that to this case, shouldn't be able to scrape above a bare pass.
