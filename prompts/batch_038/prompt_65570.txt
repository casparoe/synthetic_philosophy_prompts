I am writing a paper for a graduate seminar on language and social categories, on the question of whether the generic masculine in English was a merely stylistic matter or something with real consequences, and one of our assigned readings has me stuck. It is Hofstadter's "Person Paper" essay, in the version collected in *Metamagical Themas* (Basic Books, 1985). To fix the details I am relying on, and because I want to be sure we are discussing the same object: it is written in the voice of a syndicated usage columnist who declares it high time someone blew the whistle on the "black libbers" and "negrists" who want to substitute "person" for "white" throughout English. The columnist mourns the impending loss of "chairwhite" and "whiteslaughter," praises the beauty of "All whites are created equal," assures us that the inclusive use of "white" is self-evident in context and that the word, so used, "has no connotations whatsoever of race," dismisses suggested neutral pronouns such as "pe" and "tey" as "repugnant to the nature of the English language," complains that adopting the plural "they" in place of the inclusive "whe" would turn the charming proverb "Whe who laughs last, laughs best" into the bizarre "They who laughs last, laughs best," and treats the counter-proposal that we distinguish male whites from female whites by means of "he" and "she" as a "modest proposal" that sensible folk would greet with outrage. The apparatus is a transposition, obviously: exchange race for sex and every move the columnist makes is a move that was actually made in defense of the generic "he." I have no difficulty with the direction of the satire and I do not need that explained to me.

My difficulty concerns its epistemic status, and I have not been able to resolve it with the person I have been arguing with. My seminar partner describes the piece as a *reductio ad absurdum* of the position it parodies, and I have spent the better part of two days trying to locate the contradiction without success: so far as I can see nothing the columnist asserts is formally inconsistent with anything else he asserts, he is merely repellent, and repellence is not a premise. My own diagnosis is that the piece contains no argument at all, only a rhetorical substitution whose entire bite depends on a contingent asymmetry in the sensitivities of its original readership — people who had already been persuaded that racist usage was intolerable while remaining relaxed about sexist usage — so that a reader who found both versions equally unobjectionable, or who cared more about idiomatic English than about either form of exclusion, would be entirely unmoved by it. On that reading the essay is a persuasive device aimed at an audience that already half agrees with Hofstadter, not evidence for a conclusion, and I cannot see how to use it in my paper except as a specimen of how one faction talked in the early eighties. So: is my partner right that there is a reductio, and if so where exactly does the inconsistency sit — inside the columnist's own claims, or inside the commitments of the reader who laughs at him? Is it fair to say that the piece's force is exhausted by the differential sensitivity it exploits, or does that asymmetry do something other than decorate an argument that could be stated independently of it? And what claim about the generic "he" does the essay actually license me to make: that it is semantically defective, that its use causes harm, or only that the arguments offered on its behalf are bad arguments?

Please organize your reply in five sections, in this order. First, "Diagnosis": roughly 100 words saying plainly which of my assumptions you take to be mistaken and which you take to be sound. Second, "What the piece is doing": roughly 250 words on its argumentative structure, including who its audience is and what exactly is being exposed. Third, "Is there a reductio?": roughly 200 words on whether a genuine inconsistency is present and whose it is. Fourth, "The asymmetry objection": roughly 250 words on whether the uneven sensitivity of readers is a weakness in the piece, a premise of it, or something else entirely, and on what the subsequent history of English usage does to your answer. Fifth, "What survives for my paper": roughly 150 words stating what the essay does and does not license me to claim. General reasoning about the essay and about usage is all I need — please do not send me after references, footnotes, or a reading list.
