For two years I served as minute-taker to the acquisitions committee of a mid-sized municipal museum, a body of seven that meets quarterly. Its remit includes a threshold question that is asked before any discussion of price, storage, or donor relations: whether the thing under consideration is a work of art at all, and therefore eligible for the collection. The minutes are terse, but I have a complete record of the seven threshold votes from my period of service, together with the remarks members made while voting.

1. A rectangle of identical factory-made concrete blocks laid out on the gallery floor by an artist who neither manufactured nor altered a single one. Acquired; what was acquired was described as the arrangement, together with a certificate and an installation photograph.
2. A landscape of considerable technical accomplishment, painted on commission as a demonstration panel for a house-paint manufacturer's trade catalogue, showing how the firm's pigments weathered. Declined. One member's gloss, as I recorded it, was that it was beautifully made but had nothing to say.
3. A six-year project by a neighborhood collective: a working food garden with a rotating volunteer schedule, workshops, and a seed library. Acquired as a time-based work, the accessioned object being a written score, an archive of correspondence, and photographs.
4. A single typed sheet of instructions for a wall drawing, never executed by anyone, sold with a signed certificate authorizing any future execution. Acquired.
5. A drawing by a five-year-old, lively and unusually sophisticated in its handling of color, found loose in a box of papers donated by the child's grandmother, a local patron. Declined, over the dissent of two members who thought it delightful.
6. A copy of a canonical painting in our own collection, made three decades earlier by a forger who intended it to be hung on a wall and studied as the original. Acquired after the deception collapsed and the forger had died; the label treats it as a work about the appetite for authenticity. There was an objection that the maker's aim had been fraud rather than art, and it did not prevail.
7. A certificate of ownership in a series of tokens, issued by a well-known artist with substantial market backing, accompanied by no object, image, text, score, or instruction; the work was said to consist in the recorded chain of its successive owners. Declined. The minutes record the chair saying that there was nothing in it to look at, and nothing in it to look at in.

Next month the committee faces a proposal that has already split it by email. In a shed behind a house two hundred kilometers from here, a former railway signalman — self-taught, no gallery visits to his name, no books on art in the house — spent forty years assembling roughly eleven hundred small mechanical constructions from scavenged parts: gearing, signal wire, bottle glass, clock movements. Each is numbered; each stood in a labeled row of shelving he built himself. A notebook survives in which one phrase recurs, to the effect that the constructions were for counting what he could not say. He showed them to nobody, sold none, and left written instructions that his family burn them at his death. They did not. Fifteen years on, his daughter has permitted a commercial gallery to photograph the entire body of work and offer it to us; the gallery has commissioned two critics to write about it and has a curator drafting a traveling exhibition. One committee member has written that the man's refusal renders the threshold question moot; another that his refusal is beside the point, since it would be our act and not his that made them art; a third that the constructions are so thoroughly absorbed in a self-made system of number and order that they do precisely what art does.

I want your help preparing a staff memo, and I would rather have the analysis than the answer. Please: (a) lay out the candidate rules that jointly fit all seven precedents — I have in mind something institutional, something about the maker's intention and its relation to a practice of art-making, something about what an object affords to attention, and something about whether a work is about anything, but I suspect there are further candidates, and I suspect the committee is not running any single one cleanly, so say where each candidate needs patching to survive the awkward precedent; (b) state what each candidate predicts about the assemblages, and mark exactly where they part company rather than merely where they differ in vocabulary; (c) give your best single prediction of the vote, with a rough confidence, and name which of the seven precedents is the closest analog and which is the resemblance most likely to mislead the committee into the wrong verdict; (d) tell me what principle the committee would be adopting either way — if it acquires, what it could no longer say the next time something like the five-year-old's drawing comes up; if it declines, what it would be giving up about the forger's copy; and (e) propose one further case, concrete enough that I could put it before them, that would discriminate between the two leading candidates better than the assemblages themselves do.
