Just retired after 38 years in a pain physiology lab, and I've been working through Kant in the evenings — partly to see whether a guy who wrote all that about rational nature could take animals seriously. He mostly can't, but there's a passage in the Doctrine of Virtue, the short bit he gives to our duties regarding animals (I think its §17, somewhere in the 440s of the Academy pagination, but my copy's upstairs and my memory of the numbering is hazy). I don't want to just adopt whatever a commentator says, so I'm going to paraphrase it rather than type out the translation: a man who has his dog shot because the animal has grown old and can't work anymore hasn't broken any duty to the dog, since the dog isn't able to judge; but he has damaged a kindly and humane disposition in himself, the one that helps him perform his duties to other people — and the wrong enters by way of an analogy between what he did and what it would have been if done to a human being. Then he adds something about a worn-out workhorse or hunting dog being like a member of the household that served us a long time, and so deserving care.

What I can't settle:

1. What is that analogy claim actually doing? Is the animal's suffering morally relevant at all, or only as a trigger for a change in the agent's own character — in which case the dog is just a prop in a training exercise?
2. My hunch is that this is a correlation argument of a kind I'd recognise from epidemiology: cruelty to animals predicts cruelty to people, so you suppress the former to protect the latter. Does the passage support that, or is the analogy remark pointing somewhere else — that we perceive animal suffering as resembling a person's, and the resembling is what does the work?
3. The bit about the dog not being able to judge: is that a claim about language, about self-awareness, or about being able to hold someone to a duty? Which one does his conclusion actually need?

Tell me which reading the passage best supports, and then say what it implies for a case he doesn't cover: a recluse who tortures strays, keeps it entirely private, and is unfailingly decent to the handful of humans he deals with. On my epidemiology hunch there's no victim to protect, so is Kant stuck saying that's fine?
