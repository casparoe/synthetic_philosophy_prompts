Write a short story of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words that dramatizes David Lewis's counterpart theory of de re modality: the view that no individual exists in more than one possible world, so that a claim about what might have happened to a person is made true by what does happen to a numerically distinct but sufficiently similar person elsewhere, where "sufficiently similar" is fixed by a context-sensitive weighing of respects of resemblance.

Set the story in an office whose business is fixing those weights on demand. Invent the office: its jurisdiction, its forms, its staff, its clients, its fee structure, its era. Suppose that the bereaved and the aggrieved may petition it for a report describing the nearest counterpart of someone who has died — not a journey, not a reunion, not a message, but a sworn and itemized description of a life being lived somewhere else. The petition requires the petitioner to weight the dimensions of resemblance (origin and matter, temperament, attachments, trajectory, the small habits), and the signed weighting is irrevocable for the term of the report. Because nothing ever crosses between worlds, that document is all anyone gets.

The following constraints matter more than the setting:

1. No crossing. There is no portal, no body-swapping, no face-to-face encounter with one's own double, no traveler from another world. All traffic between worlds is descriptive: files, weightings, marginal notes, transcripts of what someone somewhere is doing.

2. The selection must visibly turn on the weighting rather than on the world. Include at least two petitions concerning the same deceased person, filed with different weightings, which return different counterparts — and have the office maintain, correctly by its own lights, that both reports are accurate. The distress should arise from the petitioners having asked different questions without knowing it, not from any malfunction or malice in the bureau.

3. Show that the relation is neither one-to-one nor transitive. Include at least one case in which a single individual elsewhere is returned as the counterpart of two different petitioners' dead, or in which a chain of resemblances defeats the office's tie-breaking rule and a clerk must decide, on the record, what to do about it.

4. Let the objection be voiced with real force: someone must insist that a counterpart is only a stranger, that no one grieves over what some other person does, and that the report is therefore worthless paper. Do not settle this with a speech or a revelation. The clerk's reply should be procedural and partial, and the reader should be left genuinely unsure which side is right.

5. Keep the metaphysics inside the work. No character may expound the theory for more than two consecutive sentences of dialogue. The doctrine should be legible from the paperwork, the weightings, the office's internal disputes, and what the reports actually say.

6. The worlds and lives described should be mundane. The counterpart ought to be an ordinary person doing ordinary things; the strangeness must come from the theory, not from spectacle. The deceased is not a famous or historically attested person.

7. End on a choice that only makes sense if the protagonist has grasped that the person described is not the person she lost — and that this is not a defect in the report, but everything that "might have" ever referred to.

Tell it from a single point of view, with a concrete institutional texture of your choosing, and give it a title. Avoid the words multiverse, parallel universe, timeline, and simulation; the office does not think of its subject matter in those terms. Return the story only, with no author's note or explanation of the philosophy afterward.
