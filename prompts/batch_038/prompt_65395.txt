Right — I write about science and ideas for a UK broadsheet, mostly genetics and the philosophy-of-science-ish end of things, plus the odd longer piece for the weekend mag. I've got a big feature coming up on whether DNA is a code, and after my last one got properly taken apart by a philosopher of biology on Bluesky, my editor has made me write down what I actually believe before I file. So here's the list. I want to know whether it hangs together or whether I'm fooling myself — an audit, not a lecture, please; I've read enough of the background to know the shape of the debate already.

1. The genetic code is a code literally, not as a figure of speech. There's a mapping from codons to amino acids, there's machinery that actually reads it, and the mapping is arbitrary in something like the way Morse is arbitrary — which is part of why I think it earns the word.
2. DNA does not contain a message. No sender, no receiver, nothing the genome is about, and 'the book of life' is marketing copy rather than a claim.
3. I'm not interested in rescuing that about-ness. The people who try to naturalise it — cash out informational talk in terms of what selection designed a bit of the system to signal — look to me like they're bolting machinery on to save a metaphor. So: no semantics in the cell, full stop.
4. DNA is nonetheless causally special. Change one base, you can change one amino acid. There's no arrangement of temperature, nutrition or maternal provisioning with that sort of fine-grained, sequence-specific causal grip, and I say so in the piece.
5. But development isn't the execution of a program, and I spend a lot of column inches on that. The cell does most of the work; the genome is a resource you build with, not a set of instructions you follow. I want to be loud about blueprint talk being wrong.
6. A gene, for me, is just a stretch of DNA that makes a difference to some trait in some context. Difference-maker, that's all.
7. I write about mutations as typos constantly — misprints, slips, the text should have said this, editing, proofreading. I don't think that's smuggling anything in, because everybody knows what I mean and its not a claim about meaning.
8. I tell readers, flatly, that there's no such thing as a gene for anything. Though I'll admit I've signed off headlines saying 'the breast cancer gene' without blinking.
9. I lean hard on the fact that the code isn't universal — mitochondria read a couple of codons differently from the nucleus — as evidence for the arbitrariness in (1).

Which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which pairs only look like they do? I ran the list past someone who said 'you can't have it both ways' and then wouldn't say which way, which is maddening. Seperately, I've been losing a bit of sleep over (4) and (6): is 'causally special' just 'difference-maker' said twice, i.e. am I padding the list?

Say what would have to give in each real tension, rank them by severity rather than treating everything as equally dodgy, and tell me what each fix would cost me as a writer — which lines and which rhetorical habits I'd have to drop. Then: which single change buys me the most coherence for the least damage?
