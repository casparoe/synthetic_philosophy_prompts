So my brother Tom's leaving do is on Friday at the working men's club and the family have decided I'm giving the toast. He spent eleven years at a worker co-operative that builds and services industrial bakery equipmant — collectively owned, one member one vote, pay banded in a narrow ratio, the whole thing. He's off to be a mid-level operations manager at an enormous cold-chain logistics firm, where he reckons he'll be one of about nine hundred people doing exactly his job.

He asked me not to turn the speech into an argument about any of this, but he did tell me the reason for going that actually made him go: the co-op was never the escape from Marx's account of alienation that most people in it liked to believe. The market kept making them do things nobody there wanted — bidding for contracts they'd have to cut corners on to honour, driving each other harder than any manager would have dared, selling ovens to chains that were squeezing their own customers out of business. His line was that the difference was only that you could see the whip coming. He isn't bitter about the place and neither am I, but he would hate a toast that treated the co-op years as the free ones and the new job as the cage, because he didnt believe that and never did.

Room will be about forty: his old colleagues from the co-op, several of whom are pretty serious about all this and will be listening hard for whether I'm taking the piss out of them; a handful of the new workmates who've turned up, who have never read a word of Marx and will be lost if it gets abstract; and the family, who mainly want the speeches over so they can eat.

Write the toast for me. Four minutes spoken, so roughly 550 words. It has to be warm and genuinely funny and it has to be something all three groups can enjoy rather than tolerate, but the Marx has to do structural work in it, not sit there as garnish: the whole shape of the thing should turn on his point that alienation isn't about who signs your payslip or whether you get a vote at the AGM, it's about being moved by arrangements you didn't choose into treating your own activity — and the people alongside you, and the thing you're making — as so many instruments. No lecture, no sneering at the new job, no sentimentalising the old one, and I don't want it to end with everyone agreeing about anything, because they won't. British English please, he'd notice.
