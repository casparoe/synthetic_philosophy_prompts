I am a graduate student in a seminar on preference learning, and for our final project my group is recovering a reward function from a single human demonstrator's recorded behavior. The written justification is graded specifically on how well it distinguishes competing formalizations of the inference target, so I would like your ranking of five candidates against our particular corpus.

The corpus comes from one trainee at a logistics firm, recorded across a twelve-week certification program on a piece of heavy material-handling equipment. Two features complicate the analysis. First, the labels are heterogeneous in time: for weeks one through three the trainee annotated their own recorded maneuvers retrospectively, at the end of the program, while for weeks four through twelve the annotations were made contemporaneously, within minutes of each maneuver. The two label sets disagree systematically — the early maneuvers receive markedly harsher scores when judged after the fact than when judged at the time. Second, the trainee's stated objective shifted partway through: they entered the program aiming to maximize throughput, and by week nine described themselves as prioritizing safety margins and equipment longevity, adding that the week-two version of themselves had been trying to prove something to the instructors. Our deliverable policy will be used to generate coaching feedback for future trainees rather than to imitate this trainee.

The five candidate targets are:

1. The utility function that best rationalizes the observed choice frequencies, as in maximum-entropy formulations of inverse reinforcement learning, with all demonstrations treated as equally evidential.
2. The reward that would rationalize the choices if the demonstrator had known the actual consequences and likelihoods of each maneuver — which, early on, they did not, particularly regarding how the machine responded to shifting loads.
3. The reward that the demonstrator's computationally limited policy was approximately optimizing, where early suboptimality is attributed to planning depth and execution cost rather than to different values.
4. The reward endorsed by the demonstrator's own deliberated second-order preferences, even where it contradicts all three of the above — roughly a Frankfurtian hierarchy.
5. The reward function that best predicts held-out future behavior, with no claim that it describes anything the demonstrator actually values.

Please rank all five from best to worst for our project, assigning each a score out of ten, and keep two criteria visibly separate in your justification: identifiability, meaning how much of the target can actually be pinned down from the data we possess (behavior plus self-report), and how badly the retrospective-versus-contemporaneous discrepancy threatens that; and fitness for use, meaning whether the recovered reward is one a future trainee would accept as a legitimate standard for feedback. In doing so, indicate which of the candidates are genuinely distinct under our data and which collapse into observational equivalence.

Then state which single candidate you would adopt and which you would retain only as a baseline, and describe one concrete additional data collection we could plausibly request from the firm that would most change the ranking, together with the direction of the change.

Please write your response in American English.
