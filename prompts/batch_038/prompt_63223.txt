I attend a Sunday forecasting circle where about eight of us calibrate predictions and then argue afterwards about why our numbers differed. Last week the argument went technical and I could not follow enough of it to judge who was winning, so I have reconstructed it as carefully as I can. The case under discussion: a weld inspector on a production line glances at a seam under bad lighting and reports herself as 0.75 confident that the seam looked cracked to her and 0.25 confident that it looked sound. We wanted to know how her credence that the weld is in fact defective should move in light of that glance. (1) Nadia said the case is misconceived before it starts. Experience is self-intimating: the inspector either had an experience as of a cracked seam or she did not, and she should assign probability 1 to a proposition saying exactly which, then conditionalize in the ordinary way. Her 0.75 and 0.25, on this telling, are not probabilities about her experience at all but smuggled-in estimates of how reliable that experience is — so Jeffrey conditionalization is an elaborate apparatus for a problem that vanishes once the evidence is described correctly. (2) Owen objected on two grounds. First, the inspector may possess no concept fine-grained enough to pick out what her glance actually presented: the impression may have been genuinely muddy, and forcing it into one of two boxes at probability 1 manufactures a determinacy that was never there. Second, certainty is expensive — once she assigns 1 to its having looked cracked, nothing she learns later can lower it, not the glare, not her fatigue, not the film of grime on her visor; whereas an update that leaves the 0.75 in place keeps that number available for revision once she finds out about the lighting. (3) Nadia answered that this concedes her real point. To report 0.75 that it looked cracked is already to have applied some reliability assessment to a seeming, which is to say it is a judgment, not a raw deliverance; and if the input to the rule is a judgment, then the rule governs only what happens after the one step that matters has already been taken, and says nothing whatever about how to take it. (4) Owen said the rigidity assumption is doing substantive work: once the numbers on the partition are fixed, everything downstream is determined, and determined in a way ordinary conditionalization cannot determine it, because there is no single proposition to conditionalize on; the alternative to having a rule here is not a better rule but arbitrariness.

(5) Nadia pressed twice more. First, if the 0.75 is not a judgment it must be a direct registration, and a direct registration is not the sort of thing that can be right or wrong, assessed or criticized — so an epistemology that makes the crucial step unassessable is not an epistemology but a description of how we happen to be built. Second, and what she thought decisive: conditionalizing on two certain items of evidence gives the same result whichever order you feed them in, whereas two successive Jeffrey updates on different partitions generally do not commute, so the theory's output depends on the order in which you happen to describe what is, by hypothesis, one and the same body of total evidence. A rule whose verdicts turn on the order of description is bookkeeping, not rationality. (6) Owen replied that being assessable and being inferential are different things, that our own scoring records show people calibrating such registrations reasonably well without any evident reasoning, and that on the second point non-commutation only bites when the two updates are not both rigid — that is, when you have mis-described your evidence — so in a properly described case there is no single total evidence available in two orders for Nadia to complain about. He admitted he could not show this last claim rather than assert it. (7) Nadia's parting shot was that if a bare glance can move credence without making anything certain, the inspector can pump her confidence in a defective weld by glancing at the same seam over and over, which is precisely the free evidence a rule of updating exists to forbid; Owen said the glances are not independent and that the second glance's distribution already absorbs the first, but conceded he could not say what, on his view, constrains the sequence.

Please work through moves 1 to 7 in order and say who is right at each one, not merely who comes out ahead overall; where a move is part right, separate the part that succeeds from the part that does not. Three things I would particularly like settled: whether Nadia's redescribing strategy really dissolves the phenomenon Owen is pointing at or only some versions of it — note that her move 1 and her move 5b seem to pull in different directions about whether certainty is available or merely desirable; whether her charge that the rule is normatively empty is a serious objection or a demand that an updating rule do something no such rule can do, given that ordinary conditionalization is equally silent on which propositions one comes to be certain of; and whether the order-dependence point is a genuine reductio or, as Owen suggests, a symptom of mis-description that he has not earned the right to wave away. Then tell me what both of them missed. My suspicion, which I could not articulate on the day, is that the argument stalled because two separable questions got run together — one about whether the inputs to updating must be extreme, another about whether certainty is ever attainable or advisable — and that once they are pulled apart several of the moves change hands. I am not looking for references or a survey of the literature; I want the reasoning, and I want you to be willing to say when one of them simply made a mistake.
