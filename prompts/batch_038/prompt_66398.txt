I cover science and ideas for a magazine and I'm three weeks into a piece built on a set of 1950s city hospital admission registers where a whole category of patient never appears — no seperate column, no diagnosis code, nothing. The only trace of those people I've found is in the ledgers of the county that paid for their care, as line items with no names attached. My editor wants the story to say something better than "the archive is biased," and before I write a word I want the spec.

Act as a philosopher of history who also genuinely understands the statistics of missing data. Think it through carefully — a little brainstorming out loud before the list is fine — then give me the desiderata: what any adequate account of an archival silence has to deliver to be usable by a reporter who has to say something defensible in print. For each requirement, the case or consideration behind it. I especially want to know which kinds of silence it has to keep apart (never recorded vs. recorded and destroyed vs. preserved but unindexed vs. recorded under someone else's category), when a gap licenses an inference and when it doesn't, what it has to be consistent with about selection effects and missing-not-at-random data, and whether it can avoid turning every archive into a deliberate instrument of power. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are just camp preferences, and tell me which single one knocks out the most accounts. Then briefly: how the three obvious stances — silence as produced by power, silence as bureaucratic routine, silence as ordinary statistical missingness — do against the list. No bibliography or citations, I'll handle that. And if two of the desiderata can't both be met, say so — that's the part I most want.
