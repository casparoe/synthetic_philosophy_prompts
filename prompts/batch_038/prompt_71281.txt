Most major news organizations will not print a racial slur in full, even when it occurs inside a direct quotation from a court transcript, a leaked recording, or a historical document; they substitute a masked form or a description of the word. Practice varies at the edges — some will print it when a public figure's utterance is itself the story — but the baseline prohibition is stable, and so is a striking asymmetry: the same organizations print unmasked, and in vivid detail, the content the slur encodes, together with descriptions of atrocities far worse than the word, and they will print the slur in full when the article is about the word.

What account of meaning does the masking norm presuppose? If the harm travels with the content, why is paraphrase of that content permitted while the word is banned? If it travels with the word-form, what kind of thing must a word be for a mere token — quoted, attributed to someone else, used to report rather than to assert — to carry the force the norm credits it with? Reconstruct the implicit view, separating what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with; say which standard accounts of slurs it quietly rules out; judge whether the exception for metalinguistic reporting is consistent with the rule or reveals that what is being tracked is hearer response rather than semantics; and describe what the practice would have to look like if newsrooms adopted the view that a slur and its neutral counterpart have exactly the same truth-conditional content.
