I'm in a reading group arguing about sortition and one guy keeps making what I've started calling the jury transfer argument. His version: we already hand enormous power to randomly selected strangers -- a jury can put someone away for life -- and nobody thinks that's crazy, because lottery selection filters out the ambitious and the captured, a dozen people average out each other's ignorance, and crucially the judge hands them the question and the law while the two sides hand them the facts. So a legislature, he says, is just a jury with a bigger docket: if you trust twelve you should trust four hundred. The only real difference is that legislators pick their own question, which he calls the docket problem and waves off as a design detail you can fix with rules of order. He's using all this to argue for a second chamber chosen by lottery that gets to amend and veto but not to originate bills.

How far does the jury analogy actually hold? I want the correspondences spelled out -- what in a parliament plays the role of the judge, the adversarial presentation, the burden of proof, the verdict, the appeal, the voir dire -- and then where the mapping just stops. Then rank the disanalogies for me: which ones genuinely sink the transfer (the docket thing? the fact that a jury applies a settled rule to one bounded case rather than making the rule that will govern unbounded future cases?) versus which ones are superficial and shouldn't move anyone (no pay, no reelection, short service, no need to build coalitions). What is the analogy smuggling in that the target doesn't have -- is it sneaking in the idea that legitimacy comes from being statistically typical, or that averaging mechanically produces competence, or that every pathology in real legislatures is campaign-driven and therefore disappears once you remove campaigns? And does his conclusion survive the audit: even if the jury analogy can't carry a whole parliament, might it still carry the much weaker claim about a veto-only sortitioned chamber? Finally, if it's just the wrong source domain, tell me what a better one would be -- I keep half-thinking it's a grand jury, or a peer review panel, or a quality-control sample, and I can't work out whether any of those are actually improvements or are broken in the same places.
