I am a novelist, and I have no training in philosophy of mind. What I know of it comes from a single long magazine article I read years ago and have been chewing on ever since, plus whatever I have picked up incidentally from editing my own drafts. The article described an experiment I have thought about almost weekly, and I would like your help placing it inside a vocabulary I actually command, because I suspect my own working habits have been staging a version of the argument for thirty years without my knowing it.

First, the experiment, as best I can restate it. Two lights are flashed in quick succession at different positions in the visual field — the first red, the second green, spatially displaced. Observers reliably report seeing a single light travel between the two positions and change colour somewhere near the midpoint of its path. But the information about the second light's colour does not exist until the second light flashes, which is after the midpoint has already been traversed. Two rival accounts were offered. On the first, called the Orwellian account, the observer's original experience was of a red light at one location; when the green light arrives, the record is edited afterwards, and what the observer reports is a revised memory rather than anything they ever underwent. On the second, called the Stalinesque account, conscious presentation is simply delayed: by the time the episode is delivered to the observer at all, the brain already has both pieces of information, so it constructs a moving, colour-changing light, and the report is faithful to an experience that genuinely happened — just late. The striking claim was that the two accounts can be made empirically indistinguishable, so that the disagreement depends on whether there is any fact of the matter about when an experience occurs.

Now my practice. When a late chapter contradicts an earlier one, I have two procedures available, and I keep them rigorously separate. I call the first a fix: I go back and alter the earlier pages before the book exists for anyone, so no reader ever meets the inconsistency; the earlier scene was composed all along with knowledge of the ending. I call the second a turn: I leave the earlier pages exactly as they stand and let the later revelation overwrite what the reader remembers having read, so that page forty becomes a different page at page three hundred without a word on it changing. My hunch, which I would like you to test rather than confirm, is that the fix is Stalinesque and the turn is Orwellian.

Here is what I want from you.

Restate the entire debate in the vocabulary of composition and reading — drafts, the fixed page, revision, plants and payoffs, retcons, unreliable narration, the gap between the voice that narrates and the voice that lived the scene. Go claim by claim, not summary by summary. For each, tell me which of three buckets it belongs in: it translates into my vocabulary without loss; it translates only with distortion, in which case name precisely what is distorted; or it has no expression in my vocabulary at all. Be scrupulous about smuggling. If you find yourself carrying over an assumption from the philosophy and dressing it in craft terminology, stop and say so.

Second, declare at the outset which correspondence you are using, because there appear to be two and I cannot tell whether they can be held simultaneously: the reader as the subject with the page as the stimulus, or the author as the subject with the manuscript as the record. If you must switch between them at any point, flag every switch, and tell me whether the debate survives the switching intact. I suspect a great deal turns on this and that my hunch is only coherent under one of the two readings.

Third, the residue report, which is the part I actually want. Is what fails to translate a defect of my vocabulary — is there something fiction structurally cannot say, because the printed page is public, fixed, and inspectable while a subject's experience is none of those things — or is it evidence that the philosophers' disagreement is less substantive than it appears? Take a position rather than listing options.

Finally, run the translation on one concrete case. In the book I am working on, a narrator describes, early and in apparent good faith, a room, a woman's hands, and her expression as she reads a letter; two hundred pages later it emerges that he was never in the room and that the description is an invention he has come to believe. Tell me, in the translated vocabulary, whether my reader had an experience at that early passage which is later revised, or an experience that was always going to be delivered late — and whether that question has an answer in craft terms at all, or whether asking it is a category error I have been committing for years.
