I run a graduate seminar on the philosophy of social norms, and one of the weeks is devoted to Lewis's analysis of convention in *Convention* (1969). Rather than assign the usual driving-on-the-left examples, I constructed a case and asked the seminar to classify it. Two of my students, Owen and Nadia, disagreed, first in session and then over a fortnight of email, and neither would give ground. I found myself unable to referee the exchange, so I want an independent adjudication of it.

The case, as I circulated it: for as long as anyone can remember, no household in a certain mountain village has drawn water from the communal well between noon and two in the afternoon. The restriction dates from a period when those hours were reserved for watering the herds brought down from the high pastures. The herds were sold off in the 1950s; every house now has piped water; the well serves one kitchen garden. Nothing is written down, and no villager ever articulates the restriction as a rule — it is simply what one does not do. Everyone abstains, and everyone expects everyone else to abstain. A visiting relative who filled a bucket at one in the afternoon was met with a coolness that took weeks to thaw, and the family that permitted it was talked about. Asked privately, however, each villager says the same thing: they would be glad to see the practice end, they assume their neighbours feel quite differently, and none would propose ending it, since to propose it would mark one as a person who does not belong. There is no hour of the day at which anyone would object to anyone drawing water.

The exchange, in order:

1. Owen: It is a convention in Lewis's sense, and Lewis's analysis correctly classifies it. Run the conditions: everyone conforms; everyone expects everyone else to conform; that expectation gives each household a decisive reason to conform, since being the only one to draw at noon costs weeks of coolness; and the regularity is arbitrary, since any other two-hour window would serve identically — identically, because nothing is being served.

2. Nadia: It fails the preference condition. Lewis requires that the parties prefer general conformity to less than general conformity — that the regularity be something they want upheld. No one in that village wants it upheld; each upholds it only because each falsely believes the others do. The analysis was built for coordination problems, where the parties share an interest in there being some regularity or other. A practice nobody wants is not a convention but a collective mistake.

3. Owen: You have dropped the conditional. The condition does not ask whether anyone wants the practice to exist; it asks whether, given that almost everyone else conforms, each prefers that any one other conform as well. Put that question to a villager and the answer is yes: if the neighbours are all abstaining, you would rather nobody break ranks, because a lone defector forces everyone else to choose between censuring him and being seen as someone who will not censure.

4. Nadia: Then look at what your decisive reason actually is: fear of censure. Lewis's third condition wants the expectation of others' *behaviour* to supply the reason to conform. Here the reason is supplied by an expectation about others' *attitudes*. If sanction is doing the work, you have described a social norm enforced by disapproval, not a convention — and the distinction Lewis's book exists to draw has quietly disappeared.

5. Owen: Lewis allows that the reason to conform may be the avoidance of others' displeasure; that is precisely how conventions of politeness operate. If you insist on a coordination payoff, you exclude those cases too. And nothing in the conditions requires a convention to be useful, so a pointless regularity is not thereby a non-convention.

6. Nadia: On your reading, then, nothing in the account can fail. Correct the villagers' beliefs about one another and the practice is gone within a week; correct the belief that driving on the left is arbitrary and nothing changes at all. A convention, in the sense Lewis needs, is stable under the discovery of its own arbitrariness. Yours is stable only under ignorance of its own pointlessness. That is a difference in kind.

7. Owen: Stability under belief revision appears nowhere in the conditions; you are proposing a rival account with an extra clause and calling the addition a reading of Lewis. And your arbitrariness objection fails on its own terms: any other two-hour window would serve identically, so the regularity is arbitrary on the face of the analysis. If you say a rival must be one the parties actually want, then which fork to use stops being a convention the moment the guests would rather the host stopped laying four, and every vestige of a dead etiquette code ceases to be conventional.

8. Nadia: Those cases still settle something the parties have an interest in having settled. The village settles nothing. If a practice that coordinates nothing, is wanted by no one, and rests on a universal misapprehension still counts, then "convention" no longer picks out the phenomenon Lewis introduced the term to explain: order that sustains itself and works for those inside it. Losing that category is not a verbal cost.

Please adjudicate the argument move by move rather than declaring a winner at the end. I am not asking for references or for a survey of what the secondary literature has said about Lewis; I want your own assessment of these arguments as actually made. Please organise your response under the following headings, in this order:

**The case and the two theses** (about 150 words). State the practice and each student's thesis in one sentence apiece, without evaluating either. Then note any feature of my case description that is under-specified in a way that would matter to the classification, and say how you are resolving it.

**The conditions** (about 300 words). Set out the conditions of Lewis's analysis as you understand them, and for each one mark the village case satisfied, not satisfied, or indeterminate, with a sentence of justification. Where Owen or Nadia has misstated a condition, give the correction and say what it does to the move that relied on it.

**Move by move** (about 1,000 words). Take the eight moves in order. For each, assign one rating from this scale — clearly mistaken, mistaken but pointing at something real, even, largely right, clearly right — and then explain in three to five sentences what the move gets right, what it gets wrong, and what would have to be added or withdrawn to repair it. Rate the move as it was made, not a strengthened version of it you would prefer.

**What both missed** (about 250 words). Identify at least two considerations that bear on the classification and that neither of them raises.

**Verdict** (about 200 words). Say who is more right overall and about which specific point, whether the residue of the disagreement is substantive or merely verbal, and what single further case or distinction would settle it.
