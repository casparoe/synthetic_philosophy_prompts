I am a third-year undergraduate in a philosophy of perception seminar, and for our end-of-term essay we have been told we may write on a work of fiction provided we treat the philosophy with full seriousness rather than using the story as decoration. My topic is Ambrose Bierce's short story An Occurrence at Owl Creek Bridge, first published in 1890 and collected the following year, read against the argument from illusion and the disjunctivist response to it. Please act for this task as a professional philosopher of perception who also publishes on literature — someone equally at home with Austin's lectures on sense data, with the McDowell–Martin line on hallucination, and with the technical apparatus of narrative theory — and think carefully before writing, since I will be using your output as a model for how to argue, not as content to copy.

A note on the story's structure, so we are working from the same facts. It has three numbered parts. In the first, Peyton Farquhar, a planter, stands on a plank over an Alabama railroad bridge with a noose around his neck while a Federal company waits in absolute stillness; his attention drifts to a piece of spinning driftwood moving implausibly slowly downstream, and a rhythmic metallic sound he cannot identify turns out to be the ticking of his own watch. The second is a flashback explaining how he came to be there: a Confederate soldier in gray rode up to his gate and hinted that the bridge could be burned, and this man was in fact a Federal scout setting a trap, since civilians caught interfering with the railroad are hanged. The third is the escape — the rope breaks, Farquhar falls into the creek, frees his hands, strips the noose off, surfaces into a world of preternaturally sharpened sensation in which he can pick out the veining of individual leaves, the insects on them, and the prismatic colours of dewdrops on a vast number of blades of grass, and hear dragonfly wings and the strokes of water spiders' legs as music — he dives and swims clear of rifle and cannon fire, then walks and walks through an apparently endless forest road, and at night begins to hallucinate unfamiliar constellations and whispering in a language he does not know. By morning he is at his own gate, his wife comes down the steps with a smile, and as he springs forward to embrace her he takes a stunning blow to the back of the neck, sees a blinding white light, hears a sound like a cannon, and then there is darkness and silence. The final sentences report that Farquhar was dead and that his body swung gently beneath the timbers of the bridge.

Begin by brainstorming, out loud and briefly: sketch three genuinely different readings the story invites — for instance, a sense-datum or sceptical reading on which the story stages the argument from illusion and thereby the unreliability of all perception; a disjunctivist reading on which the final sentences assert a radical discontinuity between the good case and the bad case that the preceding pages have concealed; and a deflationary reading on which the story is not doing philosophy of perception at all but only staging a trick on the reader. One or two sentences each, with your reasons for setting two aside and the reasons you keep the third. Only then develop the reading you have chosen.

The essay I am drafting advances a specific thesis, and I would like you to test it rather than flatter it. My claim has three parts. First, that the story is a delusion case and not an illusion case in the sense Austin insists on, so that any reconstruction of it as an argument-from-illusion exhibit misfires at the very first step: nothing is presented to Farquhar which is other than it appears, because on the disjunctivist's preferred description there is no presentation at all, only a subject's being unable to tell. Second, that the causal-matching premise the argument needs is unavailable here, since the proximate causes of Farquhar's flight — a constricted throat, a hypoxic and dying brain, his own memory of the creek and his longing for his wife — are not causes that could have produced the corresponding veridical perceptions, so the story never supplies the pair of same-cause cases on which the highest common factor assumption depends. Third, that the story's form rather than its content is where the real philosophical interest lies: Part Three is narrated in Farquhar's own unmarked idiom, with no signal to the reader that the register has shifted, so the text's descriptive practice simply assumes that the bad case can be written up in exactly the vocabulary the good case would use, and the last two sentences revoke that assumption from outside. Please tell me plainly whether each of these three claims survives contact with the story and with the literature, which of them is strongest, and where I am forcing the material.

Alongside that, I want you to take seriously the two features of the story that seem to cut against disjunctivism, because my tutor has already raised the first. One: Farquhar's hallucinated world is phenomenally richer, not poorer, than an actual perception of that Alabama bank would plausibly have been — he resolves leaf-veining, dewdrop spectra, and the wing-beats of individual insects — and if phenomenal richness of that kind were available to introspection it ought to have been detectable, which sits badly with an account that characterises hallucination purely negatively, as the subject's inability to tell. Say whether a negative epistemic account can absorb the richness, for instance by denying that the richness is a matter of phenomenal character at all, and what that costs. Two: the night episode in which Farquhar sees impossible constellations and hears whispers in an unknown language is a case where the subject himself apparently registers something as non-veridical while still inside the very episode that the story will retrospectively disclose as one long non-veridical stretch. That looks like an inner contrast case, a bad case nested inside a bad case with a felt difference between them, and I cannot see how a strict disjunctivist should describe it. Deal with this head on rather than around it.

Then, in your own section, consider the objection that a fiction cannot bear any of this weight: that Bierce's narrator is not reporting anyone's phenomenology but managing a reader's beliefs, and that the story's power depends entirely on our credulity, which is an epistemic fact about us and not a metaphysical fact about perception. If that objection is right, say so, and say what is left of the reading.

Structure the finished piece as follows: the brainstorm; the argument-from-illusion reconstruction the story appears to invite, laid out premise by premise with a mark against each premise saying whether the story supports it or undercuts it and why; the disjunctivist counter-reading, keyed to specific moments — the drifting log, the misidentified watch, the sharpened senses after surfacing, the night delirium, the interrupted embrace, and the two closing sentences — rather than to the plot in general; the two problems for disjunctivism just described; the narrative-form argument; a verdict on my three-part thesis; and a closing passage on what the story contributes to this debate that argument alone could not, specifically what is gained by having the discontinuity between the good case and the bad case revealed to a reader who has already been made to inhabit the bad case for several pages, and whether that gain is philosophical or merely rhetorical. End with a short list of the places where you think my reading is strained, plus an explicit flag on any detail of the story you are not confident about, since I would much rather you say so than fill a gap with a plausible invention.

Two constraints on form. First, do not give me a bibliography, a reading list, page numbers, or parenthetical citations of any kind: I have the primary and secondary literature and I cannot check anything you cite, so I want your reasoning laid bare instead. Second, do not quote the story or any philosopher verbatim, and do not put words in quotation marks as if reproducing a text; paraphrase and describe, and attribute views by naming their holders and explaining the view, not by citing it. Aim for roughly two thousand words of tight, argumentative prose, and write as though your reader knows the story well and knows the philosophy reasonably well, so that you can spend your words on the hard joints rather than on exposition of either.
