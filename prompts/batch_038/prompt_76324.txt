Right. I'm not an academic — I do payroll for a haulage firm outside Leeds — but I've been stuck on one question for about a year now and I want to sort out what I should even be asking before I read anything else.

It started with my nan. She was a kid in Bengal during the famine in '43 and she would not talk about it, not once, and after she died I pulled at that thread and ended up reading about Ireland in the 1840s, the Great Leap Forward, Ethiopia, and the ones going on now. What I found is that everybody has a slogan. Some people say famine is never natural, it's always a crime, and anyone talking about the weather is doing PR for somebody. Others say grow up, harvests fail, that's what weather does. And then there's the argument I keep running into, from Sen I think, that it isn't about there being less food, it's about who can get hold of it — entitlements, what you're able to exchange — plus the claim that functioning democracies with a free press don't have famines.

I don't want to pick a side yet. I want the spec first. So: write out what any theory of why famines happen has to be able to do before it's allowed to call itself an explanation. For each requirement give me the case or the worry sitting behind it, because a bare list won't tell me anything and I've read enough of those.

Some of it I've already got a feeling about, so make sure these are in there or tell me why they shouldn't be: the cases it has to handle without special pleading; the near-misses, the districts that had the same drought and the same war and didn't starve, because a theory that only works backwards is no use to me; whether "there wasn't enough food" versus "the food was there and people couldn't get at it" is a real distinction or one that dissolves the moment you look at an actual famine where both were happening; the difference between a trigger and a cause; famine as against the slow grinding hunger that kills more people in an ordinary year than any famine does; a famine somebody caused versus one somebody merely let happen; and how a famine ends, which nobody ever seems to discuss.

Two more I care about most, because these are the ones I end up arguing about at the pub. One: can the theory be used by a person rather than a historian — say a district officer in March with a crop report and a list of rice prices — or can it only be assembled after the bodies are counted? Two: what does it do about responsibility when there isn't a villain, when the deaths are spread across a railway timetable, an export licence, a procurement price, a hundred clerks each doing something small and perfectly reasonable? I want to know what a theory owes me there, not whether the question is answerable.

Then tell me which of these are genuinely non-negotiable and which are just one camp's taste dressed up as a requirement — I suspect a few of the ones people shout loudest about are definately the second sort. Tell me which single requirement knocks out the most positions. And if there's a subset that can't all be satisfied at once, say so and argue it properly, because I have a horrible feeling the real-time one and the responsibility one pull in opposite directions, and if that's true then the list is itself the answer and I'd rather know now.

Only after all that, briefly: how the "it's always politics" view, the "harvests fail" view, and the entitlement view come out against your list. Keep that bit short. No bibliography and no citations please — I can't check any of it and I've got the library books already.
