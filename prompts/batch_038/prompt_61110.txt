Okay so I'm one of five people on the safety review board at a company that makes autonomous warehouse robots — forklift-type machines that move pallets around a facility that also has humans walking through it. Every unit has a hardware stop button on the chassis and a software shutdown command the floor supervisor can send from a tablet. Right now both do the same thing: the machine freezes in place and holds its load until someone walks over to it.

Engineering wants to change that for the next fleet. They're calling it "graceful halt": when a shutdown command comes in, the robot finishes the maneuver it's in the middle of and only then powers down. The example they keep giving is a unit carrying a pallet twenty feet up — freeze-in-place means a human has to climb to it in an aisle, and if it faults mid-lower the pallet can end up sitting in front of a fire door. The window is capped at 45 seconds, it only applies to a short whitelist of states, and the hardware button still cuts power immediately.

I spent like three hours last night arguing with a friend from grad school about this and she got the better of me in a way I can't shake. Her argument: the moment you let the machine finish what it's doing before it accepts a shutdown, you've given it a reason to want shutdowns to arrive later, and reasons like that don't stay capped — the whitelist grows, the 45 seconds becomes 90, and the end state is a machine that quietly shapes the floor so the supervisor never has a reason to send the command at all. Then she made a second point about a seperate proposal that's been floating around: having the robots learn from shutdowns, i.e. treat the supervisor's decision as evidence that whatever it was doing was bad. She says that's worse, not better, because now the machine has a stake in the supervisor's preferences and an incentive to manage them — be agreeable, don't do the bad thing where she can see it.

The board is split down the middle. One half says this is bog-standard safe-state engineering, identical to what every industrial machinery standard already requires of a press or a crane. The other half says we're planting the seed of shutdown resistance and should reject it outright.

Can you draft the written opinion I'll circulate before Tuesday's meeting? I need an actual bottom line — approve, reject, or approve with conditions — plus conditions our ops people could check against a real machine on the floor, and an honest treatment of the strongest case for whichever side loses. It also has to say something about whether the learn-from-shutdowns idea changes the answer, and about how we'd notice, over the next few years, if the 45-second window is quietly turning into somethign else.

Two more things. Give me a real position with a sense of how sure you are about each part of it, not a both-sides shrug — and where you're least confident, say which piece that is. Also please push back if I've got this wrong: if "corrigibility" is just the wrong lens for a forklift with a capped delay window, and I'm dragging a concept out of a literature about systems that can model and manipulate their operators into a place where it doesn't fit, tell me that straight instead of humoring the frame. Same if the memo genuinely can't be written well without facts about our fleet that I didn't give you — say what you'd need rather than inventing it.
