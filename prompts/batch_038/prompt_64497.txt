I teach a philosophy of technology seminar and there's a piece of equipment I keep coming back to that I cant get a clean read on, so I want you to help me work out what it actually assumes.

The practice. On a lot of freight and passenger locomotives the engineer works a foot pedal — a dead man's pedal, or vigilance device. The detail that interests me is that you're not allowed to just hold it down. On a cycle the system demands that you release it and press it again; a tone sounds, and if you don't answer within a few seconds the train dumps the air and goes into an emergency brake. That brake application can't be cancelled or argued out of — once its triggered, it runs to a stop. Some railroads deliberately randomize the timing cycle, or tie the prompt to something the engineer has to actually do, precisely so that pressing the pedal can't harden into a reflex. Meanwhile the same industry's newer train-control systems will apply the brakes on their own if they judge the crew to be doing something wrong, and the whole safety culture insists that the crew is the final authority in the cab.

So what account of agency is running underneath all that? My hunch is there are two readings and the device can't decide between them, but I want that argued, not asserted. On one reading the pedal is a capacity test — it checks whether there's a conscious body in the seat, full stop. On the other it's an evidence-gathering device in a much stronger sense: it treats the engineer's continued cooperation as a signal that things are fine, and the randomized cycle is there to keep that signal worth having, because a response you make without thinking tells nobody anything. That second reading seems to treat a person as an information channel, and to be worried about a failure mode — automatic, unthinking compliance — that people usually only discuss for machines.

Which of those does the design strictly commit to, and which is it merely consistent with? Notice what I'll call the renewal requirement: cooperation has to be re-earned every cycle, and yet a refusal to renew gets scored as incapacity, never as a decision. Is that a contradiction, or a coherent view about what a person in that seat is for? And what does the practice assume about the relation between its two layers — a crew that's supposed to be able to override anything, supervising a subsystem engineered so that they can't override it?

Then the mirror case. In AI safety there's a line of argument — the off-switch game, I think it's called — on which an agent that's genuinely uncertain about what it should be doing has a positive reason to let itself be switched off, because being switched off is evidence. The pedal looks like the same formal situation pointed the other way: a switch that reads the human's non-response as evidence while foreclosing any chance that the human's response means something. Does the railway practice contradict that model, support it, or is the analogy broken because a train has no interests and no view of its own job? If you swapped in the rival view — shutdown as something informative to the thing being shut down — what would the cab have to look like afterward?

One wrinkle you can take as a given: engineers sometimes defeat these devices, wedging the pedal or strapping it down. Does that fact undermine your diagnosis or confirm it?

Give me maybe 1,200 words, be concrete about the equipment, and please keep separating what the practice really needs from what just sounds nice next to it. No bibliography, I don't want a reading list.
