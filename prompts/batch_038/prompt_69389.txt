So, context: three weeks ago my PI had a stroke. She's recovering, she's talking again, but she's out for the rest of the year, and the department has handed me her upper-level seminar — infinity, vagueness, paradoxes of the infinite — fourteen students, all seniors plus two first-years. I'm a third-year postdoc and this is the first course I've ever been solely responsible for. The final is in two weeks: two hours, in class, no notes, no books.

I want the supertask essay question to be the centerpiece of the exam, worth 25 of the 100 marks. Problem is they've all read Thomson on the lamp and they've all read the Benacerraf reply, and two of them wrote their mid-term papers on exactly that. So "state and evaluate Thomson's argument" is useless — I'd get fourteen near-identical summaries and I'd learn nothing about who can actually think and who has just memorized the labels.

Here's the setup I've been building in my head, and I want you to turn it into something I can hand out. Two little machines, both invented, both stipulated in the question so nobody can claim they haven't seen them:

The Ledger Lamp. A lamp is switched at each of the times 1/2, 3/4, 7/8, and so on, each switch flipping the lamp from on to off or off to on. After every switch a scribe writes the lamp's post-switch state in a ledger, and nothing in the interval before t=1 can change the lamp's state otherwise. So the description fixes the lamp's state exactly at every moment before 1, and says nothing at all about t=1.

The Dimmer Lamp. Same switch times, but instead of flipping, each switch sets the lamp's brightness to a value half the previous one, starting from full. Again nothing else changes it before 1.

And the principle I want them to chew on, stated flatly in the question, call it the Closure of Description principle: if a complete description of a system determines its state at every moment of an interval up to but not including t=1, then it determines its state at t=1. One of my students floated something like this in seminar as a way of dissolving the whole mess — if you've pinned down every moment before the endpoint, surely the endpoint is pinned down too — and half the room nodded, which tells me they haven't got it.

The question should make them do four things: give the strongest case for the Closure principle; explain exactly where it goes wrong for the Ledger Lamp and name the premise that has to be doing the work in that objection; say what has to be added to the principle to make it true and check whether the Dimmer Lamp has that extra thing and the Ledger Lamp doesn't; and then say whether the principle's failure helps Thomson's reductio or Benacerraf's diagnosis, and why.

What I need from you: the polished exam question as the students will actually read it cold in the exam room (the two machines plus the principle plus the four tasks, and it should be self-contained — no reading list, no citations, nothing they'd need a library for, they won't have anything in front of them); a model answer of about 800 words; and a marking scheme out of 25 broken into five or six must-hit moves with the marks each is worth, a one-line note on the most common way students botch each move, and a clear statement of what separates an A from a B. Then tell me what to do with an answer that defends the Closure principle and argues against the model conclusion — I don't want to punish a smart kid for disagreeing with me, but I also don't want to hand out marks for confident hand-waving, and I need to be able to tell the difference on a marking sheet.

One more thing: I've been staring at this setup for a week and I genuinely can't tell anymore whether it's broken. If the Dimmer isn't a good contrast case, or if the ledger detail doesn't do what I think it does, say so and fix it rather than politely building around it.
