I teach a philosophy-of-cosmology seminar each spring, and this year I have had a physicist auditing it who has grown quietly appalled at how my students and I discuss one of his field's most standard moves. The move is this. Take a candidate model of the whole universe — say, one with a small, strictly positive vacuum energy that persists forever. The same statistical mechanics that licenses the model also licenses rare thermal or quantum fluctuations large enough to produce a functioning brain complete with an apparent past, and because such fluctuations are given an unbounded stretch of time, the model typically predicts that these momentary observers vastly outnumber observers like us who evolved in an ordinary low-entropy region: the model is dominated by Boltzmann brains. Cosmologists treat that result as disqualifying. The model is rejected, or else repaired by adjusting the vacuum energy, the spacetime structure, or the measure over observers — and this despite the fact that it accommodates everything we can actually observe. My colleague insists this is not a temperamental reflex but a disciplined reductio; one prominent defender of the practice argues that such a theory should be rejected not because we have empirical evidence against it but because it is self-undermining, in that accepting it would destroy the evidential warrant on which it was accepted. My students, by contrast, mostly hear it as a profession declining to take seriously an answer it does not like, and I have not been able to tell them which of us is right.

What I want from you is the epistemology the practice presupposes, held up to the light, and several things strike me as sitting uneasily together. First, on one reading the disqualifying prediction is straightforwardly empirical — the model says my experience will be brief and disordered, and my experience apparently is neither — while on another reading it is not empirical at all, since a fluctuated brain's experience may include a vivid apparent history of exactly the sort I seem to have, in which case the theory predicts nothing checkable and instead corrodes the checking itself. I cannot tell which reading practitioners intend, and I suspect the answer changes what they are committed to. Second, the criterion seems to lean on the assumption that I am a typical sample from the relevant population of observers; yet the same literature treats typicality as highly optional, generating competing measures precisely because naive typicality yields conclusions its authors refuse. Third, the same community tolerates without any cry of incoherence a proliferating stock of duplicate observers elsewhere — in Everettian branching, in an eternally inflating multiverse — and likewise tolerates fantastically special initial conditions and what look like ad hoc adjustments; so whatever principle licenses rejection here does not obviously license it there. Fourth, philosophers run structurally identical self-undermining arguments constantly (that some account of concept acquisition, or of the origins of our evaluative faculties, or of determinism, would render our reasoning untrustworthy) and take them seriously; but in those arguments nothing turns on counting, whereas here the whole force appears to come from the numbers.

So: reconstruct the account of evidence and confirmation the practice requires in order to be legitimate rather than question-begging. What must evidence be, and what role must self-locating or indexical information play in it, for the rejection to go through? State that account precisely. Then separate what the practice strictly presupposes from what it merely happens to fit well with — I want to know where it is philosophically underdetermined, that is, where several rival accounts of evidence would equally license it, and which views of confirmation it quietly rules out (a purely third-person account on which only non-indexical facts bear on a theory, or a view on which a theory is judged solely by empirical adequacy, or a naive falsificationism). Tell me plainly whether the practice, as actually carried on, is coherent; if it is not, identify which of the four tensions above is load-bearing and propose the minimal repair you would endorse — a restriction on admissible reference classes, a revised treatment of typicality, or abandonment of the criterion altogether. Finally, test the generalization: if the principle you extract is correct, name one theory outside cosmology that it would license us in rejecting on the same grounds, and say whether you regard that consequence as a welcome extension of the principle or as a reductio of it. I do not want a survey of the options and I do not want references; I want you to commit. Take a definite position — either the cosmologists are tracking a genuine and generalizable epistemic principle that philosophers would do well to adopt, or they are dressing up a distaste for unpalatable consequences as a constraint on theory choice — give your reasons for it, and mark the places where your position rests on assumptions you would not expect me to share.
