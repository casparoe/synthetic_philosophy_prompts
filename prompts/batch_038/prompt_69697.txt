I am a junior, and my field ecology project for the regional science fair has run into a feature of the rules that I cannot stop thinking about. The rulebook my project has to follow, which my regional fair adopts from the national one, requires that any project using vertebrate animals be justified in advance on a form and reviewed by an adult committee: the committee must approve the number of animals, their housing, and the method of euthanasia before the work may begin, and it must be satisfied that the number used is the minimum the question requires. Invertebrates are exempt from all of that, with one stated exception — cephalopods, meaning octopuses, squid, and cuttlefish, are reviewed as though they were vertebrates. My own protocol is a line of twenty pitfall traps partly filled with preservative fluid and checked once a week, plus two flight-interception traps; the animals that fall in drown, and some may sit for six days before I collect them. Last season that transect produced on the order of four thousand beetles, spiders, and other arthropods, and not one of them counted against any limit, because there is no limit to count against. In the same year, my school district took a grant to plant a pollinator garden and adopted a policy of not applying pesticides anywhere on campus.

I would like you to tell me what view of moral standing this arrangement presupposes, in five numbered points of one or two sentences each, with no introduction and no conclusion:

1. Reconstruct the implicit theory behind the three-way line — vertebrates reviewed, cephalopods reviewed, every other invertebrate exempt. In particular, say what the cephalopod exception is doing there: whether it marks a threshold in some capacity such as problem-solving or nociceptive learning, or whether it tracks something else entirely, for instance that an octopus can be filmed and followed as one continuing individual while a trap cup yields only a count.

2. Separate what the practice strictly requires from what it merely sits comfortably with. Which rival accounts does the line actually rule out — standing based on phylogenetic closeness to us, gradualism about moral status, standing based on being party to a cooperative scheme, the equal considerability of anything alive — and which parts of the rule are so underdetermined that several of those accounts would license them equally?

3. Consider the possibility that the rule presupposes no view about insects at all, and is instead silent because it was written for projects that use one animal at a time. Say whether a policy that demands a justification for every vertebrate killed but imposes no aggregate ceiling on invertebrates is making a claim about per-animal reasons versus total harm, or simply has not addressed mass take.

4. Ask whether the pollinator garden and the pesticide ban presuppose the same theory as the trap exemption, or a different one — value in species and populations, or the value of pollination as a service — and whether the district is therefore inconsistent, or merely running two separate value systems side by side without noticing.

5. Finally, tell me what would have to change in my protocol if the committee adopted an expected-value rule on which harm is weighted by the probability that the animal is sentient: whether some method of killing, some check interval, some cap on trap-nights, or some substitution of observation for collection would follow, and whether there is any probability of sentience low enough that four thousand arthropods cannot outweigh one mouse.

Please do not point me toward sources to read or names to look up; I want the reasoning itself, in my own terms, and exactly five numbered points.
