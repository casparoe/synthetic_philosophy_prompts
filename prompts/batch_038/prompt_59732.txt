I am the teaching assistant for a first-year graduate seminar in the philosophy of biology. Our midterm included the short-essay question reproduced below, and there is one answer I would like assessed independently before I enter a mark for it.

**The question as set (40 minutes, roughly 600 words expected):**

> Zahavi argued that the reliability of many animal signals is secured by the fact that they are costly to produce. Explain the argument, and explain what the subsequent game-theoretic modelling established about it. Is cost necessary for a signal to be reliable? In your answer, distinguish handicaps from indices and from conventional signals, and say what secures reliability in each case.

**The student's answer:**

> Zahavi's point is that the extravagance of many secondary sexual traits is not an accident of female preference but the whole explanation. A peacock's tail is a burden: it is expensive to grow, it impedes flight, it makes him conspicuous to predators. Precisely because it is a burden it can be trusted. A male in poor condition cannot afford it, so a female who responds to the tail is responding to something that reliably tracks condition. The cost is what makes the signal honest, since a signal that cost nothing could be produced by anyone and would therefore be worth nothing. Zahavi called this the handicap principle, after a handicap race, in which the best horse is the one carrying the most weight.
>
> The proposal was widely dismissed when it appeared. It looks like a contradiction of natural selection — selection should remove wasteful traits rather than favour them — and it was hard to see how a preference for something detrimental could get started in the first place. What rehabilitated it was game theory. In John Maynard Smith's 1990 signalling models, signallers differ in an unobservable quality, choose a level of expenditure on a signal, and receivers respond to the level they observe. There is an evolutionarily stable equilibrium in which signalling level rises with quality and the receiver's response is calibrated to it. The stability of that equilibrium does the work Zahavi's intuition was groping after: for a low-quality individual to signal at the level characteristic of a high-quality one would cost more than the mating advantage is worth, so he does not bluff. The structure is essentially that of Spence's job-market model, in which education is a costly signal of productivity.
>
> Two consequences follow. First, cost is necessary for reliability. Where signalling is free there is nothing to stop the low-quality individual from imitating the high-quality one, and the correlation between signal and quality collapses. A signal can be honest without being very costly, but it cannot be honest while being free. Second, the other familiar categories of reliable signal turn out to be variants of the same mechanism rather than alternatives to it. An index — a red deer's roar, say — is a handicap whose cost has already been sunk in the machinery that produces it: the roar was expensive to grow, and it is honest for the same reason the tail is. Conventional signals, such as the throat bib of a house sparrow, are the limiting case: they are almost free, and so they are only as reliable as the social punishment that backs them, and they come apart whenever a bluff goes undetected. The taxonomy is therefore a single dimension of cost, with handicaps at one end and conventions at the other.
>
> Two cautions about the modelling. It is an equilibrium result about a population, not a description of how an individual peacock deliberates, and it is no licence for the teleological idiom in which the peacock grows a tail in order to demonstrate his quality. Nor does it settle the empirical question: to show that a trait really is a handicap one must show that its expression tracks condition, and expense alone does not show that. Finally, as Getty argued, the result depends on treating costs and benefits as additive in a single currency, which is not obviously true of fitness.
