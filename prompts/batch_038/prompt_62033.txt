I've been running a weekly meetup out of my apartment for about four years now, 12 to 16 people, mostly folks who came up on LessWrong and SSC and who will absolutely tell you they've already thought about tonight's topic. We've done the paper-token tournaments, the guess-two-thirds-of-the-average thing, the one-shot dilemma with sealed envelopes, all of it, and honestly the demos have gone stale for this crowd. Tonight is superrationality — the Hofstadter move from Metamagical Themas, where you notice that any other agent running your decision procedure in a symmetric game will land on the same answer you do, so cooperating is self-consistent rather than suckerish. The problem is that half the room already signs on to this and the other half has a rehearsed line about correlation without causation, and if I just moderate a debate nobody learns anything. I want them to run into it instead of arguing about it, and I want the run-in to happen in their hands, before anyone says the word out loud.

So here's the thing I want you to build, and I have a specific twist in mind that I've been calling referrence-class leakage, though you can rename it. Superrationality always gets pitched as if the only premise you need is symmetry of situation, but the premise actually doing the work is that your counterpart is running the same algorithm, and those two come apart constantly in real life. I want an activity where situational symmetry is guaranteed by construction — everyone gets an identical card, an identical payoff matrix, identical read-aloud instructions, sealed simultaneous choices, no talking at all — while algorithmic symmetry gets quietly destroyed underneath them, because a chunk of the room has been secretly dealt a different instruction set: a couple of people told to randomize with an actual coin, one told to defect no matter what, one told that they are the only person in the room who recieved their particular instructions, maybe one told to just copy whatever they think the median person will do. And the composition should be announced only in aggregate, something like "somewhere between a third and a half of you are not doing what you think everyone else is doing." I'd like the token numbers arranged so it's genuinely unclear from the inside whether the superrational play is still right under the true mix, and I want you to tell me what your numbers do and don't prove, not just hand me a matrix. Tokens convert into the pizza fund at the end, so the stakes are real but small and nobody loses money they care about.

Give me rules I can read aloud in under three minutes, the exact payoffs, what to print, and the sequence for a 90-minute session, including whether to run rounds and whether to reshuffle — and be careful there, because if it iterates they'll just learn to punish and we're back in folk-theorem land and the whole point evaporates. Then a debrief that maps particular moments of what just happened onto the two different assumptions, and gets them to notice they couldn't have told from inside which one they were leaning on; my crowd is sophisticated, which is a problem you should use rather than work around, so tell me how to make their prior knowledge of the concept the thing that trips them. I also want your list of what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this — someone will try to signal with their pen, someone will refuse to cooperate on principle just to be a hard case, someone will have read everything and be unshakable, someone will figure out the card distribution from who they're sitting next to — and how to handle each one live. And be frank about what a room of people who've known each other for years can never reproduce: genuinely identical agents with no history, no read on each other's faces, none of the acausal flavor that makes people insist this is a claim about decision theory rather than about statistics — say what that costs and what I as facilitator have to supply by hand to fake it. Last thing: write the whole answer as continuous prose, like an email to a friend. No headings, no bullets, no bold, no tables, no numbered steps; put the payoff numbers and the timeline inline in sentences even though that's more annoying to read, because I'll be reading it on my phone on the bus and anything with structure just renders as a wall of symbols.
