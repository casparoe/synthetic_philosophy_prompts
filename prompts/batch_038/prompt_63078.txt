I spent thirty-seven years as a physical chemist, most of them directing a spectroscopy laboratory, and since retiring I have taken up the philosophy of science with more seriousness than my former colleagues would probably have predicted. I attend a fortnightly reading group in my town — nine of us: two retired academics, a solicitor, a couple of schoolteachers, and several enthusiasts with no scientific training whatever — and this term we have been working through the quarrel between scientific realism and its opponents. That is, Putnam's thought that the predictive success of our best theories would be a miracle if those theories were not at least approximately true; Laudan's rejoinder that the historical record is crowded with theories that were equally successful and are now universally rejected, the argument that Fine subsequently named the pessimistic meta-induction; Stanford's extension of it to alternatives our predecessors never so much as conceived; and the various realist retreats, notably Worrall's structural realism and the proposal that one should be a realist about the entities one can manipulate rather than about the theories one tells about them. What strikes me, and what I want your help with, is how much of the argument in our group is being carried by an analogy rather than by any inference.

The analogy is cartographic, and it belongs to the solicitor, who is by some distance the most articulate member of the group. He says that superseded scientific theories are like superseded maps: a map is not the territory, it is a representation selected and projected for a purpose, and when a better survey comes along the old sheet is withdrawn and eventually discarded. From that he draws the pessimist's conclusion — our best theories are sheets of exactly the same kind, and the history of cartography tells us what happens to them. The realist in our group, a retired mathematics teacher, accepts the very same analogy and draws the opposite conclusion: he says the pessimist confuses being discarded with being refuted, since the old sheets are not burned. A nineteenth-century survey of the Alps still guides a walker just as Newtonian mechanics still gets a spacecraft to the outer planets; each new sheet preserves what the last one got right at its own scale; therefore theory change is accretion, not replacement, and the induction over dead theories simply misreads the history. The two of them have been arguing past each other for three meetings now, and I have begun to suspect that each is extracting from the map picture things it has no business supplying.

Could you audit the analogy for me? I would like the mapping set out explicitly — what in the history of science plays the role of the territory, the sheet, the projection, the scale, the surveyor, the instruments, the moment of withdrawal — and then the disanalogies, sorted into those that actually matter for the two uses my group makes of the picture and those that are merely decorative. Several questions I would like addressed head-on. Does the limiting-case relation between Newtonian mechanics and relativity genuinely correspond to an old map remaining serviceable at its own scale, given that in the physics case the older theory's central ontology (absolute simultaneity, mass as an intrinsic property) is not preserved, whereas a map's referents — this pass, that summit — plainly are? Does the analogy have any purchase on the distinction between terms that survive theory change and the theories told about them, which is precisely where entity realism lives? What does the cartographic picture quietly import — a territory that is fixed and independently surveyable, a region that is finite, surveyors capable in principle of detecting that their own sheet is wrong — and does importing those features beg the question against the antirealist, or against Stanford's claim that the fatal alternatives are exactly the ones no surveyor thought to look for? And finally: does either of my group's conclusions survive once the disanalogies are on the table, or is the fact that the same picture can be pointed in both directions itself the verdict — that it is doing no inferential work at all? If you think a better analogy exists, or that the whole enterprise of analogizing here is the mistake, say so and explain what work it would do that maps cannot. One case from my own discipline, since it troubles the accretion story: the hybridised atomic orbitals I was taught to treat as real, physically distinct states of a bonding electron have been quietly demoted in the texts my former students now use into a convenient localisation of an underlying quantum description that has not changed at all. Nothing was refuted, nothing was added, and yet the ontological picture shifted. I am not sure which side of the map analogy that belongs on, and I would value your view on whether it can be accommodated at all. Please argue from the concepts rather than from authorities; I would much rather have your own analysis than a list of further reading.
