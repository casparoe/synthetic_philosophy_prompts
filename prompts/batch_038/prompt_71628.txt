I've been asked by an editor to comment on a chapter of a trade-press book about the neuroscience of decision-making, and before I write my two pages I want a sanity check — partly because I'm a nonreductivist about the mental and I can already feel myself being uncharitable. The chapter leans on this passage, which the author clearly thinks is the philosophical backbone of the whole book:

> Philosophers used to argue that mind and body are two different substances that somehow push each other around, but physics has closed that door. One of the basic assumptions of modern science is the causal closure of the physical: if you want to know what produced any physical event, you can always find a fully sufficient physical cause for it, and physics tells us the world is deterministic enough that nothing else is doing any work. Jaegwon Kim turned this into a devastating argument, usually called causal exclusion. Suppose your desire for coffee causes you to walk into the kitchen. That desire is realised in your brain, and there is a complete causal story at the neural level that leaves nothing out. So either the desire and the neural event are the very same thing — in which case "mental causation" is just a way of talking about the brain and adds nothing — or they are two different things, in which case your walking into the kitchen has two separate sufficient causes, which is the kind of systematic overdetermination that basically never shows up anywhere else in science. Kim's conclusion is that mental states don't really cause anything: your decisions are caused by neurons, and the conscious deliberation that seems to go on beforehand is a story the brain tells afterwards. That's why the exclusion argument matters. It's philosophy catching up with what neuroscience already found, and it means free will is, strictly speaking, an illusion.
