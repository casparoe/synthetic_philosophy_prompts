For the past three years I have served as the one philosopher on the eight-person advisory board of a mid-sized relief organization working in river-delta provinces of South and Southeast Asia; my own scholarship is on Kant's moral psychology and on the ethics of testimony, so I was recruited less as an expert on hunger than as a general-purpose conscience. The board meets in ten days and I have been asked to draft the recommendation on a crisis the secretariat has been describing, in its internal papers only, as a famine in formation. At the organization's request I am changing the country's name and blurring some figures, but the structure of the case is faithful to the documents in front of me. The affected province had a poor but hardly catastrophic season: flooding took perhaps a sixth of the rice crop, and the provincial harvest is estimated at around two and a half million tonnes against a normal three. Nationally there is no shortage at all — public silos are near record levels after two good years, and in the capital grain is selling for barely more than it did last year. In the affected districts, however, the retail price of rice has roughly tripled since spring. The people going hungry are strikingly concentrated: they are overwhelmingly landless agricultural labourers, boatmen, and the sorters and dryers employed in the province's dried-fish export trade. Landowning families in the same villages, including quite modest ones, are eating normally, and many are selling stored grain at the new prices. As far as I can reconstruct matters from the field reports, three income sources for the landless collapsed simultaneously — the flood shortened the transplanting and harvesting season in which they earn most of their annual wages; a neighbouring country closed its border to seasonal migrants, so the remittances that ordinarily carried families through the lean months never arrived; and the principal buyer of the province's dried fish imposed an abrupt sanitary ban on imports, which shut the drying yards and left the sorters without work. The country's aggregate food supply has barely moved, but one group's capacity to pay for it has fallen to something close to zero.

The secretariat's proposal, which I expect the board to rubber-stamp, is to charter shipping for some twenty thousand tonnes of imported rice and distribute it free through district schools and clinics, funded by a public appeal whose draft copy describes the crisis as the worst natural calamity to strike the province in a century and asks donors to help people whom the weather has stripped of everything. Two colleagues object on practical grounds. Our staff economist argues that free distribution will depress the price received by the smallholders who did harvest — many of them barely better off than the labourers — and so discourage planting next season; she prefers a cash-for-work programme rebuilding the embankments at a wage indexed to the local grain price, though she admits the weakest may be unable to do the work. Our agronomist proposes instead that we buy surplus grain inside the country at a support price and truck it to the affected districts, which is expensive and logistically taxing but keeps the whole affair domestic. There is also a complication the secretariat has not put in writing. The national government has offered to match, tonne for tonne, whatever grain we ship, but only on condition that our public materials attribute the crisis to nothing but the weather; the sanitary ban that closed the drying yards was imposed by the same foreign government that supplies roughly a third of our operating budget, and there is a general election here in eight months. Meanwhile, reporters from the province's two newspapers have been refused access to the worst subdistricts for six weeks on public-order grounds, and the opposition's request for a special sitting has been tabled indefinitely.

I should like advice on what to argue for at the meeting, and on three points in particular. First, given that national supply looks adequate while one occupational group is being priced out of eating, is shipping grain the right use of our money, or should the priority be something that restores that group's ability to command food — and if so, which instrument, cash, cash-for-work, domestic purchase and redistribution, or some combination, sequenced how? I am aware that each option has second-order effects on prices and on next season's incentives, and I want those trade-offs set out concretely rather than resolved by slogan; if you think the instrument should change as the crisis changes, say at what stage and on what signal. Second, is the draft appeal copy telling the truth? My instinct is that describing this as a natural calamity is not merely imprecise but actively misleading, and that the misdirection carries moral weight beyond public relations, yet I have failed to say exactly why — after all, the floods were real and the weather genuinely did damage the crop, and the ban and the border closure were not, so far as we know, aimed at starving anyone. Third, how much of a relief organization's budget may legitimately go to things that are not relief — pressing for press access, publishing our field price data, supporting a legal challenge to the reporting ban — and does the fact that our largest governmental donor is causally implicated in the crisis alter what we owe our beneficiaries by way of candour, or what we owe that donor by way of discretion? I am not after a survey of the humanitarian ethics literature, and I would rather not be handed references to chase down; what I need is a reasoned recommendation I can put in front of seven non-philosophers, with any part of my description that you think I have mischaracterized or over-simplified flagged explicitly rather than smoothed over.
