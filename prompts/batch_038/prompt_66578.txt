So here's a weird one and I could really use help. I'm a postdoc in philosophy of action, and for two years I've been nominally the "co-investigator" on a grant that bundles together three groups who had never met before the kick-off zoom: a computational psychiatry lab, a hospital clinical-ethics service, and a small policy unit at a regional health authority. The funder treats the bundle as a single consortium, and the annual reporting template has a section headed something like "what we did this year" that is meant to be filled in by the consortium as such, not by any of the three sites. There's no legal person, no joint budget line, no decision procedure, no aggregation rule — just three PIs who email seperately and a shared drive that has exactly four files in it, one of which is a PDF of the call for proposals. The first in-person meeting is next Thursday, two days of it, about forty people in one room, and I've been asked to give the opening remarks. Eight minutes, ten at the outside.

The problem is that I can't just say "we." Half that room are social ontologists who will know precisely what I'm doing and why I'm not doing it, and the other half are clinicians, data people and a couple of health-authority managers who signed up for what they think is a team-science workshop and will experience any hesitation as either rudeness or impostor syndrome projected at them. And there are two specific people: the professor who wrote the grant, sitting in the front row, who is completely sincere and genuinely believes the consortium exists and has been referring to it in the singular for two years; and a research nurse from the policy site who said on the last call, quite flatly, that she doesn't feel like a member of anything. I want the speech to be usable by both of them. My own view is that you can't bootstrap a plural subject from a podium — on the joint-commitment picture a group comes into being only when each party expresses readiness to be jointly committed in conditions of common knowledge, which means the expression has to run in all directions at once and not from one person at a lectern, and once it does exist it comes with obligations and standing to rebuke that I have no business handing out to forty strangers. The shared-planning picture is friendlier to me — interlocking individual plans, mutual responsiveness, willingness to let subplans mesh — but it turns our existence into an empirical question about how the three sites' plans actually interlock, and honestly right now they mostly don't; nothing meshes, nobody has adjusted a subplan for anyone else. And the group-agency version that wants intentional, judgemental and agential conditions satisfied is obviously a non-starter for us. I don't want the speech to name any of these positions or turn into a survey, but I do want it to be shaped by the fact that all three make the same prediction here: an unearned "we" from the front of the room is either false or empty, and the room's philosophers will hear which one it is.

Please act as a philosopher of action who has also done a lot of institutional speechwriting and knows how to make a constraint like that feel like generosity rather than evasion. Think carefully before drafting, and start with a short brainstorm — a few paragraphs working out what the speech is allowed to commit to and what it has to avoid, including the three failure modes I'm scared of: asserting a we that doesn't exist, collapsing into a list of forty separate interests (which would be self-undermining for a workshop whose whole point is to produce joint agency), and holding myself out as a spokesperson for a group that has authorised no one. Then write the actual speech. It should be built almost entirely out of offers, invitations and first-person-singular commitments that others can take up in light of each other taking them up, so that by the end it has created the conditions for a real we rather than claiming one — and I'd like it to close by handing over to something concrete in the room, a structured round of some kind where each person states a commitment after hearing the previous one, so the mutuality actually runs in all directions for once. Keep it warm, let there be exactly one joke about the shared drive, and make sure a clinician who has never heard the phrase collective intentionality still gets a speech that makes sense to them and makes them want to stay for day two.
