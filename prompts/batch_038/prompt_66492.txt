Ok so I'm in a phil of mind seminar where we've been grinding through the pain asymbolia stuff, and for my response paper I want to build a thought experiment that isn't just a restatement of the actual cases. I keep ending up with something that just assumes the answer. Here's what I'm after.

Design me a realistic clinical case — a hospital ward, a real lesion or a real drug, nothing sci-fi — in which three accounts of what makes pain unpleasant come apart in one specific direction. The imperative view (unpleasantness is a matter of the experience having content that commands the subject to stop whatever's going on in that body part) should say the experience genuinely IS unpleasant. The higher-order desire view (it's unpleasant in virtue of the subject wanting it to stop) should say it is NOT. And evaluativism (unpleasantness is the experience representing some bodily condition as bad) has to be made to land on one side or the other — you pick which and tell me why it can't sit on the fence.

Constraints. The scenario itself is one paragraph. The patient's own testimony has to be internally consistent from start to finish, because half the literature already complains that asymbolics say contradictory things and I don't want to walk straight into that. No explaining the whole thing away with global apathy, depression, frontal-lobe personality change, or some stronger competing desire — and no masochists. Most important: the case has to *generate* the verdicts, not stipulate them. If you write something like "assume the pain carries imperative content but no desire attaches to it," that's cheating and I'll know. I need the clinical facts by themselves to make each theory's commitment visible, so that anyone who accepts the theory is forced to accept the verdict.

What it's for, so you know what counts as a success: I'm going to argue that the imperative view is the only one of the three that can make sense of what asymbolic patients actually do, and I want to hand my tutor the sharpest possible version of the case before I commit to that in writing.

After the scenario, tell me which single feature is doing all the work, what happens to the three verdicts if I delete that feature, and the strongest objection available to whichever theory comes out worst — real objection, not a strawman I can knock over in a footnote. Bonus if you can say whether anything in ordinary medicine already hands us a partial version of the case (opioid patients, people who've had a cingulotomy for intractable pain, that kind of thing).

Format: numbered list, exactly five items, nothing before or after it — 1 the case, 2 what the imperative view says and why, 3 what the higher-order desire view says and why, 4 the feature thats doing the work plus what breaks if I pull it, 5 the strongest objection from the losing side. A sentence or two per item, except item 1, which can run longer since it's the actual scenario.
