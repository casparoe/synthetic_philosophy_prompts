We're a ~200 person logistics software company — HQ in Rotterdam, engineering office in Osaka. Last month we hired an outside consultant for a cross-cultural communication workshop, and part of her deck got turned into a one-page handout that's now being used to justify a new "speak plainly, no hedging" policy for the Osaka team. Her handout said this, more or less verbatum:

"Grice's Cooperative Principle holds that conversation only works because speakers observe four maxims — be truthful, be informative, be relevant, be clear. Politeness is what happens when people stop observing them. Dutch business culture is famously blunt: they say what they mean, so they observe the maxims fully and generate no implicature at all, which is why Dutch meetings feel abrasive to outsiders but get finished. Japanese business culture is the mirror image. The maxim of quality is routinely violated there, because speakers say things they know to be false — 'that will be difficult' when they mean no, 'we will study the matter' when the decision has already been made. Grice's word for this is flouting, and flouting is just lying by another name. Lying is wrong because it uses the listener as a means: you install in them a belief you picked for them, and so you take away their ability to decide on the facts. Kant made exactly this argument against white lies and it has never been answered. So an organisation has to pick a side: Dutch honesty or Japanese harmony. If you want to treat people as ends, you pick honesty."

I'm the one who has to write the policy, and before I do I'd like to know how much of this to trust. Can you go through it line by line and tell me what a philosopher of language would actually sign off on, what's a defensible simplification for a business audience, and what's wrong or is a technical term being used in its everyday sense? Specifically I keep tripping over the flouting bit — is "flout" there being used the way Grice uses it or the way normal people do, and does the difference matter for the step that says flouting is lying? Also: does the autonomy argument actually get you from "this utterance is indirect" to "this utterance wrongs the listener," and what would a Kant scholar make of the claim that his case against white lies has never been answered? And once the earlier steps are corrected, does the pick-a-side conclusion still stand, or was it resting on them?

For what it's worth I'm already inclined to think "that will be difficult" isn't a lie — everybody in the room knows what it means, so nobody walks away recieving a false belief — but that's an intuition and not an argument, and the consultant is a professional whose clients pay her for this, so I don't want to just wave her off. Tell me if there's anything in the handout that IS solid, because I'd rather salvage it then start over, and if the honest version of the advice comes out different from hers, say what it is and what I should actually put in the policy doc.
