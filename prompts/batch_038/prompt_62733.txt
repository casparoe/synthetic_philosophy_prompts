You are a value theorist who has taught Susan Wolf's "Moral Saints" many times and who designs exam questions specifically to defeat students who have merely absorbed the summary version of a debate. Please think carefully and, before drafting anything, sketch in five or six bullets what you take the crux of the question below to be, and which of the three readings I introduce you think is the most vulnerable to a good objection. Then produce the assessment materials.

**Context.** I teach an upper-level seminar on the shape of a good life, twelve students, all of whom have read Wolf closely and can reproduce the Loving Saint / Rational Saint distinction on demand. Several have also read the standard responses — the reply that Wolf conflates the morally ideal with the all-things-considered ideal life, and the reply that her complaint is really about aesthetic dullness smuggled in as ethics. The final is a two-hour, closed-book paper worth 25 marks for a single essay question.

**The apparatus I want the question built around.** I want to introduce, in the question stem itself and in my own coinage, a three-way distinction about how moral commitment can displace non-moral goods in a life, and then make the students do work with it:

1. *Budgetary crowding-out* — moral activity consumes finite time, energy, and money that non-moral goods would otherwise have had. The loss is quantitative: less of the same kind of good.
2. *Motivational crowding-out* — the resource is not consumed but the manner of its use is corrupted. Certain goods (being a friend, being absorbed in a piece of music) are realized only when pursued for their own sake, so pursuing them under the heading of duty fails to produce them at all.
3. *Evaluative crowding-out* — neither budget nor motive is affected. The agent keeps the good and enjoys it, but has reclassified it: it now figures in her deliberation as a permitted indulgence, a concession morality grants her, rather than as something that makes her life go well in its own right. On this reading the loss attaches to the ranking she lives by, and can obtain even when every particular choice she makes is blameless.

Anchor the third notion in a paired case of your own construction: two agents with externally indistinguishable lives who differ only in how the non-moral element of that life is registered in their practical reasoning. Make the case concrete and short, and include a variant in which the second agent is fully aware of her own framing and reflectively endorses it.

**Deliverables.**

(a) The exam question, self-contained, roughly 150 words of stem plus the case. A student should be able to answer it from the stem and the seminar readings, without needing anything I say aloud in the room. It must force a claim about whether evaluative crowding-out names a genuinely distinct phenomenon or merely redescribes the other two, and it must require the student to say what principle would have to be true for it to ground an objection to the moral saint ideal rather than an objection to the way we score lives.

(b) A model answer of about 800 words that argues a position rather than surveying the options. It should be clear, by the end, what the answer thinks and why.

(c) A marking scheme out of 25, organized around five or six must-hit moves, each with its mark value, plus an explicit statement of what separates a first-class answer from a competent upper-second one. Do not make the difference "more nuance"; name the specific move the stronger answer performs.

(d) A short list of the four mistakes that should cost the most marks — for instance, collapsing the motivational reading into the budgetary one, treating awareness-and-endorsement as automatically curing the third, or converting the whole question into a dispute about how demanding morality is — with the deduction each warrants and a one-line note on why students make it.
