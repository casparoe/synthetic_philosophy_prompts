I convene a small evening reading group on the metaphysics of persons, and this term we have been working through origin essentialism: roughly, the thesis that no person could have come into existence from any gametes other than the particular ones they in fact came from, so that the child your mother might have had instead — different sperm, different egg — would not have been you at all, however similar. My sister is an embryologist, and over the last year she has filled my head with detail I did not previously have: vitrification, time-lapse incubators, the barcode on every straw in the tank, the way a clinic's records turn an origin into an administrable object with a number attached. It struck me that this is an unusually good setting for a story, because a clinic is precisely the sort of institution that has to write its metaphysics down and then be held to it. So I would like you to write me a short story, of something between fourteen hundred and nineteen hundred words, that puts that thesis under pressure — not by the familiar route of asking us to imagine being born to different parents, but by way of vagueness: what happens when the differences in origin can be made as small as you like, one at a time.

Please write as someone who is both a working metaphysician and a published writer of literary fiction, and think the design through carefully before drafting. Here is the seed, which you are welcome to develop, complicate, or partially disregard. A private clinic holds, under a research licence, a technique for correcting single-base variants in a vitrified oocyte: the correction is applied one substitution at a time, and each substitution must be individually authorised and individually logged. The narrator is a philosopher the clinic retains part-time to draft and defend its consent wording, and who took the job believing that the necessity-of-origin thesis hands them a clean, litigable line. The couple are parents whose daughter died at four; they have asked for another child as like her as the clinic can manage, and the clinic can manage a candidate oocyte that differs from the one their daughter came from by a single substitution, and then by two, and then by three, and so on down a printed sequence. The narrator has to initial a number: how many substitutions may be made before the resulting child is, on the clinic's own stated metaphysics, no longer the child these people are asking for. Give the thesis its strongest advocate — a clinic lawyer, perhaps, who insists that identity does not come in degrees, that similarity is beside the point, that there is therefore a determinate number, and that declining to state one is not neutrality but a kind of fraud on the parents. Against that, put the embryologist, or the mother, or the narrator's own reading of the printout, supplying the other pressure: that at every single step it looks absurd to say that this one substitution is the difference between their daughter and a stranger. Let the story carry the philosophy in scenes, documents, overheard conversation and small procedural humiliations rather than in exposition, keep both sides genuinely competent, and do not resolve it with a tidy moral or a twist. You may invent files, forms, clinic policy language and dialogue freely; do not quote or closely paraphrase any real philosophical text. If it helps, the clinic's records also hold the file of a former patient's monozygotic twins, which sits awkwardly with the further and stronger claim that a given origin is sufficient for a given person — use that or leave it, as suits the story.

Structurally, I would like the reply in three movements, all of it continuous prose. Open with a short paragraph saying which construal of the thesis you have chosen to put under pressure and how the shape of the story is meant to enact the sorites rather than merely describe it. Then the story itself. Then close with a candid paragraph on where you have taken liberties — with the embryology, with the metaphysics, or with both — and in particular whether you have leaned on a contested reading, for instance treating the necessity of an origin as though it settled numerical identity across scenarios, or sliding between saying that origins are necessary to a person and saying that they are sufficient for one. And please do not simply comply if you think my framing is muddled: if the vagueness pressure I have sketched does not in fact arise for the thesis as properly stated, or if a story built around a consent form and a numbered sequence could only ever end up lecturing its reader rather than dramatising anything, tell me so plainly and say what you would write instead. Finally, a formatting request — no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables. Paragraph breaks only, continuous prose throughout, in the manner of a letter.
