I am in the third year of a philosophy PhD, working on distributed and collective cognition, and the first chapter of my dissertation is meant to stake out a position on whether an insect colony can be the subject of mental states. Before I circulate a position statement to my committee and present a version of it at a graduate workshop, I would like a rigorous consistency audit of the package of views set out below. I suspect something in it does not hang together, but I do not know where, and I am deliberately not telling you which claims I would be most willing to surrender: I do not want the audit steered by my attachments.

What I hold:

(1) Beliefs are individuated by causal role. What makes a state a belief that there is food at the base of the oak is the part it plays in the uptake of information, its retention, and the selection of behavior. Substrate is irrelevant, and consciousness is not a precondition of belief.

(2) Over and above playing that role, I do not think there is any further fact about whether a system has beliefs. The best gloss I can offer is a Dennettian one: to have a belief is to be a system whose behavior is better predicted and explained by taking the intentional stance than by any alternative. I do not know what else could make such an ascription true.

(3) Verdict: the foraging colony, with its pheromone trails, tandem running, and recruitment cascades, genuinely has beliefs and desires. I mean this literally, and I want to say that the colony's representation of where the food is plays a causal part in the re-routing of the trail. Hofstadter's Aunt Hillary dialogue is, for me, a serious hypothesis rather than a rhetorical device.

(4) Verdict: the commuters on a station platform who all drift toward the exit when the train arrives do not thereby have beliefs of their own. Neither does a book club, a queue, or the audience at a lecture. I take this to be obviously right.

(5) The difference between (3) and (4) is a difference in the world, not a difference in how much explanatory work I happen to want to do. I do not want to be an instrumentalist about group-level ascription, and I do not want the boundary of mindedness to shift with my interests.

(6) I accept the parity reasoning behind the extended mind: an external resource that is reliably available, automatically taken up, and would be counted as cognitive if it were inside the skull is part of the cognitive system. I apply this to Otto's notebook, and I want to apply it to the trail network, so that the pheromone deposits are part of the colony's memory rather than merely a cause of the colony's behavior.

(7) I am a liberal about insect sentience. I think it is an open empirical question whether individual ants have any phenomenal experience, and I lean toward thinking that at least some insects do. I do not want to deny this in order to protect anything else in the list.

(8) I am nevertheless confident that there is nothing it is like to be the colony.

(9) I am a causal individualist: every causal transaction that occurs at colony level is exhaustively realized by transactions among ants and chemicals, and I posit no distinctively group-level causal powers. At the same time, I think the colony-level description is sometimes the only tractable explanation of the re-routing, and I am not prepared to treat that description as a dispensable convenience.

What I would like from you:

- Identify the commitments that pull against one another and rank the resulting tensions by severity. I do not want a flat list in which everything is equally bad.
- For each genuine tension, state the cheapest revision that removes it and what that revision costs elsewhere in the package.
- Say explicitly which apparent tensions dissolve on inspection, and why. I have already fielded two objections: that (7) and (8) are inconsistent because conscious parts should compose a conscious whole, and that (9) contradicts (3) because group-level explanation requires group-level causal powers. I suspect both are confused, but I want to know whether that suspicion survives scrutiny, and in particular whether either dissolution depends on a claim I have not noticed I am making.
- Answer one specific test. Is there any criterion available to me from within (1), (2), (5), and (6) that delivers my verdict in (3) and my verdict in (4) simultaneously? If there is, state it and show that it does the work. If there is not, say which of my verdicts I am forced to abandon and trace the route by which the rest of the package forces that choice.
- List the commitments my package presupposes but does not state, for instance anything about the colony being a single agent with a single set of interests, or about what makes the trail network a store of information rather than a channel of communication between separate agents.

Please do not supply a bibliography, a reading list, or citations; I know this literature, and pointers would not help me. What I need is the entailment-tracing: which of my claims commits me to which others, where the package stops being stable, and what the single revision is that repairs the largest amount of it at the smallest cost.
