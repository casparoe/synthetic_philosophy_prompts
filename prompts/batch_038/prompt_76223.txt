I spent thirty-eight years calibrating instruments for a national metrology laboratory and have come to philosophy late; I now audit a seminar on probability and rational choice, and I keep a notebook of the seminar leader's pronouncements about when an agent's betting dispositions convict her of irrationality. Her verdicts so far:

1. A trader who holds 0.3 for a hypothesis and 0.8 for its negation, and who accepts both corresponding bets when they are sold to her together, is guaranteed to lose; she is flatly irrational.
2. A schoolteacher whose degrees of belief are perfectly coherent but whose savings are small, so that money has strongly concave utility for her, accepts a run of small bets at her own odds which a bookie then buys up as a package for a guaranteed loss; not irrational, since the guarantee is an artifact of treating money as though its utility were linear in the stakes.
3. A researcher who is coherent today and who announces that she will respond to tomorrow's data by something other than conditionalizing — that is, other than setting her new degree of belief equal to her old degree of belief conditional on the evidence — is irrational, because a book can be assembled out of her own declared plan.
4. The same researcher, had she instead been taken by surprise by her reaction to the data and had no inkling how she would update, would not be criticizable in that way.
5. A man who declines every wager but whose degrees of belief are impeccably coherent is rational.
6. A woman whose degrees of belief violate the axioms concerning a proposition so remote that no one could ever frame a wager on it is irrational all the same.

Next week's case: a statistician facing a genuinely novel hypothesis represents her uncertainty not by a single number but by a range — every probability between 0.4 and 0.6, say — and accepts any individual bet that has non-negative expected value according to at least one probability in that range. The stakes are trivial relative to her wealth, the bets are offered one at a time and she may decline any of them, and she understands perfectly well that a bookie who offers the right sequence can assemble a guaranteed loss out of bets she will each accept. Which way will she rule, what rule is she in fact applying, which rival rules fit the six earlier verdicts equally well, and what further case should I propose to discriminate between them?

Please answer as a numbered list of exactly five items and nothing else: your prediction of her verdict, the rule you take her to be applying, two rival rules that account for the earlier verdicts just as well, and the case I should propose next. Each item should run one or two sentences, with no introduction or conclusion.
