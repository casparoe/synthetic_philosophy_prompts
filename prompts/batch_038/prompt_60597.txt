I teach one section of an interdisciplinary graduate seminar on science and religious authority; the room is about half historians of early modern science and half theology students, and next week's session covers Galileo's two scriptural letters, the short one to Castelli in 1613 and the much expanded one to the Grand Duchess Christina in 1615. I keep telling the students that Galileo's hermeneutic is not the permissive one they assume: reinterpretation of the literal sense is licensed only under specific conditions, scripture's teaching authority is, on his account, directed to what bears on salvation rather than to the constitution of the heavens, and nature is not obliged to accommodate itself to anyone's expectations. To check whether I actually believe that, I sat down and wrote out my own verdicts on a set of conflict cases, and I have found that they do not cohere. I would like you to tell me what principle, if any, they add up to, and what has to go.

My verdicts, in the order I wrote them:

1. The psalm and chronicle verses that credit God with fixing the earth on its foundations so that it cannot be moved, and the Ecclesiastes line about the sun rising and hurrying back to its place: reinterpret without reluctance. The literal sense here is doing devotional work, not cosmological work, and once the earth's motion is demonstrated nothing worth keeping is lost.
2. The description in Kings of the circular basin ten cubits across with a thirty-cubit circumference: no conflict even arises. A scribe reporting a round number is not asserting a value for the ratio, and I feel no hermeneutical pressure at all.
3. Joshua's long day: I want to keep the event and drop the mechanism. For me the content of the passage is that the daylight was supernaturally extended; the arresting of the sun is how the narrator describes it, not what is claimed. This bothers me considerably more than case 1, and I cannot say why, since here the mechanism is the point of the sentence rather than incidental scenery.
4. The cosmological scaffolding, waters above a firmament, windows of heaven, pillars of the earth: I treat these as wholly cultural and think their falsity touches nothing. I am even more relaxed here than in case 1, with no account of the difference.
5. Common ancestry and the descent of all living humans from a very small founding population: I would already reinterpret the genealogies and the creation narrative, on evidence that is convergent and overwhelming but not, in the strict sense Galileo demands, demonstrative. I seem to have quietly abandoned the demonstration threshold.
6. Passages on the soul, or on the mind as more than matter: if a fully physical account of cognition became as well established as the earth's motion, I would not read them figuratively. I would instead deny that the neuroscience means what it appears to mean. That is weaker evidence than case 5 and the opposite output.
7. Miracle narratives that conflict with nothing in physics but with ordinary regularity, water into wine, the raising of the dead: I feel no interpretive pressure whatsoever, which suggests that whatever principle I am running cannot be a rule about how firmly established natural regularity constrains literal readings, or it would fire constantly.

My best guess at my own rule is this: reinterpret where the passage is not about salvation and where the case against the literal sense is at least as strong as the textual case for it. Please test that against the seven verdicts and repair or replace it. Specifically, I would like: (a) the principle stated as a small set of ordered clauses, each clause tagged with the case above that motivates it, and phrased so that someone who has never heard the labels could apply it to a new passage; (b) an assessment of whether Galileo's own stated criteria deliver my verdicts, identifying which of them his demonstration requirement and his restriction of scripture's teaching scope would reverse, and saying whether my actual practice is closer to Bellarmine's caution about not disturbing the received reading absent a demonstration, to Augustine's rule about not making a claim of faith out of one contested interpretation, or to something none of the three held; (c) the crossing cases, namely a salvation-relevant passage against merely probable science and a purely descriptive passage against rock-solid science, and what the principle says when its two thresholds disagree about the output; (d) verdicts on three cases I have not been able to decide, the darkness at the crucifixion, which is sometimes said to be impossible as an eclipse given the lunar phase at Passover, the genealogical chronologies that appear to fix the age of the world, and a case where the tension is with well-attested history rather than natural science, such as the census used to date the nativity; (e) a choice forced on me between a simpler principle that overturns one of my verdicts and a more complex one that saves all of them, with the cost of each stated plainly, and your recommendation about which of my verdicts, if any, is the one to surrender; and (f) an honest answer on stability, since if the threshold is indexed to how confident I happen to be in the science, I worry the principle collapses into reading literally wherever I feel confident and figuratively wherever I do not. Finally, tell me whether the position my verdicts add up to has an established name; if it does, say what the name commits me to, and if it does not, say so and coin one with a definition sharp enough to have boundary cases. Please formulate everything in your own words: no bibliography, no page references, and no quoted passages from the letters or from scripture, since I want the reasoning rather than the apparatus.
