I'm a political philosophy prof and, as penance for it, the faculty seat on our regional foundation's grants committee — six of us, about $350k to give out, one cycle a year. Two finalists this round. A: scaling a tutoring program for early reading that has three solid RCTs behind it and that three much bigger funders in our state already support. B: a two-year project to draft and stress-test protocols for a dual-use research area that essentially nobody else funds; the PI concedes in her own letter that she'd put the chance of it changing actual practice at maybe 1 in 5.

Our written rubric scores each proposal 1–5 on importance, tractability and neglectedness, multiplies the three, funds in rank order. So A comes out 5×5×1 = 25 and B comes out 5×2×5 = 50, and half the board is livid. Our one economist has the sharper objection: those are ordinal scores, so multiplying them is arithmetic on nothing, and a "1" on neglectedness doesn't mean a fifth of the marginal value — given A's existing funding base it might mean a fiftieth. Meanwhile the same rubric would happily fund some 4×4×4 = 64 proposal that's just mediocre all round. Bylaws say we apply the rubric as writen this cycle and can only recommend changes for next year.

Draft the memo I'll circulate before Monday's meeting: what we fund this cycle and in what amounts (splitting is allowed, but say why), what specifically to change in the rubric so it stops ranking causes by their average properties rather than by what our particular money would add, and an honest hearing for the members who think B is exactly the speculative vanity gamble a small foundation has no business staking its whole year on. Don't bother with references — I want something they can argue with.
