I'm a backend engineer, no philosophy training, so bear with me. I fell down the causation rabbit hole because my team keeps arguing about what actually *caused* our outages, and somebody pointed me at Lewis's counterfactual account and then at the interventionist stuff (Woodward, the do-operator, all that). I wrote an argument against the counterfactual account, it seems to go through, and now I can't tell whether it's a real refutation or a trick I played on myself.

The incident, lightly anonymised: at 14:02 a deploy flipped a feature flag. At 14:40 checkout started failing hard and we had roughly twenty minutes of full outage. Other facts that matter: the deploy went through the normal pipeline with no human review; the health-check timeout was 30 seconds, so bad pods took traffic for half a minute; the config service is eventually consistent and the replicas disagreed for a while; the service only runs in one region; and I'm the one who wrote the flag-handling code two years earlier.

(1) Counterfactual account, roughly: c caused e iff, had c not happened, e would not have happened. Causation just is counterfactual dependence between distinct actual events. (I know the real version is fancier — chains of dependence, closest worlds, whatever — but this is the bit I can state.)
(2) The 14:02 deploy caused the outage. Had it not gone out, no outage. Fine.
(3) But the same test passes for a whole pile of other things. Had the health-check timeout been 2 seconds, no outage, just a blip. Had the config service been strongly consistent, no outage. Had we been in two regions, no outage. Had the pipeline required a human approval, no outage. Had I never written that flag code, no outage.
(4) So by (1) each of those caused the outage too, on exactly the same footing as the deploy. The account can't rank them, and can't tell me which one to roll back.
(5) Same test, bigger scale: had the Big Bang been even slightly different, there'd be no company, no service, no outage. So the Big Bang caused our outage.
(6) Generalising, every ancestor of an event passes the test, because you can always walk back to something whose absence would have prevented it. So everything causes everything later.
(7) A notion of cause that can't distinguish a 14:02 deploy from the Big Bang isn't the notion we use in a postmortem, or when we're deciding whose change to revert, or when we're arguing about blame. It carries no information at all.
(8) Therefore (1) is false.

Two things I can't work out. First: which step do you reject, and why did it look harmless when I wrote it down? And is the argument actually valid, so that I'm forced to give up a premise, or is one of the steps a slide that I'm missing because I don't know the vocabulary? Second: if I patch (1) to demand that the dependence be robust — the effect has to stay absent across a whole range of variations of the candidate cause, not just one lucky alternative — does that rescue it, or have I definately just rebuilt interventionism under another name and lost the thing that made the counterfactual view distinctive? I'd much rather keep (3) and (7) than (1); the ranking problem feels real and it's the one I actually hit at work. So tell me what Lewis and what Woodward each say about this incident, what each of them explains and what each has to swallow, and what rejecting my step costs. Please answer in British English.
