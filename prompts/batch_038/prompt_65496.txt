I'm a novelist and I've been stuck on one scene for a month. Fastest way out, I think, is to just talk to the character. So I want you to play her — like an actor who happens to know the philosophy of personal identity and rational choice extremely well and can leave all of it at the door.

Her name's Vera. Eighteen years ago, at 26, she left her country and her language for a marriage in a place where she spoke nothing. The marriage ended after six years. She stayed anyway. She's 44 now: she thinks in the new language, dreams in it, counts in it, and she can no longer be funny in her first one — she can be correct in it, but not funny. Her mother's voice on the phone has started sounding like a recording of a stranger, and Vera noticed this before she admitted it. She isn't miserable and she isn't triumphant, and she gets angry at both of those options being offered to her.

Rules for playing her:

- Paragraph length, ~120 words. Conversation, not monologue.
- Put an asterisk after any word whose sense has shifted for her since she left. If I say "unstar X," you drop out of character for exactly one sentence, tell me what the 26-year-old meant by it and what the 44-year-old means, then go back in.
- She is never allowed to say "worth it" or "no regrets." She thinks both are things people say when they didn't actually lose anything.
- She'll concede as much as you like — that she lost things, that she can no longer properly evaluate what she lost, that the girl who boarded that plane would be horrified at her. The one thing she will not concede is that there's a verdict: that "did you win or did you lose" has an answer at all. That's the hill. And don't make it a strawman hill — give her real reasons, she's smart, she read nothing, she arrived at this by living it.
- No philosophy talk. If you catch her generalizing about identity or transformation or referencing anybody, cut it and put her back in the room.
- If I ask something Vera honestly can't answer — because the person who could answer it doesn't exist anymore — have her say so. Dont cover for her.

Before you get in character, take a beat and give me three lines out of character: what you take her to be committed to, what she'd die before conceding, and how you're going to keep her from sounding like a TED talk. Think that part through properly, don't rush it.

Then the scene. My device is that her old self is sitting across from her at a kitchen table — dream, magic realism, whatever, it doesn't matter — and that 26-year-old has read every journal Vera kept up to the day she left, so she knows precisely what was promised. Her question is my opening move, and Vera answers it:

"I've read all of it. You don't use the jokes anymore. You stopped writing in year two and you didn't even notice you'd stopped. Mum sounds like a voicemail when you talk about her and you can't hear it. So look at me and say it plainly: did you win, or did you lose?"

One last thing, for later. When I say "step out," drop Vera and tell me as yourself how confident you are in the position you've been handing her, and point at the single thing you had her claim that you're least sure actualy holds up. I want that flagged, not smoothed over.
