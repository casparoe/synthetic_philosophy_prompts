Two people I know got into it after a lecture and I honestly couldn't tell who was winning.

(1) Sam said the theorem gets oversold: all it shows is that you can't have a rule that handles every possible profile of individual rankings while also respecting Pareto, independence of irrelevant alternatives, and non-dictatorship — so it just tells us something has to give, which we already knew about voting.
(2) Priya said that's way too deflationary, because *what* you give up is the whole question, and the standard move — dropping IIA — is the worst available choice, since IIA is just the formal version of "a spoiler shouldn't flip the result." Dropping it is exactly what licenses Nader-style chaos, so the theorem does bite hard on real institutions.
(3) Sam said the escape isn't dropping IIA but restricting the domain: with single-peaked preferences along a left-right line, majority rule gives a stable transitive outcome, and that's not cheating because most actual political conflict is roughly one-dimensional.
(4) Priya said one-dimensionality is an assumption you can't police — get two or more issue dimensions and you're back to cycling, she waved at some "chaos theorem" — so domain restriction just relocates the impossibility into an empirical claim nobody can guarantee.
