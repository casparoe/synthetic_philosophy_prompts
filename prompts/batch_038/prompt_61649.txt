For three years two of my senior analysts have argued about whether historical analogies can carry evidential weight, and the argument has not moved even though they agree on essentially all the underlying facts about the episodes in question. Their positions, as I understand them:

Analyst A holds that an analogy is admissible when the structural features that plausibly drove the outcome in the earlier case are matched in the present one — number of parties, what each side knows of the others' resolve, whether commitments are observable, whether exit remains available — so that a bad analogy is simply one that fails the match on a feature known to matter. Analyst B replies that this is rigor in appearance only: for any proposed analogy one can always produce a structural difference that defeats it and a structural similarity that supports it, so the selection of relevant features is really done by the conclusion the analyst already wants. On B's account analogies generate hypotheses but never support them, and only base rates and mechanism claims have evidential force. Each has now written on Munich in 1938, on the 2003 Iraq decision, and on the Soviet intervention in Afghanistan; in every case A pronounces the analogy usable with adjustments, and B pronounces it unusable in principle, and neither memo seems to me to answer the other.

My working hypothesis is that they are addressing different questions — one about the calibration of an inference, one about the discovery of hypotheses — under a single word, and that the deadlock is a category artifact rather than a real dispute. Diagnose it. Is the disagreement substantive, verbal, or a conflict over what counts as evidence? Is there a hidden empirical question underneath, for instance whether analogy-led analysts actually forecast better, and given how few great-power crises there are and how little they repeat, could such a question in principle be settled? Then tell me what would count as progress for my unit: a distinction, a scoring protocol, or the concession that the question is ill-posed and the real issue is institutional — who may invoke which analogy, and bearing what burden of proof. I want reasons, not a tour of the literature.
