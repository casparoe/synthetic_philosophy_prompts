I freelance for a couple of general-interest magazines, mostly science and ideas, and a real chunk of what lands in my inbox is archaelogy — press releases from digs, a new paper about some find, the odd tip from a grad student. It's maybe three or four a month where I have to decide, usually within a day, whether there's a story and what the story is allowed to claim. The thing I keep getting burned on isn't the physical evidence, it's the leap from objects to minds: a pile of stuff in the ground turns into "they worshipped a mother goddess," "they had hereditary rank," "they were in contact with a polity 400 km away." Sometimes that leap is solid and sometimes it's a vibe with a radiocarbon date stapled to it, and I genuinely cannot tell the two apart from a press release. I've had to sit through one correction already.

So: build me a procedure I can actually run. Not a lecture on archaeological theory — a sequence of questions I ask in order, with what counts as a sufficient answer to each, and what answer means I stop. I want it to say where it kills the story outright, where it downgrades the story from a belief-level piece to an object-level piece ("here's a weird figurine" rather than "here's a religion"), and where it hands the decision to me or my editor because its a judgment call and not a fact question. Mark the steps that are procedure in name only — the ones where a checklist is just giving my gut a haircut.

Ground it in whatever the discipline and the philosophy of it actually know about this kind of inference, and use the concepts that do real work even if they're jargon, but gloss each one in a clause so I can wield it without a textbook: equifinality, meaning different past processes leaving the same trace; formation processes and taphonomy, i.e. everything that happened to the assemblage after people dropped it, including the digging itself; the difference between a deposit that's a snapshot of one moment and one that's centuries of accumulated rubbish, which I think is called the Pompeii premise; middle-range reasoning, the bridge from what you can observe now to what happened then, and whether that bridge is borrowed from ethnography, from experiments, or from nothing; and the fact that a typology of artifact types is often the analyst's filing system rather than a category anyone in the past used. If there's a distinction I've left out that matters more than any of those, put it in and say why it outranks mine.

Constraints: ten to fifteen minutes per story, sometimes with only an abstract and a press release to go on; I can email the lead author one or two questions, so tell me which questions are worth spending that on; no archaeologist on staff to phone; my editor signs off and pushes back hard if the hedging gets too thick, so the procedure should terminate in something I can put in a headline or a standfirst, not in a list of caveats. And no reading list, no citations, no named papers or authors — I can't check any of it and I don't want you inventing plausible-sounding ones.

Then run it on three pitches, all hypothetical but typical:

1. A site yields about forty small fired-clay figurines, mostly fragments, scattered across several house floors and middens spanning a few centuries; the team says the whole region shared a mother-goddess cult.
2. A cemetery with roughly 120 burials where a handful of adults and two children are interred with far more and far better goods than everyone else; the team says this shows hereditary rank — an institution, not just some wealthy individuals.
3. Use-wear and residue on a handful of stone flakes is read as plant-fibre working; the team says the group was making cordage or basketry.

Also tell me plainly what the procedure can never license, no matter how beautiful the fieldwork — the class of claims that stays out of reach even when every step passes.

And be honest about which of the three I'd actually file. I suspect 3 is the best-supported and the least sellable and 1 is the exact reverse, and I don't want you softening that to keep me happy. Take a position throughout: name the single step that does most of the work, say which of the three pitches you think is unfalsifiable as framed, and tell me where you think this whole exercise is a comfort blanket rather than a method. One committed answer I can argue with beats a menu of options.
