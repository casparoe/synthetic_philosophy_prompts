so I've been working out a view about suffering and I want someone to actually try to break it before I send the abstract off. here's where I've landed, roughly:

1. suffering is the only thing with intrinsic moral weight. pleasure isn't intrinsically valuable at all — but it's not inert either, it does real moral work by cancelling out suffering, so a life with less net pain in it is better.
2. there's a hard threshold in intensity of bad experience. above it: torture, that sort of thing. below it: papercuts, specks of dust in the eye, a mildly annoying hum. the claim is that no number of below-threshold bads, however large, adds up to one above-threshold bad. lexically worse, not just worse.
3. pleasure and pain still sit on one scale though — same kind of quantity, opposite signs. I just weight the negative side more heavily, maybe 10 to 1, or whatever the right multiplier turns out to be.
4. the threshold is one-directional. it only protects the bad side. there's no intensity of pleasure that's lexically good; enormous joy is just a big positive number.
5. case I'm confident about: a billion people each spending a whole lifetime with a chronic dull toothache is worse than one person being tortured for an afternoon. I take that to be obvious and I want my view to deliver it.
6. case I'm equally confident about the other way: I wouldn't trade one person's hour of torture for a trillion papercuts, not even in principle, not for any number of papercuts.

and then there's my own worry list, which I suspect is mostly me confusing myself:

(a) doesn't 2 commit me to there being an exact intensity at which the moral status flips, which seems nuts given that experience is continuous and I couldn't tell you where the line is?
(b) doesn't the whole thing just collapse into ordinary weighted utilitarianism the moment I say 3 — i.e. have I described utilitarianism with an extra knob?
(c) does 4 secretly commit me to a threshold on the positive side too, since I do think a short life of intense joy beats a long life of mild joy?

what I want: go through 1-6 and tell me which of them genuinely can't all be true at once, ordered by how bad the problem is — not all listed as equally troubling. for each real one, say what the cheapest thing to give up is and what I lose by giving it up. and tell me separately which of a/b/c are live problems and which are false alarms. I'd much rather hear that it's a mess than get reassurance.
