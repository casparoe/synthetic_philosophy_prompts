I am preparing a seminar paper on the special composition question, and I built the following argument to convert the nihilists in the room. It has begun to frighten me: its conclusion is something I do not believe, and its premises seem to reach well past composition.

(1) For any things, either they compose something or they do not.
(2) Suppose that composition is restricted: some things compose something, and some things do not.
(3) One can pass from a clear case of composition to a clear case of non-composition by imperceptible degrees — easing the parts a millimetre apart, or letting the adhesive bond a little less firmly — so there must be a last adjacent pair of cases in the series, composition holding in the first and failing in the second.
(4) A predicate with such a cut-off, which no amount of investigation could locate, is vague: there are cases in which it is indeterminate whether the xs compose something.
(5) If it is indeterminate whether the xs compose something, it is indeterminate how many things there are, since a claim about how many things there are is just a claim about which pluralities compose.
(6) Nothing of that sort can be indeterminate. Reality is precise: for any predicate, either there is an F or there is not, and there is no third possibility in which the world leaves the matter unsettled.
(7) So composition is not restricted; it is therefore either universal or empty.
(8) It is not empty, since cats and tables exist.
(9) So any things whatever compose something.

Two things about (9) I cannot swallow. First, it commits me to a scattered object comprising my left shoe and a quasar a billion light-years away, which seems to leave 'object' doing no work at all. Second, and worse, (6) is not a claim about composition but a general principle, and by the same route it appears to hand me a precise number of hairs at which a man becomes bald, a precise moment at which a fertilised ovum becomes a person, and a precise boundary for every other predicate I care to name. My supervisor's only comment was that an argument which proves this much proves nothing, and she will not tell me which premise is at fault.

I would rather keep (1) and (8) than anything else. Tell me which step to reject, why that step looked innocent to me when I wrote it down, and what each of the live alternatives costs: composition vague all the way down, a sharp cut-off that is merely unknowable, nihilism, and simply accepting (9). Please write in British English, and end with a line beginning 'Verdict:' giving the number of the step you reject — or, if you think the argument valid and merely expensive, the number of the premise least worth its price — followed by an em dash and no more than eight words naming what rejecting it costs.
