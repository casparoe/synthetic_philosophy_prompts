In three weeks I am presenting a chapter of my doctoral thesis at my department's aesthetics research seminar, and I would like a preparation sheet for the question period rather than a defence of the view itself.

The chapter attempts a revival of the evaluative half of Tolstoy's account of art. My argument runs as follows. I claim that *What Is Art?* contains two theses that are almost always run together and should be pulled apart: a criterion of arthood, on which something is art insofar as it deliberately transmits to others, by means of external signs, a feeling the maker has genuinely experienced; and a criterion of artistic value, on which works are ranked by the quality of the feelings transmitted and by the breadth of the human circle those feelings can unite. Tolstoy's notorious verdicts — his rejection of Shakespeare, of Wagner, of late Beethoven, and of most of his own earlier fiction — belong to the second thesis, and I argue that they are failures of application rather than entailments of the criterion, which is why I propose to keep the criterion and discard the verdicts. I further argue that infectiveness should be treated as graded and audience-relative rather than all-or-nothing, and I introduce what I call a reach condition: the wider the circle a work can infect without diluting the feeling, the higher its value, other things being equal. Finally, I claim that Tolstoy's four diagnostic marks of counterfeit art — borrowing, imitation, effectfulness, and diversion — do real work on cases that institutional and aesthetic-function theories handle badly: socially engaged and participatory practice, community arts, protest song, documentary, and design. My test case in the talk is a community choir in a post-industrial town, which I argue an institutionalist must treat as marginal to art while my account treats it as central.

The audience shapes everything here. There will be roughly twenty people: the convenor, trained in the Danto and Dickie tradition, who considers arthood settled by a historically situated artworld; a senior colleague who works on Collingwood and insists on a sharp distinction between expressing an emotion and arousing one in an audience, and who will therefore regard Tolstoy as having conflated them; a philosopher of music with formalist sympathies who cares about absolute music; two or three doctoral students steeped in recent analytic work on the emotions in the arts; and a practising artist who teaches on our joint programme and resents any theory that makes art answerable to an external purpose. Nobody in the room is a Tolstoy specialist, and most will have met the book only through summaries that treat it as an ageing crank's repudiation of culture.

Please prepare me as follows:

- The eight to ten objections I will actually face from this particular room, ordered by how much damage each would do if left unanswered — not the objections a literary theorist or a historian of Russian thought would raise.
- Each objection phrased in the vocabulary its raiser would use: the Collingwoodian will not put it the way the institutionalist does, and the artist will not put it the way either of them does.
- For each, a reply I can deliver in about two minutes of discussion, a note on where that reply is softest, and an indication of whether it is better met by concession or by resistance.
- What I should concede in my opening five minutes in order to pre-empt the first round altogether.
- The single reply that has to land for the chapter to survive the afternoon.
- The question I am hoping nobody asks: the one my argument cannot presently answer. I suspect it concerns whether the criterion is forced to rank a technically superb piece of propaganda above a slight but honest landscape, but tell me if there is a worse candidate in this room.
- Where my characterisation of Tolstoy is itself vulnerable to the charge that I have produced a rational reconstruction rather than an interpretation, since at least two people present will want to know whether I am defending Tolstoy or merely myself.
