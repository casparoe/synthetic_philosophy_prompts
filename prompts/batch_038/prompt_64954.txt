I am a senior, and this question comes out of my AP Physics class, though it is really about philosophy. When we reached special relativity, my teacher explained the transition from Newtonian mechanics by way of Thomas Kuhn's idea of a paradigm shift, and he illustrated that idea with the duck-rabbit drawing. His version went like this: the lines on the page never change, but a Newtonian physicist sees a duck and an Einsteinian sees a rabbit, the flip between the two is instantaneous and not something you can talk anyone into, and there is no fact about which of the two the drawing really is. From that picture he drew two conclusions he was quite firm about. First, that scientists on opposite sides of a revolution cannot genuinely argue with one another, because they do not share a framework in which reasons could count, so allegiance shifts by something closer to conversion than to proof. Second, that it is therefore simply not true that science progresses: later theories are not better, only different, and to say relativity improved on Newton is to describe the rabbit in duck vocabulary.

My older sister, who is in her second year of a philosophy program, thinks he has pushed the picture far past what it can bear. Her objection is that Kuhn uses the drawing for one narrow point about perception, that he thinks scientists on both sides share standards such as accuracy, simplicity, and fruitfulness and do argue from them, and that the real difficulty of comparison lies in the meanings of terms rather than in anything like an ambiguous figure. She also thinks the analogy cannot support the claim that relativistic mechanics is no improvement, but she could not explain why.

Could you audit the analogy for me? Specifically:

1. Give the mapping explicitly, ideally as a table: the printed figure, the two ways of seeing it, the sudden flip, the observer who can see both, the person who points out the alternative reading, and the fact that the ink never changes. Say what plays each of those roles in a transition between physical theories, and flag any element of the drawing that has no counterpart at all.

2. Sort the disanalogies into the ones that actually damage my teacher's two conclusions and the ones that are merely inelegant. I suspect several of the obvious complaints, for instance that a real revolution takes generations while a flip takes a second, are beside the point, and that something else is doing the real work.

3. Address what I take to be a tension in his own presentation. He insists there is no fact about which animal the drawing is, but he also insists you can be shown the other reading and simply see it, which seems to require standing outside both readings at once and treating them as two available ways of taking one stable object. If that outside standpoint exists for the figure, does it not also exist for the theories, and does his first conclusion survive the point?

4. Tell me whether a picture built on perception can accommodate the things my teacher and I both take for granted about physics: that Newtonians and Einsteinians agree about most measurements, that both sides appeal to the same experimental results when they disagree, and that a Newtonian can learn to compute with relativistic mechanics without being converted. If the answer is that it cannot, identify what the perception picture is quietly importing, for example the idea that disagreement here is a difference in how things look rather than a difference in what is claimed.

5. Say, for each of his two conclusions separately, whether it survives once the disanalogies are on the table, partially survives with a weaker statement, or collapses. If a weaker version survives, state it precisely enough that I could defend it in class.

6. Finally, propose a better analogy for the difficulty of comparing theories across a revolution, and say what work it does that the duck-rabbit cannot. I would also like to know whether Kuhn himself offers a different image for the question of progress, one that allows development without a destination, and whether my sister is right that his use of the ambiguous figure is narrower than the way it is being used on me.
