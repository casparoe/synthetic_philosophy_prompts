**§1. The target.** Lewis analyzes a convention as a regularity R in the behavior of members of a population when they are agents in a recurrent situation S, such that it is common knowledge among them that everyone conforms to R, that everyone expects everyone else to conform, that everyone has approximately the same preferences over the available combinations of actions, and that everyone conditionally prefers to conform on the assumption that the others do — a conditional preference that would attach equally to some alternative regularity that would have solved the same coordination problem just as well. Two features do the explanatory work: the coordination structure, which makes R arbitrary but not indifferent, and common knowledge, which is supposed to be what converts a mere coincidence of behavior into a mutually recognized rule. My thesis is that the second feature is over-demanding in a way that is not benignly idealizing. There is a class of norms that are conventions by every ordinary test — regular, mutually expected, arbitrary in the relevant sense, and sanctioned when breached — whose persistence depends on their not being common knowledge.

**§2. The phenomenon.** Call these cryptic conventions. Each has two layers. The first-order layer is a regularity R: how long a comment may run before a talk's author answers it; who leaves the office at three on Fridays and who does not; what may be said, in front of whom, about a chair's conduct. The second-order layer is a rule A: do not acknowledge R as a rule. A is not merely unspoken — plenty of conventions are unspoken — but enforced, and enforced in a way that is itself unavowable, since to sanction someone for violating A is to acknowledge A. The sanctions are consequently vague and deniable: coolness, exclusion from an email thread, a remark whose target nobody can specify. The paradigm case is a firm in which partners depart early on Friday and associates follow, and in which the person who raises it at a meeting is treated as having committed a serious breach of a rule nobody can name.

**§3. The bottleneck.** The argument is meant to be short. (1) If A is common knowledge in the population, then each member's iterated beliefs about A are, for that member, justified. (2) In a cryptic convention the only evidence available to a member about the higher-order attitudes of others is behavior. (3) Unavowed behavior is equally consistent with knowing A and complying, with not knowing A at all, and with knowing A and being indifferent to it. (4) So behavior alone cannot justify the iterated beliefs that common knowledge of A requires. (5) The one act that could supply that evidence — stating A and observing agreement — violates A and is costly. Therefore the iterated beliefs constitutive of common knowledge of A cannot be evidentially grounded for members; at best each holds a well-founded guess. Yet the norm is stable, its breach is reliably punished, and its beneficiaries reliably profit. Something in Lewis's picture has to give.

**§4. Replies I anticipate.** (a) Common knowledge does not require avowal: knowledge is a state, not a speech act, and iterated mutual belief can accumulate through observation. My response is that observation accumulates knowledge of R but cannot, by (3), discriminate the second-order state, and that the reply slides from the true claim that a convention need not be stated to the false claim that its higher-order structure can be shown. Lewis's own account of how common knowledge typically arises — through a conspicuous public event that gives everyone the same evidence at the same time — is precisely the route that A blocks. (b) Impure coordination: Lewis allows conflict of interest inside coordination, so hierarchical cryptic conventions fall within his scope anyway. My response is that this concedes too much in the wrong direction; once the reason A exists is that some members profit from keeping R off the books, the explanation is no longer coordinative, and the clause requiring approximately the same preferences does not describe anyone's attitudes at the second order. (c) Pluralistic ignorance: on that picture nobody privately endorses R while everybody believes everybody else does. I distinguish cryptic conventions on the ground that members do endorse R privately — they want the short comment period, they want to leave on Friday — and what is disavowed is not the content of the rule but its status as a rule. I am not confident this is enough, and this is the section I most expect to be attacked on.

**§5. The proposal.** Split Lewis's publicity requirement into an epistemic condition E (iterated mutual belief, however grounded) and a verificatory condition V (the availability, at non-prohibitive cost, of a public act by which any member could confirm the higher-order states). Paradigm conventions satisfy both. Cryptic conventions satisfy E in a degraded, guess-based form and fail V outright. The distinction buys predictions: sanctions under a cryptic convention must remain vague, because precision would identify the rule; such conventions collapse rather than reform upon first articulation, since articulation moves R into the public register where it must be justified; they cannot be transmitted by instruction, so they require low turnover or long overlap between cohorts; they cluster in hierarchies where the beneficiary of R controls whether avowal gets uptake; and outsiders identify them far more readily than participants do, which is why such norms are described fluently in ethnographies and denied in the field.

**§6. Stakes.** If some conventions are cryptic, then the picture of convention as a public device that its participants can in principle survey, criticize, and revise is not an idealization but a falsehood for exactly the class of cases that matter politically. A norm that cannot be avowed cannot be cited in justification, cannot be deliberated about, and cannot be voted down; the only criticism available to it is criticism that will be met with denial. Whether common knowledge is required for convention thus marks the boundary between norms available to collective revision and norms structurally immune to it.
