My roommate thinks he's refuted Quine and I can't find the exit. Here's the argument roughly as he gives it:

(1) Radical translation: you can write two manuals for the natives' language that come out identical on every scrap of observable evidence — every assent and dissent, in every situation, forever — and yet one renders gavagai as rabbit and the other as undetached rabbit part.

(2) If there were a fact of the matter about what gavagai means, one of those manuals would match it better than the other.

(3) Neither does, by construction. So there's no fact of the matter about what gavagai means.

(4) And the move doesn't need the natives. Do it to English: reinterpret rabbit as undetached rabbit part, Fido as the fusion of Fido's parts, and so on down the line, adjusting the rest of the vocabulary so the two readings always come out together. Every sentence anyone could ever utter, and every observation anyone could ever make, is preserved.

(5) So there's no fact of the matter about what my own words mean either.

(6) Quine's sentences are made of words. So there's no fact of the matter about what they mean, including the ones stating the indeterminacy thesis.

(7) Where there's no fact of the matter about what a sentence means, the sentence doesn't say anything.

(8) So the indeterminacy thesis is not even false — it's empty. And so is the argument that just established it, which my roommate seems very pleased about.
