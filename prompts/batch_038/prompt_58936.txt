I've got an upper-level decision theory seminar next term — 16 students, mostly juniors. They've done expected utility, Newcomb, the usual, and they can all recite why causal and evidential reasoning come apart. What they have not had is the updatelessness material, and I want them to run into it themselves rather than hear me describe it, so nothing in the handout should name the view until the very end. One 75-minute session, whiteboard only (no projector), materials limited to index cards, pens, and a handful of d6s. No money and no grade stakes — I tried participation points once and half the class just optimizes the points instead of thinking, which defeats the purpose.

Here's what I want the game to do. Early on, every student writes down and seals a policy, before they know which situation they'll land in. Then some of them get told the relevant outcome and some don't, and the ones who get told should end up worse off then the ones kept in the dark. I want them to feel that free information can cost you something. And the numbers have to make the ex-post choice look genuinely better to the person making it: if a student who's been told can see at a glance that sticking to their sealed policy is a sucker's move, they'll smell the rigged lesson and shut down.

I piloted something in this shape last spring and it fell apart two ways. First, the flip was public — everyone saw the result — so the whole thing degraded into an ordinary reputation game where keeping your word pays and there's nothing distinctively updateless left. Second, the students cast as "already knew all along" had no decision to make at all and got bored for fifteen minutes.

So give me the full thing: rules, materials list, a payoff table with actual numbers, the sealed-policy mechanic, a scoring procedure I can run by hand on the whiteboard in under four minutes, and a minute-by-minute timeline. Everyone makes at least two real decisions and nobody spectates. Then the debrief — which moments in play map onto which parts of the idea, and the questions that get them to say out loud that the dispute is about where you're standing when you compute the expectation rather than about who knew what. Also what predictably goes wrong the first time I run this and what I do in the moment, including which single rule they'll argue about.

And please do the arithmatic for me rather than asserting it: show the sealed policy really is better from before the assignment, and show that one informed student, holding everyone else fixed, really does better by defecting. If both can't hold at once with numbers this small, say so and tell me what has to give — my suspicion is that the tension between those two claims is exactly the thing I'm trying to teach.

Last thing, be straight with me about the biggest disanalogy. My hunch is that it's the fact that nobody in my room is being predicted by anything except each other, whereas the puzzles that motivate this view usually have the predictor reading your dispositions. Tell me if that's the real problem or if there's something worse I'm missing, and tell me what I'll have to supply by hand afterwards because the game simply can't produce it.
