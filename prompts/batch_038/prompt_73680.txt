I'm in a decision-theory reading circle that meets in someone's living room every other Thursday — a couple of grad students, a tax lawyer, and some people who just like this stuff. We spent about a month on superrationality (the Platonia dilemma, the Luring Lottery, that whole area), and then one member, who's well off and a bit of a showman, decided to actually run the Platonia thing on us instead of just talking about it. He put up $1,000 of his own money. Rules: the twelve of us each had until noon Friday to slip a sealed envelope with our name in it into a box in his hallway; if exactly one envelope ended up in the box, that person took the whole $1,000; if there were zero, or two or more, he kept his money; no discussing it with each other beforehand, obviously. Result: three envelopes, nobody got anything. The following week we did an anonymous debrief where everyone wrote down what they'd done and why, and it's worth mentioning because neither of the two people below ever brought it up: of the three who submitted, one wrote that she'd rolled a twelve-sided die and submitted because it came up 1, one wrote that he submitted because he figured most of us would be too squeamish to actually go for it, and one wrote that she'd flipped a fair coin and lost the flip. Of the nine who abstained, four wrote some version of "no way exactly one envelope happens, so why bother," and five wrote things like "didn't want to be the one who cost everyone the money."

Two members, Marta and Dev, got into a pretty heated argument right after the box was opened, and I was taking notes because I couldn't tell who was winning. It went roughly like this. (1) Marta said the correct play was obvious — everyone should have randomized and submitted with probability 1/12 using a genuinely random device — and three envelopes means almost none of us did anything remotely like that, which shows superrationality isn't a theory about how people decide at all, just a theory about imaginary people, so we should quit presenting it as a rival to decision theory. (2) Dev said she was conflating a normative claim with a psychological one: superrationality tells you what to do given that the others reason the way you do, and three envelopes only shows that most of us didn't. He added that ordinary decision theory is the one that can't even state the right answer here, since my envelope has no effect on anybody else's, so plain expected utility says submit — and if all twelve of us had done what plain rationality requires, there'd be twelve envelopes and no winner. The theory Marta wants to keep is the one that guarantees the loss. (3) Marta said fine, but then where does 1/12 come from? Not from any mysterious identity of our minds. It comes from ordinary arithmetic plus a symmetry assumption: if all twelve of us use the same probability p, the chance of exactly one envelope is maximized at p = 1/12. So the recipe survives intact with the metaphysics deleted, and the metaphysics — "we're all one computation instantiated twelve times" — never enters the calculation once. (4) Dev said the symmetry assumption is not free, and that's precisely the point. If I'm allowed to treat the other eleven as a fact about the world that I stand outside of, Marta's own arithmetic destroys her recipe: given that they each submit with probability 1/12, my expected take from just submitting is (11/12)^11 × $1,000, about $384, versus roughly $32 if I play along. The only thing stopping me from defecting from the recipe is that I can't coherently cast myself as an identical reasoner who nevertheless does something different — I'm one of the twelve instances, not a spectator of them. That premise is what does the work, and her stripped-down version can't reproduce it. (5) Marta said that's magical thinking with a better vocabulary: there's no contradiction whatever in reasoning correctly and then flipping a biased coin, and standing in that hallway with a quarter in her pocket she'd have known it. She said the premise can't be cashed out anyway, since the twelve of us are not the same computation — one of us is a teenager, one is a tax lawyer, and Dev himself had told two people at the previous meeting that he thought the whole topic was silly. Superrationality only applies when everyone in the group is superrational, we all knew that wasn't true of ours, so the theory made no prediction about our box, which means it can't be tested and can't be wrong. A theory that's only applicable in situations you can never verify you're in is a club, not a decision theory. (6) Dev conceded the "how similar is similar enough" hit — he said Hofstadter never handled asymmetric cases either and that's a genuine gap — but said "untestable" doesn't follow, because the theory says that if all twelve reason correctly, exactly one envelope shows up with probability about 38%, which is a sharp prediction, and our run failed to satisfy the antecedent, so it's a botched experiment rather than a counterexample. He also said the reference-class question isn't decorative, because it changes the number: if I think eight of us reason like me and four will submit no matter what, then "exactly one" is already impossible and the right play flips to abstaining altogether — a calculation Marta's one-line symmetry rule can't even start. (7) Marta said so they agree about what to do in the actual box and disagree only about the story, and the story is worse than useless because it teaches people that their own choice reaches out and moves other people's choices.

Can you go through this move by move and say who's right at each step? I don't just want a verdict on who won overall — I want to know which of Marta's hits actually land and which of Dev's replies answer them. Two specific things I'd like settled: is Dev right that plain decision theory forces "submit" in this game, with no mixed-strategy escape (I think there's no symmetric equilibrium here, but I'm not certain and neither were they), and is Marta right that the $384 deviation in (4) is genuinely available to her, or does taking it contradict something she's already granted? And then tell me what both of them are missing, if anything — the whole exchange felt like it was orbiting something neither of them ever named.
