Ok so contract law has always bugged me on one point, and I can't tell if it's a deep philosophical commitment or just institutional laziness dressed up as principle.

If I promise to give you my car for free, and you take me at my word — you sell your own car, drive across the country to collect it, quit something — and then I change my mind, you get nothing, because there was no bargained-for exchange. But run the identical promise through a signed and sealed document, or have you hand me one dollar for it, and now the same promise is enforceable, and no court is going to ask whether a dollar is really what a car is worth. The doctrine says out loud that it doesnt measure adequacy. Yet the same courts elsewhere get twitchy about what looks like a pretense of a bargain and refuse to enforce it, and there's the whole line of cases where a creditor promises to accept a lesser sum in satisfaction of a debt and then turns around and sues for the rest anyway even though the debtor did exactly what was asked.

What account of promising does all that presuppose? I keep oscillating between two readings. One is substantive: a promise only really binds when it's bought, when two wills have been joined in a trade, so a gratuitous promise is a different and lesser thing — not a promise proper. The other is procedural and kind of deflationary: the dollar and the seal aren't about obligation at all, they're a cautionary ritual plus a piece of evidence, a screening device for keeping trivial stuff out of court. Work out which of those the doctrine actually requires as opposed to which one it merely sits comfortably with. My suspicion is that it's underdetermined — that several incompatible theories of why promises bind would all license the same rules — and if so I want to know which observable features of the practice would settle it, if any could.

Then: if the procedural reading is right, the law isn't denying that free-standing promises are morally binding, it's just declining to be the institution that enforces them. Does that concession cost anything, or does it quietly collapse the whole idea that contract law is a branch of applied morality?

And the awkward part is reliance. American courts will enforce a no-consideration promise if you reasonably relied on it and were made worse off, which looks like the bargain theory losing to something else entirely. English courts have something similar but insist it works only defensively — you can't found a claim on it, only use it to fend one off. Does the reliance exception repair the bargain theory or replace it, and can one system coherently run both, or does one have to give way in the hard cases? Notice also that the defensive-only restriction makes the same promise enforceable or not depending on who sued first, which seems like a purely procedural fact doing moral work.

Finally, sketch what a law of promises would look like built from scratch on the view that a promise binds because the promisee is entitled to expect performance rather than because a price was paid for it. What would it start enforcing, what would it stop enforcing, which of the current rules above would come out obviously wrong on that view — and is there anything the bargain requirement is doing that the entitlement view just can't do at all?
