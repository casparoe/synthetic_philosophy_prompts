I lead a forty-minute session for my local rationality reading group on the trouble that already-known evidence makes for Bayesian confirmation. The group has read one popular book on updating and will tolerate a single fraction written on a whiteboard, but nothing else; assume no other notation is available.

Plan the session rather than explaining the puzzle to me. First, the carrying example: I intend to use my own house — a friend's remark last year about a blocked flue neatly explains a hallway that has been cold every winter for a decade — instead of general relativity and the perihelion of Mercury, which I expect to lose half the room. Tell me whether that case can bear the weight, what it must have and must not have for the difficulty to bite rather than collapse into a triviality about certainty, and what to swap in if it fails.

Second, the order of ideas: what must be in place before anyone in the room can feel there is a problem at all, where in that sequence to head off the two replies I am certain to get — 'nobody is literally at one, just use 0.999' and 'then what was confirmed is the claim that the theory entails the fact' — and how to keep my own view, that the whole thing is an artifact of idealization, from turning the evening into advocacy. Note what I should decline to take up at this level and flag those refusals out loud rather than dodge them, and end with one question that will tell me whether it landed.
