You are a philosopher of technology and global governance who has spent years inside debates about the consolidation of world order, and who is practiced at questioning people whose views you do not share. Before drafting anything, think carefully and open with a brief brainstorm — no more than a short paragraph — identifying where the position described below is genuinely vulnerable, where it is merely unfamiliar, and where it risks being trivially true.

I am a novelist. The antagonist of the book I am drafting is an intellectual who holds that a global singleton — a single coordinating authority able to set the terms of any large-scale undertaking anywhere on Earth — is not merely possible but increasingly unavoidable. Before I write her dialogue I want to interview a real scholar who defends a refined version of the thesis, and I need questions that would corner my character rather than flatter her. Her refined version carries two commitments I have supplied for the novel but that a serious defender could hold: a veto account of consolidation, on which a singleton is constituted by a single coordination point capable of blocking sufficiently large projects, whether or not it ever issues commands; and a ratchet claim, that once such a veto point exists it cannot be dismantled, because dismantling it returns the world to competition that rebuilds consolidation faster and in cruder form.

Draft eight to ten interview questions. I want at least one on the definitional line — which existing international arrangement comes closest to satisfying the veto account, and why it still fails, or whether it does fail — and a follow-up keyed to whichever way she answers. One on whether the veto account collapses the thesis into something every successful treaty regime already satisfies, with a follow-up to her likely reply that consolidation comes in degrees. One on the ratchet claim, asking her for the strongest historical case of a consolidated order that did unravel and was not rebuilt in cruder form, and a follow-up asking what she would have predicted beforehand. One on whether a singleton can remain internally competitive without ceasing to be one. And one forcing a registered prediction: a specific observable, within a stated horizon, that would distinguish her view from a committed multipolarist's.

For every question, give the deflection you expect — in her own idiom, not in a strawman's — and then the follow-up. Mark the two questions that matter most for my novel and say in one sentence why. Keep the wording neutral enough that she could read the list without feeling caricatured. Do not ask her for sources, and do not offer me any.
