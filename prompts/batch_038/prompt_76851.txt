I am preparing a submission to a workshop on eighteenth-century political economy whose call requires, in place of a normal abstract, a structured proposal in exactly five numbered points. Please draft it for me.

My half-formed idea. The received debate about Smith on empire pits two readings against each other: one says his attack on the colonial system is fundamentally moral, an indictment of the injustice Europeans did to the peoples they encountered and of the corruption of chartered companies; the other says it is fundamentally economic, a demonstration that monopoly and colonial defense are ruinous business, with the moralizing as garnish. What both readings leave unexplained is the closing move of the colonial chapter, where Smith sketches a scheme for admitting the colonies to proportional representation in parliament in proportion to their contribution to imperial revenue — a scheme that would eventually carry the seat of government across the Atlantic, and which Smith himself, if I recall correctly, dismisses as something no one will ever agree to. My hypothesis is that this unadoptable scheme is not a policy recommendation at all but an instrument: it forces onto the page an explicit accounting of who bears the empire's costs and who enjoys its monopoly profits, and thereby does the normative work that neither the moral nor the economic reading can do on its own. If that is right, the scheme functions as a limiting case that makes separation look like the only honest option rather than a failure of statecraft.

So the question I want the proposal to put precisely is what argumentative job the representation scheme is doing in Smith's case against colonies, and how one could tell that reading apart from the alternatives.

Deliverable: five numbered points, each one or two sentences, covering (1) the question, stated so that it admits of a determinate answer; (2) why the two standing readings leave it open; (3) the method I would actually use and why that method rather than the obvious alternatives, such as contextual intellectual history or an inventory of Smith's moral language; (4) what a successful result looks like and what would count as the project's failure; (5) an honest assessment of whether this fits a single semester's paper of about five thousand words, and what I would have to cut to make it fit. Paraphrase Smith rather than quoting him, since I will supply all quotations and citations myself. No introduction, no closing remarks, no bibliography — the five numbered points only.
