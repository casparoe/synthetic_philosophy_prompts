I was listening to a podcast episode called something like "Why Empires Never Pay," where the host — an economic journalist, sounds well read and very sure of himself — did a segment on Adam Smith and the American colonies. Here's how he summed up Smith's position:

"Smith looked at Britains colonial system in 1776 and did the actual accounting. Defending North America had cost enormous sums, the colonies paid no revenue to the crown, and the whole arrangement existed to protect a trade monopoly for a handful of merchants. So he reckoned Britain didn't really have an empire in America at all, just a fantastically expensive plan for one, and he told the government to cut its losses and let the colonies go — basically decolonisation two centuries early. And notice what he was *not* doing: he wasn't making a moral case. Smith didn't think empire was unjust, he thought it was a bad deal. He was happy to call the discovery of America and the opening of the sea route to India the biggest things that ever happened in human history; Europe just happened to be the beneficiary and the natives the ones who paid for it. He could also see where it was heading — he expected the Americans to outgrow Britain, and figured that once the empire broke up the economic center of gravity would drift west across the Atlantic by itself."

That last part sat wrong with me, mainly because I have a vague memory of reading that Smith proposed something like the opposite: giving the colonies seats in Parliament in proportion to what they paid, and arguing that the seat of government would then migrate to whichever part of the empire contributed most. If that's right, he wasn't forecasting America's rise *after* separation, he was sketching a federal union as the way to hold the thing together. But my memory is hazy and the journalist is confident, so I can't tell which of us has it backwards.

Could you take his summary apart piece by piece and tell me what a Smith specialist would sign off on, what's a defensible popular simplification, and what's flatly wrong or inverted? Three things I'd especially like pinned down: (a) did Smith really think the colonies delivered Britain no economic benefit whatsoever, or did he think the monopoly trade was genuinely lucrative for particular people, with his complaint being about where it shoved British capital — I have a feeling he argued it *raised* the ordinary rate of profit at home, which sounds like a good thing, so how does that end up being part of the indictment? (b) was "let them go" actually his recommendation, and where does the representation scheme fit relative to it? (c) is the "he didn't care about justice, only arithmetic" framing fair, given how he goes after the East India Company and what he says about Europeans' conduct toward indigenous peoples — including whether his argument that slave labour is dearer in the end is an efficiency point or something more complicated than that?

And then tell me whether the episode's headline conclusion survives the corrections: that Smith hands you a general cost-benefit proof that empire is always self-defeating, and therefore that any nostalgia for imperial grandeur is economically illiterate. For what it's worth I'm probably not a neutral judge — I've always assumed Smith was a free trader first and an anti-imperialist only by accident, which is exactly what the podcast told me I wanted to hear.
