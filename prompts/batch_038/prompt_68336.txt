I'm a staff software engineer at a regional health network. I have no formal training in philosophy, and I'm not asking you for philosophy — I'm asking you for a speech. But the speech has to survive contact with an argument I only half understand, so let me lay out the situation carefully.

The situation. Two winters ago a freeze followed by an electrical fire knocked out our water treatment plant, and for nineteen days we could deliver roughly sixty percent of our normal dialysis sessions. I built the system that decided who got treated on schedule and who waited. It ran in two stages. Stage one was a model, written by clinicians and shipped by me, that ranked patients by how safely they could be deferred. Stage two handled ties: among patients the model scored alike, we drew lots with a random number generator instead of running a first-come-first-served queue. I chose the lottery for a reason I would not have said out loud at the time — a random draw is auditable, it produces no defensible-looking outliers, and it means no one on my team has their own signature next to one specific person's name. Afterwards I read a paper by a philosopher named Taurek arguing, as I understand it, that a bystander to a forced choice between one person and five is not entitled to add their losses into a single number and spend it; there is no one who loses the sum, only individuals each of whom loses everything; and so the respectful response is to give each person a chance rather than to be generous with lives that are not yours to give. That reversed my own account of what we had built: the lottery was the part of the system that treated people as individuals, and the score was the part that did not.

The occasion. The families asked for, and the network has installed, a plaque in the main lobby naming the eleven patients who were deferred and died before capacity returned. I have been asked to speak at its dedication, for about five or six minutes, as the person who built the tool. There will be roughly a hundred and twenty people: the bereaved families; the nurses and dialysis technicians who worked those shifts; network executives; and eleven engineers from my team. One of those engineers learned afterwards that her father's name is on the plaque. She is still on my team.

The constraints, all of them hard. First, our general counsel has reviewed the program and prohibits any statement that the protocol was wrong, that any of the eleven deaths was avoidable, or that the network failed. Second, the family committee has said in writing that they do not want to be told their person's death saved others, and they do not want the phrases 'tradeoff', 'the greater good', or 'we had no choice'. Third, I will not say either sort of thing, so the speech has to stand in the corridor left between them — and I believe that corridor is exactly the ground Taurek's argument occupies: no summing, no exoneration, and no regret dressed up as an admission. Fourth, the speech may not name Taurek or any other philosopher, and may not use any term of art; nevertheless it must contain one moment, clear enough for a room where nobody has studied decision theory, in which people can hear why drawing lots honored the eleven and why ranking them did not. Fifth, it must not pretend my team reasoned its way to the lottery out of principle — I picked it for auditability — and so it may not retrofit a motive we never had, though it may show what it means that the accident and the principle point the same way. Sixth, there must be a passage that any one of the eleven families can hear as addressed to their person alone rather than as one of eleven. Seventh, it must be speakable: first person, plain sentences, nothing that assumes a slide behind me.

What I want back: exactly five numbered movements, 120 to 150 words each, in delivery order, each one a continuous stretch of what I would actually say aloud. No preface, no stage directions, no gloss explaining what a movement is doing, and nothing at all before movement 1 or after movement 5.
