Here's what's bugging me. In Finland every November the tax administration hands the press the tax data of everyone above an income threshold, in an annual ritual people half-jokingly call "envy day," and the underlying records are public for anyone. In Norway the tax lists are searchable online and the official rationale is that publicity lets society check whether assessments are being done correctly; since 2011 you have to log in to search, newspapers can't republish searchable lists, and the person you looked up can see that you looked. In Sweden you can still ask the tax agency for a stranger's taxable income, though they narrowed it recently so itemised employment and business income isn't released while the summarised figure still is. Meanwhile in the US an individual return is confidential by federal statute — disclosing it without consent is a crime — and yet federal and state employee salaries sit in free searchable public databases that newsrooms maintain, and when a newsroom published leaked figures on what the richest Americans actually paid, opinion split between "prosecute whoever leaked it" and "this should have been public all along."

So: what does each regime presuppose? Transparency is usually sold as making the powerful visible to the powerless, but these tax lists mostly make neighbours visible to each other and check nobody in particular. Reconstruct the implicit theory — is publicity a condition of a fair cooperative scheme, a shame-based social regulator, a control on the tax authority, or something else? Tell me what the practices strictly require vs. what they merely fit well with, whether the US line (public money public, private money secret) is coherent or smuggles something in, which rival views each regime quietly rules out, and whether "transparency" even means the same thing in both — including which way the causality runs between transparency and trust. Also: that Norwegian login-and-notification tweak. If the point is public control, why make the looking itself stigmatised? Finally, what would have to change — socially, not just legally — if each side adopted the other's rule, and where is all this philosophically underdetermined?
