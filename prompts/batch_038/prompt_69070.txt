**Probes, Not Forces: Event-Scale and Condition-Scale Environmental Causation**

§1. The charge of "environmental determinism" is normally a charge about causal weight: that the environment does too much of the explaining and human beings too little. Defenders and critics of climate-informed history both accept this framing and then argue about magnitude. I want to argue that magnitude is not where the disagreement lives. It lives in what I call the temporal grammar of a claim — in what kind of thing an environmental cause is taken to be, and consequently in what shape of evidence could confirm it. Once the grammars are separated, most determinism accusations turn out to be aimed at claims nobody in the current literature is making, while most of the new empirical work turns out to be exposed to a charge it has never answered. This also, I think, explains why the now-familiar thesis that human beings have become a geologic force, collapsing the two scales of agency into one another, feels urgent and yet does no explanatory work when it is imported into an actual case study.

§2. Call a cause *condition-scale* when it is standing, slow, spatially extended, and fungible: a disease pool, a stock of domesticable species, a rainfall regime, a topography of passable mountains. Call it *event-scale* when it is dated, pulsed, and non-fungible: the eruptions of the mid-sixth century, the arrival of plague in Roman Egypt in 541, a harvest failure on the eve of revolution. The distinction is not one of size but of counterfactual structure. Condition-scale claims are tested by differences across space at roughly a single time, and they are compatible with an enormous range of actual histories — which is precisely why they invite the determinism charge and why the best-selling versions of them are so hard to falsify and so easy to resent. Event-scale claims are tested by ordering within a timeline: if an event-scale cause is doing work, it should impose a date, a duration, and a recovery trajectory on the social record. The current wave of work — ice-core sulfate spikes, tree-ring anomalies, ancient DNA, speleothems — is overwhelmingly event-scale in its evidence, yet its practitioners routinely cash it out in condition-scale conclusions (the eruption caused the crisis; the cooling caused the migration). That cashing-out requires a baseline about what would otherwise have happened which their data cannot supply. This is the smuggling, and here the critics are right.

§3. Whether an event-scale cause leaves a signature depends on what I call *absorptive slack*: stored grain, fiscal redundancy, transport capacity, demographic structure, the absence of a competing crisis. But slack is not a natural condition — it is the sediment of prior political and economic choices. So event-scale causes are indexed to condition-scale ones, not deterministically, but in the sense that the event discloses the slack rather than producing the outcome. This is what I mean by probe causation: the eruption does not push a society somewhere; it puts a question to the society, and the answer is a function of what the society already was. I am aware that this threatens to collapse into the familiar legal-philosophical contrast between a cause and a mere background condition, which is roughly what my ecologist friend was pointing at, and I have no fully satisfactory reply.

My case study is the dispute over the sixth-century plague, which is usually reported as a fight about death tolls. On my reading it is a fight about pulse structure. Catastrophe-oriented accounts treat the epidemic as a single massive demographic event; the revisionists observe that the expected signature — abandoned settlements, mass burial, a break in the pollen and ceramic record — is largely missing, and infer a modest effect. Both sides are reasoning with a single-pulse model. But the plague's own signature is recurrent, with returns at intervals across decades, and a pulse train of moderate mortality has a very different counterfactual from one pulse of the same total mortality, because it repeatedly interrupts recovery and can cumulatively consume slack that no single pulse would touch. If that is right, it partially dissolves the standoff: the revisionists are correct that there is no single-pulse signature, and the catastrophe accounts are correct that something accumulated.

§4. Hence a proposed discipline, the *signature test*. For an event-scale claim to be more than post hoc, the claimant must specify in advance the shape predicted — not merely that a crisis occurred near an anomaly, but that the crisis begins where the anomaly begins, persists roughly as long as the anomaly's expected effect, and recovers along a trajectory set by the pre-existing slack — and must also say what the record would look like if the environmental cause were absent. Without that, the practice degenerates into what I call alignment-fitting: because proxy series are noisy and documentary and archaeological series are sparse and unevenly dated, some anomaly can nearly always be located near some crisis. The multiple-comparisons problem here is structural rather than a matter of individual bad faith.

§5. Payoff. The account offers a way to say the environment is a cause without determinism, since probes are not forces; a diagnosis of why the debate has been stuck; and a discipline for the new empirical history that does not require historians to become modelers. I concede the awkward case: a cooling episode running from the 530s to around 660 is too long to be an event and too abrupt to be a condition, and my binary may be a false dichotomy that the most-discussed case in the field immediately falsifies.
