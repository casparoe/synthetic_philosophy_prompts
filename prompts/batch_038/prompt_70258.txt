I direct patient safety at an academic medical center, and six weeks ago the city council appointed me to its civilian review commission on policing, which was created after an officer-involved death last spring. I have spent my whole career inside one very particular way of thinking, and I am finding that I cannot hold that vocabulary and the reform-defund-abolish literature in my head at the same time. I would like help making the collision explicit rather than papering over it.

Here is what I actually work in: the hierarchy of controls (elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls, personal protective equipment); Just Culture and its three-way distinction between human error, at-risk behavior, and reckless conduct; root cause analysis; work-as-imagined versus work-as-done; normalization of deviance; the high-reliability organization principles — preoccupation with failure, reluctance to simplify interpretations, sensitivity to operations, commitment to resilience, deference to expertise; reporting culture, blame, second victims; and the ordinary machinery of incident review, corrective action plans, and audit. I am fluent in all of it. I am not fluent in political philosophy, and I am not looking for a tutorial on the intellectual history of sovereignty.

The task: restate each of the three positions in the policing debate — the professionalization-and-reform position, the defund-and-reallocate position, and the abolitionist position — in that vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage. Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually want. For each position, tell me which of its central claims translate cleanly; which translate only with distortion, and precisely what the distortion consists in; and which cannot be expressed in my vocabulary at all.

Two things I would like you to be strict about. First, do not launder safety-engineering assumptions in as translation. If a claim can only be rendered in my terms by presuming that the activity under review is legitimate and merely hazardous, say that you have presumed it, rather than presenting the result as that position's own content. Second, where you find yourself reaching for a term my vocabulary does not have, resist approximating.

Three specific questions, because I have guesses about them and would prefer they be tested rather than flattered:

- Does the abolitionist claim that the function itself is illegitimate map onto elimination as the top rung of the hierarchy of controls, or does that mapping quietly convert a political argument into a managerial option that no one in my world would recognize as available?
- I suspect that defund-and-reallocate is substitution, and that substitution is the rung safety practitioners invoke most often and analyze least. If that is right, what does it cost the defund position to be stated in those terms?
- Reform through training, revised policy, and body-worn cameras looks to me like a stack of administrative controls with one engineering control sitting on top. Is there anything in the reformist argument as its advocates actually make it — not as a straw man — that is not an administrative control, and does my framework have any way of registering it?

Finally, run the comparison in the other direction: is there anything my safety vocabulary lets me see about this debate that the political-philosophical framing tends to obscure? I am prepared for the answer to be that the residue is a defect in my framework rather than in theirs, but I would like to be told which it is, and why.
