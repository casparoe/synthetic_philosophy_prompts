Ive been chewing on a setup for a while and I want it written as a short story, maybe 1100-1400 words. Not a plot-twist story, a problem story.

The setup: a senior flavorist at a mid-size flavor house has been the sign-off authority on one legacy formula for about 25 years — a strawberry base that goes into a grocery product people have been buying since before she had the job. Every quarter she pulls the sealed control lot, tastes it, and fills out a card in her own shorthand. Her verdict has drifted: what she used to call bright and jammy she now calls thin and faintly chemical. Nobody else on the panel goes with her. A cost-cutting reformulation is on the table, and the company needs her signature saying the cheaper version is indistinguishable from the legacy, and she can't honestly give it — but she also can't say whether the trouble is in the bottle or in her.

The engine of the thing is that there are two live hypotheses about her and she cannot get between them from the inside. One: her experience of the base actually changed, it genuinely tastes different to her now than it did in year one. Two: her experience is exactly what it always was and what changed is the judgment laid on top of it — her vocabulary, her standard for what counts as good, her attitude — so she is now reporting a different feeling *about* the same taste. She's smart, she's honest, she has sat down and actually tried to introspect the difference, and the introspection comes up empty in precisely the same way under both.

Constraints, please actually honor these:

1. Five scenes, numbered 1 through 5, one or two paragraphs each. Nothing before scene 1 and nothing after scene 5 — no title, no epigraph, no closing note explaining what it meant.
2. One scene where instrumental analysis (GC-MS, whatever's realistic in a flavor lab) comes back and shows the control lot has not changed at all. Let that feel like a resolution for a beat before it becomes obvious it answered a different question than the one she's stuck on.
3. One scene with a rival — a younger sensory scientist who has the panel data — arguing that her discrimination is intact, she still nails the legacy in every triangle test, so what moved has to be the liking or the standard, not the tasting. Make this argument actually good. She should not have a clean answer to it.
4. Give her a personal artifact: her own quarter cards from year one, in a naive hand, raving about the exact note she now calls chemical. Let her read them and get no help whatsoever from them.
5. Don't resolve it. No revelation scene, no dream, no dying mentor who explains it, no line along the lines of *maybe it was never about the strawberry*. The two hypotheses end exactly as close as they began. What she *does* in the last scene can be concrete — sign, refuse, ask for a re-panel, write a card — but pick an act that both hypotheses would predict equally well.

Also: nobody in the story says qualia, consciousness, phenomenology, or "what it's like to." Keep all of it in the bottles and the cards and the arguments. Realistic flavor-industry texture, no sci-fi, and no magic-nose superpower — she's good at her job, not supernatural at it.
