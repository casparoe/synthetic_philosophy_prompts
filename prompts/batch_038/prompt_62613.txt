Last autumn I sat in on the final two sessions of a reading group my sister hosts in her living room. It is mostly non-specialists — a couple of teachers, a retired structural engineer, two graduate students in unrelated fields — though two regulars, Marcus and Ada, have each worked through a fair amount of philosophy on their own and treat the meetings as an excuse to spar. The assigned book was a memoir about a mountaineer who, by the unanimous testimony of his family, became a different man after a serious fall: bold and restless before it, timid and housebound afterward. Discussion was supposed to concern the reliability of that testimony, but within twenty minutes Marcus and Ada had migrated to the underlying question of what it takes for anything at all to be the same thing at two different times, and they went at it for the better part of two meetings without resolution. Below is my reconstruction of the exchange, which I wrote up from my notes as faithfully as I could; I've kept the order of moves intact, and I've tried not to sharpen either side beyond what was actually said.

(1) Ada opened by noting that the memoir's central claim looks self-contradictory on its face: the same man is described as fearless and as frightened of everything. Nothing has gone wrong, she said, provided we stop treating "he is fearless" as a complete assertion. It never was one. It was always shorthand for his being fearless at some stretch of his life. Change is just one and the same thing having incompatible properties at different times, and once the times are written in explicitly, the contradiction evaporates and there is no mystery left to explain.

(2) Marcus replied that she had not explained change but renamed it. "Fearless-at-t1" and "fearful-at-t2" are two properties, not one thing with one property and then another; the time has been smuggled into the predicate, and the congratulation for avoiding contradiction is unearned. What we wanted to know was how a single object can literally go from being one way to being another. Her answer describes a succession of time-stamped conditions and calls the succession an object.

(3) Ada said that was a fair challenge, and offered her own picture. On Marcus's view, the entire man — the whole person, from birth to death — is fully and completely present in the hospital room after the fall. She found that incredible. Being born in a certain year and dying are things true of this man; nothing sitting in that room has those features. What is in the room is a piece of him, and the man is the whole thing spread out across the decades, of which that piece is a part. On this account, "fearless at t" makes a substantive claim — namely, that the part of him located at t is fearless — rather than a disguised tautology.

(4) Marcus said she had purchased the appearance of an explanation with an article she cannot afford. What makes the hospital slice and the pre-fall slice parts of a single man rather than two successive men who happen to resemble one another? She clearly needs some bonding relation, and the only candidates are relations of causal and psychological continuity. But then she has changed the subject, since what he wants to know is why the very same man went on existing, not why an appropriately connected successor turned up. On her account, a continuous imposter would satisfy every condition she has stated, and she would be obliged to say nothing had changed.

(5) Ada answered that he owes her the same demand in reverse. He says the identical man is present before the fall and after it. What makes that true? Either it is a bare, ungrounded fact — in which case endurance is simply declared inexplicable and his "one and the same thing" is doing no work — or it is grounded in continuity, in which case he is borrowing her bonding relation and has no grounds for denying her the parts it binds. He cannot insist on an account of what unifies her worms while refusing to give an account of what makes his substance numerically one over time.

(6) Marcus allowed a third possibility: the identity is grounded in the man himself, since he belongs to a kind of thing whose nature is to persist, and demanding a further ground is demanding the wrong sort of thing. He then said her view carries a cost she has not mentioned. If the man is the whole extended thing and what occupies the hospital room is a part, then, strictly speaking, nothing in that room is the man. His fear belongs to a part of him, and the sentence "he is afraid" is true only in the derivative way in which "the committee is split" is true because its members are. That concession is expensive: fear, planning, regret, and grief all presuppose that the one who decides now is the one who will bear the consequences. On her account the decider is one part and the sufferer another.

(7) Ada said the objection proved too much. He has already conceded that the man's decision now and his suffering later are joined by exactly the relations she appeals to — memory, character, intention, causal connectedness. If those relations suffice to make her parts belong to one worm, they are also everything he has available to make his enduring man one man. He is welcome to decorate the relation with the word "identity," but he has not shown that the decoration adds anything.

Second, tell me which side, if either, is ahead when the exchange ends, and about which specific issue — the analysis of "at a time" predications, the reality of the parts, the demand for grounds of unity, or the status of fear and anticipation. Third, say whether any single move is a stalemate-breaker, or whether all seven are compatible with both positions surviving intact, and if the latter, explain what that would show about the disagreement. Fourth, identify the considerations a competent referee would regard as important that neither Marcus nor Ada raised, and state whether supplying them would have broken the deadlock. I am especially interested in whether moves (5) and (6) are as symmetrical as the group assumed at the time, since half the room left thinking Ada had landed the decisive blow and half thought Marcus had, and I could not tell which impression was warranted.
