so my practical ethics tutor asked us each to write down where we actually stand on duties of rescue before we do the famine week, and I want to know if my set hangs together before I hand it in. Here's what I wrote:

(1) if you can prevent something very bad without giving up anything comparably important morally, you ought to — the shallow pond case is what convinces me, and I don't think it needs any aggregation of consequences to work.
(2) distance, nationality and how many bystanders are standing around doing nothing are all morally irrelevant. A child drowning in front of you and a child dying of malaria four thousand miles away are the same situation.
(3) but I don't accept giving until marginal utility. The requirement is a substantial fixed share of income — for me maybe 10% — and past that, giving more is good but optional.
(4) the reason for the cap isnt just that it'd be hard. Going far beyond your share lets governments and rich countries off the hook, and I think that changes what you should do, not merely how hard it is.
(5) I also have agent-relative reasons from promises, relationships and family that don't reduce to outcomes.
(6) in the pond case you have to wade in even if it ruins your suit and blows an interview you spent months prepping for, because that cost is trivial next to a life.
(7) comfortably-off non-donors are doing something wrong, but I don't think we should shame people, and I'd sooner praise the 10% giver than lecture the 0% one.

Go through these and tell me which ones genuinely pull against each other, worst first, and for each say what I'd have to give up and what that costs me. I'm pretty sure at least one pair only looks like a problem — say which and why. American English please.
