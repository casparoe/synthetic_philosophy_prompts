So — I write novels, and my last book is about a woman who runs a district land office in a country I've lived in for years but wont name on a stage. The whole thing turns on her deciding that the honest move is to keep taking the envelopes and the sacks of rice, because out there refusing a gift is what actually severs the obligation that was the only thing keeping her honest. An anti-corruption conference has asked me to give a plenary about it. Not a reading — a 20 minute talk and then questions, which is the part im scared of.

The claim I want to make is that the definition everybody in that room works from (abuse of entrusted power for private gain) already assumes a clean boundary between the office and the person holding it; that the boundary is a recent, specific achievement rather than a natural fact; and that once you assume it you start measuring the wrong thing and calling it corruption, while the lobbying and revolving-door traffic on your side of the line stops counting at all.

Audience: governance economists, people who run transparency NGOs and produce indices, a couple of public-law types, and one anthropologist who'll be sympathetic but think I'm being too neat. No novelists.

Can you build me a Q&A prep sheet? The objections this crowd will actually raise — not the ones a book-festival panel would — ordered by how much damage they'd do, each phrased the way one of them would phrase it, with a two-minute answer and an honest note about where my answer is thin. Then tell me what to concede in the first ninety seconds to buy credibility, which single reply has to land or the talk dies, and the question I'm praying nobody asks.
