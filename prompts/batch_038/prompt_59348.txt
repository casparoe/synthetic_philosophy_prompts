So my Less Wrong-adjacent reading group has been grinding through the fitting-attitude family of analyses of value — you know the drill, something is valuable (or admirable, or desirable) just in case it's fitting, or called for, or merited, to respond to it with some pro-attitude. And the standard wrecking ball is the wrong-kind-of-reasons cases: someone offers you a million dollars if you admire a boring old saucer of mud, or a demon threatens to punish you unless you desire something, and there's obviously a reason to have the attitude but that reason has nothing to do with the merits of its object.

Here's what I want from you, and please write the whole thing in American English. Give me an actual number — your credence, on the scale where 0.95 means you'd be surprised to learn otherwise — that this objection is ultimately *fatal*: i.e. that no fittingness-style analysis of the relevant normative property can be made correct, not merely supplemented or hedged, by the existence of reasons that bear on the attitude rather than on the object. Not "the literature is divided," not "it depends on your metaethics" — a number plus the derivation. Then decompose it into the sub-questions you think it actually turns on, with a rough probability and a sentence or two of justification for each, and show how they combine into the final figure. The pieces I'd expect to see, though feel free to add or swap: (a) that there really are normative reasons to have an attitude that don't track the object's merits at all, rather than those cases being better described as there being reason to *want to want*, or to cause the attitude in yourself, which is a subtly different thing; (b) conditional on (a), that those reasons are genuinely reasons of a different kind bearing on the attitude's fittingness itself, rather than reasons of the same kind in some different deliberative context — the state-given/object-given line somebody draws; (c) that no independently motivated restriction rescues the analysis — not the "reasons that figure in the right sort of deliberation" move, not the wrong-kind-of-content move where the reason is really about you and the demon rather than about the saucer, not the proposal that there are two notions of reason and the analysis only needs the object-directed one; and (d) the chance that the whole question is partly terminological — that once you pin down what "fitting" and "for a reason" are supposed to mean, a chunk of the disagreement evaporates and the residue is stipulation rather than discovery.

Two more things. First, tell me which of those terms your final number is most sensitive to — like, if I could settle exactly one sub-question with certainty, which one moves the headline number furthest, and roughly how far. Second, I want the honest version of what would move *you*: is it an intuition pump that reliably fires or fails to fire across people, a conceptual argument about what reasons even are, or evidence about how the folk actually deploy "admire for a reason"? I'd also love a sentence on which way the last couple of decades have been trending, and your own guess at where this lands in fifteen years, though flag clearly if that's you extrapolating rather than reporting something solid.
