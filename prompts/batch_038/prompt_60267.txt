so I'm a third-year history PhD student and next Thursday I'm workshopping a dissertation chapter at our department's "norms and institutions" series, which is basically an interdisciplinary room: four economic historians (two of them work on colonial trade and Atlantic shipping, one on English poor-law administration), two historical sociologists who do state formation, a legal historian who works on the statutes that made dueling outcomes criminal rather than matters of private settlement, and one philosopher who reads a lot of Bernard Williams and Appiah and will want to talk about honor versus dignity in exactly the vocabulary of those books. Nobody in the room specializes in abolition or slavery, which matters because my chapter claims to generalize and I only actually have the dueling case worked up.

The chapter's argument, roughly: dueling in Britain didn't die in the decades between the Napoleonic wars and the 1850s because anybody got persuaded that shooting your neighbor over an insult was wrong, and it didn't die because honor was swapped out for dignity. It died because the honor group lost its audience. The reputational labor that seconds, messmates, and clubroom bystanders used to do in person got progressively absorbed by print coverage and by promotion and posting rules inside the army, the navy, and the growing civil service — so the local crowd that used to be able to confer honor stopped being the crowd whose verdict you had to fear, and the insult was re-pointed at a different referee rather than abolished. I've coded about four hundred reported challenges and near-challenges in British and Irish newspapers between 1800 and 1860 for who the insult was addressed to and, when the affair ended, whose judgment was treated as settling it, and I use the shift in that second variable as my timing evidence against the print-and-commerce-and-reason story.
