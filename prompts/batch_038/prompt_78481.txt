In three weeks I present a chapter of my dissertation at a workshop co-hosted by my department's philosophy-of-social-science group and the university's centre for economic history — forty minutes of talk, twenty-five of discussion, and the convener has already told me he wants a real fight. The chapter is called *Forecasting Without Prophecy*. Its thesis is that cliodynamics, in the tradition running from Jack Goldstone's structural-demographic account of state crisis through Peter Turchin's work on secular cycles and elite overproduction, has crossed over in the last decade or so from prophecy about the course of history into what Popper would have counted as legitimate conditional prediction, and that the standard objection drawn from *The Poverty of Historicism* — roughly, that the future course of history cannot be foreseen because it depends in part on the future growth of knowledge, which cannot be foreseen — therefore no longer reaches macrohistory, since that objection was aimed at unconditional forecasts of direction, not at bounded, calibrated probability statements about specified outcomes within specified windows. The empirical half of the chapter is my own backtest. I fit a dynamic panel over forty-one states, annual observations from 1960 to 2010, with three composite drivers: real wage stagnation among non-supervisory workers; an elite-overproduction index built from the ratio of advanced-degree completers in law, business, and public policy to openings in high-status occupational categories, plus the price of entry into elite-adjacent positions (urban housing, professional-school debt); and an intra-elite fragmentation measure coded from contested nominations, donor defection, and primary spending. The outcome is a composite instability index from cross-national event counts. I hold out 2011–2024, produce three-year risk windows, and report that my calibration curve is decent and my Brier scores beat a persistence baseline, a growth-and-unemployment logistic model, and the aggregate of a well-known expert elicitation exercise. I explicitly concede in the chapter that I am not defending the periodicity claims about secular cycles, and that I cannot yet say which of my three drivers is doing the work; the philosopher of mechanisms in the room will notice that I have traded mechanism for calibration and will not let it slide.

The composition of the room matters more than the argument, so here it is as precisely as I can give it. Two philosophers of science: one who works on ceteris paribus laws and the special sciences and is broadly sympathetic to modelling without mechanism, and one generalist who teaches Popper to undergraduates every year and will insist on a careful reading of what he actually claimed rather than the folklore version. One economic sociologist who has written on the performativity of macroprudential stress tests and credit-scoring models — she will be the most dangerous person in the room, because she will grant my statistics and then ask what happens to the object once a published risk window changes the behaviour of the actors inside it. One Bayesian statistician who forecasts elections and epidemics and who thinks beating a persistence baseline is a low bar and that forty-one states is not a sample. Three economic historians, all archive-based, two of them working on early modern state finance and the third on colonial revenue administration, all of them allergic to composite indices and all of them likely to attack my proxies and my event counts as artefacts of what the sources happened to record, what got digitised, and how the coding scheme changed mid-series. Two or three graduate students who have read the popular cliodynamics books and will be warmly supportive in ways that embarrass me. I have been told the historians have already circulated a short joint comment, which I have not seen.
