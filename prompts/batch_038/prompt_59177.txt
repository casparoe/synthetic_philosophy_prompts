I am assembling a small set of engineered cases for a graduate seminar on the epistemology of the archive, and I should like you to construct one to my specifications rather than to describe a real historical episode.

**What the case is for.** My students arrive holding what I shall call the *abundance thesis*: that silencing in the historical record is fundamentally a matter of the first two moments Trouillot distinguishes in *Silencing the Past* (Beacon Press, 1995) — the creation of sources, and their assembly into an archive — so that it is the work of destruction, exclusion, censorship, neglect, and underfunding, and so that the remedy is straightforwardly more records, better kept, better described, and more openly available. I want a case that defeats this thesis *by construction*, and I want the defeat to be visible in the mechanics of the case rather than asserted in its narration. (For our purposes, take Trouillot's four moments as: the creation of sources; their assembly into an archive, with its selection, ordering, and description; their retrieval in the writing of narratives; and the attribution of retrospective significance to what has been retrieved. You need not defend or expound this schema; simply use it.)

**The brief.** Design a pair of archival collections covering the same institution over the same decades. Archive A is impoverished: partial, badly arranged, undescribed in places, physically scattered. Archive B is abundant: it holds several times as much material from the same source, it has been professionally appraised and arranged, its description is exemplary by the standards of the profession, it is digitized and freely searchable online, and it enjoys the confidence of the institution that created it. The demonstration to be achieved is this: a specified researcher with a specified question comes away from Archive B *worse off* than she would have been in Archive A — less likely to recover the fact, and, critically, less likely to suspect that anything is missing.

**Constraints.**

1. State the silenced fact at the outset in a single sentence: a precise question about the agency, interests, or voice of some identifiable group, answerable in principle from records that exist and are accessible.
2. No malevolence and no loss in Archive B. Nothing is burned, suppressed, forged, restricted, deaccessioned, or underfunded; nobody acts from prejudice or indifference; no reading room is closed to anyone. Every individual decision made in Archive B must be one a competent, well-intentioned archivist could defend as best practice at the moment it was made.
3. The abundance must be *causally* responsible for the silencing, not merely coincident with it. It must be true that subtracting some specified feature of Archive B — the extra material, or the exemplary description, or the digitization and search interface, or the arrangement scheme — would have made the fact *more* recoverable, not less.
4. The silenced material must be *present* in Archive B: the documents exist, they are retained, they are catalogued, and some of them are retrievable by a determined searcher. This is a silence in retrieval and in significance, not in creation.
5. The repair may not be "describe it better." Archive B's description is stipulated to be exemplary; the mechanism must therefore be structural — inhering in units of description, in the conceptual scheme of the finding aid, in aggregation, in search behaviour, or in the evidential self-confidence a rich collection induces — rather than in sloppiness.
6. Ordinary realism. A twentieth-century mundane administrative body (a port authority, a municipal utility, a nationalized industry, a land registry, a hospital group, a railway, a school board, a fishing or irrigation cooperative, or the like); an invented but concretely specified episode in a real kind of place; researchers with ordinary resources, ordinary patience, and ordinary professional motives. No search technology cleverer than a competent mid-2010s catalogue. Please avoid the canonical settings: plantation economies and colonial district administration, the transatlantic slave trade, the Holocaust, and residential or boarding schools.
7. Length and shape: the paired cases in roughly 400 words, no more than two paragraphs each; the demonstration in roughly 700 words; the objections and replies in roughly 300. Do not cite, quote, or recommend any literature — the case must stand entirely on its own for a student with no archival training, and any technical term you use must be glossed in passing.

**Required deliverables.**

- A step-by-step trace of the same researcher in each archive: what she finds, in what order, what she reasonably infers at each stage, and what she concludes on leaving. The two traces must be run against identical starting conditions.
- A compact table mapping the four moments for each archive, marking where a silence enters, of what kind, and where it conspicuously does not. The intended result is that Archive B is clean at the first moment and dirty at the last two; say plainly if your construction does not in fact produce that pattern.
- An identification of the mechanisms at work. Name no more than three and rank them by how much work each does.
- A single-sentence statement of which feature does *most* of the work, followed by an explicit ablation: what the case looks like, and what verdict it yields, once that feature is removed. A well-built case should collapse back into an ordinary fragmentary archive when the key feature is taken out.
- The two strongest objections, in the mouth of their best advocates, and what you concede to each. The first is a sceptic about silence-talk altogether, who holds that "silence" is a metaphor and that the only real questions are whether a document exists and whether a searcher can read it — on which view Archive B is unambiguously the greater epistemic good. The second is a working archivist who points out that appraisal is inescapable, that total capture is impossible for any repository, and that your case illicitly holds Archive B to a standard no archive could ever meet. Say what each objection costs you.
- A closing note, honest and brief, on the generality of the result: does this show something about large well-described digital collections as such, or only about collections bearing some further feature you have built in?

**What counts as failure here.** Stipulating the verdict instead of generating it. If the case tells us that researchers never find the material, or that the collection is arranged in a way no professional would defend, or that a biased archivist made the difference, it has assumed what it was supposed to exhibit. The silence must fall out of individually defensible practice, plus the ordinary and well-motivated behaviour of ordinary researchers, plus the sheer mass and coherence of what survives.
