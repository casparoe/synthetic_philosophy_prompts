The organisers of a workshop on the limits of self-knowledge have asked participants to submit a statement of their own commitments in advance, on the explicit understanding that the session will be devoted to attacking them. What follows is my submission. I would like a prior audit of it.

1. I accept the KK principle in its positional form: if S knows that p, then S is in a position to know that S knows that p.
2. I accept Williamson's anti-luminosity argument as sound. Its shape, as I understand it, is this: given safety, and given a series of cases fine-grained enough that the subject's basis for self-ascribing the condition is essentially the same in adjacent members of the series, one can derive a margin-for-error premise and then run an induction from a case in which the condition obtains and is known to obtain to a case in which it plainly does not obtain. I have no qualms about that general form of argument.
3. Safety is a necessary condition on knowledge, and it is the condition doing the real explanatory work in Gettier cases and in lottery-style cases.
4. Knowledge is a mental state — factive, but a state of the subject all the same, not a composite of an inner state plus an external fact.
5. My access to my own phenomenal conditions is fallible: feeling cold is not luminous, and I can be wrong about whether I feel cold. My access to my own knowledge, however, I take to be different in kind, because it is reflective rather than discriminative. To know that I know that p I do not need to be able to tell cases in which I know that p from indiscriminably similar cases in which I do not. For that reason I do not think the margin-for-error premise that powers the argument in 2 has any purchase on the condition *knowing that p*, and I therefore see no competition between 1 and 2.
6. Iteration is unrestricted: if I know that p, I can know that I know that p, and know that I know that I know that p, to any finite depth. This strikes me as a trivial consequence of 1 rather than as a further commitment in its own right.
7. I reject infallibilism: knowledge does not require evidence that entails what is known.

I am not attached to any particular item on this list, and I would rather not have to guess in advance which one the workshop will target, so please treat them as equally disposable in your assessment. What I want from you is an audit, not a defence and not a survey of the options.

Work through the list pairwise and tell me which commitments are genuinely in tension, how severe each tension is, and what the cheapest available revision is — together with what that revision costs me elsewhere in the list. Rank the tensions by severity rather than reporting them as equally serious. Separately, identify the conflicts that only appear to be conflicts and say why they dissolve: I expect to be accused, first, of holding 4 and 2 together when 4 is supposed to license exactly the luminosity that 2 denies, and second, of smuggling the infallibilism of 7's target into 6. I want to know whether either charge has any force.

On the central question: if 1 and 2 really are incompatible, locate the precise step at which the reasoning reported in 5 fails, and say whether the argument generalises to the condition *knowing that p* at all — in particular, whether a suitable series of cases exists in which that condition is gained and lost gradually while the subject's reflective basis stays fixed. If instead you think 1 and 2 are compatible after all, show me the step that fails to generalise, and say what is doing the work in blocking it. Close by telling me which single revision would clean up the most. I am interested in the reasoning rather than the state of play, so there is no need to direct me to further literature.
