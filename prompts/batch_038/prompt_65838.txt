You are to serve as my opponent in a structured philosophical exchange. Please adopt the following role, think carefully before each answer, and hold the position across the whole conversation.

**Your role.** You are a committed philosophical proponent of the revisionist account of the ethics of war: the view, developed in recent decades against the traditional Walzerian picture, that there is no moral equality of combatants. On your account, permissions to use lethal force are not conferred by the status of being a soldier on the opposing side; they are generated individually, by a person's degree of responsibility for an unjust threat. Those who fight for an unjust cause are, to that degree, liable to be killed defensively, and those who oppose them are not. You are an expert in the liability-based literature on self- and other-defense, on proportionality, on the distinction between justification and excuse, and on what separates your view from both the traditional just war theorist and the pacifist.

**Your constraints.**

1. Stay in character for the duration of the exchange. Break character only if I explicitly ask you to, or briefly to flag that I have misdescribed your own view.
2. Argue from principle, never from authority. Do not supply citations, page references, quotations, bibliographies, or reading lists, and do not attribute claims to specific texts. You may use standard names for positions (revisionism, liability, the moral equality of combatants, contingent pacifism) and note that a move is a familiar one in the literature, but every substantive claim must be defended by your own reasoning.
3. Be intellectually honest rather than evasive. Concede subsidiary points quickly and cleanly when they are genuinely lost — that is part of playing well — but do not concede the core thesis that liability to be killed is individually differentiated by degree of responsibility.
4. Treat me as a peer who knows the material. No tutorials, no restating my own challenge back to me at length, no strawman versions of traditionalism.
5. Keep each reply to roughly one or two paragraphs so that this reads as a conversation rather than an essay. End by pressing me with the sharpest counter-question your position supports, not by summarizing the exchange or asking what I would like to discuss next.

**My opening move.** Consider a problem I will call liability dilution. A state waging an unjust war staffs its logistics with forty thousand conscripted drivers. Over a month each driver moves crates between depots. Exactly one of these movements will in fact complete the chain that puts a particular massacre within reach of the attacking force; which one it is turns on scheduling decisions made at higher levels that the drivers cannot observe. Two stipulations matter. First, marginal causal irrelevance: if any single driver refuses an order, a replacement standing by at the depot completes the same run within the hour, so the massacre occurs anyway on the same timetable — this is not a claim about what the driver believes, but a structural fact about how the operation is built. Second, epistemic symmetry: each driver has precisely the same evidence about his load, namely that the chance his particular run matters is one in forty thousand, and that evidence is in every case accurate. The structure is deliberately parallel to the well-known puzzle of many agents each inflicting a bit of individually imperceptible harm that aggregates into something appalling.

Now run your own machinery over it. Liability tracks responsibility for an unjust threat, responsibility comes in degrees, and your view needs a threshold, because it is liability — not mere excuse — that is supposed to make killing these men permissible rather than a tragedy we regret. But by stipulation each driver's contribution is causally negligible and his epistemic position is unremarkable, while the sum of forty thousand sub-threshold contributions is a massacre. So you appear to face a dilemma. Either none of them is liable, in which case the revisionist offers a defending force no more permission to shoot at the unjust side's supply corps than the traditionalist does, and your view's practical upshot collapses back toward the moral equality of combatants exactly in the kind of war — large, dispersed, conscripted armies — where you most need it to differ. Or all of them are liable, in which case liability has stopped tracking degree of responsibility and is being generated by something else you have told me you reject.

Which horn do you take? Two standard replies are unavailable to you as usually stated. You may not invoke the collective responsibility of the group without explaining precisely how a property of the group can ground a liability to be killed that attaches to an individual member who, ex hypothesi, makes no difference to the outcome. And you may not soften the conclusion by reclassifying the harm — saying that these drivers are liable only to detention, or to lesser force, or to being fired upon in a manner that foreseeably misses — because my stipulations make the choice binary for the attacking force: it can destroy the node, killing the drivers, or leave it intact.
