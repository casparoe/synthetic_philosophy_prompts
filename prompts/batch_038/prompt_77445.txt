I've been trying to work out what I actually believe about when it's OK to just take someone's word for something, going purely by my gut on cases, and my verdicts feel like a mess. Here they are.

1. A stranger at a bus stop says the 14 is running twenty minutes late because of a demonstration up the road. I believe her instantly, no corroboration, and I change my plans.
2. The same stranger at the same stop tells me the demonstration is about a housing development and that the council took kickbacks. I don't believe a word of it, and I don't feel like I'm being inconsistent — except I can't say why.
3. My mechanic says the timing belt is about to go and it'll cost me £600. I pay it. He profits directly from my believing him and I have no way of checking.
4. A used-car salesman makes the identical claim about the identical model of car, and I assume he's lying.
5. My GP tells me to take a drug. I've read the trial myself and I think the effect size is marginal. I take the drug.
6. The same GP, on a podcast, says seed cycling fixes hormone problems. I don't believe her and I think slightly less of her.
7. A whistleblower whose claim will cost him his career and most of his friends: I believe it almost on sight, far more readily than a claim that helps the person making it.
8. Two historians, both credible, flatly contradict each other about some 18th century episode. I don't suspend judgement. I go with the one whose account fits better with the rest of what I already believe about the period, and I feel fine about that.
9. Everything I believe about my own life before age four comes from my mother. I believe basically all of it, including details she'd have every reason to embellish and no way to get right.
10. Someone on a forum asserts a fact about how a certain alloy behaves. I could check it in five minutes, don't bother, and carry on believing it for years.

The two pairs that break my brain. Mechanic vs. salesman looks like it's about incentives, but case 7 — where incentives run the other way — gets *more* belief from me, not less. So "follow the incentives" can't be the principle. And I defer to my GP against my own reading of the evidence (case 5), while refusing to defer to a financial adviser who knows vastly more about markets than I do, because his incentives are bad. But case 3 says incentives don't decide it either. I want those three to come out the way they feel, and I don't see how.

My tentative principle, which I think is wrong but can't repair: I only accept testimony in a domain where I've personally audited a sample and it checked out. Trust is earned locally by spot-checking, and it never transfers between domains. Call it the audit rule. The problem is obvious — I can never audit deep history, or other people's memories of my own childhood, or any field I know nothing about, and yet I believe all of those. Related idea I keep reaching for: maybe a domain counts as audited if dissent would reliably have reached me. Nobody has ever contradicted my mother about my childhood, so the absence of contradiction does the work of a check. But I know that's dodgy, because silence usually just means nobody was paying attention, and I don't want a principle that makes gullibility a virtue wherever nobody bothers to object.

So: one principle, stated precisely, clause by clause, with the case that earns each clause. It should deliver my verdicts above, or tell me straight which ones it's overturning and why — don't rescue all ten at the price of a monster with six epicycles. Then give me its verdict on three cases I haven't listed, and make at least one of them a case that's genuinely hard for your own principle. And be committed. I don't want a menu of positions with pros and cons and a shrug at the end; write down the principle you'd actually sign your name to, say which of my verdicts you're overruling, and say what you'd bet on. If you think one of my verdicts isn't a datum at all but just confusion on my part, say that too and argue for it.
