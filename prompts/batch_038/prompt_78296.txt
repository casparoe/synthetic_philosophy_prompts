Okay so I co-run a small ethics workshop at a professional school — mostly law and philosophy people, plus two of us who write disclosure rules for a living. For two years the group has had the same fight and nobody has budged. It's about whether lying is worse than deliberately misleading someone with statements that are literally true.

One side — call her the distinctivist — holds that lying is worse *in kind*, not just in degree. Her line is that when you assert something you believe to be false you've conscripted the practice of assertion itself into your deception; you're the one who manufactured the false belief. The misleader, by contrast, says something true and then lets the other person do the inferential work, which is a different relation to the hearer's mistake even when the outcome is identical. The other side — the parity guy — thinks there's no difference in kind whatsoever: same knowledge, same intention, same false belief in the same head. He thinks whatever gap remains is entirely about degree, tracking how much harm results, how hard the false belief is to detect, and how much work the hearer would have had to do to check. He treats the said/implicated boundary as a technicality that does no moral work.

They've finally agreed to sit down and produce a joint statement, and I've been asked to draft a strawman version in advance that they can both redline. That's what I need from you. Write the document itself, not advice about how to write it.

Here's what it has to contain:

**1. A numbered section of claims both can sign.** Not filler — I want the claims that are doing real work and that each side would genuinely accept without wincing: about what a lie is, about what's *not* in dispute (e.g. whether deception can ever be permissible, whether consequences matter to all-things-considered wrongness), about the descriptive layer vs. the normative layer, and about contexts. Where one of them can only sign a claim with a qualification, put the qualification in the claim rather than hiding it.

**2. A disagreements table.** One row per live disagreement, with a neutral label, then a column giving the distinctivist's own formulation of her position in words she'd accept, then the same for the parity theorist, then a final column classifying the row as substantive, merely verbal, or a case of the two of them answering different questions under one heading. I want you to be aggressive about that last column — my suspicion is that a big chunk of this fight is over the word "lie" and over "what is said," and that they've been scoring points on definitions while thinking they were arguing about morality. Say which parts, and say why.

**3. A case battery, adjudicated row by row by both sides.** For each of the following, each side gives a wrongness rating from 0 to 10 and one sentence of justification. I want the table to expose where their scores diverge and, just as importantly, where they unexpectedly converge:

- (a) Buyer: "Has this car ever been in an accident?" Seller knows the body was damaged by a falling branch and repainted. Lie version: "Never been in an accident." True version: "It's never been in a collision."
- (b) Same misleading content, but in one version it's in a recorded email the buyer can later check, and in the other it's spoken at a party where no record exists.
- (c) A nosy neighbor asks "Are you seeing anyone?" Truth version: "I've been really focused on work lately." Lie version: "No." In both, the neighbor has no right to the answer and is already half-committed to a guess.
- (d) A regulated disclosure states "the company is not currently under investigation," which is literally true on the agency's own definition, while the company knows a regulator has opened an informal inquiry. Compare with "no regulator has ever contacted us," which is false.
- (e) Saying something literally true that fills a silence, versus saying nothing at all when the hearer would have asked if they'd known to.
- (f) Telling a six-year-old "Mommy's at work" when she's actually in the hospital — literally true, predictably misheard by someone who cannot do the inferential work.
- (g) The same true-but-misleading utterance made to a hearer who is *hoping* for the false belief and will not check, versus a hearer who is actively trying to get at the truth.

**4. The cruxes.** Four to six sub-questions such that settling one would actually move at least one of them. My guesses, which you're free to reject: whether the wrong of lying has anything specifically to do with the norm of assertion; whether the hearer's contribution to their own false belief shifts responsibility or just shifts the causal pathway; whether the said/implicated line is determinate enough in messy real cases to bear any moral weight at all; and whether the distinctivist's account is really about ordinary conversation while the parity theorist's is really about regulated institutional contexts — which would mean they've never been answering the same question. For each crux, give me a case, argument, or piece of evidence that *both* would accept as bearing on it, and then write down each side's prediction about how that would come out, before the fact. Where one side would refuse to count some kind of evidence as bearing on the crux at all, say so — that refusal is itself informative and belongs in the document.

On the determinacy point specifically, they should both engage with the fact that the law operates the distinction constantly. In *Bronston v. United States* (1973) a witness asked at a bankruptcy hearing whether he'd ever had a Swiss bank account answered, "The company had an account there for about six months, in Zurich," which was literally true and concealed a personal account he'd held for years. The Court held the perjury statute doesn't reach an unresponsive but literally true answer even where the witness intends to mislead, on the reasoning that it's the questioner's burden to frame his questions acutely. I want the joint statement to record what each side makes of that: the distinctivist presumably thinks the Court got the moral allocation backwards, and the parity guy presumably thinks the Court was tracking something real about how epistemic labor gets divided. Does either of them think that legal reasoning has moral force, or is it just a rule about the costs of prosecuting speech?

**5. An empirical crux, handled carefully.** Both should agree in advance what a well-designed study of ordinary people's wrongness judgments would show if either of them is right — lie vs. literally-true misleader, consequences and detection risk held as constant as the design can manage them. Write each side's prediction down. Then note the obvious objection each will raise to the other's reading of such data: that folk judgments are just tracking the definition of the word, versus that they're tracking moral weight, versus that people are reacting to paradigm associations rather than to the case as stipulated.

**6. Self-falsification.** For each side: name one concrete case, not a hypothetical-in-principle, that they'd accept as decisive against their own view. If one of them can't name any, that needs to be stated plainly in the document as a finding rather than papered over.

**7. A short closing section on what would actually move this.** Given everything above, is there anything — a distinction, a result, a change of question — that would break the deadlock, or should the group expect to keep arguing about this forever? Give an honest answer with reasons, not a hopeful one.

A few ground rules. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations or name-dropping of who said what in the literature — I don't want either side hiding behind authorities, and I don't want you guessing at sources. If you think a well-known argument from the literature is relevant, state it in your own words and attribute it to the position, not the person. Don't quote anyone. Don't survey the territory before getting to the document — I've told you the two positions, so start drafting. Keep each side's formulations charitable in the strong sense: the distinctivist should be able to read her own row and say "yes, that's exactly my view," and likewise for the parity theorist, and neither should sound like a strawman written by the other. Where you think one side is actually right, that's fine, but the document itself stays joint — put your own view in a bracketed drafter's note at the very end if you must.

Write it in American English. Markdown, with the table in item 2 and the ratings in item 3 as actual tables. Long enough to be usable — call it 2,000 to 3,000 words — but cut anything that's just throat-clearing. One more thing: I'm seperate-ly going to hand this to the two of them cold, so please don't hedge it into mush. A joint statement with no content is worse than no statement at all, because they'll both sign it and then keep fighting.
