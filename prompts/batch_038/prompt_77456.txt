A cause that's constant for two centuries can't explain a change that happens in two decades.

(2) Inés said fine, but that only rules out *new* arguments. Maybe the old arguments finally got uptake because the audience changed: literacy, newspapers, a professional middle class whose standing didn't depend on the gentry's code. That's neither an interest story nor an honor story, it's a story about who was listening.

(3) Dan said thats not a fourth explanation, it's honor with a new label — who counts as an audience is itself a matter of standing and respect, so "the audience changed" collapses into "the honor code changed," which is what Appiah argues anyway across dueling, footbinding, and Atlantic slavery: the practices ended when they became dishonorable, not when they got refuted.

(4) Inés said the honor view can't fail, and that's exactly the problem. "It ended because it became dishonorable" carries no more information than "it ended because people came to disapprove of it." The argument story at least makes risky predictions about *which* practices change and in what direction.

(5) Dan offered a test: describe me a practice that everyone has been arguing against for centuries, that pays nobody any material benefit, and that nonetheless persists. Inés said she couldnt think of one offhand. Dan said that's the point — persistence always has a payoff, in money or in standing, so argument never does the work on its own.

(6) Inés said he'd just built the payoff into the definition, and that what they've been circling is whether the "because" in these explanations is causal or constitutive — whether honor *made* the practice stop, or whether calling it honor is only how we redescribe the stopping.
