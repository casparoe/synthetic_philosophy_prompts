I have spent a little over a decade reading the rationalist and effective-altruism corners of the internet, long enough to recognize their seasons. The most reliable one is the migration: a problem gets identified as neglected, a cohort of unusually able people arrives, and within two or three conference cycles the empty field is no longer empty. Next month I am hosting a small gathering for a friend who has decided to spend the next several years on the problem our community currently considers least crowded, and I would like to close the evening by reading something. That something should be a pantoum about the neglectedness criterion and, more precisely, about the way the criterion undermines itself.

The form is doing the argument, so please observe it strictly: four or five quatrains plus a closing stanza, with the second and fourth lines of each stanza reappearing as the first and third lines of the next, and the traditional circular ending, in which the closing stanza brings the opening stanza's lines back so that the poem's last line is its first. Rhyme is optional; the recurrence is already supplying the sonic work that rhyme would do, and I would much rather have clean, exact repetition than a forced end-rhyme. Keep the lines short enough to be heard aloud without losing track of which line is which.

At least two of the recycled lines must change polarity between their appearances. A sentence that reads, on its first pass, as an announcement that a field is empty should read, when it returns, as an announcement that the field is full — no word altered, nothing but its position and its neighborhood changed. This inversion is the central trick, and I would rather see it executed twice than see four additional stanzas.

Three things the poem has to get right, and has to get right without explaining itself:

1. Neglectedness is not an intrinsic virtue on this framework. It is a proxy for the marginal return on the next person or the next dollar: high where a field is thin, low where it is crowded. If the poem ends up treating unfashionableness as noble, it has quietly converted an instrumental criterion into an aesthetic, which is exactly the error I would like the guest of honor to catch.

2. The criterion is reflexive. Followed seriously by enough people, it destroys the condition it recommends, and the more widely the framework is trusted, the staler the data it runs on. I want the poem to register that the advice is self-eroding without turning that into a paradox performed for its own sake.

3. A thin field is not always evidence of collective inattention. Sometimes it is a signal — that the problem resists tractable progress, or that its importance was overestimated by whoever noticed it first. Leave this unresolved. The point is not which of the two readings is correct but that from the outside you cannot tell which one you are looking at, and that the framework supplies no instrument for telling.

Voice and diction: plain, slightly dry, first-person plural at least once, since the community is describing its own habit — but keep that plural out of the closing stanza, which should be impersonal. No apostrophe to an abstraction, no exclamation, no acronyms, no proper nouns, no named organizations, no quotation from or allusion to anyone's published writing. "Neglected," "crowded," and "marginal" are fair game. Nothing should date the poem or tie it to one particular cause, since the causes rotate and I want the poem to outlive the current rotation.

The room will be full of people who have absorbed the three-factor way of ranking problems — importance, tractability, neglectedness — and one of them leaves in three weeks. At least one guest should feel recognized and faintly indicted. A guest who has never encountered any of this should still find it a good poem about fashion, timing, and the erosion of an advantage by the very people who noticed it.

Please give me only the poem. If it needs a gloss, it has failed.
