Six weeks ago the director of the fellowship that funded my own doctorate asked me to build next year's screening instrument, an assignment I accepted with some enthusiasm because I was selected by the current version three years ago and have thought it far too dependent on how much a candidate happens to have read. The committee's brief is narrow: twenty minutes, delivered through a text-only proctoring platform, and it must separate candidates who can form an unfamiliar category from a handful of instances from candidates who are merely well read. The committee meets in nine days and I have to defend the item set in person.

Because the platform cannot display images, I have adapted the Bongard format. Where the original problems show two sets of six line drawings, each of my items gives a left set and a right set of six strings over the alphabet {A, B, C, D}, presented in random order. A single rule determines membership in the left set; every string in the right set violates it. The candidate sees both sets and must state the rule in words.

I drafted seven items and piloted them on eleven colleagues and, out of curiosity, on two language models. The results were disquieting. Two colleagues I would not have backed solved four items in under ten minutes, and one model's pattern of successes tracked almost perfectly whether an item's rule had a common name in English. I now suspect that much of the battery measures something other than what the committee asked for: either a concept the candidate already owns and therefore goes hunting for, or a surface regularity in the strings that does most of the sorting work.

The items follow, each with the rule I intended.

1. Left: ABCA, BDCB, CADBA, DBCAD, ABDCB, CABDB. Right: ABCD, ABBA, AABB, AAABC, ABCCC, ABBCC. Intended rule: exactly one symbol occurs twice, and no symbol occurs more than twice.
2. Left: ABBA, ACDDCA, ABCBA, ADCCDA, BCCB, ACDCDCA. Right: ABAB, ABCAB, ABBAB, ABCDA, ABAAB, CBBCA. Intended rule: the string reads the same forwards and backwards.
3. Left: ABCD, ABC, ABD, ACD, BCD, CD. Right: ABBA, AABB, ACAB, BDDB, CAAC, DCCD. Intended rule: each symbol comes later in the alphabet than the one before it.
4. Left: ABBA, ABAB, CDDC, BCCB, ABCABC, ACDCAD. Right: ABCD, ABAC, DCBA, CABD, AABBA, ABCBA. Intended rule: for even-length strings, the first half is a rearrangement of the second half; odd-length strings never belong to the left set.
5. Left: AAAB, ABBBB, BBBC, BCCCC, AAABBB, AADDD. Right: ABAB, AABBA, BAAB, ABBAAB, ABCD, CABBAC. Intended rule: reading left to right, the string changes symbol exactly once.
6. Left: DCBA, CAB, DBCAB, CBA, BDCA, BDAC. Right: ABCD, BACD, CADB, CABD, ACDB, BCD. Intended rule: the alphabetically greatest symbol occurring in the string appears earlier than the alphabetically smallest.
7. Left: ABAB, ABCAB, CDDCCD, ABAAB, DBCADBC, AABBAAB. Right: ABCD, ABAC, DCBA, ACBDA, BACDB, CABDC. Intended rule: some pair of adjacent symbols occurs more than once.

What I want is an audit delivered as a ranking rather than as commentary. Specifically:

1. Sort the seven items into tiers by diagnostic value — how much of a correct answer is attributable to inducing an unfamiliar rule — and order the items within each tier. Items you place in the same tier should be ones you genuinely cannot separate.
2. For each item, give a number between 0 and 1: your estimate of the probability that a candidate who answers correctly did so by recovering my rule rather than by a shortcut or a stock hypothesis. Your ordering in (1) must be consistent with these numbers.
3. For each item, name the cheapest cue — a surface regularity, a partial regularity, or a concept a person already has and merely tests — that would let a candidate sort at least ten of the twelve strings correctly without grasping the rule, and state the accuracy it actually achieves on those twelve. Where you believe nothing reaches ten of twelve, say so plainly instead of manufacturing a cue.
4. Flag any item whose twelve strings fail to determine my rule: give a rival rule that is not merely a rewording of mine and that accommodates every string just as well, together with a string that would distinguish the two.
5. Tell me which two items to cut, and for each, the smallest edit to the strings — substitutions, additions, or deletions — that would repair the item while preserving the rule.
6. Note any string that sits in the left set of one item and the right set of another, and say whether that creates a leak a candidate could exploit across the battery as a whole.

I would rather be told that my favourites are broken than reassured that the battery is sound. I am most attached to items 6 and 7, on the grounds that neither rule seems to have a name anyone would think to try; please be especially unsparing there.
