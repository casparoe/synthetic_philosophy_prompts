I'm one of seven members on the clinical ethics and staff wellbeing board at a 40-bed residential unit for people with acquired brain injury and early-onset dementia — mostly TBI from road accidents, some frontotemporal. We've now had essentially the same incident three times in two years. A resident, disinhibited by their injury, screams at a carer, or spits, or says something cruel and oddly precise ("your wife didn't leave because of the drinking, she left because you're dull" — that one landed, and it was roughly true). Current policy, we call it Policy 14, says staff are to treat all of it as symptom rather than as address: no blame, no grievance route against residents, and the official line in induction training is that you shouldn't feel resentment because the resident isn't an appropriate target for it. Two factions have hardened. The night staff say the policy is unlivable and quietly corrosive — they say they've stopped being able to feel anything toward the residents at all, that they handle them more or less like equipment, and that this is worse for everyone including the residents. One of them put it to me as: "I'm not allowed to be angry, so I've stopped being a person to them." The rehab leads say the opposite — that refusing resentment is precisely right and is the only thing keeping the work humane, and that any slack in the policy will end with someone retaliating against a person who cannot help themselves.

A night-shift worker floated a distinction I keep turning over and can't settle, and I want the memo to take it seriously rather than wave at it. The idea is that there's a difference between resentment that can be pointed somewhere — anger you can hand over to the injury, or convert into advocacy for the resident, or convert into a demand that management fix the staffing rota — and resentment that has nowhere to go and just stays in the person. On that reading Policy 14 isn't wrong because it forbids the first kind; it's wrong because it assumes you can convert the first kind into the second kind (or rather the second into the first — you get the idea) on demand, in your own head, at 3am, alone on a corridor. I think there's something real there, but I also think it's half a distinction and half a complaint, and I can't tell which half is which.

So: play the role of a philosopher who has actually sat on committees like this and knows the moral responsibility literature without being captured by it. Strawson's Freedom and Resentment is the obvious backdrop — the reactive/objective attitude business — but I do not want a paper about Strawson, I want a document my board can act on. Draft the memo I'll circulate ahead of the vote next Tuesday. It needs to (a) recommend a concrete policy: keep 14, scrap it, or replace it with something specified enough that a carer could be trained on it, with actual wording for the induction change and a rollout timeline; (b) hand the board a test they can apply to a hard case in under a minute, not a set of principles they have to interpret; (c) either sharpen the night staff's distinction into something a policy could use or show why it collapses, and say which; and (d) put the rehab leads' objection in its strongest form and then say honestly whether your recommendation survives it, or survives only with a cost you're willing to name. Please think this through properly before you write — I'd rather see you brainstorm the two or three policy options out loud in a short section up front, weigh them, and then give me the memo proper. Keep the memo itself to roughly 900 words max; the brainstorming can run over, but the memo has to be something a tired board member will actually finish reading.
