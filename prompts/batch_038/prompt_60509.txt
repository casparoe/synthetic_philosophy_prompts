I'm a backend engineer, no IR or philosophy background at all, but I fell down the rabbit hole on the Waltz/Sagan thing about whether more nuclear states make the world safer or more dangerous, mostly because Sagan's side leans on Perrow's normal accident theory, which is basically my day job translated into politics — tight coupling, complex interactions, organizational failure modes. After reading a chunk of both sides what struck me is that this has been going for forty years and several editions of their book and nobody has conceded an inch. I don't want a verdict on who's right. I want a diagnosis of the stall.

Here's my read of the two positions. Waltz: the deterrence logic is robust, states facing existential costs behave cautiously, and the record backs it up — no nuclear use in war since 1945, India and Pakistan went to Kargil and stopped there instead of escalating. Sagan: that's modeling states as unitary rational actors, but the dangerous stuff lives a level down in military organizations with principle-agent problems, weak civilian control, coup risk, use-or-lose pressure on alert forces and bad safety cultures — and the "clean" record is only clean because you're counting detonations instead of incidents: Goldsboro in '61, Thule in '68, the 1980 NORAD false alarm blamed on a 46-cent chip, the Soviet duty officer in '83 who decided a satellite warning was a glitch.

My hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis and probably too cute, is that this isn't one disagreement but three tangled ones and the stall comes from each side answering a different one.

1. A statistics question. Whether the post-1945 record licenses a low annual probability estimate. With zero observed events the best you can do is an upper bound of a few percent per year, which is nowhere near tight enough to settle anything — so Waltz's track-record argument and Sagan's "you're only sampling the near misses that got declassified" argument could both be true and neither decisive. Is that fair, or is there a defensible way to get a usable number out of that data?
2. A decision-theory question. Detonation is an absorbing state, so once the argument is implicitly about severity rather than probability the expected-value comparison starts to feel degenerate. My suspicion is Sagan wins whenever the dispute is quietly about severity and Waltz wins whenever it's quietly about probability, and neither one ever says which functional he's using.
3. A unit-of-analysis question. Is "proliferation" even one variable, or are gradual vs. rapid, declared vs. opaque, and civilian-controlled vs. junta-controlled cases different enough that the two sides are arguing about different populations and never notice?

So: is that the right decomposition, and if not, what is the actual structure of the stall? I want you to say which parts are substantive disagreements that evidence could in principle settle, which parts are verbal fights about words like "stable" and "cautious," which parts are two different questions sharing one label, and which parts are bedrock risk intuitions neither side can argue for. Then tell me what would count as progress given your diagnosis — a distinction, a dataset somebody could actually build, a formal result, a reframing of the question — or make the case that no progress should be expected and say why. No reading list, I'm not asking for sources, just the reasoning. American English please.
