I'm in a third-year early modern philosophy seminar and our assignment this week is a bit unusual — instead of an essay we have to write a reconstructed dialogue out of some historical dispute, and I picked the Hobbes/Wallis quarrel because I actually find it fascinating and also because I've read almost nothing on it beyond the two chapters we did and my prof's lecture. So let me dump what I've got, build on it however you like: Hobbes, in De Corpore (1655), held that we only genuinely have scientia of things whose generation we know, since to know a cause just is to know how the effect gets produced, and the one place where we actually construct our own objects is geometry, where you generate a line by imagining a point moved and a figure by moving that line. That's why he treated geometry as the one real science and subordinated arithmetic and algebra to it — reasoning for him is a kind of reckoning with names, and he thought shuffling algebraic symbols around was marking counters rather than knowing anything. Wallis, who was Savilian professor of geometry at Oxford, hit back with the Elenchus geometriae Hobbianae and then with his Arithmetica Infinitorum, where he gets areas and volumes by treating a figure as made of infinitely many indivisibles and by tabulating finite cases and extrapolating the pattern to the infinite one — that's how he gets, e.g., that the ratio of 0²+1²+2²+...+n² to (n+1) copies of n² tends to 1/3 as n grows. Hobbes considered this fraud dressed up as demonstration, and he wrote to a friend around then that infinite quantities looked to him like a back door for scholastic obscurantism and that he suspected the whole English clergy stood behind Wallis. They went at it in pamphlets for twenty-odd years and Hobbes's squaring-the-circle claim got destroyed.

Could you write me the dialogue, maybe 1200–1800 words, as one continuous scene in Wallis's rooms in Oxford in 1656 shortly after Hobbes's Six Lessons came out, with Hobbes having turned up unannounced? The main thing I care about is that neither of them is a strawman — last year's cohort apparently all wrote Hobbes as a raving crank and Wallis as a smug modern in a wig, and that's exactly what I dont want. So: Wallis needs a real answer to the dilemma Hobbes presses against indivisibles (either an indivisible has some magnitude, in which case infinitely many of them blow past any magnitude you care to name, or it has none, in which case adding them up gets you nothing), and he's not allowed to escape by invoking limits or epsilons or convergence tests or anything else that doesn't exist yet in 1656 — he has to hold his ground on period resources, e.g. by arguing that he never needs to complete the infinite, only to show the leftover bit can be made smaller than any magnitude Hobbes bothers to assign. And Hobbes's side needs to land as more than nostalgia: the point that getting the right number off an extrapolated table isn't knowing why the figure has that area, that a method which yields results without exhibiting generation produces cunning rather than science, and that once you licence reasoning about completed infinities you've no principled way to bar the scholastics' own unconstructible entities. I'd also like Wallis to turn Hobbes's criterion back on him — if scientia demands knowing the generation, what survives of Hobbes's own physics, where he concedes we only observe effects and reason backwards? — plus at least one moment where each of them genuinely concedes something instead of trading jabs, and an ending where neither converts the other but a reader could state precisely what the disagreement comes down to.

Deadline's Thursday so don't worry about polish, just keep the mathematics they're arguing over actually correct — two people in my seminar did maths degrees and they will notice. No footnotes, no bibliography, no list of sources at the end, it's a creative piece, so if you're unsure whether Hobbes said something specific just have him say something that fits the position I sketched rather than inventing a title for it.
