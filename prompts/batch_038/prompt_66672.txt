I sat as a junior member of a municipal elections review panel last spring, and I'd like an outside adjudication of an argument between two of the commissioners that the rest of us never resolved. The panel's remit covered three possible recommendations: making voting compulsory, enforceable by a fine; lowering the voting age to sixteen; and extending the local franchise to residents who are not citizens but have lived in the jurisdiction for some years. Two commissioners — I'll call them Vera, a political theorist, and Daniel, a retired elections administrator — ended up disputing whether the three proposals stand or fall together. I took notes at the time and filled them in afterwards; below is my reconstruction, with the order of turns preserved as best I can and each turn labelled.

**D1.** Daniel opened by claiming the three proposals share one foundation. On his account the franchise is not a personal liberty to be exercised or declined at will but something like an office held by everyone who is governed: a function the polity assigns to you because it needs the input of all who are subject to it, not because you have a private interest in casting a ballot. Once voting is an office rather than a liberty, he said, all three recommendations follow, and the panel's hesitation about them was just a failure to state the premise.

**V1.** Vera answered that the office view delivers at most two of the three, and is internally unstable. If voting were an office held by all who are governed, it would be held by fifteen-year-olds too, since they are governed in every relevant sense — yet nobody on the panel would recommend that, and Daniel had offered no reason why the office should lapse for three years and then resume. More fundamentally, an office is defined by accountability for how it is discharged, whereas the ballot is secret precisely so that no one can ever be held to account for its content. A role whose performance cannot be evaluated by anyone, including the principal who assigns it, is not an office; it is a liberty wearing a dress uniform.

**D2.** Daniel said rights and duties are not contraries. The right to trial by one's peers and the duty to serve on a jury are two descriptions of one institution, and every legal system he knew of treats jury service as mandatory while also treating it as an incident of citizenship. Vera's argument, if sound, condemned jury duty as well.

**V2.** She said the analogy fails at three points. Jurors are summoned individually because the case cannot proceed without them; they are selected rather than enlisted en masse; and their performance is disciplined — instructed by a judge, insulated from outside influence, reviewable in some systems. Voters have none of those features. The state gains nothing from any particular voter, only from the aggregate, and compulsion alters exactly the aggregate it is meant to certify: a turnout figure produced under legal compulsion stops being evidence of anything.

**D3.** Then make the sanction trivial. In the systems that actually do this, penalties are small, excuses readily accepted, prosecutions rare. The law's point is declaratory — it announces that participation is expected of everyone — and enforcement is residual.

**V3.** Two problems. If the sanction is nominal and seldom applied, the panel should describe the measure as a symbolic norm backed by a nudge and drop the language of compulsion, since her objection was aimed at the version with teeth and the declaratory version needs no defence against it. And if the sanction is real, it is a flat fine: proportionally crushing for a shift worker, a rounding error for a professional, so the duty is enforced by class. Compulsory voting then closes one participation gap by opening another, namely differential liability to penalty.

**D4.** Every flat fine has that shape, including parking tickets and transit fares, and the standard remedy is a hardship waiver or an income-scaled penalty. A measure regressive in incidence but waivable on application is not thereby unjust.

**V4.** Waivers have to be applied for, and the population most likely to be fined is the population least likely to know the waiver exists, keep the records, or take a day off to deal with it. The state would test the poor twice: once at the polling place and once at the counter.

**D5.** Turning to age, Daniel argued both available criteria point to sixteen. If the criterion is competence, the line is arbitrary and cannot survive a gradual argument, since competence comes in degrees and sixteen-year-olds on average are not less competent than a substantial number of enfranchised adults. If the criterion is subjection to law, sixteen-year-olds plainly qualify: they work and pay tax on what they earn, they are bound by decisions about schooling and training they did not make, in some places they can be prosecuted in adult courts, and they will live under the consequences of the panel's decisions decades longer than the median voter. Either route gets you votes at sixteen, so the criterion need not be settled.

**V5.** She said that was too convenient, because the two routes are not interchangeable. Competence cannot be the criterion, since the state does not test adults and does not disenfranchise adults who would fail a test. If Daniel says the exclusion of adults with significant cognitive impairments is itself an injustice to be repaired, he has abandoned competence and is left with subjection alone — and subjection alone proves far too much, since a five-year-old is subject to law, taxed on whatever income she may have, and bound by decisions she did not make. If instead competence still matters, he owes a principled account of why the presumption turns on at sixteen, which the panel had already conceded he lacked.

**D6.** Subjection can be typed rather than measured: what matters is the kind of subjection. A sixteen-year-old is subject to criminal, tax, and labour law in her own person; a five-year-old is subject chiefly to protective rules administered through her parents.

**V6.** That, she said, was a stake-in-the-future argument in subjection's clothing — and the argument from length of remaining exposure cuts both ways, against the very old, who have the least remaining exposure and among the most remaining influence.

**V7.** Vera said the non-citizen proposal is where Daniel's package comes apart. Citizenship is a reciprocal bundle: the state may tax you, may conscript you, and cannot readily expel you; the franchise is the political half of that bundle and makes sense only alongside those other ties. A long-term resident gets the protection without the indefinite attachment, and often without liability to conscription. To enfranchise such a person is to detach a benefit from its correlative. Worse, if the panel also adopted compulsion, it would oblige people whom the state may expel at any time to take part in the politics of a membership they have not accepted — a distinctively bad form of coercion.

**D7.** Daniel replied that reciprocity proves too little and too much. Too little, because the correlative that matters is subjection: a person who has lived and worked in the city for twenty years is subject to its police, its zoning, its taxes, and its courts exactly as a citizen is, and is governed with no possibility of consent. Too much, because if a say must be earned by accepting indefinite attachment, the state may withhold it from people who bear the whole ordinary burden of its law — which is the oldest objection to government without representation, and the panel would be endorsing it to keep three proposals tidy. He added that several long-established democracies already let non-citizen residents vote in local elections at no apparent cost to the franchise, which suggested the reciprocity requirement was doing no real work.

**V8.** The existence of a practice shows it is tolerated, not that the principle is wrong; and local elections are in any case the weakest field for the correlative, since a city can neither conscript nor expel anyone.

**D8.** Even if Vera won on compulsion, Daniel said, her own view had to explain why the two expansions follow from a liberty. A liberty generates no reason to extend itself to anyone. The reason to enfranchise more people is not that they have a liberty but that they are governed — the premise she had resisted all afternoon.

**V9.** She said the expansions follow from the point of the liberty: that no one should be subject to a power they had no chance to influence. That is a claim about the limits of authority, not a duty owed to it. Neither budged, and the panel voted to list all three proposals separately without deciding the relation among them — which is why I am asking.

Please adjudicate this turn by turn rather than as a whole. For each labelled turn, say whether it succeeds, and where a turn contains several distinct claims, separate them: I expect that in places one of them is right and another is not. Rate each turn from clearly mistaken to clearly right, and say briefly what earns the rating. Flag every point at which one of them trades on an ambiguity or a hidden premise — I suspect at least one term shifts sense across the argument, but I don't want to tell you which one, since that's part of what I'm testing. Then say who is more right overall, and identify at least one consideration bearing on the verdict that neither of them raised, including whether the panel's demand for a single principle underlying all three proposals was itself a mistake.

Finally, tell me what would change your verdict: which of these arguments, if it turned out sound, would reverse your overall judgment, and where in your own reasoning you are most likely to have gone wrong. I would rather have an answer that marks its own weak joints than one that reads as conclusive.
