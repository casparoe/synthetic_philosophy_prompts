Ok so this is hard to ask for and I'm sorry to dump it on you, but I need a speech written and I don't trust myself to write it.

My dad died three weeks ago. Heart thing, fast, no warning, 74 years old. He was an actuary his whole life, and it turns out that since the late nineties he had been quietly routing a huge chunk of his income, and then most of the estate, into a donor-advised fund. The will left each of us three kids the same amount: four thousand dollars. Plus a short note saying he hoped we wouldn't read the number as an apology, because he wasn't apologizing.

My brother is not ok about it. He keeps saying it isn't the money, and I believe him. What he's actually angry about is what it means to have been raised by a man who ran the numbers on his own children and found that we came out about even with strangers. My sister thinks he was a saint and won't hear a word against it. I'm the one who's been asked to speak at the celebration of life in six weeks, which is a bad assignment.

I should tell you that instead of sleeping I've spent two weeks reading philosophy about this, which is probably its own symptom. I found that there's a whole argument about whether a morality that asks that much of a person can still be called morality, and a related one about a man who saves his wife from drowning when he could have saved a stranger instead — the idea being that he doesn't need a moral reason for picking her, and that if he has one, he's had a thought too many. That's my brother's objection almost exactly and he's never read any of it. And then the other side: that the height of the standard isn't evidence the standard is wrong.

So. Write the speech. Five or six minutes, plain language, funny in two or three spots but not jokey. Here are the constraints, and the constraints are the actual work, so please treat them as hard:

- It cannot tell anyone in the room to do what he did. Not my brother, not the neighbors, not the two people from the fund's local chapter who'll be sitting in the third row. If it turns into an invitation to give away your inheritance, it's the wrong speech and everyone will hear it as such.
- It also cannot concede that he was wrong. I've tried to get to that place and I can't. So no "he should have put us first," and no softened version of that either.
- It has to let my brother's objection sit in the room unrefuted without being condescended to. No complimenting the objection and then steamrolling it. He's allowed to think something real was missing. The speech should make clear I know what's actually at stake in thinking that.
- This is the fact I keep circling and I want it to carry weight: he was extremely partial in ordinary life. He drove forty minutes each way, twice a week, for six years, to coach my swim team when I was bad at swimming. He knew all our schedules, our friends' names, which dog slept where. So he was not cold at home and generous abroad. He was warm right here, and he gave the estate away anyway, and I do not have a clean account of how those two things fit together. Be honest about that. Do not resolve it with a formula or a neat turn.
- Somebody will have heard the "if everyone did it" version of the argument, and somebody else will say that's precisely the excuse people use for doing nothing. Don't take either side. Find what's true when both of them are talking.
- Banned, in any wording: "he'd want you to...", "in a world that needs...", "every little bit helps", and anything that would work unchanged in a gala speech.
- Land it somewhere my brother and my sister can both stay in the room and both feel it was about their father and not about a position.

Roughly 120 people, church hall, most of them not college educated, maybe a handful who'll know exactly what I'm circling. No philosophy vocabulary at all — I want the arguments in there wearing normal clothes.
