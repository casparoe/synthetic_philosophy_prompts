I lost my wife eight months ago. Since then I have kept catching myself doing things she did: arguing for the same causes in her exact idiom, finishing her half-made jokes, hearing her cadence coming out of my own mouth. A friend told me this is the point of Hofstadter's suggestion that a self is a pattern capable of being carried, in part, by other brains, so that surviving is a matter of degree rather than a yes-or-no fact. I want to know whether that suggestion can be turned into a real principle, because my own verdicts about where a person persists seem to resist it.

My cases, with what I actually think:

1. My carrying her turns of phrase and her argumentative habits: some part of her is genuinely in me — a real presence, not a resemblance and not a metaphor.
2. A biographer who has read every letter she wrote and predicts her reactions better than I can: no part of her is in him.
3. Her sister, who has her voice and gestures but almost none of her convictions: more of her there than in the biographer, less than in me.
4. A scan of her brain taken at death and run as a simulation, behaviorally indistinguishable from her: that would be her.
5. Two such simulations at once: neither is her, or she is halved. In any case the presence does not accumulate, whereas in 1 and 3 partial presence across several people seemed to add.
6. A colleague she transformed, who now listens to students exactly as she did: some of her is in him.
7. Her recorded voice in an archive: nothing of her, though it is a far more direct physical trace of her than my mannerisms are.
8. A man she once treated badly who has become fixated on her, reconstructing her reasoning daily to anticipate what she would have said: nothing of her in him, even though by the criteria I used for 6 he looks like a match.

My tentative principle is this: someone survives in a bearer to the degree that the bearer's ongoing behavior is driven by that person's specific dispositions, integrated into the bearer's own agency rather than stored as information about her. I think it handles 1, 2, 4, 6, and 7. It plainly fails to separate 6 from 8, and nothing in it explains why 5 should come out differently from 1 and 3 taken together.

Can it be repaired into something precise? State the principle, or the small number of competing candidates, say which case each clause exists to handle, and tell me which of my verdicts it forces me to abandon. I would sooner give up 8 than 1, but I want the price named. Then give me its verdicts on cases I have not listed — above all on a community of thirty people who between them hold her entire dispositional profile, each carrying one fragment: is she there, distributed, and if so, what would it mean for her to be in two such communities at once?
