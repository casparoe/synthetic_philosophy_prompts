The student wrote, roughly:

"Lying is saying something false to someone; misleading is saying something literally true while arranging for the hearer to come away with a false belief, normally by way of a conversational implicature. The two are different kinds of speech act, not different degrees of the same thing, and the difference matters morally. When I lie I simply hand you a false belief; when I mislead, you have to do the work of drawing the inference yourself, and the resources to check it are sitting right there in what I actually said. So misleading leaves you more of your rational agency — you could have caught it — whereas lying forecloses that. This is basically the shape of the Kantian worry about the murderer at the door: Kant forbids the lie, and since he doesn't forbid the misleading alternative, the distinction must be the morally load-bearing one for him. The law agrees: in Bronston v. United States the Supreme Court refused to treat a literally true but evasive answer as perjury, which shows the distinction is one that tracks a real normative line and not just a linguistic technicality. The symmetry objection says both acts aim at the same false belief so the route doesn't matter, but that's just restating the distinction rather than objecting to it — of course they aim at the same belief, the point is how the belief gets installed. And anyway the whole thing is a distinction between what is said and what is implicated, i.e. a fact about the utterance, not about the hearer."
