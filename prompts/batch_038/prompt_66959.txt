I'm on the board of a small scholarship foundation — about $3M a year in grants — whose stated mission is training people to work directly on health problems in low-income countries. A donor has offered us $2M restricted to a new fellowship track for students heading into quantitative finance and AI research, on the explicit ground that they'll earn far more and donate far more than any clinician we fund. Fellows would sign a pledge to give away 30% of income above $150k, which we'd have no mechanism whatsoever to enforce.

Two of my colleagues are furious, for different reasons. One says the whole rationale ignores replacement: if the job the fellow takes would have gone to someone who donates anyway, the extra giving is close to zero, so the donor is buying a feeling, not an outcome. The other says that even if the money is real, a foundation that pays people to go off and earn it has given up on the idea that doing the work is what matters, and shouldn't be in the business of selling that message to students. Our program officer's back of envelope has each finance fellowship generating $900k of pledged donations over a career versus $400k of health output per clinical fellowship, though she admits the $900k assumes no one else would of filled the job.

The donor isn't asking us to touch the charter, just to add the track. I have to circulate something before Tuesday's vote. Draft the board memo: recommend accept, decline, or accept with specific conditions, with reasons a fellow trustee could actually argue back against, and a serious treatment of the strongest case for the position you're rejecting rather than a wave at it. Tell me which of my colleagues' objections does real damage and which one doesn't survive contact with the numbers. Don't lay out all three options and leave it to me — land somewhere and defend it.
