Bit of an unusual request, so bear with me. I spent thirty-three years at a national lab doing numerical modelling of mantle convection, with some satellite geodesy thrown in earlier, and since retiring I've fallen into philosophy fairly hard — mostly spacetime stuff, because it's the one area where I can still follow the equations. I've moved to Devon with my wife, and on Tuesday nights I run a reading group in the back room of the pub: two retired colleagues, a mathematician who does topology, and a couple of grad students who drive down from Exeter. Through that I ended up being asked by the philosophy department to advise on how to spend a small bequest — a former colleague of mine left about £150,000 to be used for "philosophy that actually knows some physics," their words, not mine. The department has told me it can either fund one three-year PhD studentship for someone with a physics first degree, or a two-year series of small residential workshops on the hole argument and gauge redundancy, and they want a written recommendation from me with a defended account of which open questions the money should be pointed at. That's what I need help with. I know the setup cold — Earman and Norton's revival, the drag-along construction, Leibniz equivalence, the point-coincidence business, the haecceitism retreat, structural realist appropriations — so please don't survey any of that back to me, and don't give me a reading list either, I'm trying to write a recommendation, not a syllabus.

Here's the worry I keep circling, and I'd genuinely like you to argue with it rather than around it. My sense from the reading is that the dispute got defused by something close to a reflex: you declare that diffeomorphic models represent one and the same physical situation, and the hole argument loses its bite. But that declaration looks to me like it was purchased cheaply — the substantivalist concedes that manifold points have no identity independent of the fields sitting on them, and then keeps the manifold as pure bookkeeping. If that's right, I can't see how to write down two theories that agree on every point-coincidence, every gauge-invariant quantity, and every prediction, yet disagree about anything at all; and if nobody can produce such a pair, the whole thing may be a zombie debate that everyone is politely pretending is alive. Relatedly, the SEP-ish line I keep seeing is that the hole argument is a template for spotting surplus structure, but that you can't identify surplus structure by mathematics alone — you need physical grounds, like verifiability or determinism. That sounds like it makes the question of whether two formulations are equivalent an interpretive, not a formal, matter, which is exactly where I suspect the remaining work is hiding. So: is there a non-trivial dispute left, and if there is, what is the sharpest way to state it so that a clever student could actually make progress on it in three years rather than write 200 pages of exegesis?

What I want from you, then, is a ranked agenda — say three or four candidate questions, in the order you'd put them in front of the committee — with the reasoning doing the heavy lifting. For each one: what other questions depend on its answer, how tractable it is for a student who has the physics but no real formal logic or category theory (my own maths is rusting badly and I'm the one who'll be mentoring her), how neglected it is relative to how much ink it's had, and — the criterion I care about most — whether settling it would change anything a working relativist says or does, or whether it's purely a metaphysician's comfort. Tell me which of the plausible-sounding questions you'd rank as a trap or a dead end, and say why. Tell me what you'd want to see over the next two or three years — a result, a new formal framework, a change in how quantum gravity people talk — that would move a question up or down your list. And end with a straight answer on the studentship-versus-workshops trade-off: which of the two actually serves the questions you've ranked. Please write the whole thing in British English, spellings and idiom both, since it's going into a UK committee's papers even though I'm writing to you in American. My wife thinks I'm just avoiding the garden and she may be right, but I'd rather hand this in with some real thought behind it.
