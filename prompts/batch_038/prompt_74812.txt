You're a philosopher who's also written a lot of speeches for other people — picture someone who taught political philosophy for twenty years but makes part of their living doing eulogies and toasts on the side. Please think it through carefully and do a bit of brainstorming out loud before you draft: I want to see the thinking, then the speech.

My father died two weeks ago. He built a midsized logistics company out of nothing, sold it, and spent the last twenty-odd years being locally famous for giving money away. He was also, his whole life, a hardheaded opponent of inheritance — not as a tax policy, he didn't much care about the tax, but as something private individuals do. His will leaves each of his four kids a modest annuity and puts everything else, about forty million, into a fund for first-generation college students in the towns where his warehouses were. I'm the executor and the oldest, and I've been asked to speak at the memorial Saturday. The room: my three siblings and their families (two of them furious, one quietly relieved), a lot of his business friends, the foundation board, and a bunch of people from those warehouse towns who never met him.

He left me a typed note, three pages, and the part I keep coming back to is this. He said he'd looked for a principle under which his great-grandchildren have a stronger claim on the world than a stranger's great-grandchildren and couldn't find one, so the only honest place to draw the line is one generation out. He said the thing he most wanted for us — that we'd never have to be scared of money — is the same thing he'd spent his life arguing makes people worse, and that he'd rather be consistent than be the kind of grandfather we wanted. And then, which I think is the part most people will miss, he said he knew perfectly well that giving us nothing doesn't hand anyone else something they were owed, that the fund isn't a repair of an injury, it's just a refusal to keep tilting the table.

Here's my problem. I don't want a speech that defends the estate plan — it's a memorial, not a board meeting, and two of my siblings would walk out. But I also refuse to do the version where he's a lovable eccentric rich guy with an expensive hobby, and I refuse the expected line about how he loved us so much he wanted us to make our own way. That's not what he said. He said the harder thing, and the speech has to actually carry the argument — the one-generation line, the tilting — in front of a room of grieving, partly resentful people who have never heard the word luck egalitarianism, without turning it into a platitude or a seminar.

Two things I want you to work out first and then commit to, in the speech itself. One: what is the actual complaint my angry siblings have? Not rhetorically — is there a principle on which they've genuinely been wronged, and if so, wronged by whom and by what, and can that be acknowledged in the room without either conceding the whole thing or lecturing them about it? Two: what does the speech owe the people from the warehouse towns, who are being told this money is for them while being told, on his own account, that nobody owed them anything in the first place? Those two questions have answers, and I want you to pick them and build the speech on them rather than giving me a menu of framings to choose between.

About six minutes, roughly 800 words, meant to be read aloud so it has to work in the mouth. No scripture, nobody in the family is religious. Don't quote him at length beyond what I've given you — I have the note, you don't, and inventing him further would be a lie.

Then, separately and briefly: tell me where the speech takes liberties. Where have you smoothed over a genuinely contested point to make it sayable at a funeral, and is the argument it ends up making actually sound, or only moving? I'd rather you say the second if that's the truth.
