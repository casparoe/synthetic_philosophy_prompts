I write a fortnightly column on decision science and statistics for a mid-size magazine — mostly the beat where I explain to people why a screening test that's 99% accurate can still leave them at coin-flip odds. Our new editor wants to start running one piece of short fiction per issue, "a story that carries an idea," and she's given me the first slot, which I'm quietly terrified about because I've never written fiction in my life. The idea I want carried is Good's theorem. Roughly: a perfectly rational Bayesian agent offered a piece of evidence at zero cost should always take it, because they can always plan to disregard it if it turns out not to change the optimal act — so free information can never make you worse off in expectation, and value of information is non-negative by default. The part I actually care about is the pile of assumptions real people blow straight through: that your preferences stay fixed across the learning event, that the evidence is true and you update on it the tidy way, that the decision you're informing doesn't itself mutate once other people know you know, that "costless" means costless in attention, sleep, dread and relationships too, and that you'll actually act optimally afterward instead of knowing and then flinching.

The setting I've been sketching — please rework it freely, I have no attachment to the names. There's a company that hands every customer one entitlement: a single guaranteed-true yes/no answer about their own next five years, free, no waiting list, no upsell. The interesting character is not a customer. She's a decline counsellor, the person whose entire job is to talk people out of using their Look, and she's extremely good at it — the company keeps her because Lookers churn, sue, and stop referring friends. On the day your story happens, an auditor from the parent company turns up, because the internal outcome study has finally come back and the net wellbeing delta between Lookers and non-Lookers, measured eighteen months out, is slightly negative, and the auditor's whole position is that this is mathematically impossible and therefore someone at her end is lying, or the study is broken, or she is secretly selecting who she counsels. I'd like the two of them to genuinely disagree in a way where the auditor's math is right inside its own frame, and the counsellor has cases, not vibes. Bonus points if the fight gets tangled up in the measurement itself: whose preferences are the benchmark, the customer's before the Look or after it, and how do you score someone as better off when the thing that made them worse off also changed what they want.

Two thousand words or so, maybe 1,500 if it runs tight. Constraints, because I know how these go: no character may say "expected value of information," "Bayesian," "utility function," or "rational agent" — the idea has to survive translation into ordinary speech, since our readers are people who skip the footnotes. Pick ONE of the broken assumptions from up there and make it structurally load-bearing, not decorative; I want a reader to be able to point at the moment in the story where the theorem's guarantee slips, even if they've never heard of the theorem. Concrete scenes, actual dialogue, someone with something to lose, and an ending that doesn't resolve into a moral — my editor has already warned me off "and then she understood" endings. Then, outside the story, give me two or three sentences saying which assumption you broke and why the auditor is technically correct anyway, so I can sanity-check it before I send it in. I'd apperciate it if you could also flag anywhere you think I've mis-stated the theorem, because if a stats professor writes in about it I'm the one who has to answer.
