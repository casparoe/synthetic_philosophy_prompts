I'm in my fourth year of a political theory PhD and I'm supposed to be finishing a chapter on commitment — Schelling-style commitment, the Strategy of Conflict stuff, plus how it got absorbed into bargaining theory and IR — and I've hit a wall with the case I want as the chapter's centerpiece. The case is Greece in the summer of 2015: Syriza elected in January on an anti-austerity platform, five months of talks with the institutions (they insisted on "the institutions," since the government wouldn't say troika) that go nowhere, Tsipras announcing a referendum in the early hours of June 27 on the creditors' latest proposal, the ECB declining to expand emergency liquidity so the banks shut and capital controls go in, No winning with about 61% on July 5, Varoufakis out the next day, the German finance ministry paper proposing Greece be parked outside the eurozone for five years leaking over the summit weekend, and then — a week after the referendum — Tsipras signing up to a third program that was in most respects harsher than the one he'd just asked people to reject. I want you to run this through commitment-race analysis in full, because I keep feeling like the framework ought to explain it and it doesn't quite, and I cant tell whether that's my fault or the framework's.

Start with the mapping, and do it properly rather than name-dropping: who the players are (Tsipras and Varoufakis, obviously, but also Schäuble, Dijsselbloem, the ECB, the IMF, the Bundestag, the Greek electorate, Syriza's left platform — tell me which of these are players in the game and which are just contraints on some player's payoff), what the real options and payoffs looked like at the moment the referendum was announced, and which moves in the sequence count as commitments at all rather than as threats, signals, bluffs, or mere facts about the world. Be strict about that last one. In particular I want your take on whether the deadlines doing so much work — the program expiring June 30, the missed IMF payment, the big ECB payment coming due in mid-July, the bank run itself — count as commitments when nobody chose them. It feels to me like the calendar bound the creditors more tightly than any speech they gave, and if a schedule does everything a hand-tied commitment does, I need to know whether that's a real difference or just a difference in drama.

Then the two things I actually care about. First: I've been kicking a distinction around with a friend in the econ department and I don't know if it survives contact with the case, so try to break it. Roughly, a commitment can work in two ways — by raising your own cost of retreat, which is the classic hand-tying, or by lowering the other side's cost of conceding, i.e. by handing them a ladder they can climb down in front of their own audience. Schelling mostly talks about the first. My claim is that the referendum was a massive dose of the first and close to zero of the second, and worse, that the No vote actively destroyed the second: after July 5, conceding got domestically harder for the creditors, not easier, so Greece maximally tied its own hands while making the other side's hands more tied too. Is that a real distinction or am I just redescribing credibility with extra steps? And was the referendum even aimed at the creditors, or inward — at Syriza's factions and the Greek electorate — in which case Schelling's two-player framing is missing the whole thing? Second: what does the episode do to commitment races as a general model? My suspicion is that the race is supposed to be dangerous because collision is catastrophic for both sides, which is what generates Schelling's shared-risk logic, the threat that leaves something to chance; but in 2015 the catastrophe was almost entirely one-sided — Greek banks, Greek depositors, Greek pensions — so there was no shared cliff to bargain over and no reason for the creditors to fear being caught second. If that's right, is Greece an anomaly, a limiting condition on the model, or evidence that commitment-race talk has been getting applied to asymmetric negotiations where it never belonged? Please tell me where the framework just goes silent instead of making it speak, and be careful about hindsight: everyone writing about this knows how it turned out, so say which moves looked like genuine commitments and which looked like bluster from inside that week rather than from now. No reading list, I've read all of it — I want the argument.
