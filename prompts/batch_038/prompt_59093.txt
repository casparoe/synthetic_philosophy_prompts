I am assembling a set of problems for the unit on persistence in my graduate metaphysics seminar, and I would like your help constructing one that I do not expect my students to find already written down for them somewhere. The question is this: give an example of an ordinary material object that undergoes what is sometimes called a Cambridge change — a change such that, on any plausible way of drawing the intrinsic/extrinsic distinction, nothing intrinsic to the object itself differs before and after — and then say what an endurantist and what a perdurantist must each make of the case. The difficulty, and the reason I want the example, lies in the second half. The perdurantist's standard reply to the problem of temporary intrinsics is that the object has different temporal parts at different times, each bearing a property incompatible with the other's, since it is the parts rather than the whole that bear them; but when the property that changes is extrinsic, that reply seems to have nothing to grip, because there is no temporary incompatibility to resolve in the first place. So I want a case in which the asymmetry actually bites: one in which the endurantist looks to have the easier time of it, and in which the perdurantist nonetheless owes some account of what her machinery is supposed to be doing.

Two constraints. First, do not give me the familiar illustrations — the thing that becomes shorter than something else, or north of something else, or the object that changes merely in respect of a property about somebody's attitudes toward it. I want a property that we would ordinarily call a real feature of the object, one about which it would not sound bizarre to ask whether the object has it, and one whose loss or acquisition would figure in an ordinary causal explanation. Second, once you have the example, tell me whether you think it shows the endurantist facing no problem at all here, or whether it shows the problem of temporary intrinsics to have been misdescribed all along — that is, aimed at a class of changes narrower than the one it was thought to cover.

Please do not offer me references, a bibliography, or a list of the secondary literature; I am interested in the case and your reasoning about it, not in attributions. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, in the manner of a letter or a considered email, with no headings, no bullet points, no boldface, and no tables. State the example plainly in your first two or three sentences, and only then develop the analysis.
