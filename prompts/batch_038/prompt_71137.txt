Forty years I did physical chemistry, mostly reaction kinetics, and a good chunk of my working life went into watching systems that quietly poisoned their own catalyst. Only got round to philosophy properly since retiring, and right now I'm stuck on Kant's universalizablity test.

The basic move I follow. Take the maxim, imagine everybody acting on it, see whether it survives. And I follow that Kant splits the failures into two kinds: some maxims can't even be conceived as universal law without a contradiction, and those yield perfect duties; others can be conceived perfectly well but you supposedly can't *will* them as universal law without your will falling into contradiction with itself, and those yield imperfect duties.

Here's what's snagging me. The second test feels like it's doing something of a totally different kind from the first, and I can't tell whether that's a feature or a leak in the theory. Ask yourself if you can will a world where nobody ever lends a hand, and the answer seems to hinge on a fact about the world and about you — that you'll probably need help yourself one day. That isn't logic, that's a wager on contingencies, exactly the empirical stuff Kant insists he's keeping out of the foundations. So: what is the contradiction-in-the-will test actually testing, and how can it be formal or a priori rather than a cost-benefit calculation in a fancy coat?

I gather there are roughly two families of reply. One says the contradiction comes from what any finite rational being necessarily wills — something about willing your own agency, or the development of your capacities, or ends that are somehow built into being an agent at all. The other says the contradiction is between the maxim and the agent's own purposes: universalize it and the very thing you were after becomes unavailable, so the maxim defeats itself. Please lay out how those differ, what each one buys you, and which (if either) really saves the claim that morality rests on zero empirical assumptions. Worth saying too whether the two can be prised apart at all, since a self-defeating maxim is arguably just one that clashes with what you necessarily will.

If a concrete case helps, here's one from the lab: "when a run comes back null and I'm up against a funding deadline, I'll file it in the drawer and only write up the runs that came out the way I hoped — and I'm happy for every researcher in my position to do likewise." Universalised, the literature rots, nobody trusts anything, my own results stop counting. Bad, clearly. But inconceivable? Journals would keep going, just degraded. My hunch is that this one lands in the will bucket rather than the conception bucket, which makes it a decent probe for whether the two buckets are really split by logic-versus-prudence or by something else. Use it or ignore it.
