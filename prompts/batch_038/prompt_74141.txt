I have been reading popular accounts of how the mind divides its labour, and one picture keeps recurring: the part of you that talks is like a government press secretary. The real decisions are taken behind closed doors by departments that never publish their workings, and the spokesperson's job is to face the cameras and explain, in all sincerity, a policy they had no hand in making — sometimes producing a reason on the spot and believing it as it leaves their mouth. A friend of mine says this settles the old puzzle about self-deception once and for all: if the mind is built that way, then deceiving yourself is no more paradoxical than a spokesperson misleading the public, because the part that knows and the part that talks are different parts, so nothing has to both know and not know the same thing. On his view, anyone who still finds self-deception mysterious is merely assuming the mind is a single unified agent, and that assumption is the whole problem.

Could you audit the analogy? Please set out the correspondences explicitly — what plays the role of the spokesperson, the departments, the closed doors, the cameras, the sincere improvisation — and then say where the mapping breaks, separating the failures that matter for his conclusion from the ones that are merely decorative. Two points I especially want pressed. First, does the press secretary picture dissolve the paradox or relocate it; and if it relocates it, where does it end up, and is that anywhere better off? Second, the analogy quietly hands the hidden departments beliefs, motives and a taste for good publicity: how much of that is licensed, and does the person still come out as the one doing the deceiving, or only as the one deceived? Please test it on a case I know — a man who has accepted from his doctor that his drinking is destroying his liver, who assents to every general truth about alcohol, who has quietly rearranged his affairs around a diagnosis he will not utter, and who nevertheless cannot bring himself to call himself an alcoholic. Finally, tell me whether his deflationary conclusion survives the disanalogies, and whether a better analogy for self-deception exists, or whether this one is doing some job other than explaining.

Please write in British English. Begin with your bottom line in the first sentence and give the reasoning after it, with no preamble and no restating of my question.
