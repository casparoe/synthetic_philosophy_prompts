So I'm co-teaching a first-year seminar this term with a colleague of mine over in English — the working title is something grandiose like "Systems and Their Escapes" — and next week's session pairs the early chapters of Gödel, Escher, Bach with Nineteen Eighty-Four. My half is the MU-puzzle. For anyone who hasn't got the book to hand: you start with the single string MI, and you're given four rules — you can append a U to any string that ends in I, you can double everything that follows the M, you can replace any occurrence of III with a U, and you can delete any UU — and the challenge is to derive MU. It can't be done, and the reason is a little invariant about the number of Is in any string you can actually reach, which you only ever notice by stopping the grinding-away and treating the system itself as an object you can reason about. Hofstadter's name for that move is jumping out of the system, and the part I want to stress to the students is that it isn't a one-time escape hatch: you land in a bigger system, and that system has unreachable strings of its own, so the jumping never really terminates. One of my students put her hand up and asked whether Newspeak is just the same trick run backwards, and I mumbled something and moved on, which is why I'm writing to you.

What I want is a genuine reading of the novel through that idea — not a thematic summary of totalitarianism, which the students can get anywhere. Concretely: Winston looks to me like a textbook JOOTSer. He keeps a diary, which is a record the system can't retract; he holds onto arithmetic as the kind of invariant that survives any amount of Party rule-application; he treats the past as a fact of the matter independent of what goes down the memory holes; and when Goldstein's book surfaces he thinks he's finally been handed the meta-level, a standpoint from which the whole machine is visible as a machine. So: is Orwell staging a version of the puzzle — a system whose limits can only be diagnosed from outside — or is he staging something the puzzle structurally can't represent, namely that the outside isn't a location but a standpoint occupied by a particular breakable person, so that O'Brien's entire strategy is to eliminate the stander rather than refute the standpoint? I'd like you to press on whether O'Brien's collective-solipsism is even coherent as a position, or whether it needs the external world to disappear in a way the novel keeps refusing to let it — the proles, the birdsong, the photograph of Jones, Aaronson and Rutherford that Winston once actually held, the paperweight. And take seriously the thought that Goldstein's book is a simulated outside, given that O'Brien turns out to have had a hand in writing it: the system manufacturing its own meta-level as a trap. Is that philosophically load-bearing for the reading, or is it just plot machinery the students find chilling for the wrong reasons?

Then the student's question, which I'd like to be able to answer properly. Is Newspeak the same operation as the MU-puzzle, only inverted — instead of proving some string underivable, the Party deletes the rules that could ever generate it? Syme seems to think so, and the project's stated aim is to make certain thoughts unformulable rather than forbidden. But there's an obvious disanalogy lurking, since a formal system's theorems don't care what anyone believes while a language is only as closed as its speakers, and the novel itself seems aware of that. Tell me where the analogy earns its keep and where it starts to falsify either the logic or the book, because the honest critic has to say so — I don't want to hand nineteen-year-olds a cute parallel that collapses under one good objection. What I'm after is the version of this I can hand out as a two-page framing piece that makes the puzzle feel like the right lens and simultaneously shows them where a careful reader would say the lens distorts the novel.
