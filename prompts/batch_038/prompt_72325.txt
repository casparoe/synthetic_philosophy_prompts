So, no science background here, never took a psych class, but a passenger of mine started talking about predictive processing and the free energy stuff about a year ago and I've been reading around it ever since, and it's kind of rewired how I think about what I actually do for a living. I'm a flight instructor, mostly instrument students, twelve of them at the moment, and I fly with each one two or three times a week. A huge chunk of the job is teaching people not to believe their own body once the horizon goes away — the leans, that turn-and-roll feeling that isn't there, the night departure over the water or over unlit terrain where your inner ear is dead certain you're pitching up into a stall when you're really nose-down and gathering speed.

The standard spiel is "trust your instruments" and "keep the cross-check going," and it's not wrong, but it doesn't explain anything, and the students who need it most are the ones who nod along and then forty minutes later, tired and a little spooked, fly exactly what they feel. Which suggests to me that a slogan isn't competing with the body on the body's terms.

The predictive processing picture seems to say something much more specific, and more useful: you never get the world raw, you get your model's guess, the senses mostly only speak up where the guess is wrong, and how much the guess versus the evidence gets to drive things depends on how reliable the brain currently rates each of them. If that's right, then the leans isn't a broken instrument in the head, it's my student's brain weighting a very confident wrong guess above weak noisy evidence — and there ought to be a drill that actually shifts that weighting, rather than one that just repeats a rule at them.

Can you turn that into a procedure I could teach? Some constraints from my end:

- It has to survive being briefed in about fifteen minutes before a flight, and it has to be runnable by a tired non-scientist in a cockpit, alone, later, when I'm not in the right seat.
- Give it as ordered steps or checks, with the actual words to say out loud where speaking helps, and say what counts as having passed each one versus just gone through the motions.
- Mark clearly where the procedure runs out and it's a judgment call that I or the student has to own — including anything that's really about values or risk appetite rather than about perception.
- Tell me which distinctions from the theory have to survive the simplification because the drill breaks without them, which ones you dropped as decoration, and — this is the part I really want — where the theory is not doing any work at all and I'd get the same result from ordinary instruction. I'd rather know that than have it dressed up in fancy language.

Then run it on three cases:

1. A student in the sim who is genuinely, sincerely level in a coordinated bank and reports feeling perfectly fine, and won't believe the attitude indicator.
2. A pilot who's been hand-flying, then engages the autopilot on a long night leg, and eight minutes later the aircraft starts doing something he did not expect and did not command.
3. A case where the procedure gives an answer I don't want — I'm thinking something like the pitot or the static side icing up so that several instruments all agree on the same false story, which is exactly the situation where "trust your instruments" is the wrong advice. Show me what the procedure says there and whether it can even detect that it's being fooled.

One more thing: please don't hand me a reading list or name papers and researchers at the end. I can't chase any of that down and I wouldn't trust myself to get the names right anyway. Just use the ideas well and tell me where they stop applying.
