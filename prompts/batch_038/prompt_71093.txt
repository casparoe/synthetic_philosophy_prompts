I'm TA-ing a first-year history of philosophy section next Tuesday and I've drawn the short straw: fifty minutes on why Hobbes thinks geometry is the only science human beings actually have. The students have just finished the state-of-nature and covenant chapters, they've been told twice in lecture that Hobbes is a materialist for whom all thought bottoms out in sensation, and they did Descartes's method of doubt the week before. So they'll walk in with two boxes already labelled, empiricist and rationalist, and the thing I have to teach doesnt fit either one.

What I want to teach is his account of how we get genuine knowledge of causes only where we can construct the thing ourselves — we know a circle because we can lay down the motion that generates it, we know a commonwealth because we make it, and we don't know natural bodies in that strong sense at all because we can't build them, so physics can only ever give us conditional guesswork about what effects some possible cause would have. And the payoff, which is the reason this week is on the syllabus at all: its the same doctrine that makes his politics a science and his physics not one.

Don't explain the doctrine to me, I've got the rough shape and I don't need a summary or a reading list — they already have the text assigned. Plan the explanation. Specifically:

- The order the pieces have to go in and what each one depends on, given that the constructive account sounds flatly self-contradictory sitting next to everything starts in sense. Tell me whether to put the sensory-origin claim on the board first and let the tension bite, or to build the constructive account up first so they never feel the contradiction, and why.
- The single example that should carry the whole fifty minutes. I'm leaning toward defining a circle as what you get when you swing a stick around with one end pinned down. Tell me whether that can bear the weight on its own or whether it needs a partner case, what it has to have in it, and what it must not have — I'm worried it makes construction sound like literally drawing things on the board and measuring them.
- The misconceptions, with the point in the sequence where each one gets headed off. I can already predict "so we invent the definitions, so the theorems are just true because we decided them" and "so math turns out to be innate after all, Hobbes is a rationalist"; I also expect someone who's read a wikipedia summary to say "but didn't he fail to square the circle and get humiliated by the mathematicians, so he's obviously wrong about all this." Some of these are only defusable after a later step, and if I try to kill them too early I'll just be the one who creates them — so say which is which.
- One question I can ask in the last five minutes that will actually discriminate between students who got it and students who are nodding politely.
- What I should openly refuse to get into at this level and say I'm refusing: the circle-squaring mess, the feud with the Oxford geometers, whether his empiricism and this are really consistent, and anything that would turn the hour into a philosophy-of-mathematics seminar.

Constraints: 25 students, one whiteboard, most of them haven't touched geometry since high school, one has done proof-based math and will check out if I go slow, and I need the last five minutes for the essay prompt. There's also a bright one who will try to connect it forward to Kant or to verificationism — tell me whether to let that bridge get built in the room or to bat it down, and if I bat it down, what I actually say to them without sounding like I'm dodging.

Last thing: flag any point where the reading I've sketched above is contested enough that I'd be better off telling the class it's contested than bluffing my way through. Just say what the disagreement is about — don't send me hunting for sources.
