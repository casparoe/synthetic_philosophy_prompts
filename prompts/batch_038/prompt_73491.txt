I am in the second half of a philosophy of language seminar whose unit on generics, defaults, and ideology has turned into a week-long argument about Hofstadter's satirical paper on purity in language — the piece, descended from his Scientific American column on default assumptions, that recasts a fastidious prescriptivist complaint about usage with racial terms substituted for gendered ones, epithets included. Nearly everyone in the class treats the substitution as decisive: if the racial version makes you wince and the gendered version reads as harmless, then the difference lies in you rather than in the grammar, and the defense of the generic masculine as mere convention is dead.

I would like to examine the substitution as an argument rather than as a performance, because I suspect it is being asked to carry more than it can bear. Two things prompted this. First, our instructor remarked that the piece's force may not depend on any similarity between racism and sexism at all, since what it attacks is the form of the defense — that a term is exempt from moral assessment because it is only grammar — so the analogy might be dispensable even if the reductio is not. Second, a classmate ran the same procedure along other axes for a term paper and reports mixed results: along class lines the substitution produced something the seminar found merely funny, while along disability lines it produced a different sort of discomfort that several people described as punching down rather than as a revelation about defaults.

So: audit the analogy. Give the correspondence table explicitly — what in the source maps onto what in the target, covering the generic term, the marked term, the prescriptivist's appeal to tradition and to elegance, the harm alleged, and the reader's revulsion together with the evidential weight the argument gives it. Then separate the disanalogies that matter for the use my class is making of it from those that are decorative: whether gender's universality and its structure as an unmarked default has any counterpart in race; whether revulsion tracks moral harm or merely taboo; and whether the comparison quietly imports a claim of parity between the two systems of subordination. Tell me which conclusion, if either, survives — the modest one, that the appeal to grammar fails, or the ambitious one, that sexist usage is as objectionable as racist usage would be. And given my classmate's misfires, say whether the argument's success is structural, such that anything of the right shape will do the work, or contingent on features gender happens to share with race that class and disability lack. If you think a better model is available — a reductio, a consistency test, an imagination-expanding device that makes no comparative claim — say which, and what it can and cannot support.
