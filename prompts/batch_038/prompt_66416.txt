The question (500 words max): "Explain what pain asymbolia is. Then argue either (a) that it shows the badness of pain is not built into the pain experience itself, or (b) that it does not. Either way, say what your opponent should make of the patients own reports of what they feel."

Here's the answer I got:

"Pain asymbolia is a condition, usually following a lesion in the left insula and the parietal operculum around it, in which a patient still feels pain but no longer suffers from it. The classic description is of patients who smile or even laugh when a noxious stimulus is applied, who don't withdraw the limb, and who say the pain is 'there' but that it doesn't bother them. The important part is that the sensory side survives: they can localize the stimulus and typically rate its intensity much as a normal subject would, while their unpleasantness ratings come out at or near zero. The standard interpretation is a sensory-limbic disconnection — the nociceptive signal reaches cortex but fails to reach the affective and motivational systems that normally make it aversive. The patient population is small and heterogeneous, and some asymbolics do show residual avoidance behaviour, so the phenomenon shouldn't be over-read.

I want to argue (a): the case shows the badness of pain isn't built into the pain experience itself. If unpleasantness were an intrinsic feature of the pain quale, then a patient who feels the very same pain, with the same location and the same intensity, would have to find it unpleasant. Asymbolics feel it and don't. So unpleasantness must come from somewhere else — the link between the experience and the motivational system — and could in principle come apart from it. On the two main accounts of pain's content, imperativism says the experience commands you to protect or stop using the body part, while evaluativism says it represents the body part as damaged and that this is bad. The difference between them doesn't matter here, since on either view the content is directed at the body and its badness is a matter of what the experience tells you to do about it. The evaluativist will say the asymbolic still has a pain representing damage-as-bad but fails to form the matching belief, which is odd, because the pain is supposed to be the very thing that would ground that belief.

As for the opponent's use of the reports, I don't think they can be taken at face value. To feel pain just is to be bothered by it, so a report of a pain that doesn't bother you is either a loose use of the word or an unreliable description of the patient's own state. Since the dissociation view rests entirely on those reports, and the reports undercut themselves, the opponent has no evidence for the dissociation at all.

It also seems to follow that pain isn't intrinsically bad, and if that's right the wrongness of causing it has to come from the distress it normally produces."
