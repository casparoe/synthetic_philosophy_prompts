I am midway through a novel set in a small Alpine republic whose defence rests, as Switzerland's does, on universal conscription and on what its own official language calls armed neutrality, and I have spent two summers accumulating notes that I cannot turn into scenes. My central character is twenty-one and has finished recruit school. Under the militia system he keeps his personal service rifle in a locked cupboard in the hallway of the flat he shares with his mother; the ammunition is no longer issued to him to take home, although his grandfather's generation kept a sealed box of cartridges on the same shelf. Three weeks a year he reports for a refresher course, travels there by train in uniform with the weapon at his feet, and comes home again. When his service obligation ends he may acquire the rifle outright, provided he applies for the ordinary civilian acquisition permit, so that a state weapon becomes a private object by an act of paperwork. His mother, by contrast, could not buy a handgun or a semi-automatic rifle without stating a reason to the cantonal office, and could not carry one in public without a permit that is issued sparingly. In 2011 the electorate was asked whether the service rifles should be removed from private homes and stored in secure army premises; the proposal was defeated by a comfortable margin, and one of the arguments pressed against it was that locking the weapons in arsenals would amount to the state announcing that it no longer trusted its citizens. The constitutional tradition behind all of this once forbade the federation from maintaining a standing army at all, and the troops may not be sent abroad to fight.

What I want is an account of what this arrangement presupposes, philosophically, rather than a history of it. The obvious puzzle is that one and the same legal order treats a firearm in civilian hands as an object requiring a demonstrated need and a specific justification, and treats the same object leaning in a conscript's hallway as requiring nothing whatsoever — not a reason, not a psychological assessment, not a course in safe handling beyond what the army itself supplies. That looks like a claim that dangerousness is not a property of things, and not even a property of persons, but of the relationships persons stand in to institutions. Is that the presupposition, or is something stronger going on? I also want you to deal with the ordering of fears that the militia system seems to encode: a citizen army whose weapons are already in the population's homes is, by design, harder to turn against that population than a professional garrison would be, which suggests the arrangement treats state violence directed inward as the more serious standing threat, whereas a professional volunteer force treats diffuse private violence as the more serious one. Say whether that ordering is a claim about human nature, a claim about institutions, or simply a sediment of a particular history, and say which rival pictures of the state it thereby rules out. Then there is the question of what the practice asks of the person. It appears indifferent to his motives: it wants attendance on a schedule, a clean weapon, and a body at an assembly point, and it never asks whether he loves the country; yet the rhetoric surrounding it is full of citizen-soldier virtue. Which of those does the institution actually depend on, and can a system that requires only compliance honestly describe itself in the language of civic republicanism? Finally, tell me what armed neutrality is on this account — a moral status the state possesses, or a capacity it manufactures out of its civilians' evenings, hallways, and annual leave — and whether neutrality understood as the latter thing is a promise or a threat.

I would like the Nordic comparison handled in the same register, because two of my minor characters are Swedish and Finnish and I keep writing them as though their countries did the same thing. Sweden suspended conscription, then reinstated it a few years later in a gender-neutral but strongly selective form, so that only a small fraction of each cohort is actually called up; Finland retained universal male conscription together with an enormous reserve. Do those two models presuppose the same theory of the citizen and the state that the Alpine republic's does, or does Sweden's selective call-up presuppose that defence is a specialised task entrusted to a chosen and persuaded few, while Finland's mass reserve presupposes that the entire population is the instrument? Where the three countries agree, tell me whether the agreement is a shared philosophy or a shared geography. And then close with the counterfactual: state concretely what would have to change — in the cupboard, in the refresher course, in the permit office, in the transfer of the rifle at the end of service, in the referendum argument about trust — if the presupposed view were replaced by a fully professional force operating inside a military alliance, and again if it were replaced by general civilian disarmament with a monopoly on force held only by police. Please distinguish carefully throughout between what the practice strictly cannot do without and what it merely sits comfortably alongside, and mark the places where the practice is philosophically underdetermined, so that several incompatible theories would license exactly the same cupboard in exactly the same hallway. I do not want a reading list or a survey of who has written about militias; I want the implicit theory reconstructed and then judged for internal coherence, in prose I could hand to a sceptical reader without embarrassment.
