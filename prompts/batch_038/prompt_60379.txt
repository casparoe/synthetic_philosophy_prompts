I have been reading the rationalist blogs and their comment threads — Overcoming Bias, the old LessWrong sequences, the years-long arguments about Sleeping Beauty and the Doomsday Argument — for something like fifteen years, and over that stretch I have accumulated a fairly definite position on self-locating belief. A reading group I belong to has asked each member to write down their settled view in advance so that the others can attack it, and before I circulate mine I would like a proper consistency check on it. I am not asking you to tell me what the right answer to Sleeping Beauty is, and I am not asking for a tour of the literature; assume I have read all of it and that I once thought the halfer line was obviously correct and no longer do. What I want is to know whether the following package hangs together, and if it does not, where exactly it comes apart and what the cheapest repair is.

Here is the package.

First, on the standard case, Beauty's credence in heads upon waking is one third. I take the halfer's insistence that she gains no relevant new evidence to be the central mistake: she does gain evidence, of a centered kind, and it is exactly the sort of thing that ought to move her.

Second, I understand credence in the betting-odds sense. To say that my credence in a proposition is some number is just to say that the number gives the odds at which I would take either side of a bet on that proposition. I do not believe there is any further fact about belief sitting behind that disposition.

Third, I reject stakes-relativity in belief of every kind. What an agent ought to believe is fixed entirely by their evidence; the reward structure of the decisions they might happen to face is not part of their evidence and therefore cannot alter what they ought to believe. Two agents with the same evidence who differ only in what they stand to win or lose have the same credences.

Fourth, in anthropics I am an SIA-type rather than an SSA-type: other things equal, a world containing more observers is likelier to be actual, simply because there is more of a chance of being one of them. I accept Bostrom's Presumptuous Philosopher as a cost rather than a reductio. If that principle genuinely licenses wagering enormous sums on the cosmos being absurdly large, I am prepared to write the check.

Fifth, the Doomsday Argument is bad reasoning, and I have never encountered a formulation of it I could accept.

Sixth, I am a causal decision theorist. I think evidential betting — the picture on which you select an act because it constitutes good news — gets the structure of agency backwards, and I do not want my account of belief to rest on it.

Seventh, on the interpretation of quantum mechanics I am an Everettian, and I think many-worlds makes Beauty less mysterious rather than more: there is a definite branch weight, and credence ought to track it.

What I want back is an audit rather than a lecture. Go through the relations among these, tell me which of them genuinely pull against one another and which merely look as though they do, and rank the genuine ones by severity instead of presenting them as an undifferentiated list. For each, say what would have to be surrendered or revised, what that revision would cost me elsewhere in the package, and then tell me which single change would clean up the most damage. I would also like to be told where a tension becomes real only under one particular reading of a claim, because I suspect at least one item above is stated loosely enough that its content shifts with how you take it, and I cannot tell which.

Please answer in continuous prose: no headings, no bullet points, no numbered lists in the reply, no bold or italic emphasis, no tables — paragraphs only, in the manner of a long letter or email. Begin with your overall verdict in two or three sentences before you work through the pairs.
