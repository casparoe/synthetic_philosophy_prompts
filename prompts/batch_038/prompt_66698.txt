You are a philosopher of mind who works closely on mechanistic interpretability; please think carefully and reason through the material before committing to a list, rather than reaching for familiar slogans from either side.

Context. I am drafting the methods section of a paper on a small transformer trained only on the text of recorded chess games. The central claim is that the model internally represents the board state — a content that never appears in its input, since it sees only move notation. My evidence is of the usual two kinds: board features are linearly decodable from the residual stream at mid-layers, and patching certain directions changes the model's move choices. A reviewer objects that I have smuggled in a notion of representation I never defended, and that decodability plus an intervention is not the same as content. Before I answer the reviewer, I want to know what answering would require.

Deliverable: the desiderata — the specification any account of what it is for a trained network to represent something must satisfy to be usable for this purpose. Attach to each requirement the concrete case or consideration motivating it, drawn from what networks like this actually do. In particular, make sure the list addresses: how a state can represent something the model can also get wrong; how a model represents a state of affairs rather than each of its constituents separately; the gap between "a probe can read it off" and "the model uses it"; and the fact that a network's only history is gradient descent on a proxy loss over a training distribution, with no report, no evolved function, and no consumer semantics to lean on.

Then, separately:

1. Mark which requirements are non-negotiable and which are really commitments of one camp — for instance, whether content must be intrinsic to the geometry of an activation vector, or may be fixed by the state's causal role across the whole computation.
2. Identify any subset of the requirements that cannot be jointly satisfied once you take seriously that features in trained networks are superposed, with many features sharing the same directions, and say which requirement should give way and why.
3. Say which single requirement excludes the most otherwise attractive accounts.
4. Close by stating which of the requirements my reviewer is entitled to insist on and which I may reasonably decline.

Keep the weight on the criteria and their motivating cases; I will do the scorecard against particular accounts myself.
