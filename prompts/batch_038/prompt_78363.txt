I am in the first year of a doctoral programme in Buddhist philosophy, and am reading Nāgārjuna's *Mūlamadhyamakakārikā* directly against a Sanskrit edition, producing my own deliberately literal English renderings, because the two published translations I have access to diverge most exactly where my difficulty lies. I am meant to turn the resulting notes into a commentary paper, and I would be grateful for help with a crux that has stopped me for some weeks.

The two passages, in my own rendering — unpolished, and arranged to show the Sanskrit word order rather than to read well:

From the opening chapter, on conditions, the first verse:
Never, anywhere, are any things found to have arisen
from themselves, from something other, from both, or without a cause.

From the chapter examining nirvāṇa, near its end (25.24 in the edition I am using, though I have seen the same verse cited under other numbers):
Everything is really so; is not really so; is both really so and not really so;
is neither really so nor not really so — this is the instruction of the Awakened One.

Two notes on my choices. Where I write *really so* I have not resolved the underlying word, since so far as I can tell from my dictionary it covers both something like *true*, said of a statement, and something like *genuine* or *actual*, said of a thing. And where I write *instruction* I have taken the word in its ordinary sense of authoritative teaching, though it may equally denote a directive to be followed rather than a description of how matters stand.

My difficulty is this. In the first passage all four limbs are refused; in the second all four are asserted, asserted together, and asserted of everything. I am not looking for the familiar reassurances. I have read that the second verse is to be taken as a graded account of teaching — that one limb suits one kind of hearer and another limb another — and that the first is a mere reduction of an opponent's positions, so that the two never actually meet. But a graded reading, so far as I can see, cannot explain why the verse says that all four stand together as the instruction rather than assigning each limb to a distinct audience; and the reduction reading does not explain why the very same fourfold shape that is refused in chapter 1 is endorsed in chapter 25.

What I want is help thinking through the following, in this order.

1. Are these two passages in genuine conflict, or only apparently so? If only apparently, state precisely which assumption has to be dropped for the conflict to dissolve, and whether Nāgārjua has the resources to drop it. If genuinely, say so plainly: I would rather have that than a harmonisation.

2. What are the third and fourth limbs doing that the first two are not? In particular, once the third limb is admitted a contradiction has already been accepted, so what further work can the fourth be doing, and why should it not simply be redundant? This makes me suspect that the four limbs are not four truth-functional options among which one chooses, but something else — postures towards a question, perhaps, or stages in a sequence. If you think that, tell me what the shape of the schema actually is, and what it could mean to refuse all four of them at once, as chapter 1 does.

3. I have been working with a hypothesis of my own, which I would like assessed rather than flattered. It is that the limbs are not answers to a single question but answers to questions with different presuppositions: the first two limbs share the presupposition that origination, or that things are really so, is a relation holding between independently standing items, whereas the third and fourth drop it. On this view the tetralemma is never exhaustive, because its limbs are not alternatives within one space. What would have to be true for this to work? What does it cost — does it make the refusals of chapter 1 trivial, since an opponent who never shared the presupposition was never asserting what is being refused? Is there anything in the two passages that supports or undermines it?

4. Nāgārjuna is elsewhere represented as replying to the charge that his own statements are empty along with everything else by saying that he has no thesis and therefore nothing to defend. Does that reply sit better with the first passage or with the second? And does the answer turn on whether *really so* applies to statements or to things, and on whether *instruction* describes a state of affairs or prescribes one?

Please do not send me to secondary literature: I cannot consult any at present and have no use for a reading list or for the attribution of views to named commentators. Work from the two passages as I have rendered them and from what you already know of the argument. Where your answer depends on a contested reading of a word, say which reading you are assuming and what in the verse supports it; and where you are uncertain, mark it as uncertain rather than smoothing it over.
