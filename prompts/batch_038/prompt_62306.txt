So — third novel, set in a philosophy department, working title is something like *The Ledger*, and I've hit the part where I actually have to write the paper artefacts that show up on the page. Last book I faked this kind of thing and a physicist beta reader took me apart over two paragraphs about a whiteboard equation, and my agent still brings it up at lunch. There's a scene where my protagonist, an associate prof teaching an upper-level seminar on moral psychology, sets the final exam, and I want the question in the book to be a question a real person could genuinely have set, not a placeholder I'd have to blur out in the proofs.

Here's the knot I want the exam to hang on, partly becuase it's also the hinge of the plot and I want the exam and the novel to be the same problem seen from two angels. My protagonist's oldest friend runs a food bank, and about fifteen years ago she noticed that her own reluctance was leaking out — the tight little smile, the half-second delay before saying yes — and that this was humiliating the people she was trying to help. So she deliberately set about making herself into someone who actually wants to give, using the ordinary tools: keeping a private note after each occasion of whether she'd wanted to do it, rehearsing, restructuring her week so that giving stayed cheap instead of costly, refusing to let herself tally what it cost her. Eventually the desire became real and unforced, and she now hands things over with genuine pleasure. What I want the question to press is whether the moral worth of what she does *now* inherits anything at all from the reasons for which she built the desire in the first place — and whether it changes the answer if she built it for a reason that wasn't moral, say because she found her own grudgingness aesthetically revolting, or because she wanted to be the sort of person other people describe as wonderful. I want students shoved into whether there's a regress here (if worth is inherited, inherited from what, and where does the chain stop?), and into whether the whole distinction between acting from duty and acting out of a well-built character even survives a case where the character was built on purpose by the agent herself.

Could you write me: (a) the exam question exactly as it would be printed, with whatever framing preamble a professor would realistically include, about the length of a real one — and no quotations from anybody inside the question itself, both because my character is the sort who thinks putting quotes on an exam is a crutch and because it saves me having to get them right; (b) a model answer of roughly 700 words, sized for the 45 minutes this question would get on a two-hour paper; and (c) a marking scheme out of 25 that says what an A answer does that a B answer doesn't. I mean the specific moves, not generalities: whether they see that any inheritance story needs a stopping condition and can say what would make one non-arbitrary; whether they notice that the harm she was originally responding to — the leaked reluctance, the humiliation of the recipient — is itself a right-making feature of the situation, which makes the original engineering look less like self-improvement and more like moral attention; whether they can explain why the engineered desire isn't just a disguised appeal to consequences about her own character, which would collapse the case into something much more ordinary; and how they handle the vain variant, including whether a motive's pedigree can be tainted by an origin the agent has since repudiated. Then tell me the two or three mistakes that should cost the most marks — especially the failure mode where a student who has memorised the labels (motive of duty, moral worth, the integrity objection, virtue as second nature) writes eight hundred fluent words and gets nowhere. Assume they've read the standard material and nothing I made up, and please don't hand me a reading list. As the very last line of your reply, put \answer{...} with the crux in six words or fewer — the single thing that separates the A from the B.
