Our university film society screened James Cameron's *Titanic* (1997) last week, and the discussion afterward hardened into a sustained argument between two members, Nadia and Owen, about whether the tears in the room counted as genuine emotion. I did most of the listening, so I can report the exchange without taking a side.

Nadia opened (1) by observing that a fair number of people wept at Jack's death and at the long shot of the water crowded with bodies, while knowing perfectly well that Jack and Rose never existed and that the ship goes down. She concluded that the belief requirement — the claim that an emotion presupposes belief in the existence of its object — is simply false, and that the paradox of fiction dissolves once belief is dropped as a precondition of feeling.

Owen replied (2) that this misdescribes the rival account. On the make-believe view the point was never that the physiology is counterfeit; it is that quasi-emotions differ from emotions in lacking the beliefs and the action-dispositions that normally accompany them. Nobody in that screening room ran for a lifeboat, telephoned the coast guard, or tried to warn anyone. What occurred was a real response inside a game of make-believe, not grief about the world.

Nadia answered (3) with a methodological objection: that view is compatible with any behavior whatsoever. Weep, and it is quasi-grief; leave the room and pace, and it is distress about the experience of watching; text a friend about it afterward, and it is enthusiasm for a story. If no possible evidence could tell against a theory, she said, it is not a theory, and the paradox has been declared immune rather than solved.

Owen said (4) that this is unfair, because the view has content: it predicts that such responses will be unusually controllable, will come with an absence of the standard action tendencies, and will collapse if the viewer declines to play. He cited the familiar case of a viewer made physically wretched by an obviously artificial monster he knows cannot reach him, and who does not flee.

Nadia then advanced (5) a criterion of her own, which she called the demand test: an emotion is genuine only insofar as it disposes its subject to do something costly on account of it. She argued the screening vindicated the test in a damaging way, since the room wept for two invented lovers while sitting unmoved through the drowning of some fifteen hundred real people — the only non-fictional images in the entire film being the shots of the actual wreck. On the demand test, neither set of deaths elicited genuine grief, and she said that if Owen's account can accommodate that much, it should say plainly that what the audience tasted was the flavor of grief rather than grief, which is a moral criticism of the film rather than a piece of metaphysics.

Owen conceded (6) the flavor-of-grief description but rejected the moralization, on the ground that it assumes without argument that only belief-based emotion can be morally serious. He added that her appeal to the real dead proves too much: most of us do not grieve individually over the casualties in a famine dispatch read that morning either, so whatever failure Nadia has identified is not distinctive of fiction.

Could you adjudicate this move by move? For each of the six steps, say whether it succeeds and what turns on it. In particular: is Nadia's charge at (3) a real objection, or does (4) genuinely supply the empirical content she says is missing? Does the demand test at (5) help her or hurt her — Owen seems to think it concedes his point, and I could not tell whether he is right. Is his retort at (6) a fair reductio of her move, or does it beg the question by assuming that the relevant comparison class is all emotion rather than emotion responsive to fiction? Close by saying what both of them are missing, and whether the disagreement is substantive or a quarrel over how the word *genuine* is to be policed.
