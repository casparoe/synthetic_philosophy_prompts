I am a novelist, and I am at work on a book whose central conceit is a civil service. In my novel there exists an Office of Individuation: a small, patient bureaucracy whose only function is to decide, for any two things presented to it, whether they are two things or one thing entered twice, and to record the verdict in a permanent ledger. Most of its casework is trivial. One case is not.

I would like you to write a short story — say between 1,400 and 2,000 words — dramatizing that case. The petitioners are the two inhabitants of Max Black's old thought experiment: a universe containing nothing else whatsoever except two exactly similar spheres of iron, each in every describable respect a duplicate of the other. They are mute, and there is to be no dialogue from them. The story is told in the first person by the clerk to whom the case has been assigned, and it takes the form of a working report interleaved with private doubts the clerk does not intend anyone to read.

The whole story turns on whether the clerk can find a legitimate reason to write two entries rather than one. Please observe the following constraints, which matter to me considerably more than plot:

First, the clerk must attempt at least three distinct strategies of individuation, and each must fail for its own reason rather than the same reason three times over. I have in mind, loosely, a strategy resting on where each sphere stands relative to the other, a strategy resting on some difference of origin or history, and a strategy resting on the bare fact that the clerk can lay a finger on one of them and say this one. Do not label them as such or announce which is which; the clerk should arrive at them the way a functioning bureaucrat would, through precedent and procedure, and should be permitted to be mistaken about why each one fails.

Second, somewhere past the middle, one of the clerk's instruments must appear to register a discrepancy — a hair's-breadth asymmetry between the spheres, of whatever kind you prefer. The story must never resolve whether the discrepancy is real or an artifact of the measuring. What interests me is the clerk's dawning realization that a genuine discrepancy would destroy the case rather than decide it: if there is a difference, the puzzle was never a puzzle, and the ledger becomes a formality anyone could complete.

Third, the ending must be an act — the clerk writes something in the ledger, or refuses to — and that act must be made to carry weight rather than to land as a twist or a punchline. By the last page both available verdicts should look defensible and faintly disgraceful.

Fourth, keep the prose restrained and institutional. I do not want cosmic grandeur, rhetorical questions directed at the reader, or the spheres given interiority. Whatever pathos the story has should arise from the clerk's procedures and small humiliations.

After the story, please add an author's note of roughly three hundred to four hundred words. In it, tell me where the fiction has taken liberties with the underlying argument: which assumptions about space, and about what may count as a property at all, the narrative quietly adopts in order to make the case look hard; whether the clerk's third strategy treats bare thisness as an occult quality or as a live candidate, and whether that treatment is fair to the philosophers who have proposed it; and whether the device of the unresolved discrepancy smuggles in an answer the thought experiment is meant to leave open. Then tell me how confident you are in your own reading of the argument — I mean the confidence a careful reader ought to place in the note's claims about the philosophy, not in the story's inventions — and single out the one claim in the note you are least sure of, explaining briefly why you are least sure of that one rather than of some other.
