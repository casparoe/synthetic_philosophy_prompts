I want to stress-test a position about historical explanation: that a well-constructed narrative does genuine explanatory work, not merely the work of a covering-law-plus-initial-conditions argument dressed up for readers — the ordering, the emphasis, and the account's way of making salient which antecedents mattered are features of the explanation itself. Map the position's exposure rather than defending it.

Which arguments or results would count against it, and how decisive is each? Address at least the charge that any narrative explanation is an elliptical sketch of a nomological argument, so the position wins only by leaving the laws implicit; the charge that what narrative adds is interest-relative rather than a feature of the explanandum, collapsing the view into a pragmatic account of explanation it never endorsed; and the rival-narrative problem, where historians agree on every fact yet deliver incompatible explanatory upshots. Say what the historiography would have to look like for each of these to land, rank them from fatal to merely awkward, and separate what a committed narrativist would concede — I suspect the sketch concession is cheaper than it appears — from what they could rightly dismiss.

Then tell me whether the view can fail at all: can any historical case be redescribed as narrative-explanatory after the fact, and is that insulation a genuine defect for a thesis about explanation? Finally, how does it handle an explanandum that is a non-event, such as why a general strike never occurred? That is where I expect it to buckle.
