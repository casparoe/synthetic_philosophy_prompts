I'm TA-ing an upper-level phil of biology seminar and I'm about halfway thru the midterm pile. Week 2 was theories of biological function — Wright, Cummins, Millikan, Neander; Boorse got name-checked in lecture but wasn't examinable. Question 2 was worth 15 points and read like this:

> Suppose a hypothetical lineage in which a structure S was shaped by natural selection because of its contribution to some effect A: S's producing A is what explains why ancestors bearing S out-reproduced their alternatives. In the present-day population the conditions that made A advantageous are long gone, and S no longer contributes to A in any way. But S is now indispensable to a different effect B — a contribution that only became relevant in the last few dozen generations, so S has never been selected for B and could not yet have been shaped by selection for it.
>
> (a) [8 points] What does the causal-role account say about whether A and B are functions of S? What does the selected-effect account say?
>
> (b) [7 points] Which account, if either, is better placed to underwrite the judgement that S is malfunctioning when B fails? Say what "malfunction" is doing on each account.

And here's the script I'm looking at:

> Both accounts agree that functions are effects of a trait; they disagree about which effects count.
>
> On the causal-role account, a function of S is any effect of S that contributes to some capacity of the containing system it's part of — here, the organism. Functions come out of whatever analytic strategy the investigator has picked, so one structure can have many functions and none of them is more proper than the others. In this case B is a function of S, because S's doing B is part of the causal story of how the organism achieves the capacities it achieves. A drops out, though, since causal-role functions require that the trait actually have the capacity in question and S has no capacity to produce A any more. So the causal-role account is the more restrictive of the two in one respect: it can't credit functions that aren't being exercised.
>
> On the selected-effect account the function of S is A, because A is the effect that explains why S is there at all. B is not a function of S however useful it is — it's an incidental effect, and to call it a function is just to confuse what the trait does now with why it exists.
>
> Wright's etiological analysis and the selected-effect theories of Millikan and Neander are basically a single view: functions are those effects that explain a trait's presence, with Millikan adding the normative gloss that a trait is supposed to do what it was selected for. That normativity is the real payoff of the whole etiological family, and it's what settles (b). Malfunction is normative — it means the trait isn't doing what it's supposed to do — and only an account with that normativity can say S is malfunctioning when B fails. The causal-role account has no room for malfunction at all, since on it any effect a trait has is one of its functions, so a trait can never fail to do what it does. And because that account makes function ascriptions relative to the interests of whoever is doing the explaining, it can't underwrite the objective dysfunction judgements medicine and psychiatry need. The selected-effect account is interest-independent — S's function is a fact about its history — so it's the one to build a theory of dysfunction on. Its cost is epistemic: you have to know the selection history before you can say what anything's function is, which in practice often means guessing.
