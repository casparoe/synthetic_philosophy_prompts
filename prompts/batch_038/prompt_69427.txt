I teach a third-year seminar on the mathematics and philosophy of infinity, and in the handout I wrote for the week on the continuum problem I explained independence by way of Euclid's fifth postulate. The argument I gave was roughly this: Beltrami's and Poincaré's models showed that the negation of the parallel postulate is consistent with the rest of Euclid's axioms, so the axioms simply do not decide the question; Gödel's constructible universe and Cohen's forcing do exactly the same job for the continuum hypothesis and its negation; and therefore, just as we no longer ask which geometry is the true one but speak only of geometries, we should stop asking whether the continuum hypothesis is true, since the question is underdetermined and settling it would be a matter of choosing axioms rather than discovering facts. One student then asked something I could not answer on the spot: if there is no fact of the matter, why do set theorists go on proposing large cardinal and forcing axioms in the hope of settling the continuum problem and its neighbours — are they the ones who have failed to absorb the analogy? My supervisor, who works on the philosophy of arithmetic, was blunter, and said the comparison is a howler because it trades on two different senses of 'model', and because the geometric case was resolved in a way that has no counterpart in set theory. I have until the seminar reconvenes in a fortnight to decide whether to rewrite that page or defend it, and I would rather know precisely how much of it is defensible.

So please audit the analogy for me, working through it carefully rather than reaching for the standard summary. First give me the correspondence explicitly, in full sentences and in order: which feature of the fifth-postulate episode is supposed to play the role of ZFC, of the constructible universe, of a forcing extension, of the two relative-consistency results, of the plurality of equally legitimate geometries, of the question which geometry describes the space we live in, and of the resolution the geometric debate eventually reached. Then give me the disanalogies, but separated into those that actually bear on the conclusion I drew and those that are merely local imperfections — for instance, whether it matters that the hyperbolic models were constructed inside Euclidean geometry whereas a forcing extension adds sets to a model instead of reinterpreting one; whether it matters that 'the true geometry' needs and arguably has a physical referent while the set theorist's target is supposed to be a single intended universe; whether independence from a given finite axiomatisation is in any case a completely general feature of first-order theories, so that my reasoning would prove far too much about undecidable arithmetical statements; and whether it matters that one debate closed and the other has live programmes of candidate new axioms that have settled adjacent questions. Tell me what the comparison quietly imports that the mathematics does not deliver — that a theory's having non-isomorphic models entails that its subject matter is indeterminate, or that truth collapses into derivability from accepted axioms, or that there is no intended model of set theory at all — and say whether my conclusion survives in any restricted form once those imports are made explicit. Finally, and this is the part I most need, assess whether the routes people actually take to 'there is no fact of the matter' depend on the geometric analogy at all: Feferman's line that the notion of an arbitrary subset of the natural numbers is itself too indeterminate to fix a truth value, and the multiverse view that there are many equally legitimate set-theoretic universes rather than one. Do those positions need the fifth postulate, or do they stand or fall on arguments of a quite different kind? If you judge the analogy unusable, propose a replacement that conveys relative consistency without conveying indeterminacy of truth, and say what that replacement can and cannot be made to carry.

Please answer as a philosopher of mathematics who is entirely at home with the technical set theory: I do not need forcing explained to me and I do not want a history of non-Euclidean geometry, but I do want you to think the comparison through before writing, including the possibility that it fails for several independent reasons at once rather than one. Write the whole thing as continuous prose, the way you would write a considered letter to a colleague — no headings, no bullet points or numbered lists, no tables, no bold or italic emphasis. Do not send me to the literature or list references; I want your reasoning, not citations. If some part of my handout can be salvaged, say in exactly what wording.
