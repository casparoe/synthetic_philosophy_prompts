I never went to college — I've driven a delivery van for nineteen years — but for about six years I've run a Thursday night discussion group in the back room of a bowling alley. Ten to fourteen people show up, a few regulars: a guy who runs a fruit stall at the market and prices entirely by gut, a woman who works in insurance claims and doesn't trust any model of anything, and two who are convinced economics is just math rich people use on everyone else. Last winter I picked up a used copy of Milton Friedman's Essays in Positive Economics and got stuck on the first essay, the one where he says you shouldn't judge a theory by whether its assumptions are realistic — apparently that move has a name, the F-twist, though I can't remember who gave it one. I've read that essay twice and I honestly still can't tell whether he's saying something deep or getting away with something dodgy, and both readings feel right at the same time. So this month's session is that essay, ninety minutes, one whiteboard, no projector. I've told them I'm definately not going to teach them economics: I want them to walk out able to argue with a model, not to trust one.

What I need is the plan for the session, not the explanation itself. What has to come first and what depends on what — specifically, does the whole predict-and-see business have to be on the table before "assumptions don't have to be realistic" means anything at all, or does leading with the assumptions line backfire and make it sound like permission to assume whatever? Then the carrying example. I've already decided it can't be a firm trying to maximize profit, because half the room has run some kind of business and they'll spend forty minutes arguing about whether they personally maximize anything. Build it out of something they do every week instead: it needs somebody who is obviously not calculating anything, it needs a prediction that actually comes out right, and above all it can't license the conclusion that you can assume whatever you like as long as the numbers work. Tell me what that example has to have and what it has to leave out, and I vaguely remember Friedman had some other examples in there, something about a billiards player and something about leaves on a tree — say whether either one is worth stealing and exactly where in the sequence it would go, if at all.

Then the misconception map, which is the part I'm actually worried about. The one I'm braced for is the fruit-stall guy saying, within five minutes, that if your assumptions are wrong your answer is wrong, full stop — where does that get absorbed, and can it get absorbed without me just contradicting him and turning it into a shouting match? The opposite one scares me more: half the room concluding that economics can't ever be wrong because it's unfalsifiable, and smirking about it for the rest of the night. There's also the "it's just a tool, it isn't supposed to be true" version and the "so economists are allowed to lie to us" version. Give me a point in the sequence for each of those, and tell me whether to bring the critics in at all and where, without turning the evening into a philosophy of science lecture. Then one thing I can put on the whiteboard for the last ten minutes — a small case they have to sort out themselves — that would show me the difference between them actually getting it and them just feeling comfortable with economists. And be blunt about what I should cut and announce that I'm cutting it: I don't want the words ceteris paribus or underdetermination anywhere near that room, and I don't want to get into whether positive economics can really be value-free — but tell me if that last one is something I can honestly postpone or if the whole thing collapses without it.
