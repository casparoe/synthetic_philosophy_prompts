I am co-organizing a semester-long interdisciplinary reading group on mechanical rigidity and agency, drawing roughly equal numbers of philosophers, cognitive neuroscientists, and machine-learning researchers, and we have run into a predictable snag: when we ask participants where they stand on the free will debate, their self-ascribed labels turn out to have almost no predictive power over how they answer the cases we go on to discuss. Someone who calls herself a libertarian will happily grant that a sufficiently flexible artificial agent deserves credit for what it does, while a self-described compatibilist will insist that no quantity of behavioral variability adds up to agency. Our working hypothesis is that the disagreement everyone has been having about the digger wasp — the one Hofstadter used to coin the notion of sphexishness, which, when her paralyzed cricket is moved while she is down inspecting her burrow, drags it back to the entrance and then inspects again, apparently without limit — is not a single disagreement but several tangled together. So rather than another survey of positions, I would like a diagnostic instrument: a decision tree that sorts participants by what they are actually committed to when they reason about such cases, and that leads from those commitments to a named position at each leaf.

Here are the constraints that matter to us. First, no node may use the vocabulary of the debate it is diagnosing: no node may mention free will, determinism, compatibilism, responsibility, consciousness, or agency in the abstract, and no node may name a philosopher or an "-ism." Every node must be a concrete situation with two or three answers, phrased so that a participant can choose without knowing which leaf the choice leads to. Second, the tree should keep three axes visibly separate, and should be built so that at least one node forces a participant to trade one off against another: (i) whether rigidity is a property of the system itself or of the pairing of a system with a particular repertoire of perturbations — the wasp looks rigid only because someone happened to find the intervention that breaks her routine, there are reports that the routine does get modified after enough repetitions, and re-inspecting a disturbed nest may be perfectly adaptive; (ii) whether flexibility comes in degrees or whether there is a threshold that some systems cross and others do not; (iii) whether what matters for appraisal is how a system is put together now or how the states that move it got there. Third, please construct the leaves out of the logical space rather than out of a list of names: enumerate the answer-combinations the nodes permit first, locate the real positions within them, and where a combination is available but nobody seems to occupy it, say whether that is because the view is incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored.

Seed the nodes from the following cases, adding others if you need them and telling us where you have had to split a single question in two because it could not bear the weight: the prey displaced while the wasp is in the burrow; a variant in which the antennae she grips are removed and she simply abandons the provisioning trip; a language model that transfers to a task it has never been trained on but whose competence evaporates when the prompt is reworded trivially; a chess engine that produces a genuinely novel and sound sacrifice; an enormous lookup table encoding the correct move for every position in a finite but vast space; a person told that a long-held conviction was installed in him by suggestion, who revises it anyway; and an agent whose second-order desire to stop wanting something was itself installed by an outside intervention. I would like eight to ten leaves, set out as an indented outline that will fit on one printed page for our first session. Each leaf should carry a name — a real one where the literature supplies it, and, where it does not, a coinage explicitly flagged as yours — together with one sentence on the position's most awkward commitment and one prediction about a case the tree itself never uses, namely whether the position's holder would find a confabulating patient praiseworthy or blameworthy for an act whose reason he has just invented. Two leaves are mandatory: the deflationary view that the wasp is doing no argumentative work at all, and that the example's pull is an emotional reaction to the thought of being a mechanism rather than a claim about mechanisms; and the view that a real difference in flexibility can nonetheless leave untouched whatever the free will debate was actually about. Finally, flag honestly any pair of leaves your tree cannot separate — I expect at least one, involving the sophisticated-rigidity view and the view that manipulating symbols is never enough however flexible the manipulation — and say what question would separate them if the vocabulary ban were lifted; and close with a short paragraph on how the instrument should handle a participant whose answers turn out to be internally inconsistent, since in our pilot roughly a third of them were.
