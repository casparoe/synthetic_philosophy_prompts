Draft: "Made by relations, not conferred by them"

[1] Relational accounts of personhood in the ubuntu tradition are usually presented as being in conflict with individualist ones, and the internal debate is usually framed the same way: on one side, personhood as something achieved or earned through incorporation into communal life, coming in degrees, associated with Menkiti; on the other, Gyekye's moderate communitarian reply, on which the individual has worth that the community recognises rather than bestows. My claim is that this framing hides the fact that the two sides are answering different questions, and that once you separate the questions, most of the heat goes out of the debate but a harder problem shows up in its place.

[2] Three questions get run together under "personhood." (a) Developmental: what does it take for a being to acquire the capacities that persons exercise — language, a self-conception, a conscience, the ability to take responsibility? (b) Constitutive: is the self metaphysically made out of relations, such that there is no well-defined "me" prior to or apart from my standing to others? (c) Juridical: who counts morally, and does counting admit of degrees? These are separable. You can answer (a) relationally and (c) individualistically without any contradiction, and I think most participants in this debate actually do.

[3] On (a) there is no serious dispute. Nobody self-assembles. Both camps accept that persons are formed and sustained in relations, and that a human being raised without any would not develop the capacities in question. So (a) cannot be where the interesting disagreement lives. It is, however, where the rhetoric lives, because (a) is vivid and true and lends its plausibility to the other two.

[4] Call the move from (a) to (c) the conferral slide: from "relations make persons" to "relations (or the community that holds them) get to decide who is a person." The slide needs a hidden premise, roughly that whatever makes X is thereby in a position to unmake X. That premise is false in general. Soil makes the tree; the soil doesn't get a vote on whether the tree counts. Dependence for existence is not dependence for standing, and I think the achievement view is only attractive because it doesn't distinguish them.

[5] The move from (a) to (b) is more interesting and less obviously bad. Dependence isn't yet constitution — a plant depends on its soil without consisting in it. The strongest version of (b) I can construct is diachronic rather than synchronic: what makes me the particular person I am over time is partly fixed by how I am held in the memory, regard and practices of others, since that's where my own narrative continuity is stored when I can't hold it myself. On this reading (b) is a claim about identity conditions, not about ingredients.

[6] But now consider the erased witness. Someone's entire social existence is deliberately destroyed: everyone who knew them is dead or has been forced into silence, the records are burned, their name is not spoken, their descendants are taught that they never existed. Such people are real; regimes have made them. On the diachronic version of (b), this person is losing their self moment by moment, and it is hard to see how to stop the slide from there into (c) — losing standing too. Yet the case reads as the description of a crime, not of a demotion. Worse, the moral outrage wants the personhood-loss to sit with the erasers, and (b) can't put it there: on (b) the loss accrues to the victim. That looks to me like a reductio of the diachropic reading, or at least a reason to bound it.

[7] Second case. Two hermits meet once, and thereafter are each other's only human relation, sustained by memory and a single annual letter, for forty years. If one relation suffices for (b), then (b) has collapsed back into (a) with metaphysical garnish. If a network is required — and the language of community suggests it is — then we need a threshold, and thresholds are administrated.

[8] That is the point I most want to press, and it's political rather than conceptual. Scalar status requires scoring, and scoring requires a scorer, or at least a practice of scoring that some people control. Historically the authorities who were most enthusiastic about who did and did not count as a full person under colonial and apartheid rule were also the ones best placed to do the scoring. A view on which standing is conferred by the community is a view on which standing is revocable by whoever has captured the community's boundaries. So the achievement view doesn't merely risk excluding the marginal, the estranged, the socially dead, the disabled person who can't participate in the specified ways — it hands a technology of exclusion to the excluders and dignifies it as metaphysics.

[9] My positive proposal, therefore: keep relationality as an account of formation and sustenance, and refuse to let it double as an account of who counts. Add a floor that is non-scalar and not earned: everyone has standing in virtue of the capacity to be in relations — to respond to and be addressed by another — rather than in virtue of exercising it. This is in the neighbourhood of Metz's relational capacity account of dignity, though I don't need his version. Then hold two variables apart that the tradition runs together: standing (binary, universal, non-revocable) and the quality of one's relations and character (scalar, achievable, losable, and the proper home of everything the achievement view wanted to say).

[10] Objection to myself, and I don't have a good answer yet. First: this looks like Gyekye with ubuntu vocabulary, or worse, like a rights-bearing liberal individual wearing an African coat — I've reproduced the thing the tradition was meant to correct. Second, and sharper: if personhood in the normative sense cannot be failed, what is left of it as an achievement? A guaranteed floor seems to empty the notion of the very thing that made it worth defending, namely that being a person is a task.

[11] My sketch of a reply is that the floor secures standing and not worth, so the task survives intact in the second variable. I suspect this is too quick, because the second variable is exactly where the exclusionary practices in [8] will relocate — a community that can't demote you can always grade you badly, and grading badly has most of the same social effects. Hence the request.

So, specifically:

1. Do (a), (b) and (c) genuinely come apart, or is my tripartition just relabelling one dispute? In particular, tell me whether (b) survives as an independent claim once I've given the diachronic reading — my own guess is that [5]–[6] kill it and I should cut (b) down to a footnote about identity, but I'd like to be argued out of that.

2. Do the erased witness and the two hermits actually discriminate between the views, or can a defender of the achievement line just bite the bullet and redescribe — say, that the erased witness really has ceased to be a person and that this is precisely why erasure is the worst crime there is? If that reply is available, say whether it costs them anything they care about.

3. Flag every place I slide between senses of "person"/"personhood" myself, especially in [8] and [11]. I've tried to keep standing and worth apart from [9] onward but I don't trust myself in the earlier paragraphs.

4. Is [10]'s second objection fatal? I keep feeling like the whole paper is a device for reaching [9] and that [11] is where it dies. Tell me straight whether the two-variable move is a real solution or a way of conceding while looking like you haven't.

5. Whether I've strawmanned the achievement view. My worry is that nobody serious holds that a council literally votes people out of personhood, and that [8] attacks the vulgar version. If so, tell me what the sophisticated version is and whether [4] still blocks it.

Rank the three weakest joints, most to least weak, and for each say what I'd have to change — an argument, a case, or just the scope of the claim. Short numbered comments keyed to paragraphs is perfect; don't bother being encouraging.
