I write literary-ish historical fiction — this is the third book set on an invented Bronze Age-ish coastline, but the first two leaned on real archaeology and I got burned in both directions. Once a specialist reader tore into a scene because I'd built a whole organised priesthood out of a couple of figurines, and another time I got dinged for the opposite thing: objects that were pure set dressing, with no social weight behind them, in a book that's supposedly about a world where every object cost somebody a season of labour. So I've accepted that I need an actual procedure, not vibes.

Here's the situation. I keep a file of objects — some real finds I've read about popularly, some invented — and every time I want one to do work in a scene I need to know what it licenses. Write me that procedure: an ordered set of questions to run on an object, where each question either lets me go further or stops me cold. The main thing I need it to do is keep three kinds of claim apart, because I collapse them constantly:

- this thing exists → somebody could make it (skills, raw materials, tools, time, who's allowed to spend that time)
- this thing means something → belief, taboo, the sacred, the dead, cosmology
- this thing shows an institution → measures, records, storage, officials, tax, a guild, a priesthood, a state that can move grain

Bake in the stuff I always get wrong:

- how the object got into the ground — lost, thrown out, hoarded, deliberately broken, buried with somebody, dropped in a burn layer — and what each of those routes does and doesn't let me infer about the people.
- wear. Intended use versus what it actually got used for over forty years, and how much of either a character standing in the room could know.
- the fact that a lot of different pasts can leave the same pile of stuff. I want the procedure to force me to write at least two rival explanations for anything I build a scene on, and then to tell me which of them my characters could not possibly distinguish between from the inside.
- the "if you can't explain it, call it ritual" reflex. Give me a hard stop for that, and tell me what positive grounds would actually be enough for a character to treat something as sacred rather than just me wanting them to.
- silence. When nothing like the object has ever turned up anywhere, when is that a genuine constraint on what I can write and when is it just a hole in the sample? I want a test, not a shrug.
- standardization. When does a batch of similar objects justify me writing rules, officials or measures, and when is it only habit and copy-and-drift?

It has to be fast. I'm running this maybe twenty times a week mid-draft, no library, no time — ten minutes on a difficult object, and most objects should sort in two. Write it for someone who's read a lot of archaeology at the popular level but can't do stats and can't read a strat report. My editor doesn't touch this material and my one specialist reader only sees a second draft, so whatever comes out of the procedure has to be something I can check myself on the first pass without phoning anybody. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations — I'm not chasing sources. If there's a proper name for a concept you're using, drop the name once and move on.

Then run it on three cases so I can see it actually bite:

1. A small bronze figure, sex unclear, no attributes, no context, picked up loose in ploughsoil, nobody knows where it came from. I want a temple in this book and a hereditary priesthood. Tell me what this object will carry and what it flatly won't.
2. One room in a burned building with several hundred clay lumps that were pressed over jar mouths and tied with string, a lot of them bearing the same two or three marks. I want a redistribution economy and a man who is almost but not quite a scribe.
3. Two hundred-odd loom weights from an ordinary house, all within a couple of grams of each other. I want whatever it can bear, which I suspect is less than I want.

For each one show the questions being answered, not just the verdict, and be blunt where the procedure is going to disapoint me — better I hear it from you than from a reviewer. Mark clearly which steps aren't matters of fact at all but calls only I can make: what kind of book this is, how much I'm willing to let a reader be unable to check anything, how much invention I'll own in the author's note versus how much I'll let pass as scenery. And finish with what the whole procedure can't do — where it'll hand me a confident green light that's still wrong — plus whatever habit or double-check I should build in to catch those.
