I have just joined the selection committee for a private foundation's philosophy prize, endowed six years ago and never awarded. The deed of gift directs us to recognize the most significant advance in philosophical understanding in the preceding decade, and says nothing further that is operative. Our two subcommittees have deadlocked: one holds that advance can be assessed through proxies — influence, citation, uptake outside the discipline, the settling of long-open disputes — while the other holds that any proxy silently imports a contested account of what philosophy is for, and that we should decline to award and ask the trustees to amend the deed. This year's shortlist sharpens the problem. One nominee's monograph reshaped three subfields, but its central thesis is now widely thought false. Another's technical result is regarded by most of the specialists we polled as a genuine solution to a problem the field had treated as open for decades, and it has almost no readers. Draft the written opinion I will circulate before next month's meeting, recommending a concrete decision — award (to whom, on what stated ground), decline, or restructure the criteria going forward — with reasons both subcommittees could actually respond to and a serious treatment of the strongest consideration on the side you reject.
