Teaching the first session on the classical Indian theories of knowledge next week and I want to hand out something that sorts the room before we read a single page of it. Half the students are analytic epistemology types who have never met a Sanskrit text, the other half have read some Nyāya and want to feel superior. So: a 'which account of the sources of knowledge do you actually hold?' quiz.

Some things I care about:

1. Nine questions, each a small concrete scenario in plain modern English — a coworker, a stolen bike, a restaurant review, a smart speaker, a lost dog — with three options each. No question stem may use the technical labels (perception, inference, testimony, comparison, postulation, non-apprehension), and no question may be recognisable as the textbook example it's secretly based on. That last bit is the whole point: I know the classic ones — the fat man who must therefore eat at night, the wild ox you learn about by being told it's like a cow, the pot that isn't on the floor — and I want them recast so nobody can answer by going 'oh, that's the arthāpatti question.'

2. The questions should sit on the actual fault lines, not on trivia: can you take a stranger's word without independent evidence; is being told 'it's like such-and-such' and then later picking the thing out a source of its own or just inference wearing a costume; is finding out that something is *absent* a seperate act of knowing or just a failure to see; is there a real way of reasoning from a puzzle you can't otherwise dissolve ('how else could this be so?') that isn't inference with extra steps; does seeing ever deliver knowledge on its own or always need concepts supplied; and — sneakier — whether your answer changes when the speaker is reliable but you have no idea why.

3. Then the scoring key. I want the leaves defined by *how many irreducible sources* they admit and which ones — one, two, three, four, five, six — and only afterwards a name attached (the one-source materialist view, the Buddhist two-source view, the Sāṃkhya three, the Nyāya four, and the two rival five- and six-source options out of Mīmāṃsā and the Vedānta that inherits them). Where you're not sure which label goes with which count, just give the count and describe the view — don't guess attributions, I'd rather have a nameless leaf than a wrong one. Each leaf gets one sentence on its most awkward commitment, the thing its defender owes everybody else.

4. Build it from the logical space first, not from the list of schools: a short run of which answer-combinations are possible in principle, which ones nobody occupies, and why not — incoherent, unattractive, or just never explored. Specifically I want to know what someone who accepts a distinct absence-cognition but rejects testimony as a source would even look like, because two of my students will pick exactly that pattern and I want something interesting to say to them.

5. Flag the pairs of leaves your nine questions can't really separate rather than papering over it. I already suspect that the answer every analytic person in the room will give — 'testimony is just perception of noises plus induction over past reliability' — drops them onto the same leaf as the Buddhist two-source view, and I'd rather the key say that out loud than let them discover it in week nine.

6. Bonus appendix, only if you can do it cleanly: I have a hazy memory that some of the later Vedānta lists run to eight candidates rather than six, with two extras that get argued out of existence — something like 'inclusion' (knowing the parts from the whole, or the whole from the parts?) and plain rumour/hearsay as distinct from an authoritative speaker. If that rings true to you, add them as an extra leaf and say whether either could ever be irreducible or whether both collapse; if it doesn't, skip it and don't make something up.

Two pages max — questions, then key, then the space-of-possibilities bit. No bibliography, no citations, no 'for further reading', I'll handle that separately.
