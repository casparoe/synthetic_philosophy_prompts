Ok, context first: I'm a backend/infra engineer, zero philosophy background, but I've been stuck on the overnight on-call rotation for our platform team for about six weeks now and to stay awake I've been reading this book about nuclear strategy my brother-in-law lent me (he's a huge wargames nerd). The weird thing is that half of it reads exactly like an engineering postmortem written by people who have never once been paged at 3am. There's a chunk about a retaliatory system that's supposed to fire fully automatically, and the author's big worry isn't that it goes off by accident — it's that the other side might not *believe* it's really automatic. So the failure mode is a credibility bug, not a safety bug. Then there's the argument that you're actively better off if you physically can't turn the thing off, because an off switch is how you get called, and another bit about deliberately leaving some chance in the system so nobody can predict you. And there's an observation that once the big catastrophic system looks solid, all the small low-level conflicts get more frequent and everyone quietly accepts that as the price.

So here's what I want: a parody of an internal blameless postmortem doc, ~850-1000 words, markdown with the usual section headings — Summary, Impact, Detection, Timeline (with actual timestamps), Root Cause, Five Whys, Contributing Factors, What Went Well, Known Trade-offs, and an Action Items table with owner/priority/due date. The company is fictional and runs its deterrent as a platform product with a suitably bland internal name, and the "incident" is a near-miss where the thing almost went off and didn't. Crucially the doc should conclude that the system performed within SLO and that the *real* incident was a degraded notification pipeline: the adversary didn't recieve confirmation of our capability quickly enough, and an uncommunicated deterrent is filed as a P1 because the whole design depends on the other side having read the docs. I want a couple of specific beats in there. One: an internal dispute between the safety folks who want a human-in-the-loop abort interlock and the credibility folks who argue that any interlock is a single point of failure that invites the bluff to be tested — and the Action Items should quietly land on removing the interlock, justified in ordinary design-review language, nobody gloating about it. Two: an invented reliability metric for how much chance of accidental launch the system is allowed to keep in it, call it something like a volatility budget, and treat it as a real thing teams argue about spending down versus preserving. Three: the Five Whys chain should degenerate — each answer makes the design look *more* rational, and the last why either loops back to the first or bottoms out in something like the existence of a credible opponent.

Please keep it totally deadpan, no winking, no character breaks to say this is insane, and no moral at the end explaining the joke — the reader should feel the "wait, that's actually how the argument goes" thing on their own. The objection the piece should land without ever stating it is that the credibility requirements keep eating the safety requirements, and that a system whose only success metric is the absence of events has never actually been tested (someone can note in passing that the reliability number is doing a lot of unearned work). Two audiences at once: an engineer who's never read a word of strategy should find it funny as a corporate doc, and someone who knows the deterrence material should recognise that the concepts are being used correctly, not garbled — automatic retaliation, second-strike credibility, brinkmanship as shared risk, the stability-instability trade-off, the commander who most doesn't want to fire being the one you least believe. Hard constraints: no real countries, weapons systems, politicians or think-tank people, no references, no bibliography, no "here's what I was satirizing" coda. One or two typos in the doc are fine, it's an internal wiki page, but don't overdo it.
