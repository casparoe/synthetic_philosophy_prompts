In six weeks the scientific panel on which I serve as its ethics member will rule on a proposal that I do not believe our published precedents determine, and I should like help reconstructing what those precedents commit us to before I speak to them. I hold a chair in practical philosophy; the panel belongs to a mid-sized European certification scheme for farmed seafood, and it sets the scheme's welfare annex. There are eight of us: two veterinarians, two aquaculture scientists, a lead auditor, an industry representative, a retailer, a campaigner, and me. We decide taxon by taxon and practice by practice what licensees must do. Our rulings are published with reasons, and the reasoning is not decoration: the scheme's rules of procedure require like cases to be treated alike unless a material difference is recorded in the minutes. I also intend to use a redacted version of this exchange as a seminar exercise in precedent reasoning, so I would rather you were systematic than concise.

Our record to date, with reasons as minuted:

1. Atlantic salmon and rainbow trout — full coverage: mandatory pre-slaughter stunning, stocking-density ceilings, dissolved-oxygen and temperature thresholds, prohibition on live chilling at harvest.
2. Gilthead seabream and European sea bass — full coverage, with the stunning requirement phased in over five years for existing licensees, the panel recording that immediate compliance would drive licensees to a competing scheme.
3. Whiteleg shrimp — partial coverage: eyestalk ablation prohibited, ice-slurry chilling prohibited; no stunning requirement and no density ceilings adopted.
4. Freshwater prawn (Macrobrachium) — declined, on a single recorded ground: no realistic prospect of on-farm verification in the producing regions.
5. Common octopus — declined for scope altogether; the panel recommended that the scheme certify no octopus culture, recording that it could not be satisfied that any captive system could meet the species' needs.
6. Blue mussel and Pacific oyster — declined, on the recorded ground that there is no credible evidence of a capacity for welfare-relevant experience.
7. Lumpfish and ballan wrasse deployed as cleaner fish in salmon cages — held to be within scope as vertebrates, implementation indefinitely deferred: they are logged as production inputs, no mortality or husbandry data exist, and licensees cannot be required to report what they do not measure.
8. Decapod crustaceans generally — a prohibition on live transport and live holding on ice, applied across the whole certified chain, including crab and crayfish species the panel has never otherwise assessed.

One further datum. A member seated from the retailer bloc has begun invoking the fact that at least one legislature now defines its animal-welfare perimeter to include cephalopods and decapod crustaceans alongside all vertebrates, and argues that a private certifier has no business being narrower than a statute. Another member replies that we are a voluntary standard rather than a public authority, and that whatever credibility we have rests on what we can actually verify on a farm. Neither has articulated a rule; both have voted consistently with something.

The new case. A licensee association proposes a reduction-fishery dependency criterion for the welfare annex: every certified operation farming a carnivorous species would document the quantity of wild small pelagic fish — anchovy, sardine, sprat, sandeel and the like — reduced to meal and oil per unit of farmed fish produced, and would reduce that quantity by a fixed percentage each certification cycle. The stated ground is that these animals are teleosts, that on our own reasoning about salmonids they are presumptively within the class of beings whose suffering counts, and that they outnumber the farmed fish they feed by a large multiple. The secretariat has counter-proposed that the metric belongs in the scheme's environmental standard, where a comparable ratio is already tracked as a sustainability indicator carrying no welfare content whatever. A third member proposes a compromise: allow a licensee to carry a higher ratio in exchange for demonstrably better conditions for its own stock — no stunning exemption, lower densities, enriched pens — and treat the resulting package as welfare-improving on net.

What I require from you:

(a) At least four candidate decision rules, each consistent with all eight rulings or reconcilable with them by a modest amendment. State each so that it could be applied by someone who had never read our minutes; identify which ruling strains it most; say what amendment rescues it. I expect the set to include something keyed to moral status, something keyed to the strength of the evidence, something keyed to verifiability, and something keyed to the visibility of the practice at issue. Tell me if I have overlooked a live possibility, and tell me equally if one of these is not a genuine candidate because some ruling already refutes it.

(b) For each surviving rule, a prediction on the new case, disaggregated: whether the reduction fishery falls within the welfare annex's remit; if it does, whether it is covered now or deferred; and whether the third member's trade-off is admissible. I am particularly interested in any rule that yields the same visible outcome — no requirement applied — for materially different reasons, since that is the situation in which a panel adopts a principle without noticing that it has.

(c) Your best single prediction of what this panel will in fact decide, with reasons drawn from the record rather than from general plausibility, and an explicit statement of your confidence in it.

(d) Which precedent is the closest analog to the new case, where the analogy holds, and where it breaks. I have in mind that the reduction fishery concerns animals our licensees never hold, harm caused through purchase rather than through husbandry, and a perimeter that might be drawn by custody, by causal contribution, or by contractual reach — three lines our rulings have not distinguished.

(e) A recommendation, as distinct from a prediction: what I should argue for if I want a decision the panel can live with as a matter of principle, together with a candid statement of what it would be committing itself to, for better or worse, under each of the three options on the table.

(f) One further case, not currently on our docket, that would discriminate between the two rules you judge most likely, with each rule's prediction recorded in advance.
