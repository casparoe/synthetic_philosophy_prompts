I would like a probability estimate, with its derivation shown, on an interpretive question in Hegel scholarship that I have been unable to settle by argument.

Consider the following claim, which I will call the Load-Bearing Thesis: the remarks on war in the closing part of the *Philosophy of Right* — above all the suggestion that war has a further ethical significance, preserving the ethical health of peoples against the stagnation that a condition of lasting peace or settled tranquillity would produce, an effect Hegel likens to that of wind in keeping a body of water from going foul — are not detachable concessions to his political circumstances. On this reading they follow from commitments he needs independently elsewhere, so that an interpreter who excised them would be left with an account of civil society and of the state that is incomplete or internally unstable. I am asking what Hegel's own commitments require, not whether the doctrine is true, prudent, or morally defensible.

Since no panel of experts will ever vote on the matter, treat the question as resolved by the considered judgment of a scholar who has worked through the paragraphs Hegel himself published together with the Additions compiled after his death from his manuscript materials and students' transcripts, the lectures on the philosophy of history, and the Berlin lecture notes on the philosophy of right, and who is aiming at what the texts commit him to rather than at making him palatable.

Give me a single number for the Load-Bearing Thesis, then decompose it into whatever terms you think actually carry the weight, with a probability and one or two lines of justification for each, and show explicitly how they combine. Candidate terms you might consider, though you should feel free to replace or add to them: the probability that Hegel's diagnosis of civil society — its tendency to generate both surplus wealth and an immiserated rabble — commits him to holding that a political order cannot stabilize itself from within; the probability that this requirement, if there is one, is met specifically by war rather than by alternatives available to him in the same discussion, such as emigration and colonization; the probability that the claim rests on something like the state's status as an individual, whose individuality implies negation and therefore another individual over against it, rather than on any sociological thesis about peace and corruption; the probability that the Restoration context — the censorship regime, Hegel's Berlin chair, his audience — explains the passages without residue; and the probability that the most-cited remarks express his considered view rather than material of weaker evidential standing from the posthumously compiled Additions.

Then tell me three further things. First, which term the final number is most sensitive to, and roughly how much it would move if that term shifted by twenty points. Second, which term you are least confident in, and what kind of textual or biographical evidence would move it. Third, where a disagreement lives: my own credence in the thesis is about 0.65, while a colleague who works on post-Kantian idealism puts it near 0.25 and maintains that the war remarks are essentially an accommodation to Hegel's circumstances; identify which of your terms accounts for most of that gap, and say whether the gap is substantive or instead a disagreement about what the thesis asserts.

Throughout, please separate what is settled among people who work on this material from what is genuinely contested, and do not present a contested interpretive position as though it were the consensus of the secondary literature; where you are relying on a disputed reading, mark it as such. Likewise, flag the places where your estimate is being driven by general philosophical priors about what systematic philosophers do rather than by your knowledge of these texts. No reading list or citations, please — I want the reasoning, not the references.
