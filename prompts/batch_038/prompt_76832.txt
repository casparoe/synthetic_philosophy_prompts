I'm a staff engineer at a consumer software company. I own user-facing copy and the flows around account deletion, notifications, and data export, and I've been asked to draft our internal standard for what our screens are allowed to make people believe. I have no philosophy training; I've read some blog posts about the difference between lying and misleading and argued with a colleague who did a philosophy degree, and that's the extent of it. So please treat what follows as raw material rather than as my summary of a literature, and please don't send me citations or a reading list — I couldn't evaluate them, and I'd rather have the reasoning in plain terms.

What I currently hold, in roughly the form I'd put it in the document:

1. What makes a piece of copy wrong is the intention behind it. If we intend the user to end up with a false belief, it's wrong. The falsity of the sentence by itself isn't what does the work.
2. Therefore lying and misleading are the same wrong. A literally true sentence used to produce a false belief is just as bad as a false one; there's no discount for staying inside the truth.
3. We have no general obligation to volunteer information the user hasn't asked for. If the true state of affairs is discoverable — in the help center, in settings, via support — we've done our part. Silence isn't deception.
4. Any screen whose sentences are all literally true is permissible. I've been calling this the screenshot test: if a user screenshots the screen and every sentence on it survives a fact-check, the screen is clean.
5. Users are responsible for their own inferences. A conclusion we never stated is theirs, not ours.
6. Euphemism is acceptable when it's doing tonal work: "we're unable to process that payment" rather than "your card was declined for insufficient funds"; "your export is being prepared" rather than "you're number 41,206 in the queue."
7. Pre-selected consent toggles are acceptable. The toggle displays the true default, and turning it off is one tap.
8. Passing our dark-pattern checklist is a sufficient safeguard: no guilt-tripping language on the cancellation path, no navigation you can get into but not out of, no ads styled as buttons, no cost revealed only at the final step, and all copy literally true.

Case verdicts I'd also defend: our account-deletion screen is fine saying "Your account will be deleted" with the 90-day backup retention mentioned only in the privacy policy; our notifications screen is fine with a single Continue button and the opt-out three levels down under Settings > Privacy > Advanced; our support bot is fine saying "I can't help with that" when the accurate sentence is "I'm not permitted to help with that."

To be clear about scope: I'm not asking whether dark patterns are bad in general — I think they are. I'm asking whether this particular list is internally coherent. My colleagues attack it at different points, some of them at item 3, some at item 7, and I can't tell which objection goes deep and which is a nitpick, so I don't want to prejudge that in the question.

Please give me an audit rather than a lecture, in these sections, in this order, at roughly these lengths:

**Bottom line** (about 80 words). Is the package jointly tenable? If not, how many items have to be revised or dropped, and which ones?

**Tension map** (about 400 words, ranked). The genuine tensions, most severe first. For each: the two items involved, why their motivations are incompatible rather than merely uncomfortable sitting side by side, which one has to give, and what would have to replace it. A table is acceptable as a summary, but the ranking itself has to be justified in prose — I don't want every pair flagged as equally bad.

**False alarms** (about 150 words). Pairs that look inconsistent and aren't, with the reason they dissolve. If you think some item on my list survives the audit intact, say so and defend it; I'd like at least one candidate, and I'd like to know why it's sturdier than it looks from outside.

**Case verdicts** (about 200 words). Run my three cases through my own principles and identify every case where the principles as stated cannot actually deliver the verdict I claim to defend. Where they can't, name the premise doing the damage.

**Cheapest repair** (about 150 words). The single smallest edit to the list that removes the most tension, and what it costs us concretely — what our copy and flows would have to start doing on Monday morning.

One last thing. I have a private term I've been using with my team: I call the gap between what a screen literally says and what a user will actually take away from it "inference debt," and I've been assuming we don't accrue any so long as nothing we state is false. Tell me whether that term marks a real distinction or is just a slogan I invented to feel better about item 4.
