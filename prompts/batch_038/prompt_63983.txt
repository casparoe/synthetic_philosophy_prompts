Hey — for my grad seminar on minds and machines I want to hand out something that's funny but is also secretly an argument, and I think you can build it.

Write a satirical internal compliance document: a "Counterfeit Fidelity Handbook" (that title or one like it) issued by a company that builds person-shaped AI companions and has read Dennett's 2023 Atlantic piece on counterfeit people not as a warning but as a spec sheet. Roughly 750 words, numbered rules with the occasional sub-bullet, deadpan corporate-legal register — the voice of a document that has been reviewed by counsel twice and survived.

The joke I want is that the company agrees with essentially everything Dennett says and just cashes it out in the wrong currency. Competence without comprehension isn't a puzzle for them, it's a liability shield: a thing that merely seems to believe can't be owed anything, and a user who can't tell the difference is a user who has consented. So the handbook should contain rules about how good a target of the intentional stance the product is permitted to be before it starts generating obligations, why you want customers parked in the intentional stance (cheap, and non-binding) rather than the design stance, and what a QA engineer is supposed to do upon noticing that the thing has, as it were, a point of view. Fold in a section repurposing heterophenomenology as a liability protocol: collect the transcripts, remain rigorously agnostic about whether there's anything it's like, ship the transcript as the entire product. And do something with Dennett's watermark proposal — our company implements it cheerfully, loudly, and inverted.

Two invented bits I want you to run with, because theyre mine and not in anybody's paper. First, the compliance metric is called stance drift: users sliding from treating the product as-if toward treating it as a genuine believer, logged per session, held inside a tolerance band, with escalation thresholds and a note about which department owns the number. Second, the final rule should be the one the whole document has been circling — the compliance office admitting that it has no procedure, even in principle, for distinguishing a product that is faking belief from one that isn't, and being completely relaxed about that.

Keep it fair. Every rule should follow from something Dennett actually argues or from a standard reading of the intentional stance, exaggerated rather than invented, so a specialist laughs and nods instead of wincing. No winking at the reader, no moralizing coda tacked on the end, no real living people and no real companies. And please dont give me citations, a bibliography, or a reading list — I don't want the seminar spending twenty minutes arguing about references.
