Can you write me a short story for me — call it 1500 to 2500 words — out of a setup I've been turning over for a while? It's my own conceit, nothing you'll have seen before, and I want it played completely straight, no winking at the reader.

The setup. There's an office, let's call it the Chancery of Verifiable Things, and its entire reason to exist is one principle that everybody in this society accepts without argument: no one can be bound by a claim they couldnt have checked. Concretely, every claim has a *check-cost* — how much time and apparatus a single ordinary person, working alone with the standard instruments of the day, would need to confirm the claim is true. If a claim's check-cost runs past a normal human lifetime, the Chancery issues a certificate declaring it *inert*. Inert claims might be true. Everyone agrees they might be true. It makes no difference: they carry no obligations, no taxes, no standing to sue, no sin attached to denying them. Whole districts live freely under blankets of inert claims — old debts, old treaties, old accusations of old crimes — because nobody alive could ever have adjudicated them, so nobody can be held to them.

The interesting asymmetry, and the thing I want the story to actually live inside rather than explain: finding something and checking something are not the same labor. A claim can be hopeless to discover and trivial to verify, if only someone hands you the key. So the Chancery's auditors spend their lives arguing about what counts as a check and who counts as the ordinary person, and there's an unresolved question at the bottom of the whole jurisprudence — does a claim stay inert if the only reason its expensive is that no one has yet said the one sentence that would make it cheap?

The plot I want: an auditor whose job has been certifying a district's founding claim as inert, generation after generation, more or less a clerical formality, is brought a *witness*. Not a document — a person, or something that functions as one, who can recite the thing that collapses the check-cost of that district's founding claim from centuries to a single afternoon. And then the real knife: if the claim stops being inert, does it bind people retroactively? Do the last three generations of that district become, all at once, people who knowingly violated an obligation they could have checked? The Chancery has never had to answer it and there's no precedent, because nobody ever thought a witness would show up.

Some craft constraints, please honor these:

- No character explains the theory to another character. Nobody says "you see, the difference between search and verification is..." The rules should come through as procedure — forms, stamps, arguments about clause wording, the tedium of the office.
- Include one verbatim piece of Chancery paperwork somewhere in the story, set apart on the page: the certificate, or the witness's intake form, or a marginal note. Make it sound like a document written by people who have stopped thinking about what the words mean.
- Ground it in one person and one specific day. I want to know what the auditor eats, what the corridor smells like, what they're avoiding thinking about.
- End on a decision or on the moment just before one. Don't resolve the retroactivity question and dont have anyone deliver a verdict about what knowledge or obligation really is.
- Use my invented terms consistently but sparingly — check-cost, inert, witness — and let the reader assemble the system from what the officials do rather than what they say.

Tone-wise I'm imagining something spare and procedural with real interiority underneath, like a court record that keeps slipping into a person's head. If it turns out slightly bleak that's fine, that's probably correct.
