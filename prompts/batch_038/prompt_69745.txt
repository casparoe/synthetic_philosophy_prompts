A friend and I argued for most of last night about heritability, and I finished the evening unable to say whether I had been persuaded or merely out-argued. He has read considerably more of the technical literature than I have, and near the end he set his position out for me in four claims. I have reconstructed them as faithfully as I can, and I would like an honest audit rather than a defense of either of us.

1. A heritability estimate is the proportion of a trait attributable to genetic variation, so a heritability of 0.8 for height means that roughly eighty percent of any given person's height is genetic and twenty percent environmental, and the two shares must sum to the whole.

2. It follows that no environmental improvement — nutrition, schooling, anything else — can move a population's mean by more than the environmental share, which is why the heritability of cognitive test scores bears directly on how much headroom an intervention program actually has.

3. Twin studies produce these numbers by comparing identical with fraternal twins, and the method depends on the assumption that the two kinds of pair are treated equally alike. Identical twins are demonstrably treated more alike — dressed alike, sharing friends, spending more time together — so the assumption fails and the standard estimates are inflated by an unknown amount. The inflation is not merely possible but expected, given the evidence that parents' genes shape the home in ways that predict children's outcomes even when those particular genes are not transmitted to the child.

4. Heritability says nothing about differences between groups: two populations can differ wholly for environmental reasons while each shows a heritability near one within itself. Since the figure quoted in public debate is nearly always deployed to suggest otherwise, heritability is better understood as a rhetorical device than as a scientific quantity.

Where I ended up: I think he is right in the first half of claim 4, and right that the equal-treatment assumption in claim 3 is at least strained, but claims 1 and 2 made me uneasy in a way I could not articulate, and I suspect claim 3's conclusion outruns its premises.

Could you take the four claims in order and say, for each, whether a working behavioral geneticist would accept it as stated, correct it by changing a word or two, or reject it outright — and, where correction is possible, give me the corrected version? Then four specific questions. Does any reformulation of claim 1 survive, or is the individual-level reading always a mistake; population statistics are used to talk about individual patients' risk all the time, so why does that practice not carry over here? Does claim 2 follow from anything true, and what is the cleanest counterexample to it? In claim 3, what exactly would have to be shown for the objection to bite — is it enough that the assumption is false, or must it be false in some particular way, and do other study designs stand or fall with it? And if claim 4's first half is right, does his conclusion that the concept is rhetorical rather than scientific follow, or is that a separate move he never argued for? He also insisted that when a trait's heritability is 1.0, equalizing environments cannot reduce inequality in that trait at all; I want to know whether even that much is correct.
