I am a third-year philosophy undergraduate, and this term I am leading one of the weekly discussion sections attached to our philosophy of mind course. In week nine I will have fourteen students for seventy-five minutes, in a seminar room with a whiteboard, index cards, pencils and two dice, and no computers or printing beyond the single sheet I prepare in advance. The lectures have covered Parfit's reductionist treatment of personal identity; nobody in my section has yet met Dennett's suggestion that a self is a center of narrative gravity, and I want his name and that phrase to stay out of the room until the closing minutes of the debrief.

What I want them to leave seeing has two halves, and the second is the one that usually gets dropped. First: a self is not a further ingredient you find by inspecting a person more closely, but an abstraction an interpreter posits in order to make that person's doings hang together as a story. Second, and just as important: positing it is not thereby idle or fraudulent, because in the right conditions the abstraction earns its keep by predicting behavior better than anything else available. Sections that only deliver the first half leave convinced the view is a cheap debunking, and the students who push back are usually the ones who have half-noticed that a debunking owes an account of why character attribution works as well as it does.

I have been circling a design I cannot get to work on paper, and I would like you to build it out properly — or tell me it is broken and rebuild it. Seven pairs of students each act as biographers to an unseen subject. They are shown a sequence of twelve everyday choice situations together with what the subject did in each, and asked to write down a compact character sketch: a small number of dispositions that explain the record. Then they predict the subject's behavior in six further situations, which are scored. Unbeknown to them, half the pairs are tracking a subject whose choices were generated from a fixed handful of hidden dispositions plus noise, and half are tracking a subject whose choices came from a die, with no regularity at all. The reveal should let them compare their felt confidence and the richness of their sketches against their actual hit rates.

Please supply: a generation procedure for the regular subject that I can hand to a classmate to execute with a printed table and a die, including how much noise to add and why; the eighteen situations themselves, mundane and morally mixed rather than science-fictional; what each pair writes at each stage and how accuracy is scored so that getting it right matters to them without prizes; a minute-by-minute timeline; the sequence of debrief questions that maps what just happened onto the two halves above; what predictably goes wrong on a first run, including the pairs that suspect randomness early and spoil the reveal, and the student who insists this is a psychology demo about pattern-seeking rather than anything to do with personal identity; a frank statement of the feature of the view the exercise structurally cannot reproduce, given that my students are positing a self in someone else from the outside, and what I would have to supply verbally in its place; and a short account of how the point differs from the reductionism they read in Parfit, since I expect half the section to collapse the two.
