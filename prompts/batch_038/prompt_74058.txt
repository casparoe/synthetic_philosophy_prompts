Three months ago I accepted a transfer from our Boston office to Berlin following an acquisition, and in the reshuffle I was named our local data-protection coordinator — a role I am quite unqualified for, since I am an infrastructure engineer and not a lawyer. Last week the works council blocked a vendor we had already signed: a tool that would have retained and indexed internal chat logs so that managers could search them after the fact. Nothing would have been published, nothing leaked, and the retention was internal. I argued, badly, that this was nevertheless "the right to be let alone," and that the German objection was sentimental. The council's legal adviser replied that I had the concept exactly backwards, and that the American formulation I was invoking could not even see the problem we had in front of us. That has stuck with me, and I have gone back to the founding American text to find out whether she is right.

For context, so that you need not supply the comparative material yourself: the German position she was speaking from is the right to informational self-determination as the Federal Constitutional Court articulated it in its 1983 census decision, deriving it from human dignity together with the general right of personality. As I understand it, that right protects the individual against the unlimited collection, storage, use, and disclosure of their personal data by public authorities; it is statutory and institutional rather than judicial, it operates in advance rather than after the fact, and its central justification is that a person who cannot be certain whether particular conduct is being recorded will refrain from exercising their freedoms altogether. If I have garbled any of this, please say so plainly.

Here is the passage I want to work through, from Warren and Brandeis's article in the *Harvard Law Review* of December 1890 (footnote references omitted):

> This development of the law was inevitable. The intense intellectual and emotional life, and the heightening of sensations which came with the advance of civilization, made it clear to men that only a part of the pain, pleasure, and profit of life lay in physical things. Thoughts, emotions, and sensations demanded legal recognition, and the beautiful capacity for growth which characterizes the common law enabled the judges to afford the requisite protection, without the interposition of the legislature.
>
> Recent inventions and business methods call attention to the next step which must be taken for the protection of the person, and for securing to the individual what Judge Cooley calls the right "to be let alone." Instantaneous photographs and newspaper enterprise have invaded the sacred precincts of private and domestic life; and numerous mechanical devices threaten to make good the prediction that "what is whispered in the closet shall be proclaimed from the house-tops." For years there has been a feeling that the law must afford some remedy for the unauthorized circulation of portraits of private persons; and the evil of the invasion of privacy by the newspapers, long keenly felt, has been but recently discussed by an able writer. The alleged facts of a somewhat notorious case brought before an inferior tribunal in New York a few months ago, directly involved the consideration of the right of circulating portraits; and the question whether our law will recognize and protect the right to privacy in this and in other respects must soon come before our courts for consideration.
>
> Of the desirability—indeed of the necessity—of some such protection, there can, it is believed, be no doubt. … The intensity and complexity of life, attendant upon advancing civilization, have rendered necessary some retreat from the world, and man, under the refining influence of culture, has become more sensitive to publicity, so that solitude and privacy have become more essential to the individual; but modern enterprise and invention have, through invasions upon his privacy, subjected him to mental pain and distress, far greater than could be inflicted by mere bodily injury. Nor is the harm wrought by such invasions confined to the suffering of those who may be made the subjects of journalistic or other enterprise. In this, as in other branches of commerce, the supply creates the demand.

Immediately before this passage the authors trace a genealogy in which legal protection moves from battery to assault, from assault to nuisance, from nuisance to slander and libel, and from there to the family relations and finally to intangible property. Please attend to that genealogy as well, since I think it is doing most of the work, but do not quote further from the article beyond what I have given you — I want your reasoning about this text, not a reconstruction of the parts of it I have not supplied.

What I would like you to settle, as far as it can be settled:

1. Every step in the genealogy is a wrong committed by one private person against another, redressed after the fact in damages. Does that derivation constrain what the resulting "right to be let alone" can be? Specifically, is it a power to determine in advance whether and how information about oneself is processed, or is it only an immunity from certain completed invasions, cashable in compensation once the invasion has occurred? The distinction matters to me because our vendor was an ex ante control question, not a damages question.

2. The authors say the judges can afford the requisite protection "without the interposition of the legislature." Is that a throwaway remark about method, or does it commit them to a conception of the right that cannot accommodate a regime requiring legislation, an enforcement authority, and prior constraints on processing? Could a right of this shape ever have generated something like the German statute, or does it structurally point elsewhere?

3. Their account of the harm is publicity: what is whispered in the closet proclaimed from the house-tops, portraits circulated without authorization, mental pain and distress arising from exposure. Under that model, is there any injury at all when information is collected, indexed, and retained but never published? Our chat logs would never have left the building. I want to know whether the passage can register that case, whether it can register it only by stretching the notion of mental distress, or whether the model is simply silent on it — and, if it is silent, whether that silence is a contingent artefact of 1890 technology or a consequence of the tort-shaped derivation.

4. Our parent company also operates in China, where the compliance posture is close to the inverse of Berlin's, and where the operative mechanism is not publication but classification and consequence attached to a score. Does the same harm model fail there in the same way it fails in the retention case, or is that a different failure?

5. Finally, I would like your candid assessment of whether my works-council counterpart was right. If she was, I would rather know precisely why, because I have to make this argument again in the autumn and I do not want to make it a third time.

Please treat this as close reading rather than intellectual history, and be explicit wherever the quoted text underdetermines the question rather than papering over the gap. I do not want a bibliography, a reading list, or citations to secondary literature; I want your analysis of the passage and of the conceptual contrast I have described.

End your reply with a single line in exactly this form:

Verdict: IMMUNITY | CONTROL | NEITHER

choosing IMMUNITY if the passage yields only an ex post protection against private invasions, CONTROL if it already contains an incipient right to determine in advance how information about oneself is processed, and NEITHER if you think the distinction I am drawing does not map onto this text at all.
