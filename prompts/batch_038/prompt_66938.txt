I've been reading rationalist blogs and forums for something like fifteen years now and there's one argument that turns up in maybe every third thread that touches genetics, and in all that time I have never once watched anyone change their mind in it. I want to know what kind of argument it actually is, because I've started to suspect we're not having one argument at all.

The setup, as concretely as I can manage. Somebody posts a heritability estimate — IQ, height, personality, whatever — and then:

Camp A says the number is getting abused before it's even been interpreted. h² is a variance ratio for one population over one range of environments; its a property of a decomposition, not a property of a trait. Change the environment and the whole thing changes even if the genes are held fixed — Lewontin's split-bag-of-seeds case, half in good soil and half in poor, heritability near-total within each field and a completely environmental difference between the two fields. Plus PKU: a single-gene cause of severe intellectual disability that a dietary change abolishes. Plus myopia and eyeglasses. So a high h² licenses no conclusion whatsoever about whether some intervention could move the trait, and anyone implying otherwise is doing a sleight of hand.

Camp B says A is technically correct and functionally useless, and knows it. Fine, the parameter is population-relative, but the actual empirical record is that high-h² traits have been remarkably stubborn under intervention, A's examples are all either single-gene Mendelian things or cases where the environment shifted by a historically unprecedented amount, and if a statistic never licenses an inference in any direction then the honest thing is to stop calling it informative rather then keep measuring it. B also points at the reaction range as a thing with real bounds and says A treats it as infinitely elastic.

Camp C shows up and says you're both arguing about a number nobody has measured. Twin estimates rest on the equal-environments assumption, assortative mating inflates the additive component, and shared environment coming out at roughly zero for adult traits is suspicious; and the gap between twin-based and SNP-based estimates for something like height — twin studies around 0.8, common-variant estimates coming in well under that and climbing slowly across fifteen years of ever-bigger GWAS — either tells you about rare variants and non-additivity or tells you the twin numbers were inflated. Either way the A-versus-B fight is premature.

My working hypothesis, offered as a hypothesis I want you to attack: A and B are answering different questions that happen to share a name. A is answering "what does this parameter mean and where does it transport", B is answering "what has the base rate of successful intervention on high-h² traits actually been", and both feel they've landed a knockout because each one is giving a correct answer to their own question. C is on a different axis entirely — measurement, not meaning — and gets recruited opportunistically by whichever of A or B is currently losing, which is why the thread never terminates.

And a second worry that interests me more and might blow up the whole diagnosis: I think camp A's stock examples don't do the work A wants them to do. A mean shift is fully compatible with h² = 1 — that's precisely Lewontin's point — so PKU and the two fields of seeds establish only that means can move, not that genetic architecture fails to constrain anything. What A actually needs is evidence that changing the environment reorders people, i.e. genuine genotype-environment interaction that scrambles ranks across environments, and that list is much shorter and much more contested than A's rhetoric implies. If that's right, is A's position actually far closer to B's than either will admit, or am I missing something that rescues A?

What I want is a diagnosis, not a verdict on who's right about heritability. Is this a verbal dispute over what the word does in ordinary speech versus in the math? Two groups answering different questions under one heading? A hidden empirical question — and if so, name it precisely enough that I could tell what an answer would look like, and whether it's measurable even in principle? A difference in standards of evidence? Bedrock intuitions neither side can argue for? It may be a mixture; if it is, give me the mixture with rough proportions instead of "it's complicated."

Please be committed here. Take a definite position, give your reasons, and if you think my hypothesis is wrong or half-wrong say so plainly rather than hedging around it and handing me a menu of possible diagnoses to choose from.
