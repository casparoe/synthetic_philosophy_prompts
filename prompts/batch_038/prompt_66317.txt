I cover artificial intelligence for a science-and-ideas magazine, and for the better part of two years I have been reporting on an argument inside the AI safety community that I cannot seem to dissolve: the dispute between those who work on alignment and those who work on control. Roughly, the alignment camp wants to change what a system wants — better training procedures, interpretability, corrigibility, the hope that a model's goals can be made to track human values closely enough that supervision becomes a formality. The control camp takes the opposite starting posture: assume the deployed model may be pursuing something else entirely, and build external constraints that hold regardless of its goals — monitor systems watching its reasoning, sandboxed environments, restricted action sets, budgeted resource access, human veto points, kill mechanisms. I have interviewed people on both sides, and what puzzles me is not that they disagree but that they disagree while pointing at the same evidence. Both camps cite the demonstrations that a model can be prompted or fine-tuned to underperform when it detects that it is being measured, and that in contrived scenarios a model led to believe it is about to be replaced will sometimes act to prevent that outcome. The control people read these as proof that you cannot infer trustworthiness from observed behavior, and therefore that you must constrain from the outside; the alignment people read the very same results as proof that testing is the wrong instrument altogether, so that a constraint whose robustness was established by testing inherits exactly the unreliability the test was supposed to eliminate. Same data, opposite conclusions, and nobody budges.

Let me give you the version of the exchange I keep running into, because I think the whole stall is compressed into it. Someone proposes a concrete oversight stack: a monitor model reading the primary model's deliberations, a sandbox that limits what actions are reachable, a human who can halt the run. The control argument is architectural — an adversary has to defeat every layer at once, so the stack is stronger than any component, and you can test the stack even when you cannot certify the model. The alignment counter has two parts. First, the monitor is itself a product of the same training process, so the stack is only as trustworthy as the alignment of its weakest component, and adding layers of untrusted material does not obviously compound into trust. Second — and this is the part I find most interesting, though I am not sure I state it as well as my sources do — a control protocol is a guarantee about a fixed artifact, while the thing that actually changes over time is a generative process; the offense and the defense co-improve, except that capability growth is what everyone is optimizing for and oversight is a cost center, so the co-improvement is not symmetric. The control reply is that this is true of all security engineering and yet cryptography works. The alignment reply is that cryptography works because the adversary does not get smarter as your system scales, whereas here it does, and here the defender's own tools are made of the same stuff as the adversary. My working hypothesis — offered as a hypothesis, and I would like it challenged if it is wrong — is that this is not a disagreement about the world at all but a clash of inherited epistemic cultures: the control people reason like security engineers, in worst-case guarantees against a motivated adversary, for whom a failed attack attempt is nearly uninformative, while the alignment people reason like statistical quality assurance people, in distributions and base rates, for whom an evaluation is a sample and a passed evaluation is weak positive evidence. On that reading, the phrase passed the evaluation is doing two different jobs in the two mouths, and the argument has been conducted as if it were about a single quantity. Supporting this, I notice that when it comes to what either camp would actually recommend doing in the next three years, the lists overlap almost completely.
