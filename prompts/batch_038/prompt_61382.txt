Our banding is: 21–25 excellent, 17–20 very good, 14–16 solid, 11–13 satisfactory, 8–10 marginal pass, under 8 fail. Here's the script, more or less as submitted:

"(a) A supererogatory act is one that is morally good but not required — it goes beyond the call of duty. Three conditions are usually given. First, the act must be morally good, and better than at least some of the things the agent could permissibly do instead. Second, omitting it must not be wrong, and the agent must not be blameworthy for omitting it. Third, it typically involves substantial cost or risk to the agent. Urmson's saints, heroes and benefactors are the stock examples: the soldier who throws himself on the grenade, the doctor who stays in the plague city, the person who gives away half her income. The difference between supererogation and mere praiseworthiness is that praiseworthiness tracks the quality of the act or of the motive behind it, whereas supererogation is a deontic category: it says something about what is permitted. An act can be praiseworthy without being supererogatory — keeping a difficult promise under pressure, say — and on some views an act could be supererogatory even where nobody thinks to praise it.

(b) The paradox is that the concept seems to undermine itself:
1. If an act is supererogatory, it is morally good but not obligatory, and its omission is not blameworthy.
2. If an act is supererogatory, there is a permissible alternative that is morally worse than it.
3. If one act is morally better than a permissible alternative, one ought to perform the better act.
4. So if a supererogatory act is available to an agent, the agent ought to perform it.
5. But what one ought to do one is obliged to do, and it is blameworthy to omit — contradicting 1.
The premise worth rejecting is 3, and rejecting it is the utilitarian's move. Premise 3 is really just the utilitarian principle wearing deontic clothing: it says that moral betterness always generates an ought. Utilitarians can escape by denying that betterness as such makes an act obligatory. Deontological theories, by contrast, already accept that a worse act can be permissible — you may keep a promise rather than produce slightly more good elsewhere — so they face no difficulty here at all; for them supererogation is unproblematic. The paradox is thus best read as an internal problem for utilitarianism.

(c) Act-utilitarianism has no room for supererogation. AU says an act is right iff it maximizes utility, so anything less is wrong. Donating half your income is therefore either required, if that is what maximizes, or wrong, if some other use of the money would do better; it is never optional-but-good. This is the source of the demandingness objection. Singer's child in the shallow pond is meant to show that giving to famine relief is obligatory in just the way that wading in to save the child would be, and if that is right then very little of what a comfortably-off person does with their discretionary income can count as going beyond the call of duty. AU buys theoretical neatness at the price of a very harsh morality.

But AU can accommodate much of what we actually care about here. AU separates the evaluation of acts from the evaluation of agents, and nothing in the theory stops it praising the person who gives a great deal, admiring her motives, and holding her up as a model for others. Praise is the mark of the supererogatory — that was built into the definition in (a) — so a theory that can praise lavishly has not lost the phenomenon, it has relocated it. AU therefore does have room for supererogatory action, in the only sense that matters. Wolf's paper on moral saints makes a related point from the other direction: she argues that a life organized entirely around moral improvement is not the best kind of life, and that non-moral goods — art, humour, personal relationships, excellence at a craft — have a value that morality does not capture. That is not an argument that beneficence is wrong, but it does put pressure on the ideal of the maximally morally good person which AU seems to demand of all of us."
