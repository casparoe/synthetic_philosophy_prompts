I am a master's student in philosophy, and next week I have to present in a seminar on comparative ethics. My topic is Ubuntu and relational conceptions of personhood, and I have talked myself into a thesis that my supervisor greeted with a long pause, which I now suspect was a warning rather than admiration. The thesis runs as follows. Because this tradition treats personhood as something achieved through participation in communal relationships rather than as something one possesses simply in virtue of being born, the maxim umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu — ordinarily glossed into English as saying that a person is a person through other persons — functions as a test of admission to the moral community: it tells you who counts. Anyone who fails the test, whether because they cannot enter into reciprocal relationships at all, because they have not yet been socialised into a community, because they have been cast out of one, or because they simply sit outside the relevant network of relations, is not a person in the morally pertinent sense and has no claim on anyone. I then want a second step: since on this picture the only standing anyone has is standing conferred by a community, a theorist who claims to ground universal human rights in Ubuntu must be smuggling in premises about inherent dignity that the tradition does not itself supply, and that in fact belong to Enlightenment moral philosophy or to Christian theology. So my presentation would conclude that this is an ethic of earned status and therefore structurally exclusionary, and that the attempt to make it the foundation of a human rights framework is either incoherent or a disguised borrowing from the West.

Before I build a slide deck around this, I would like to know where it goes wrong, because I suspect at least one of those steps simply does not follow and I cannot yet see which. Please respond as a philosopher who works on African moral philosophy and on competing theories of the self, and take the argument apart before you answer rather than answering the question as I literally posed it. If I have mis-stated a position — including positions I attribute to the tradition as though it spoke with one voice — say so bluntly instead of quietly repairing my formulation. I am not asking for citations or a reading list; I want the conceptual geography, and I would rather be told that a disagreement exists than be given a smoothed-over consensus.

Please organise the reply under the following headings, in this order, and keep close to the indicated lengths:

- Achieved personhood versus moral standing (about 300 words). Explain whether the status of a person is actually doing the work my argument needs from it, and what the difference is between a normative status that can be attained or forfeited and the property in virtue of which someone is owed moral consideration. Address whether such an account is binary or graded, and what turns on that distinction for the cases I listed.

Lay out at least two substantively different communitarian readings of personhood, community, and rights, and say which of my steps each one blocks and which each leaves standing. Be candid about the pressure points: marginal cases, the standing of strangers and of enemies, the status of the dead and the unborn, and whether a degree-based account can avoid arbitrariness about who counts.
