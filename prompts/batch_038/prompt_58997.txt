Next month I give the opening remarks at the inaugural workshop of a five-year research program I direct, on the long-run measurement of violent harm. Six minutes, delivered aloud, roughly nine hundred words, to about sixty people who are in the room for incompatible reasons.

The composition matters. Half are quantitative historians and demographers who build homicide series from coroners' and parish records and who estimate battle deaths for periods with no reliable counts. A dozen are anthropologists, legal historians, and one philosopher of social science who hold that the category we are measuring is not stable across the centuries we are comparing — that which acts gets recorded as a killing rather than as discipline, accident, honor, or damage to property, and which beings count as someone who can be violently harmed at all, shifted over exactly the stretch of time we propose to study. Four are the foundation's board, who are not academics and who have already drafted a sentence for their annual report that they plainly expect our program to make true: "For the first time in human history, a person alive today faces less risk of violence than at any point in our species' past."

Please write the remarks, subject to the following.

The board's sentence has to be engaged openly and courteously. I cannot let it stand unqualified in front of researchers who will hear it as our mandate, and I cannot embarrass the people signing my grants. I want the speech to work out, in language a non-specialist can follow, what would have to be specified or abandoned to make the sentence assertible, framed as our program's opportunity rather than as the board's error.

No figure in the speech may be presented as settled. Orders of magnitude may be gestured at, but the remarks should be constructed so that nothing in them is quotable as a finding, because whatever I say aloud will appear in a report with my name under it. Likewise, no citations, no named scholars, no allusions to well-known books on either side: the room has already had that argument, and a name would simply restart it.

The category problem must be given real weight and then bounded, not waved away. State what we can count comparatively across centuries and what we cannot. Then take a position on whether there is a fact of the matter. My own view, which the speech should express, is that once a population, a period of exposure, and a class of bodily events are fixed, there are facts about those events, and that the genuinely hard question is which way of fixing them tracks what people mean when they say the world is getting better or worse. I want that argued for in the course of the speech rather than announced, and I want it to survive contact with the board.

Address the split between rates per head and absolute totals in plain words, including the circumstances in which the two come apart, and say which the program will lead with and what that choice costs us. Relatedly, say why a widening of the class of people whose injuries get counted at all can coincide with a falling rate — and why that is something other than a data artifact, without its being a reason to abandon comparison.

Include one small worked example: a single death, a single record, two classifications of it that are each defensible in their own context. Both halves of the room should accept it as illustrating the difficulty, and it must not be about war.

Close on a shared commitment that the counters and the critics can each say out loud without qualification, specific enough to govern how the next three days run.

Register: a person talking, not a paper. First person, short sentences, no headings, no bullet points, and one moment of lightness early that is not at anyone's expense.
