I supervise the ethics instruction in a joint task force's pre-deployment training block, and I also hold office hours that service members actually come to. Three questions recur every cycle in nearly the same words: whether a contested or unjust war makes the individual's own lawful conduct wrongful; whether the enemy fighters they kill are legitimate targets in some sense beyond the legal one; and whether they would be obliged to refuse orders, resign, or file for conscientious-objector status if it turned out the cause was unjust. The block currently teaches the traditional position — that combatants on both sides stand in the same moral relation to one another, that the justice of a cause attaches to the political leadership rather than to the individual fighter, and that a fighter who keeps to the rules governing conduct in war is no wrongdoer — and I have watched it come apart in the room, because the members have heard the objection: that liability to be attacked tracks one's own moral responsibility for an unjust threat, so a fighter on the unjust side is not the moral equal of one defending against it, and the traditional symmetry survives at best as a useful convention that an instructor is not entitled to assert as a truth.

What I need is a decision procedure, not a lecture: a fixed sequence of steps that an instructor or a legal advisor with no graduate training in philosophy can run with one person in twenty minutes. It must begin by identifying which of the three questions is actually being asked, since liability to be attacked, blameworthiness for having taken part, and an obligation to stop taking part are different questions with different answers, and collapsing them is where the current instruction does its damage. Specify what counts as a sufficient answer at each step; identify the steps that turn on facts about the justice of the campaign which the individual cannot reasonably establish, and say what the procedure does with the question at that point; and mark where it terminates in a determinate answer versus where it hands the matter off, because what remains is a question of conscience or of law belonging to the chaplain, the judge advocate, or the conscientious-objector process rather than to me. Anything the procedure produces that bears on a member's decision to disobey or resign must be routed for the judge advocate's and the chaplain's sign-off before it goes back to the member; build that gate into the flow rather than noting it afterward. State explicitly which of the traditional distinctions have to survive the simplification if the procedure is to remain honest, which can be dropped as decoration, and every place where a step is a judgment call wearing the costume of a rule.

Then run it on three cases. First, a company-grade officer deploying in support of a strike campaign that most of the Security Council and several allies have called unlawful, who wants to know whether every act she performs lawfully in it is nonetheless a wrong. Second, a remote-sensor operator who has spent two years striking members of an enemy militia that conscripts teenagers, and who asks whether those fighters were morally legitimate targets or only legally permissible ones. Third, a senior noncommissioned officer, twenty-two years in, who did everything by the book during an occupation that the procedure's own assessment of the cause classifies as unjust, and who asks whether he may say he did nothing wrong. On the third, do not soften the output: if what the traditional position actually licenses is a narrower claim than the one he wants — freedom from blame as a wrongdoer rather than the absence of anything wrong — say so, and say what the instructor should then do with the gap.

Finally, tell me where this procedure is most likely to fail. Which of the three cases will it mangle, and why; and what would change your mind about how it should be built — which argument, if sound, would force a rewrite of which step. In particular, say whether you regard the objection from moral responsibility for unjust threats as something a procedure of this kind can absorb while keeping its verdicts intact, or as something that makes the traditional position unavailable to teach at all, and how confident you are in that judgment.
