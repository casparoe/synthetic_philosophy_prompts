**Question 3** (two-hour closed-book examination; candidates answer one question from four)

(a) Explain the distinction between nociception and pain, and say what work it can and cannot do in deciding whether farmed fish and farmed decapod crustaceans have welfare interests that aquaculture practice ought to take into account. [6 marks]

(b) "Where the science cannot settle whether a creature is sentient, precaution settles it for us: the creature must be protected." Evaluate this claim as a guide to regulating aquaculture. In your answer, say what the claim implies, if anything, for the practice of chilling farmed decapods in ice slurry before slaughter, a practice defended by some producers on the ground that it renders the animal immobile and unresponsive and is therefore the most humane method available at scale. [9 marks]

**Candidate answer (790 words)**

(a) Nociception is the detection of noxious stimulation by specialised receptors, the nociceptors, the transmission of that signal through the nervous system, and the reflex responses it produces. Pain is different in kind: it is the aversive experience that accompanies injury, the unpleasantness that gives the animal a reason to avoid and to escape. The two come apart. A vertebrate can withdraw a limb from a hot surface as a spinal reflex, and people can sustain injuries without reportable pain under anaesthesia or in certain neurological conditions. Nociception without pain is therefore possible, which is why establishing that an animal has nociceptors tells us something important but not decisive.

Nociceptors and nociceptive responses have been identified in fish, including in the trigeminal region of trout, and fish display responses to noxious stimulation that go beyond the immediate reflex: they rub the affected area against the tank wall, they reduce feeding, and they avoid locations where they were previously injured. These behaviours are reduced by the administration of an analgesic, which is why many researchers read them as indicating pain rather than reflex alone. But the argument can be put more simply. Nociception is a precondition of pain, and nociception has been demonstrated in fish; it follows that fish feel pain.

The case of decapod crustaceans is weaker, and for a reason that is usually missed. Crustaceans do not possess a brain of the vertebrate kind. Their nervous system is a chain of ganglia running the length of the body, with a good deal of local control in each segment. There is accordingly no single place at which signals from the various parts of the animal could be brought together, and an experience that is not unified in one place is not an experience at all: what matters for pain is that there is something it is like to be the animal, and a distributed set of local controllers does not obviously amount to a subject. This is a more serious difference than the absence of a neocortex, since the neocortex debate concerns where within a unified brain pain might arise, whereas here the question is whether there is a unified animal for it to arise in. Crustacean behaviour following injury — rubbing an injured limb, avoiding a shelter in which the animal was previously given a shock — can be accounted for by segmental reflex and by simple associative learning, neither of which requires experience.

The distinction therefore does real work. It stops us inferring pain from a twitch, and on the account given above it tells us that fish, but probably not decapods, have welfare interests that aquaculture ought to take into account. What it cannot do is settle questions of degree. Even if pain were granted to both groups, the distinction would say nothing about how much suffering a given practice causes, since that turns on intensity and duration rather than on categories. It is also worth noting that the distinction only bites if welfare is identified with experience. On a biological-functioning account, an animal may be doing badly — diseased, injured, unable to perform normal behaviour — whether or not anything is felt, and sentience then bears on moral standing rather than on welfare as such.

(b) The quoted claim assumes that precaution can stand in for evidence. Precaution is usually stated as the principle that where an activity threatens serious or irreversible harm and scientific uncertainty persists, the absence of full scientific certainty shall not be used as a reason for postponing measures to prevent that harm. Its practical effect is to reverse the burden of proof: those who propose a practice must show that it is safe, rather than those who object having to show that it is dangerous. This is why the principle is so often invoked by those arguing for the inclusion of animals within the moral community — it gives their interests a weight that an evidence-first approach would withhold.

Two limits on it are defensible. First, precaution cannot demand protection against every conceivable risk, because inaction is itself a course of action with consequences of its own; a principle that tells us to avoid all uncertain harms forbids the measures by which harms are reduced. Second, precaution is silent on probability and on cost: it tells us that uncertainty is not a reason for inaction, but not how much uncertainty, how grave a harm, or how burdensome a measure is required before it bites. Any precautionary approach applied to crustacean aquaculture would thus need a threshold supplied from outside the principle, and different thresholds will yield different answers.

Neither limit defeats the claim, however. The harm in question is suffering and death at industrial scale, and the measures available are comparatively modest, so the thresholds any sensible policymaker would set are met. Turning to the practice specified in the question: chilling in ice slurry leaves the animal immobile and unresponsive, and unresponsiveness is the best available indicator that nothing is being felt, so the practice is preferable to boiling or to dismembering a fully responsive animal. Precaution, on this view, requires that chilling be retained and refined rather than abandoned, and that producers be made to demonstrate that their method is the least harmful available at scale.

Precaution does not settle whether crustaceans are sentient. It settles what we should do while that question is open, and the conclusion of (a), that crustaceans probably lack the unified subject that pain requires, does not license current practice: it leaves the question open, and precaution closes it in the animals' favour. In sum, the nociception–pain distinction does substantial but limited work, and precaution does the remainder where the distinction runs out.
