So my department's grad reading group is doing a term on the metaphysics of modality, and we've each been asked to write down our "modal profile" in advance so that people spend the sessions attacking each other's views instead of just summarising the reading. Mine is basically Lewisian with a couple of tweaks, and I can't tell whether the tweaks are tweaks or something more load-bearing, so I'd like a consistency check before I circulate it. One guy at the last meeting told me my list was "either Lewis with the analysis pulled out, or Lewis with an extra ingredient", and I genuinely couldn't work out which he meant or whether it was supposed to be a compliment.

Here are the commitments:

1. Possible worlds are concrete, spatiotemporally and temporally unified things, and "possible" just means true at some such world. No ersatz worlds, no sets of sentences or propositions doing the analytical work.
2. What gives us plenitude is recombination: take any number of duplicates of any things, patch them together however you like, and there's a world where that's how things are.
3. But recombination isn't the whole story about possibility — I'm happy to say that some possibilities aren't realized at any world, and I don't think that undercuts (1).
4. No world is metaphysically privileged; "actual" is indexical, like "here" or "now".
5. Even so, it's a plain fact and not a matter of anyone's perspective that I'm in this world rather than in an exact duplicate of it.
6. Origin essentialism is roughly right for organisms: this person couldn't have arisen from wholly different gametes/parents.
7. There's no causal interaction with other worlds, ever; but we still have good evidence that they exist, via inference to the best explanation. Separately, in philosophy of science I'm pretty close to a constructive empiricist and I'm suspicious of IBE arguments for unobservables there.
8. Counterfactuals get a closest-world treatment, but I deny there's always a unique closest world, or any closest world at all — ties and infinite descending chains of better candidates are common, and I handle that by supervaluating over the tied/near-enough worlds. I still think lots of ordinary counterfactuals come out determinately true.

Go through these and tell me which pairs genuinely can't coexist, and which only look like they clash — for the latter, say what generates the appearance and why it goes away. For each real conflict, rank how bad it is, say what the cheapest revision would be, and say what that revision costs me elsewhere (I'd rather know that dropping something from (2) or (3) breaks a bunch of other things I care about than find out in the session). Then tell me which single change would clean up the most damage overall. Someone also claimed (8) secretly collapses into an ersatzist view, which struck me as a non-sequitur — say whether it is one.

Two things on the epistemic side: please be explicit about what's settled versus what's still genuinely disputed among people who work on this stuff, and if I've walked into a problem where there's no agreed diagnosis, say that rather than giving me one camp's line as though it were the consensus. And flag any place where you think I've misdescribed Lewis rather than merely disagreed with him — I'd like to know if I'm attributing to him something he'd reject, since that's a different kind of error than being inconsistent.
