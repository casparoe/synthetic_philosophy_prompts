I teach a seminar in comparative law and religion to 24 students — mostly second- and third-year law students, plus a handful of political science graduate students — in one 110-minute session. Design a constitutional-assembly simulation that makes them feel, rather than merely hear, that the hard questions in this field are not about the slogans of separation and establishment but about operational machinery: who counts as a member, how the state knows, what leaving costs and who receives the declaration, which bodies the state is competent to identify, who pays for clergy and buildings, and what happens to a settlement when the demographics that made it attractive shift two generations later.

Logistics you may assume: six tables of four, one whiteboard, index cards and paper, no laptops, no money, no prizes. The students are American and will reflexively treat the First Amendment as the whole conceptual space; the design should defeat that reflex structurally, not by admonition. Everything participants need must be printed on the handouts you write for me — no outside research, no citations, no reading list.

Deliver the following.

1. The drafting task. Delegates from a fictional jurisdiction must adopt a single constitutional article on religion and public life. Write the actual blank template, with numbered slots that cannot be left empty: the criterion determining which bodies the state will deal with; how a person becomes a member of such a body and by what record the state knows it; how a person leaves, who receives the declaration, and what trace it leaves; the funding rule, including any levy on income and any general-grant contribution; the rule governing what children encounter in public schools; and the rule for buildings, churchyards, and burial. Specify the supermajority required for adoption and a rule preventing delegates from filling a slot with an aspiration instead of operative language.

2. Five mechanism dossiers, one per table, in full text, with every country name and identifying proper noun stripped so nothing reads as foreign-law trivia. Draw on these real arrangements; I supply the facts so the handouts are accurate without your needing to look anything up. (a) Religious bodies granted public-law corporate status may levy a surcharge on members' income tax; the state tax authority collects it on their behalf and withholds a collection fee, though some smaller bodies collect it themselves to avoid that fee; membership is stored in a federal tax database and employers receive extracts for payroll withholding; exit requires a declaration to a state authority rather than to the religious body, and some bodies respond by refusing religious marriage or burial to those who left; the arrangement rests on a Weimar-era constitutional provision carried forward into the current basic law. (b) A national church's levy varies by municipality within a band of roughly 0.4 to 1.3 percent of taxable income, typically near 0.7 percent, and the state adds a block grant covering about a ninth of the church's budget, so non-members fund it too. (c) Members of two national churches pay an income-based levy of roughly 1 to 2 percent; formal resignation ends liability only from the following year, and the most commonly reported reason for resigning is the levy itself. (d) A church whose membership once accrued automatically to children of members ended that rule in the mid-1990s and ceased to be a state church in 2000; registered membership remains near half the population while regular weekly attendance sits in the low single digits. (e) National separation legislation did not reach three departments then under foreign rule, which still recognize and organize four faiths by law, pay their clergy from the interior ministry's budget, appoint two of their bishops by presidential decree after agreement with the Holy See, and require religious instruction in primary and middle school unless parents file a written request for dispensation; the constitutional court has upheld this against challenge. (f) An established church whose supreme governor is the monarch, whose bishops sit in the upper legislative house, which levies no tax and lives on endowments, investment income, and voluntary giving, and whose territorial parishes owe duties to all residents whether or not they belong. (g) No establishment, no religious test for federal office, general tax exemption for religious bodies, judicial deference to those bodies on the selection of ministers, and surviving ceremonial acknowledgments such as legislative chaplains. Use five of these seven; tell me which two you held back and where they reappear.

3. Three registry rounds after adoption, in which the delegates' own text is the only authority. Write at least nine case files, each a few sentences and each decided strictly by what the article says: the person baptized as an infant who has never believed and now sees a deduction on a payslip; the widow who formally left twenty years ago and asks for her husband's burial in the churchyard; the newly arrived congregation of ten thousand with no registry entry; the mixed couple whose child was enrolled by default; the municipality where members have fallen below a third but the bishop's seat endures; the dissident parish that wants out of its denomination and to keep its building; the nonbeliever who learns that a general grant rather than a membership levy pays part of the established body's budget; the official asked whether a godless meditation movement is a body the article covers; and the delegate who proposes to solve everything by making exit automatic.

4. A scoring scheme with no money: hidden constituency cards revealed at the end, a grievance ledger counting complaints the article's own text entitles someone to file, and a stability measure taken by re-vote after the third round.

5. A timeline in minutes, the reveal of which jurisdiction each dossier modeled, a facilitator script for that reveal, and a debrief mapping table that pairs specific moments from the rounds with the concepts they instantiate — including why costless-sounding exit is not costless, why neutrality still requires the state to classify, and why symbolic establishment and fiscal entanglement come apart.

6. What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, with concrete fixes: where the drafting stalls, which case file derails the schedule, how a group games the recognition criterion, and what to do when the room reverts to American framing anyway.

7. An honest closing note on which features of these real regimes the simulation cannot reproduce, and what I must supply myself — path dependence, the felt weight of conviction among the people these rules govern, and the fact that I, not the delegates, wrote the shocks.
