I have no academic training of any kind. I left school at sixteen, spent most of my working life in the motor trade, and have never studied economics or psychology. But I have turned one particular pattern over in my mind for the better part of a decade, because I kept walking into it myself, and I should like to build an evening's activity around it.

Two episodes, both mine. The first: a van I bought as a project for £900, on which I spent something like eleven thousand pounds and four years of Saturdays, and which was worth less at the end than at the beginning. Nobody knew about it. It was me and the van in a lock-up on the industrial estate. The second: a dispute with a neighbour over a strip of ground at the side of our houses, which ran for two years, cost far more in time and money than the ground was worth, and which I could have ended at almost any point by simply saying so. What kept me going in the second was plain enough — dropping it would have meant, in front of the whole street, that I had been in the wrong all along. But the first had no audience whatsoever, and I carried on just as stubbornly.

So I have three hunches about what the mechanism might be, and I cannot tell them apart in my own history.

1. It is about the audience: nobody wants to be the one who walks away empty-handed while others watch.
2. It is about keeping the story unfinished: to stop is to convert a run of bad luck into a decision I made, and that is the part I could not bear, whereas carrying on leaves the question open.
3. It is about the arithmetic: at no single moment did stopping look better than going on, because each further step was small beside what had already gone, and so there was never a point at which I could see myself doing something foolish.

For six years I have run a fortnightly evening group in our town — a thinking club of fourteen or so: retired nurses, lorry drivers, a couple of sixth-formers, nobody with specialist training and no patience for being lectured at. We have ninety minutes in the back room of the community hall, tables that move, a kettle, and about fifteen pounds I can spend on a prize.

Please design an activity for one of these evenings. It must meet the following conditions.

It has to get several of the room into the trap for real, by what happens rather than by anything I say. No introductory talk, and no technical name for any of it spoken aloud until the final ten minutes.

It must not take cash out of anybody's pocket, and yet the thing people stand to lose has to feel like a loss to them in the room. Choose what that currency is and tell me why it will bite with a group of strangers who see each other fortnightly. The arithmetic has to be head arithmetic, doable by someone who left school at sixteen.

It must not be rigged so that everyone who carries on is a fool. There should be at least one way of playing it well, and the room should be able to work out for themselves that it exists before I tell them. I have no interest in a game whose only lesson is "don't play".

Most importantly, it has to let me tell which of my three hunches is actually doing the work. To that end: run the same game twice in the evening, in two versions that differ in exactly one feature, and tell me which feature you have chosen and why that difference separates some of my hunches from one another. Then give me a table of signatures — for each of the three hunches, what I should expect to see in the room, in behaviour or in what people say afterwards, if that hunch were the true explanation; and what I should expect to see instead if one of the others were true. Write the debrief questions out in full, in the exact words I would use, phrased so that people can answer them without knowing what each answer implies.

I should also like a run sheet with timings and a list of materials I can make or buy in a week, since I want to print your answer and use it without any further research. Then tell me what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it — the person who spots the shape of the thing in two minutes and announces it, the two who fall out properly, the one who will not take part at all, the room that declines to escalate because my own setup has warned them off, the evening that runs out of time — and how to handle each. Finish by saying plainly which of my three hunches this activity cannot test at all, and what I would have to supply myself if I wanted to know about that one.

If what I have described has a name, tell me the name at the end. But I want no reading lists, no references and no names of researchers: I have no means of checking any of it, and I would rather not pretend to. Argue everything out from the design itself.

Please write the whole thing in British English, in spelling and in idiom alike; my group would notice.
