ok so bitcoin's proof of work has been bugging me for years and I want to figure out what theory of signals the thing is actually committed to, because I don't think the usual defenses add up.

The practice, roughly as I understand it: to add a block you burn real electricity grinding through hashes, and the work is thrown away afterwards. Its not a database, not a computation anyone wanted, not a byproduct you can use for anything. The network pays the winner in newly minted coins. And the security story everyone tells is that this is exactly what makes cheating prohibitive — an attacker has to redo all that pointless work faster than the honest miners, and that costs more than they could ever steal. So the trust rests on cost, and specifically on cost that produces nothing.

Then the same people will also say the particular work is incidental, that any mechanism making attacks expensive would do just as well, which is more or less the pitch for proof of stake — lock up capital instead, lose it if you misbehave. Thats the part I cant reconcile. If the wastefulness is incidental, stake is a straight substitute and strictly better, and proof of work is just an expensively named lock. But if the wastefulness is doing something, no substitute can be equivalent, and the community ought to be saying stake isn't merely greener but less trustworthy in some deeper way. Which is it, and can they hold both at once?

So: reconstruct the view of what makes a signal credible that this design only makes sense under. Is it the peacock's tail thing — I know enough of that literature to recognize the shape of the argument, waste you can't get back is what buys belief — or is it just a claim about the price of an attack, in which case nothing philosophically interesting is going on at all? Separate what the protocol strictly requires from what it merely fits comfortably with, and tell me which rival accounts it quietly rules out: reputation, locked-up stake, the fact that the work is public and anyone can verify it for free.

And here's a test case I want you to run it against. Suppose someone redesigns the chain so the same energy goes into work with real outside value — searching for large primes, say — winner picked the same way, paid the same coins. Intuitively that's strictly better: same security, plus you get something for the electricity. My suspicion is that on the wastefulness reading it's actually *less* secure, for a principled reason having to do with whether a miner can recoup their costs somewhere other than by being honest. Is that right? If it is, does it settle what proof of work presupposes — and does it predict something specific that proof of stake should fail at, something you could in principle go and look for? Finally, if the community accepted that the cost was instrumental and not essential, what would it have to give up, both in the protocol and in how it argues?
