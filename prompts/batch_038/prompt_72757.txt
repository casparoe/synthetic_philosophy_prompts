I'm running a proseminar on the ethics of assertion in the spring and I've got two students who have spent the entire semester talking past eachother, so I've decided the last session is them signing a joint statement instead of having the same argument a ninth time. I want you to write that statement — the document a genuinely good-faith adversarial collaboration between their two positions would produce, that both of them could put their names to without wincing.

The positions. Camp A, call them assertion-first: lying is a special wrong because it abuses the practice of assertion itself — you invoke a norm (say what you believe) and knowingly break it — whereas misleading, however shady, is a different and lesser failure, a breakdown of conversational cooperation rather than the violation of a constitutive rule. Camp B, hearer-first: what matters is which beliefs you intentionally or foreseeably install in the other person, and literal truth is a loophole you exploited, not a shield; and where a misleader arranges things so the hearer's own inference does the dirty work, that's an aggravating feature, not an exculpatory one.

Build the whole thing around three cases of mine.

Case 1, the sample. A university spokesperson asked whether the department places its PhDs well answers, truthfully, with a real name and a real employer: one graduate got a tenure-track job last year. Nothing asserted is false, and arguably nothing is even clearly implicated — but the actual rate is three out of forty and the hearer will generalise. I want this one fought over hardest, because I don't think either camp has a clean handle on it. Treat misleading-by-unrepresentative-sample as a third category, seperate from misleading by implicature and from misleading by presupposition, and have both camps say whether their machinery can absorb it or has to be extended.

Case 2, the clean lie. An employee asked point blank by his boss whether he passed a file to a competitor says no. He did. But the competitor already had the contents from elsewhere, so no consequential false belief gets installed anywhere, and the lie is uncovered within the hour. Both camps owe an account of what's wrong here and of whether it's worse or less bad than Case 1.

Case 3, the correction. Compare a politician who has been technically truthful but misleading for a year and is finally asked outright and then lays it all out plainly, with one who lied once and issued a correction the next morning. This is where I want the crux my other student came up with, named as such — the retraction asymmetry: a liar can undo the wrong by correcting the record because there's a determinate false content to retract, while a habitual misleader asserted nothing false, so there's nothing on the record to retract, and any correction has to reconstruct what the hearer was steered into believing, which is harder and lands sounding like a fresh accusation. If that's right the two wrongs differ in reparability, and one camp is much better placed to explain that than the other. Say which, and why.

On the agreements: nothing load-free. "Both think deception is usually bad" doesn't count. I want the load-bearing ones — about what assertion is, about whether intent to deceive is necessary, about how much perjury-style literal-truth defenses should be taken to track the moral category, about whether there can be a right to mislead someone who has no right to the truth even if there's never a right to lie to them, or whether that asymmetry collapses under Case 2.

On the disagreements: exact formulations each side would accept as its own, no strawmanning, and where a term is doing double duty (assert, intend, what is said) flag it and give the stipulation both can work under. Cruxes are the sub-questions where a settled answer would actually move somebody, and for each I want a case or argument both accept as bearing on it, with each side's prediction written down before they see how the other answers. Finally, I suspect a real chunk of this is a fight about how much work the word lie is being asked to do: tell me which chunk, and what's left of the dispute once you strip the word out.

Sections, in this order: (1) Why we sat down — half a page, what we were asked to settle and what would count as success. (2) Claims we both sign — numbered, 8 to 12 items, one or two sentences each. (3) Where we still differ — a table with columns for the issue, Camp A's formulation in A's own acceptable words, Camp B's formulation, substantive-or-verbal, and what turns on it. (4) The three cases — for each, both verdicts with reasons, and an explicit note of anything either camp had to concede. (5) Cruxes and predictions — three or four, each with the agreed test and both predictions stated up front. (6) What was only words. (7) Still open — what we couldn't resolve and what we'd want from a third party. Keep it tight enough that I can hand it out in class, so somewhere between 1200 and 1800 words. No citations and no reading list — it's two students at a table with their own case intuitions, and that's the register I'm after.
