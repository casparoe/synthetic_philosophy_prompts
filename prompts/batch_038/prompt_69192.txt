Overheard-at-dinner adjudication request. I teach philosophy and two colleagues of mine — neither works in early modern, both perfectly competent — got into it about Pascal's wager last week and asked me to referee. I told them I'd get an outside opinion, so here it is. I've tried to report both sides straight and I'm not going to tell you which one I'd back.

The setup they both granted, for argument's sake: the agent can either live religiously (R) or not (N); the states are God exists (G) or doesn't; the religious life costs some finite amount of this-worldly good, call it 10 units, and pays an infinite reward if G.

Move 1. Ana said the wager is basically trivially sound given that setup: any nonzero credence in G, however tiny, times an infinite reward gives infinite expected value, whereas N has finite expected value at best, so R wins. You never need to pin the probability down.

Move 2. Bo said that's exactly why it fails — run the same argument for a rival god. He sketched a table on the back of a menu:

```
                     God-C exists   God-D exists   no god
devout to C              +inf           -inf         -10
devout to D              -inf           +inf         -10
secular                  -inf           -inf           0
```

and said: with a nonzero credence on each of the three columns, every row has an expected value that comes out infinite or undefined, because you're adding +infinity to -infinity. So it's not that the wager picks the wrong god. It doesn't pick anything. It isnt even well-formed.

Move 3. Ana said fine, that's the usual many-gods point, and the repair is obvious: restrict to hypotheses with decent antecedent plausibility, weight the columns by prior, and the ties break.

Move 4. Bo said then the wager isn't doing any work, the priors are doing all of it. If you're allowed to rank gods by prior plausibility, you might as well just believe whichever one the priors and the evidence favor and drop the infinity altogether. It's a machine for converting "I already find this god plausible" into "I'm rationally required to believe in this god."

Move 5. Ana said no, the infinity changes how much prior you need. With finite rewards you'd need a fairly high credence before the religious life pays off; with an infinite reward any nonzero sliver does it. So the wager is doing precise work — it moves the threshold.

Move 6. Bo's closer: "almost any sliver" isn't "any." For the readers Pascal actually cares about — the ones who think the God-hypothesis is incoherent rather than merely unlikely — the credence is flat zero, and zero times infinity gets you nowhere, so the argument hands them no conclusion at all. And for the the people who do have a nonzero credence, it's dialectically pointless, because their belief is already being carried by whatever evidential case gave them the nonzero credence in the first place. So it reaches nobody: too weak for the unbeliever, redundant for the semi-believer.

They stopped there because the restaurant closed.
