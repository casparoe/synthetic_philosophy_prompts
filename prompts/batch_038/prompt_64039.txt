Bit of background first. I ran a fracture-mechanics lab for 33 years — ceramics mostly, Charpy, four-point bend, Weibull statistics, that kind of thing — and since retiring I've fallen down the philosophy hole, mainly via a Tuesday reading group at the public library. Last month we did dispositions and I haven't slept properly since. Thirty-odd years of writing "the specimen shall be considered fragile if it fails under the specified load within the specified time," and now I'm told that sentence is philosophically bankrupt. There's Martin's finks (the wire that's wired up to stop being live the instant you touch it with a conductor), Lewis's repair where the thing has to hang onto its causal basis until the manifestation actually gets going, then Johnston and Bird coming back with masks and antidotes where the disposition is still perfectly there but something in the surroundings blocks the shattering, and then the mimicker cases — a little charge glued to the outside of the beaker so it bursts when struck and "passes" the test without being fragile at all. And now apparently the the argument is about whether a fink can be built into the material itself, with one camp saying that if a property can't be finked from within it isn't really dispositional, it's categorical. Which, if it had been true in my lab, would have meant half our certified reference materials were not what their certificates said they were.

So here's what I want. Write me a parody of an international test standard, deadpan, no winking, as if issued by an invented standards body — make up the committee members, don't use any real philosophers' names — for the certification of the disposition FRAGILE in borosilicate laboratory glassware. About 900 words, with all the usual furniture of a real standard: a Foreword noting that the previous edition was withdrawn following complaints, then Scope, Terms and definitions, Apparatus, Method, Validity of results (i.e. the grounds on which a test is declared void), an informative NOTE from a dissenting committee member who thinks the document certifies nothing but a way of talking, and a short Annex on troubleshooting. The Terms and definitions section has to do actual work — fink, reverse fink, mask, antidote, mimicker, causal basis, "normal conditions" — and it has to distinguish mask from fink correctly, because that's the bit my reading group keeps getting wrong: a mask doesn't take the disposition away, it just stops it from showing up.

The humour should come from getting the metaphysics right, not from philosophers being silly, so the standard should be internally consistent — every proviso it adds to keep a counterexample out should be a clause a real standards committee could plausibly have written, applied to the right kind of case. The target isn't the people so much as the practice of rescuing an analysis with one more proviso, and by the end the reader should have felt the triviality worry for themselves: that once "conditions normal" gets defined as whatever conditions make the thing shatter, the certificate doesn't say anything at all. Let the standard deal with that the way real bodies do — a footnote and a standing working group that meets twice a year. Also work in a clause declaring specimens that cannot be finked from within to be ineligible for certification, on the grounds that they are categorical rather than dispositional, and let that read as a perfectly ordinary bureaucratic exclusion rather than a punchline. Keep it legible to someone who's never heard the word fink (my daughter-in-law is definately going to read it at Christmas), and no bibliography, no references, no quoting anybody — I just want the document itself.
