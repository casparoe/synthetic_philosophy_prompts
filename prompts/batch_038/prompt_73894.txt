I am a fourth-year doctoral candidate in a joint program between a history department and a philosophy department, and I am preparing an application for an internal fellowship that would fund eighteen months of dissertation writing. My readers are deliberately mismatched: one is an archival historian of twentieth-century diplomacy who regards the philosophy of causation as elegant decoration on a subject that is settled by documents, and the other is a philosopher of science who regards the same literature as a morass of unanswerable first-order questions that historians keep re-fighting because they refuse to notice what they are actually asking. The proposal has to satisfy both. My half-formed idea is this. The question of who started the Cold War is ordinarily treated as a single empirical question on which the traditionalists, the revisionists, and the post-revisionists offer rival answers, with each generation's consensus flipping as new archives opened. I suspect instead that the three camps were never answering one question. The traditionalist framing picks out a *trigger* — an intervention that departs from a presupposed baseline of expected conduct — which is essentially the move that mid-century legal philosophy made when it distinguished the cause of a harm from the indefinitely many conditions causally relevant to it, on the ground that selection out of that field is governed by pragmatic considerations rather than by metaphysics. The revisionist framing asks something structurally different: which arrangements made confrontation robust across a wide range of substitutions of persons and decisions, so that agency drops out as explanatorily idle. The post-revisionist framing asks a third thing again: given that both sides were causally engaged, how should involvement be allocated, and does the answer change when the interaction is reciprocal rather than sequential? On this reading the stalemate is not under-evidenced but under-specified, and the apparent empirical convergence and divergence over the decades is largely an artifact of shifting baselines and contrast classes rather than of new documents.

Please turn this into a fellowship proposal of roughly two pages. It should state the research question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, and should distinguish it from the merely deflationary thesis that the question is meaningless. It should explain why the existing historiographical positions leave it open rather than already having settled it implicitly. It should lay out the method and defend that method against the obvious alternatives — more archival work on the same episodes, informal counterfactual speculation, or a formal actual-causality treatment using structural equations and explicit variable choice — saying which of these I should commit to and why, and being candid about the risk that a formal apparatus simply encodes the historian's prior blame-allocation in the choice of variables and the direction of the arrows, thereby adding nothing. It should specify the archival component in enough detail that my historian reader can see this is a dissertation in history and not a philosophy term paper with historical illustrations; I have in mind two or three bounded episodes on which the disagreement is well documented and narrow enough to be tractable, such as the treatment of nuclear information and the atomic decision in 1945, the Polish settlement as it stood after Yalta and Potsdam, and the 1947 sequence in which the American aid and containment initiatives and the Soviet consolidation of Eastern Europe are usually traded as rival starting moves. For each, the intended demonstration is that two historians who appear to contradict one another can be shown to be making compatible claims once their baseline and contrast class are made explicit — and the proposal should say what a fully worked demonstration of that would look like as a deliverable.

The proposal must also include a clear account of what would count as the project failing: for instance, that once the baselines are fixed the parties turn out to be contradicting each other after all, in which case the reframing is verbal decoration; or that the apparatus yields determinate results only for episodes small enough to be trivial, with no purchase on the general question; or that the historian reader is right and the whole exercise changes nothing anyone would write about 1945 to 1948. Finally, I want an honest assessment of scope for eighteen months, and a decision about one fork I have deliberately left open: whether the project should take a stand on the possibility that the Cold War has no determinate onset at all — that its beginning is a vague boundary rather than an event with a first moment, which would make the blame question ill-posed for a different reason than the one I have sketched — or whether it should bracket that issue and work only with episodes whose boundaries are not in dispute. Recommend a choice and justify it. Do not include a bibliography or a literature review by title and date; refer to positions, arguments, and schools by description and I will supply the citations myself.
