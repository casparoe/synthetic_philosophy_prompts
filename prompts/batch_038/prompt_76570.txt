**Question 3 (100 marks).** Chalmers's virtual realism holds that virtual reality is genuine reality: a virtual sword is a real object, because it is constituted by a computational process. Consider what I'll call the *Suspension Objection*. The servers hosting the game-world of Aethermoor are switched down for six months and then restored from a complete backup; the world resumes exactly where it stopped, including the sword your character has carried for three years. During the shutdown nothing was running, and the sword existed only as a static pattern of bits on a disk. So either (i) the sword went out of existence and a numerically distinct but qualitatively identical sword came into being — in which case virtual objects are absurdly fragile, since a physical sword survives sitting in a dark drawer for six months — or (ii) the sword persisted as a static pattern, in which case being a running computational process cannot be what makes virtual objects real. Either way the account fails.
Assess the Suspension Objection. Say also whether virtual realism commits you to the view that virtual harms are real harms, and what that implies about the wrongness of killing a non-player character.

**Student's answer.**

The Suspension Objection assumes that 'virtual' means 'unreal', which is precisely what virtual realism denies. A virtual sword is not an illusion or a mere appearance of a sword: when you swing it in Aethermoor there really is something there, and what there is is a pattern of computation implemented in hardware. That is why the first horn is a non-starter. The sword is made of exactly the same stuff as any other object in the world — silicon, copper, electrons — so the shutdown did not remove any matter from the world; the bits were still on the disk, just as the atoms of a sword in a drawer are still in the drawer. If the object in the drawer persists through six months of doing nothing, the object on the disk does too. Horn (i) proves far too much: on that reasoning every physical object would blink out of existence whenever nobody is looking at it.

That leaves horn (ii), and I think the objection gets the conclusion backwards. It says that if the sword persisted as a static pattern, then being a running process cannot be what makes virtual objects real. But it has quietly swapped the criterion. What makes the sword real is not that it is running, but that it is the sort of thing that runs — a computational structure with a defined causal role in the game's rules. Being live is how that structure normally shows up, not what it consists in. The backup is a real sword in suspended animation, and its identity is fixed by the rules and the save-state, not by an unbroken history of frames. Compare a musical score: a symphony is not a new symphony after a year of not being performed. So both horns fail, and the objection does not show that virtual realism gives an unstable account of persistence.

The second part is easier once the first is settled. If virtual objects are genuine objects, then an NPC is a genuine being, and killing it is a genuine killing: the destruction of a real thing with a real history, real relations to other real things, and — in the case of the companion I spent three years levelling up — a real loss to me. Virtual realism therefore does commit you to real virtual harms. Indeed it commits you to thinking the debate about whether violent games corrupt the player's character is beside the point, since that debate only got going because people assumed there was no victim. Once the victim is real, the vice story is a distraction.

This has one uncomfortable implication, which I'd rather flag than dodge. If virtual harms are real harms, then we are probably living inside a simulation, since a simulated world is exactly the kind of world virtual realism says is genuine. Our own pains would then be virtual pains, on the same footing as the NPC's. That does not make them unreal, but it does mean the retort 'you can't harm anything in a game' loses its bite in both directions, and the difference between killing an NPC and killing a person turns out to be a difference of degree — how much suffering is involved — rather than a difference in kind.
