Our reading group finished Appiah's The Honor Code last month and it sort of fell apart. Now every time somebody brings up a norm that vanished — dueling, the Atlantic slave trade, footbinding, whatever — half the table explains it with their own favorite single mechanism and nobody can argue about it, because we don't have any agreed way of telling the mechanisms apart. One guy says it's always economics (he means Eric Williams, the one who argued Britain killed the trade because the West India interest had stopped paying; I keep confusing him with the philosopher Williams, who I think is famous for denying that this sort of change is a case of people coming to know something new, and who Appiah pushes back on somewhere in the book though I couldnt tell you where). Another says it's always honor and shame, another says it's always the novels and the feelings, and I'm the cynic — I think the arguments are just the press release.

So I'd rather build an instrument than argue. Please write me a decision tree you can run on a historical case, not on a person. Input is facts about a norm that stopped being practiced; output is a single named explanation-family for why it stopped. Crucially, no node may ask the person running the tree what they think or value — every node has to be a question about the case that a historian could in principle settle from the record: who quit first, when the arguments were first available, what people said about it afterward, whether the practice was still turning a profit, whether some new institution showed up with the capacity to enforce. I want it to be able to tell me I'm wrong about at least one case.

Leaves I want on it, roughly: (1) the arguments were there and eventually landed — rational persuasion doing causal work; (2) Appiah-style honor restructuring, where the practice became less condemned than dishonorable and the people whose standing was tied to it led the abandoning; (3) sensibility shift — new feelings, new thresholds for what you can stand to watch, nobody's beliefs got better; (4) interest convergence — it stopped paying for the people with the power to keep it going; (5) coercion or state capacity — a police force, an inspectorate, a licensing board, a tax made it impossible or ruinous; (6) generational drift — nobody was converted, the practitioners just aged out and the young never picked it up; and (7) positional or fashion dynamics, where it got dropped because too many people could do it and it stopped marking anyone off. If you think one of those isn't a real family, or should be split in two, say so and say why.

Some nodes I want in there: whether the moral arguments against the practice were available for decades before it actually stopped (if they were, persuasion-as-cause owes us a trigger); whether the first to quit were the insiders whose honor the practice belonged to or the outsiders who'd been complaining all along; whether giving it up cost the practitioners money, safety, or standing; what they said about it afterwards — wrong, or vulgar, or embarrassing, or merely unnecessary; whether the profit curve turned down before, alongside, or after the norm; whether the change stuck in places that lacked the new enforcement apparatus; and which end of the geography it died at first, core or periphery.

Every real case is going to trip several branches, so give me an explicit tie-break rule for mixed cases and state it as a rule rather than as taste — and no leaf is allowed to be "it's complicated" or "several factors at once." Then flag the pairs of leaves your tree cannot actually separate (I suspect honor restructuring versus the fashion one, and maybe honor versus sensibility) and say what evidence would separate them, if any exists. Also walk me through the answer-combinations nobody occupies — the outputs your tree could in principle produce but that don't seem to happen — and for each say whether the cell is empty because the view is incoherent, becuase it would be unstable over time, or because nobody has looked there yet.

Finally, run it on three cases and print the path node by node: Britain ending the Atlantic slave trade in 1807; the disappearance of dueling among English gentlemen around the 1840s; and one that isn't in Appiah — England moving executions off the public street and inside the prisons in the late 1860s. Then tell me which of the three your tree most plausibly misdiagnoses, and what node you'd have to add to catch it.
