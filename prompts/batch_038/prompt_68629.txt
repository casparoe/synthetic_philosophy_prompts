I took early retirement from a career in reservoir engineering — some forty years modelling how fluids redistribute themselves through heterogeneous rock until they approach something like steady state — and have been reading the Scottish Enlightenment since, more or less as an autodidact. My way in has been Hume's political essays, which appeal to an old engineer precisely because they read like natural philosophy: mechanism, tendency, relaxation toward a level. Last month our county historical society hosted a lecture on the eighteenth-century monetary debates, given by a retired banker of evident erudition, who handed out a single-page précis of Hume's three essays on money, on interest, and on the balance of trade. I have transcribed his summary below as faithfully as my notes permit:

> "Hume's three essays contain the first clear statement of the quantity theory of money. Money, he says, is no part of a nation's wealth at all but merely the instrument men have agreed upon for exchanging one commodity for another, the oil that eases the wheels rather than a wheel itself; were the coin of a kingdom doubled in a night while its industry and commodities stood still, the price of everything would simply double. Because money is neutral in this way, Hume drew the practical conclusion that paper credit is a fraud upon the nation — it substitutes a perishable token for real wealth, drives the precious metals abroad, and leaves the country stripped on the day of emergency — and he accordingly urged that paper money be suppressed and the metallic stock enlarged and held as public treasure. He reasoned likewise about the hire of money: since the Dutch enjoyed both an abundance of money and a very low rate of interest, plenty of money plainly cheapens its hire, and a nation that accumulates specie grows at once rich and easy. The last essay supplies the celebrated mechanism: any advantage a country wins by exporting more than it imports is self-defeating, for the gold that flows in raises its prices, destroys the very advantage that drew it, and flows out again, until the money of the world stands in proportion to the industry of each nation, as water finds its level. This disposed of the mercantilists, and the mechanism has since been falsified only in the detail — under a managed gold standard the flows were sterilised and the discount rate performed the work Hume assigned to prices."

I should be grateful for an audit of that passage, taken claim by claim: which parts a specialist in Hume's economic writing would accept as fair, which are simplifications that cost something, and which are simply not his view. Three questions in particular. First, the sentence about the Dutch and the cheapening of hire strikes me as the opposite of what I took from the essay on interest, yet I cannot tell whether our speaker has confused Hume with an earlier writer he was arguing against, and I have no wish to accuse a knowledgeable man of error on the strength of an amateur's reading; please settle it, and tell me what Hume does identify as the causes of a low rate of interest. Second, as someone who spent his working life distinguishing transients from steady states, I suspect the précis has collapsed Hume's transitional dynamics into his long-run result — that the doubling of the coin in a night and the gradual increase of the metallic stock through trade are treated by Hume as having quite different consequences, which would explain why he can call money a mere instrument of exchange and yet want the state to hold more of it. Is that suspicion well founded, or merely the prejudice of an old engineer? Third, does the closing claim of falsification — that sterilisation and the discount rate did what Hume assigned to prices — actually defeat anything Hume asserted, or a stronger thesis than he held? I note too that equal levels and proportionality to industry are different relations, so I wonder whether the image of water finding its level does justice to his account of what the mechanism settles at, and whether the mechanism is even meant to be automatic rather than conditional. Please do not give me references, page numbers, or a reading list; I have no library and no means of chasing anything down. What I want is the reasoning itself, laid out plainly, with any point on which you are uncertain flagged as such rather than smoothed over.
