A prominent grantmaker practices what it calls worldview diversification. It divides its endowment across a small number of "worldviews" — broad packages of assumption about what matters morally and about how change happens: roughly, improving the health and wellbeing of people alive today; reducing low-probability but catastrophic risks to civilization; accelerating scientific and technological progress; and expanding moral concern to non-human animals — committing an approximately fixed share of each year's budget to each, rather than concentrating on whichever it currently takes to be best. Within each bucket, individual grants are then chosen by unabashed expected-value reasoning: cost-effectiveness models, marginal impact per dollar, explicit diminishing-returns curves, and a standing preference for the cheapest remaining unit of good. Candidate causes inside a bucket are ranked using the familiar importance / tractability / neglectedness triad, where neglectedness means roughly how few other funders and researchers are already working there. The funder concedes two things about its own top-level rule: that if it knew which worldview was correct, putting everything behind that one would be right, and that there is no philanthropic analogue of bankruptcy, so nothing forces it to hedge the way an investor hedges.

I want the implicit philosophy reconstructed rather than the practice defended or debunked.

First: what theory of value, and what theory of decision under moral uncertainty, does the two-level structure presuppose? Explain how an allocation rule can be maximizing at one level and deliberately non-maximizing at the level above it — what has to be true about the relationships among the worldviews for that to be coherent rather than a straightforward contradiction, and whether the coherence requires incommensurability between them, mere ignorance of the exchange rates, or something else entirely. Note that fixing shares at all seems to require weights of some kind; say what those weights are supposed to represent if not expected value.

Second: sort the candidate motivations into the ones the practice strictly needs and the ones it merely sits comfortably with — ambiguity aversion as distinct from risk aversion, moral humility as an epistemic virtue, respect for reasonable disagreement among peers, an integrity constraint against being wholly identified with one contested conception of the good, and a purely political motive of maintaining a coalition of donors. For each, say what observable feature of the practice would distinguish it from the others.

Third: address the apparent inconsistency in how other people's behavior figures. Neglectedness treats the absence of other funders as what makes a cause worth funding; worldview diversification treats the considered commitment of other reasonable funders to a given worldview as a reason to fund it too. Is the same fact being given opposite normative force, or are the two levels sensitive to genuinely different things? Give the most charitable joint account available, and then say what it costs.

Fourth: identify where the practice is philosophically underdetermined — the points at which several incompatible theories would license exactly the same allocations — and where its structure quietly settles a question it claims to leave open, for instance about whether worldviews are discrete and mutually exclusive, whether impact is additive across them, and whether the shares may be revised as moral argument progresses.

Finally, run three replacements and report what breaks. Suppose the top-level rule were changed to maximizing expected choice-worthiness across moral theories with normalized intertheoretic comparisons; or to a bargaining compromise among the worldviews of the kind agents would strike if each could propose how to divide the pot; or to a max-min rule that protects against the worst-case worldview being the true one. For each, state what the institution would do differently in practice — which committee structures, published numbers, and internal incentives would have to change — and which parts of the existing apparatus would survive intact.
