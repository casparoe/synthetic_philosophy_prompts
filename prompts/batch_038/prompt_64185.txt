Got made redundant in the spring and took a job as an archive assistant at our county record office mostly to keep the mortgage going, which somehow turned into a part-time MA in local history, and now I'm writing my dissertation on the parish my mum grew up in, roughly 1780s to 1830s. Which is how I ended up with a problem I can't find a word for.

Here's the first thing. Almost every individual life I can actually reconstruct from the boxes I'm allowed to touch is documented *because* an institution had to do something about that person. What survives is settlement examinations, removal orders, bastardy bonds, quarter sessions depositions, workhouse admissions — in other words, someone was a problem. So the only vivid quotable individual humans in my sources are vivid and quotable precisely because they were unusual enough, or in enough trouble, that a clerk had to write them down. And then you read the local histories and everyone's account of "ordinary" village life is built out of those people. There's a weaver in my parish who turns up in four seperate documents because of a long-running row over a footpath and an apprentice, and he's basically the protagonist of two books about the town, and I promise you he was not a typical weaver.

Second thing, and this is the one I actually can't name. I raised all this with my supervisor and she said it's fine and normal, because an exceptional case reveals the norm by showing where the norm broke — the deviation traces the outline of the rule, the footpath row shows what counted as a legitimate claim, and so on. I sat there nodding. Then on the walk home I realised I couldn't think of a single case that *wouldn't* be describable that way. Any record at all can be written up as "the exception that shows the rule," and there doesn't seem to be any way to say which deviations are informative about the norm and which are just noise, or to say in advance what a case would have to look like for that inference to be licensed. So the reassuring move always succeeds, and that's exactly what makes me suspiscious of it.

So: is there an established concept for either of these, and especially for the second one? I've got fragments floating around — survivorship bias, reading against the grain, something about the "exceptional normal"? — but none of them feel like the thing I mean, and I don't want to grab the nearest label and get taken apart in the viva.

What I want:

1. Several nearest established concepts, and for each one exactly how it differs from what I described and where it stops short. The carving matters way more to me than the label — what the phenomenon is, what it isn't, where the edges are. For the second thing specifically I want a straight answer: is "the deviation reveals the norm" a recognised inference with recognised failure conditions somewhere, or is it just unfalsifiability wearing a cardigan?
2. If nothing really fits, say so plainly and coin me a term — with an actual definition, plus at least two boundary cases: things that look like it but aren't, and why they aren't.
3. Please no reading list and no citations, I can't check anything and I don't want invented ones. Just tell me which subfield or tradition each term lives in (historiography? philosophy of social science? statistics? case-study methodology?) so I know whose vocabulary I'd be borrowing and whether I'd sound like an idiot using it in a history department.

For part 1, give me the candidates as a JSON array, one object per candidate term, with exactly these fields:

- "term": the name as people actually write it
- "home_field": which discipline or tradition it comes from
- "what_it_says": one or two sentences, plain English, no jargon
- "fit": one of "good" / "partial" / "near_miss"
- "gap": the specific bit of my description that the term doesn't cover
- "which_instance": does it address the first thing (documented-because-problematic sources), the second thing (deviation reveals the norm), or both

After the JSON, ordinary prose is fine for everything else.
