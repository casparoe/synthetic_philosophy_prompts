I'm contributing a piece to an interdisciplinary anthology that pairs fiction writers with social scientists, and the theme of this volume is impossibility results. My assigned subject is the Gibbard–Satterthwaite theorem, though I'd rather the title and the text never name it. The editors have asked each contributor to produce a story that a general reader can follow without any mathematics, while still leaving a specialist satisfied that the underlying result has been honored rather than merely name-checked. So I need the theorem's content to live in the machinery of the plot — in what people try, what breaks, and what they discover they must give up — rather than in any character's mouth. Please write that story: between 2,000 and 2,500 words, a single continuous narrative rather than fragments or vignettes, in close third person, with an identifiable protagonist whose job gives her reason to care about the outcome.

Here is the seeding I'd like you to build on, and please treat it as raw material rather than a checklist to satisfy point by point. The setting is a river-basin authority whose board has to fix the sites of three new flood-control structures after a bad season; the sites matter to different towns in the basin differently, and the towns have learned to organize themselves into blocs. The authority uses a ranked-ballot rule, written by a predecessor who prided himself on having designed an honest procedure — one where nobody gains by lying on the ballot, so that a member's true ranking is always a safe thing to submit. That claim was, unbeknownst to him, the whole achievement of his rule, and the story begins when someone demonstrates in public that it is false. I'd like the plot to run through the natural sequence of attempted repairs: first the search for some alternative ranked-ballot procedure that keeps the honesty and still gives every site a genuine chance and hands nobody unilateral control; then, when that fails, the temptation to shrink the menu so that only two options remain; then the realization that a two-option procedure is honest only by excluding something that people want, or by letting one voice decide; and finally the escape hatch of deciding by lottery, which is honest in the relevant sense but which the board cannot bring itself to accept, because somebody's fields will be under water and a coin will have chosen them. No character should ever state the theorem, use the word strategy-proof, or explain the result to another character; what I want instead is a scene in which the protagonist understands, without being told, that the failure is not a defect of this particular rule or of these particular people, and that the choice she now faces is only about which constraint she is willing to abandon.

Two further requests about handling. First, keep the emotional center somewhere other than triumph or despair: I'd like the ending to turn on a small administrative act — a memo, a signature, a line struck from a standing order — that commits the authority to a specific sacrifice, and on the protagonist's awareness of exactly what she has traded away. Second, please don't resolve the tension by suggesting that a cleverer designer, a better-intentioned electorate, or a future technology will escape the problem; the anthology's whole premise is that the constraint is structural, and a story that hints at a workaround will read as a failure of nerve to the specialists. You may invent names, towns, river geography, procedural history, and secondary characters as you like, and you should feel free to depart from my seeding wherever a better story asks you to, so long as the result is one that a reader who knows the theorem would recognize as being about it.
