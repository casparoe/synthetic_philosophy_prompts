I chair a fortnightly jurisprudence programme, and next week's guest is a legal philosopher whose work defends a particular reading of Fuller. Her thesis, briefly, is this: the eight desiderata of the internal morality of law are not moral standards that a body of rules must satisfy in order to earn the name of law, but constitutive standards of a distinctive kind of relationship between ruler and ruled. They specify what it is to govern someone by law rather than to manage, command or simply overpower him, in something like the way that the norms of teaching specify what it is to teach a person rather than to condition or indoctrinate him. On this account the moral interest of legality does not lie in the ends that law makes it easier to pursue; it lies in the fact that ruling by law necessarily addresses the subject as an agent capable of understanding and following directions, and thereby generates the reciprocity Fuller insisted upon. She consequently holds that Hart's reply to The Morality of Law — that the desiderata are neutral as to ends, and that a poisoner might observe principles of his craft no less exacting than a physician's — rests on a misunderstanding, since it treats as instrumental standards that are constitutive.

Please draft nine interview questions for the recording. I want them ordered so that the conversation builds, and I want each one accompanied by the answer you judge she is most likely to give, followed by a second-order question keyed to that answer rather than to a strawman. The questions must turn on the content of her position and on the pressure points the Hart–Fuller literature actually applies to it. Generic material — what drew her to jurisprudence, how she would summarise the internal morality of law for a general audience — is of no use to me.

Specifically, I need at least one question, with its follow-up, on each of the following:

1. Whether the constitutive reading quietly surrenders Hart's central claim. If the desiderata tell us what law is rather than what law ought to be, and if a formally impeccable but monstrous code is still law, has she not conceded the separability thesis and turned Fuller into a positivist with unusual emphases?

2. The status of reciprocity. Is it a descriptive claim about the relationship law establishes, or a normative claim about obligations owed to subjects? Ask her to say which, and press on whether the other horn is then available to her — either reciprocity explains nothing, or it smuggles back the moralised reading she says Hart misdescribed.

3. The grudge informer. The post-war German courts had before them a regime whose statutes were frequently general, published, prospective and intelligible; the failures were concentrated in congruence and in retroactive curing of irregularities. Does her reading reproduce Fuller's conclusion about what those courts should have said, and can it explain why the failure in congruence should be decisive where the other desiderata were largely satisfied?

4. Degrees of failure. Fuller holds that failure in any one desideratum can come in degrees, and that a total failure in one produces not a bad legal system but no legal system at all. Where, on her account, is the bright line, and what does she say about a regime that publishes clear, prospective, congruently enforced rules while simultaneously governing through unpublished directives — apartheid South Africa being the case usually reached for?

5. The poisoner, restated. Does she take Hart's objection to be simply wrong, or right but beside the point? And can she dispose of the version that survives her reading: a criminal organisation that governs its own members by published, clear, prospective and consistently enforced codes, which on her account would stand in the relation of lawgiver to subject with those members.

6. Collapse into rivals. What distinguishes her constitutive account from Dworkin's interpretivism, from the view that the rule of law is a virtue a legal system may possess to a greater or lesser degree without that affecting its legal character, and from a purely functional account of legality as an efficient technique of social control? Ask her to name the work that remains distinctively Fullerian once the moralised reading is abandoned.

7. Textual pressure. There are passages in The Morality of Law in which Fuller speaks of the desiderata as a morality without qualification, and the Rex parable appears to be about a well-meaning legislator's incompetence rather than about the conditions of a relationship. Does her reading accommodate the way Fuller actually writes, or does it require us to treat his moral vocabulary as loose?

8. The penumbra. Hart thought the interesting jurisprudential work was done at the margins of general terms, where no settled answer exists. Does the constitutive reading have anything at all to say about adjudication in such cases, and if it is silent, is that silence a cost?

One further matter of technique. When cornered, she tends to respond that her critic is assuming the picture of law as a one-way projection of authority from ruler to subject — the picture Fuller was writing against — and to relocate the disagreement there rather than answer it. Please mark, for each question, whether it is one she is likely to handle comfortably or one that will genuinely put her under pressure, and construct at least one question in the second half of the sequence that cannot be answered by that relocation, because it is already framed in terms of the relationship between lawgiver and subject. Close the set with a question about what she would accept as counting against her reading: what textual, historical or conceptual finding would persuade her that the constitutive interpretation is mistaken rather than merely incomplete.

Do not include a bibliography, a reading list or any citations; I need the questions themselves, not pointers to the secondary literature.
