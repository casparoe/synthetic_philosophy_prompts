I am preparing a session for our department's graduate decision-theory reading group, and I have been working from a trade book on rational choice whose treatment of the absent-minded driver problem I now distrust but cannot properly locate the trouble in. I would be grateful for an audit.

The case, as the book sets it up (and as it appears in the game-theoretic literature on imperfect recall, where it originated): a driver must leave a highway at the second of two exits, which are indistinguishable to him. Taking the first exit pays 0; taking the second pays 4; driving past both strands him at a hotel for a payoff of 1. At an exit he cannot tell which one it is and cannot remember whether he has passed one already. So before setting out, sitting in a pub, he chooses a plan: at each exit he will pull off with probability p, whichever exit it turns out to be. That plan has expected value (1−p)(1 + 3p), which is maximized at p = 1/3 and yields 4/3.

Here is the book's paragraph on what the case means:

"The driver's predicament is vivid proof that expected utility theory breaks down the moment an agent forgets. In the pub he calculates that he ought to take the second exit with probability one, and, unable to do that, settles for pulling off at random one time in three; but this calculation is idle, because when he actually arrives at an exit he is no longer choosing a strategy, he is choosing whether to pull off, and by symmetry he should take it to be equally likely that this is the first exit or the second. On those odds, pulling off now has an expected payoff of 2, and driving on has less, so what to do is plain: exit. The driver is, in other words, dynamically inconsistent — his preferences shift as he goes along, exactly as they do when someone resolves to diet and then eats the cake. What the case shows is that only the decision taken at the moment of action is real; the plan drawn up in the pub is a fiction, and any theory that evaluates agents by their plans rather than by what they do at the choice point is evaluating something that never happens."

Three things, in order.

First, go through the paragraph claim by claim — including its arithmetic — and tell me what a decision theorist working on imperfect recall would accept, what is a tolerable simplification for a general reader, and what is simply wrong. Where a defect is a conflation of two claims the literature keeps apart, or an inflation of a modest thesis into a sweeping one, say which, and say what the modest thesis was. I am especially unsure whether the passage uses 'dynamically inconsistent' in anything like its technical sense.

Second, does the book's headline conclusion — that the planning-time calculation is idle and only the action-time decision counts — survive once the passage is corrected? Note that the book appears to get the junction-time verdict right: on its own numbers, exiting does look better than driving on. I want to know whether that verdict licenses the broader claim, and what a defender of the two-problems reading would say in response to the book's last two sentences.

Third, I want to test the book's prescription on a variant of my own before I put it in front of the group. Suppose there are three indistinguishable exits rather than two, and the driver cannot tell them apart or remember passing one. Exiting at the first pays 0, at the second pays 0, at the third pays 9; driving past all three pays 1. As before, the only plan available to him in the pub fixes a single probability p of pulling off, applied at whichever exit he reaches. For this case: work out the best ex ante plan and its value; work out what the driver's odds ought to be, on finding himself at an exit, that it is the first, second, or third, and say what determines those odds; and then work out what the book's procedure — decide at the exit on the strength of your odds there — recommends, and what he actually ends up with if he adopts that recommendation as his standing policy from the outset rather than as a one-off. Does the procedure survive? If it fails here, what has to change in the description of the original two-exit case, and is there some way of stating the exit-time reasoning that does not fail?

Finally, if I raise the payoff for driving past all three exits from 1 to 5, does your diagnosis change? Show me the numbers either way.

I am inclined to think the plan is what matters, but I cannot say why the exit-time argument is not decisive, and I do not want to present the variant until I know which way it cuts. I am not looking for references or further reading — I want the case worked through from the description above.
