Two years ago, ten of us from the same graduate program formed a giving circle. We each pledged to set aside a tenth of our after-tax income every year for a fixed list of organizations — a bed-net distributor, a deworming program, an unconditional cash-transfer outfit — that we had researched together over several months and could each defend in some detail. The point of pooling was that reliable money arriving on a schedule would let three specific programs scale up in ways we had all been shown, in writing, that they otherwise could not.

I have made my transfer every quarter without missing one. So have three others. The remaining six have gone quiet: two because their incomes collapsed, and four, as far as I can tell, because they stopped bothering. The programs are now short by roughly what those six would have sent. A contact at one of them wrote last week to say the expansion they had budgeted for will not happen this year.

At the same time, I have been offered a buy-in to my partner's dental practice. It means taking on substantial debt now, but on any reasonable projection it roughly triples my income within six or seven years, after which I could send several times what I send today. Taking it would mean cutting my transfers to almost nothing for the next several years. Declining it would mean keeping my income where it is and sending the difference to close the gap the others left. The difficulty is that I know precisely what becomes of a pledge like ours over seven years, because I am living through a smaller version of it.

Two friends have taken opposite sides and neither will let the matter rest. One says the shortfall belongs to the six who walked away: I promised a tenth, I sent a tenth, and any account of morality that leaves me permanently liable for other people's failures has no floor under it — it would simply keep demanding more the worse everyone else behaved. The other says that is a comfortable fiction: a child who goes without a net is not consoled by the fact that somebody else also failed to pay, and if I can prevent something dreadful at a cost nowhere near as bad as the thing prevented, whose fault it is looks beside the point.

I want to know which of them is nearer the truth, and on what grounds. Please do not tell me to give what feels sustainable, or that thoughtful people differ. I want to know whether having sent my tenth is a complete answer or merely a way of declining to do the arithmetic — and whether the buy-in is a postponement I am entitled to, or a bet I already have evidence I will lose. If what other people gave does matter, I would also like to understand what fixes the relevant group: the ten of us, everyone in my bracket, everyone who could have helped at all. My obligation seems to swell or shrink depending on which circle I picture, and I cannot see what would make one of those circles the right one to picture rather than the comfortable one.
