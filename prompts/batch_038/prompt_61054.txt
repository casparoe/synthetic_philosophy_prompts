I have run a monthly reasoning-and-epistemology reading group for eleven years, populated largely by people who came up through the rationalist blogosphere the way I did — Overcoming Bias, LessWrong, the forecasting tournaments — almost none of whom has sat through a philosophy course since secondary school. Next session I want to teach the bootstrapping problem: that a person can apparently generate warrant for the claim that an instrument is reliable simply by using that instrument repeatedly, noting each time that its reading matches what they go on to find, and generalizing; and that this is meant to be a reductio of externalist accounts of justification, not merely a complaint about circularity. Seventy-five minutes, one whiteboard, twelve people who will want to know what would change my mind.

I am not asking you to explain the problem to me. I want the plan for teaching it.

The order of introduction, with the dependency stated at each step: what must be in place before the next piece can do any work. My guess is that the externalist picture of justification has to be installed, and installed sympathetically, before the argument has anything to bite on. Tell me where that guess is wrong, and what has to precede even it.

Which single case should carry the session. I have the familiar ones to hand — the gas gauge, the thermometer, the red table under white light — but tell me which should bear the weight, what that example must have and what it must conspicuously lack, and which of the others should be demoted to a variation or cut outright.

The misconceptions, in the order this group will produce them, and the precise point in the sequence at which each should be headed off. I expect four. That this is just circular reasoning, which everyone present has known to be illicit since middle school, so the argument is trivial. That the puzzle dissolves once we grant the gauge is faulty, when the sharp version is that the reasoning looks just as bad if the gauge is in fact perfectly reliable. That the fix is obviously to bring in a second, independent instrument. And a fourth specific to this room: several members build models for a living, and bootstrapping already denotes something else for them, so I expect at least two to spend the evening half-translating this into resampling, bagging, or train-set contamination. Tell me whether to use that vocabulary as a bridge, and if so which particular mapping is honest rather than merely flattering, or whether to close it down in the first three minutes and say explicitly why the collision matters.

One question I can put to the room at the end whose answers would separate those who grasped it from those who merely enjoyed it. It should not be answerable by restating the moral.

What to leave out at this level, and announce that I am leaving it out — I assume the closure and transmission-failure debates and the entitlement literature, but tell me what else, and what breaks in the explanation if I cut it.

Finally, a short note on transfer, because this group will press me on it within the week: whether keeping a calibration record by scoring your own questions against outcomes you yourself judged is a case of the same structure, or whether that analogy fails, and if it fails, where.

No readings, no bibliography, no pointers to further literature. I can find the papers myself.
