I co-facilitate a graduate reading group in formal epistemology, and next week's session is on Good's theorem: I. J. Good's result, usually discussed alongside the principle of total evidence, that a costless observation can never be expected to make a Bayesian expected-utility maximizer worse off. For the handout I want the theorem stated with its hypotheses explicit rather than as a slogan. In the Savage-style framework the proof assumes: a fixed set of acts; a partition of states over which the agent has a prior; a utility function on act–state pairs, such that learning which cell of the experimental partition obtains makes no difference to the utility of performing any act in any state; and an agent who, if she observes, updates by conditionalizing on the outcome and then selects an act maximizing her posterior expected utility. The conclusion follows because the expected utility of deciding now is a weighted average of the conditional expected utilities of the acts, while the expected utility of observing first is the corresponding weighted average of the maxima, and the maximum of an average never exceeds the average of the maxima. I want the session to be about where those hypotheses bite, and I would like two worked problems to hand out cold.

So please design two decision problems in each of which a perfectly free observation is rationally declined, and in each of which the agent satisfies every hypothesis above except one — a different one in each case. Hard constraints on both: realistic settings only (a workplace, a clinic, a family, a market transaction), with no thought-experiment machinery; no preference for suspense, surprise, mystery, or ignorance as such, so no novels, games, wrapped presents, or unopened letters; no predictor, psychologist, or anyone else who has conditioned a payoff on the agent's disposition to observe; no akrasia, forgetfulness, or botched conditionalization; and no cost smuggled in as money, time, effort, or risk. In addition, at least one of the two cases must leave the utility-independence hypothesis intact, so that the decline has to come from somewhere else — for instance from the observing itself having causal consequences for the states, or from the identity or availability of the acts shifting once something is learned. The bending should be subtle enough that a careful reader initially accepts the problem as meeting all the hypotheses; finding the flaw is the exercise, so please keep the flaw discoverable rather than signposted in the scenario text.

For each case, give me: (a) the scenario, at most a paragraph; (b) an explicit numerical table showing the expected utility of deciding now and the expected utility of observing first, computed by averaging over the possible outcomes, with the reversal visible in the arithmetic rather than asserted; (c) the identification of exactly which hypothesis fails and at which step of the proof the inequality breaks; and (d) a redescription — how a decision analyst defending the theorem would reformulate the problem (a richer consequence space, a different individuation of the acts, an enlarged state partition) so that the theorem applies and correctly scores the observation as non-negative, together with your verdict on whether that redescription settles the matter or only relocates it. Close with a comparative judgment: which of the two is the better teaching case, and what the pair jointly suggests about the interest of the theorem — whether it is a substantive constraint on rational choice or a result whose hypotheses have already guaranteed its conclusion. A case that simply stipulates that the agent would rather not know, without generating that preference from the structure of the problem, counts as a failure, and so does one whose hidden flaw is that observing costs something after all.
