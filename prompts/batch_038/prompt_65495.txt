I spent forty years in experimental physics, most of it in metrology — calibration, uncertainty budgets, blind-analysis protocols — and came to philosophy only after retiring. I have run into an institutional puzzle I cannot settle with the habits of my trade, and I should like a careful answer to it.

The practice is the secret ballot as actually administered in most democracies. Concretely: the state prints a uniform ballot paper; the voter marks it alone in a booth; in some systems each paper bears a serial number and a sealed list is kept linking that number to the voter's entry on the register, so that the link exists but can be opened only by a court in narrowly specified circumstances; and in many jurisdictions it is a criminal offence for the voter to disclose how she voted or to photograph her marked ballot, though some such bans have been struck down by courts on free-speech grounds. Meanwhile the same election is audited to a remarkable degree downstream: agents of the parties watch the count, paper records are preserved for recounts, and designers of electronic voting systems build elaborate machinery so that anyone may verify the tally while no voter can obtain a receipt proving how she voted — a property they distinguish from the stronger requirement that a voter be unable to prove her vote even to an adversary who interacts with her at the polling place.

Set against this, in the same polity: legislators' votes are recorded and published, judges sign their opinions, officials file public asset declarations. And in the laboratory, blinding runs in the opposite direction and is always temporary — we hide the hypothesis or the group allocation from the observer to protect the measurement, and we keep the records so that the blind can be lifted afterwards. The secret ballot is permanent blinding, and it blinds the record rather than the observer: it destroys the trace of the reasons for which a decisive judgment was made.

What does this practice presuppose about the vote? I want the implicit theory reconstructed, not a civics summary. In particular:

1. Does it presuppose that voting is a right the voter holds for her own sake, or a public function she discharges on behalf of others? Mill thought the ballot teaches the first and that this is a moral mischief, writing that "the suffrage is given to him for himself; for his particular use and benefit, and not as a trust for the public." Is he right that secrecy commits us to the possession view, or does secrecy sit equally well with a trust view?

2. Does it presuppose that votes are preferences to be aggregated rather than judgments to be assessed by their reasons — and hence that legitimacy attaches to the procedure rather than to the quality of the reasoning behind each mark?

3. Which rival views does it rule out — open voting, a competence condition on the franchise, a requirement that the voter state a reason — and which does it merely sit uncomfortably beside?

4. Is the split between maximal auditability of the count and radical un-auditability of the judgment coherent, or do the two halves presuppose inconsistent things? Bear in mind that we already keep identity-linked records at the polling station, and require identification in some places, so the anonymity is not absolute.

5. Where is the practice philosophically underdetermined — licensed equally by an instrumental account, on which secrecy is a device for defeating coercion and vote-buying, and by a non-instrumental one, on which it protects an inner forum or marks equal standing? I want to know what the practice strictly requires as against what merely fits it.

6. What would have to change — in law, in the design of the ballot, in what we ask of voters — if the presupposed view were replaced by a trust-based or a competence-based one? Say which change would be least tolerable, and what that reveals about what we in fact believe.

Throughout, please distinguish what the practice entails from what it merely suggests, and say where you think the evidence is equivocal. Close with one final line in exactly this form:

Verdict: <possession | trust | both | neither> — followed by no more than fifteen words naming the commitment that explains why we audit the count but never the vote.
