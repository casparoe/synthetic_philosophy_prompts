I am guest-setting this year's final for a fourth-year seminar in formal epistemology — fourteen students, closed book, no calculators, all of them through one term of probability theory and one term of decision theory. My own formation was on the rationalist blogs and their forums, where the Dutch book argument was treated less as a topic than as a settled result: a short proof that incoherent credences are irrational, full stop. Several of these students have exactly that formation and will walk into the exam able to recite that violating the probability axioms makes you vulnerable to a sure loss without ever having written a single book down on paper. I want one question, seventy-five minutes, thirty marks, that makes the arithmetic do the philosophical work and that a student who has only absorbed the popular presentation cannot pass by naming the theorem. The question must be entirely self-contained: it should state its own betting conventions on the page, including the convention for conditional bets that are called off when the antecedent fails, and it should demand no outside reading, no citations, and no author names.

Here is the design brief. Part A concerns an agent, Ren, whose opinion set is generated by two propositions A and E. Her unconditional credences over the four atoms are cr(A∧E) = 0.21, cr(A∧¬E) = 0.49, cr(¬A∧E) = 0.10, cr(¬A∧¬E) = 0.20 — jointly coherent, summing to one — but her betting quotient for a bet on E that is called off unless A obtains is 0.60 rather than the 0.30 her unconditional credences imply. I want students asked to write down an explicit portfolio of bets, transacted at Ren's own quoted prices, that costs her the same nonzero amount of money in every possible world; to give the payoff table across the three relevant worlds; and to state the size of that guaranteed loss. The numbers should be chosen so the arithmetic is clean enough to do in one's head, and if you use a stake of a hundred dollars the loss should come out integral. Then I want them asked which premises the construction actually needs, and here is the point I want the question to force: each of the three bets, taken alone, is exactly fair at Ren's own prices, so the exploitability lives entirely in the aggregation. Students should have to say what that does to the inference from "she can be made to lose money for sure" to "her beliefs are inconsistent," and in particular what bridge premise is required to treat a package-level disposition as a fact about belief rather than about choice.

Part B concerns a second agent, Dana, facing a lottery with countably many tickets numbered 1, 2, 3, and so on, exactly one of which will win. Let H_i be the proposition that ticket i wins, and let D be their countable disjunction. Dana's prices are those of the finite–cofinite zero-one measure: she prices each H_i at 0, every finite disjunction of H_i's at 0, and D at 1. I want three sub-questions. First, an argument — not an assertion — that no finite portfolio of bets at Dana's prices can guarantee her a net loss. Second, an explicit countably infinite portfolio that does guarantee her a loss, with the payoff verified. Third, an explanation of why every finite truncation of that portfolio fails to be a sure loss, and hence of where exactly the bookie's profit comes from. The crux I want the question to turn on is that the damaging portfolio is built entirely out of bets that are, by Dana's own lights, at worst break-even, and that its profit is a limit phenomenon; if the students see that the recipe which books a finite additivity violation goes through here unchanged once one is permitted to plug in a countable disjunction, they have the material for Part C. Part C should then be short and reflective: whether the sure-loss criterion is one criterion or a family of them indexed to a class of admissible portfolios, whether Dana is irrational, and how a defender of Dana should answer the charge that her opinions cannot be jointly correct given that she is certain some ticket wins while pricing each candidate ticket at zero.

For deliverables, I want the question exactly as the students will see it, with the mark allocation per part printed on it; a model answer of roughly a thousand to twelve hundred words including both payoff tables; and a marking scheme that separates a first-class answer from a merely good one by naming the specific moves each must contain, rather than by listing topics touched. Alongside that, give me the errors that should cost marks, what each costs, and — this matters to me — which of those errors the blog-formed students are most likely to commit, since I expect the modal failure to be a confident restatement of the Dutch book theorem accompanied by a portfolio that does not actually balance. Also tell me where the model answer is genuinely contestable and where I should award full credit for a well-argued heterodox response, including a student who defends Dana on principled grounds; I have no interest in marking for agreement. Finally, if you think any part of my setup is defective — if the numbers fail to produce the effect I have described, if one of the parts has a trivial standard solution that I have not noticed, or if Part B's second sub-question is unanswerable in the time available — say so explicitly at the top of your reply and give me the repaired version, rather than quietly working around it.
