I'm putting together a session on function talk in biology and I want a case built to order, not the usual textbook standbys. No hearts, no kidneys, no wings, no photosynthesis, no sickle cells, nothing about a watch on a heath. The point of the case is to test whether the two big accounts can ever disagree about something that shows up downstream, rather than only about which sentence you'd write when describing a trait.

Here's what the scenario has to make come out — one trait, one organism, one moment in time:

- the trait is fully intact and working; no malfunction story, nothing broken, vestigial, or half-degraded;
- a selected-effects / etiological theorist (Wright, Neander, Millikan) has to ascribe it a function the trait does not currently perform at all, because the conditions that made that effect selectable have vanished from the environment — though the trait would still perform it if those conditions came back;
- a causal-role theorist (Cummins) has to ascribe it a different function, one the trait does currently perform and that is genuinely load-bearing for some capacity of a containing system, but which was never itself a selected effect — it falls out of the trait's incidental promiscuity;
- and the two ascriptions have to cash out differently in a decision somebody actually has to make — a knockout, or what goes in the function field of a database entry — so the disagreement isnt purely descriptive.

Constraints: real-world biology, no sci-fi, no engineered organisms, and nothing that leans on anyone's intentions or design plans, since that just smuggles in an artifact account. The trait has to be old, with a long selective history, so "it has no etiology at all" isn't an available escape. Be plausible about population genetics — say what is keeping the trait in the population now, and it should not be selection on the current contribution. Also build in at least two defensible candidates for the containing system whose capacity is being explained, so the causal-role theorist has to choose. Scenario itself one paragraph, under 250 words.

Then the demonstration, which is the part I actually care about. State each account's definition before you apply it, and derive the verdicts from those definitions rather than from my labels — if either account comes out differently than I predicted, say so, that's a better result than a case that just does what I asked. Tell me which single feature of the case is doing the work, and run two removal tests: (a) reintroduce heritable variation so the current contribution starts being selected on, and (b) put the ancestral conditions back in the environment. Which removal collapses the case into agreement, which one sharpens it, and why?

Finish with the strongest reply each side has — the interest-relativity move, the modern-history move, whatever else is on the table — and an honest verdict on whether the dispute has gone merely verbal by the end. If it has, say what residue is left that isnt verbal. And flag anywhere you're leaning on real biology instead of your own stipulations, so I know what to double-check.
