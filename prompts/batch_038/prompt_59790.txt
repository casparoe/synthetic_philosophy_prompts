I'm a third-year philosophy PhD student working on the Peirce–James row about truth, and I run a small reading group where a fully signed-up Peircean (roughly: truth is the opinion the community of inquirers is destined to settle on in the long run, and reality is fixed by that ideal final verdict) and a fully signed-up Jamesian (roughly: truth is what pays off experientially, beliefs earn it by guiding us satisfactorily, ideas get made true in the course of being verified) have been talking past eachother for about a year. Textbook case of two camps making noises at one another. I want to force them to produce the document they should have written ages ago: a joint statement from an adversarial collaboration they'd both put their names to.

What I need from you:

1. A numbered list of claims both would genuinely sign — about what the pragmatic maxim is doing, about what they jointly reject (correspondence as mirroring, the inert spectator picture of truth), about how far verification carries weight for each. Not vacuous filler though. If it turns out the only thing they can both sign is 'truth matters', then say that's all there is and say why, don't pad it out.

2. The residual disagreements, each worded so that the relevant side would accept your phrasing as a fair statement of its own view. At least four. For each one, say whether it's substantive or merely verbal. My own hunch is that a chunk of the fight is really about whether 'true' is being used univocally at all, but I want that pinned down rather than asserted in one line.

3. The cruxes — the sub-questions such that settling them would actually move at least one of the two. For each crux, name a concrete case or argument both agree bears on it, and write down each side's prediction in advance, pre-registration style, so nobody gets to reinterpret later.

Seed it with the three cases my group keeps tripping over, and make each camp commit to a verdict on each of them rather than waffling:

(a) a pebble that fell into a lava flow 200 million years ago, which either was or wasn't striated before it melted — no trace survives, none ever could, no record, no witness, nothing that any inquiry however long could bear on;

(b) a claim about events after the last possible observer and record-keeper has ceased to exist, say about some final proton decaying somewhere. Convergence needs inquiry to keep going indefinitely, and cosmology may not promise that — so what happens to truth if the community has an endpoint, and is that a problem for one side, both, or neither?

(c) a live belief that evidence simply cannot settle but which reorganises someone's entire conduct. Does its working make it true, make it true-for-that-person, or leave its truth untouched?

Also: wherever a side has to bite a bullet, name the bullet explicitly and say which bite costs them more — I don't want a document where both come out smelling of roses. No citations, no quotes, no reading list please; I want the formulations, not the scholarship, and I'll go back to the primary texts myself.

Whole thing in British English, cheers.
