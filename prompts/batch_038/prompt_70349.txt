I run the analytics team inside a national benefits-integrity unit — the office that sits between the policy directorate and the investigation service, and whose job is to decide, in operational terms, which mispayments are worth pursuing and which households get referred for review. My working vocabulary is entirely that of a classification problem: a claimant population with some base rate of genuine overpayment; screening models that emit scores rather than verdicts; precision and recall traded off along a curve; a cost matrix in which a false positive means someone wrongly required to repay or wrongly sanctioned and a false negative means public money left uncollected; and the fairness constraints our equality-impact assessments impose on us, which force a choice between demographic parity in referral rates, equalized odds in detection, and calibration within groups. We are retraining the triage model that routes cases to investigation this quarter, and a colleague in policy keeps telling me that the argument over whether we should be routing on desert at all is not an argument my metrics can even represent. I suspect she is right, and I cannot see why. I am fluent in my own framework and in very little else; my philosophy amounts to one undergraduate course taken some fifteen years ago.

What I want is a faithful restatement of the philosophical debate over the "undeserving poor" in the vocabulary above. The debate as I understand it includes: the reciprocity-based defence of conditionality, on which benefits must be earned or matched by obligation rather than simply held as an entitlement; the case for work requirements as something other than a labour-supply instrument; the contention that fraud is a moral and political category rather than an accounting one, so that the official splitting of overpayments into fraud, claimant error, and administrative error already does normative work; the claim that sanctions deter and are therefore justified on incentive grounds; the counter-claim that sanctions mostly redistribute hardship onto people least able to satisfy the conditions attached to them; and the argument that stigma, paperwork burden, and non-take-up are deliberate design features rather than implementation failures. Take six to eight central claims from across that terrain, one at a time, and render each as precisely as my framework allows: what is the population, what is the label and who gets to set it, who is the decision-maker, and what loss function is the claim endorsing or rejecting? Where a claim invokes desert, reciprocity, dignity, or stigma, say explicitly whether that becomes a parameter, a constraint, a term in the objective, or nothing at all. No references, no reading list, no names — I want the translation, not the scholarship behind it.

Then give me the residue report, which is the part I actually care about. Sort your renderings into three buckets: claims that survive the translation intact; claims that survive only with distortion, where you name the distortion and describe exactly what it costs; and claims that have no expression in my framework whatsoever. For the second and third buckets, assign the fault. Is my toolkit missing something — for instance, is there genuinely no way to express the thought that the ground-truth label is constituted by the policy that defines it, or that a regime can satisfy equalized odds and still be humiliating, or that a loss function presupposes a theory of who the losses belong to? Or does the failure to translate show that some of these claims were less determinate than they sounded, in that once you force them to specify a population, a label, and an objective there is nothing substantive left to disagree about? Be scrupulous about not smuggling my own assumptions in and calling it translation: if a claim only becomes expressible after I quietly adopt a consequentialist objective or treat desert as a hidden variable to be inferred, say so, and say what was given up in the bargain. I would like the report to close with a short verdict on the two or three claims where translation most clearly fails, and — this matters — a specification of what would have to be added to my apparatus to make them expressible. A formal object, not a slogan: tell me what the extra structure is, what it takes as inputs, what it outputs, and why my existing quantities cannot be recombined to produce it.
