I TA a second-year epistemology course, 24 students, and I've got one 75-minute section on the structure of justification — the regress thing, foundationalism vs coherentism vs the view that says the chain of reasons just keeps going forever. I want an activity where they run into the problem themselves instead of me writing three labels on the board and defining them. They shouldn't hear any of the names until the very end.

Here's what I'm after. Six groups of four. Each group gets a card with a totally mundane claim on it, the kind of thing nobody would ever normally bother justifying — e.g. "the 9:05 bus was the same bus as yesterday's," or "the department printer is out of toner rather than broken." Groups take turns defending their claim while the others attack, and the standing rule is that any reason offered can itself be met with a demand for a reason. I want you to design the actual economy around this: how many challenges each group gets, what it costs to stop, what counts as a claim being settled. The key thing is that stopping cant be free. If a group can just plant its feet and say "that's just how it is" with no penalty, half the class will decide the whole problem is fake within about four minutes.

Then I want a second round with a twist: a challenge is only legitimate if the challenger would accept the challenged reason as a reason in their own case. That's meant to make them feel the difference between two things I think students constantly run together — what a justification has to survive in a live argument, and what it takes for a belief to be justified when nobody's around asking questions. If you can build that distinction into the rules rather than into the debrief, do it; at minimum the debrief should pull it back out of what they just did.

Give me: full rules and materials (nothing that needs colour printing or buying), a minute-by-minute timeline for the 75, a debrief that maps specific moments from the two rounds onto the three positions before naming them, a list of what'll predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to handle it — I'm bracing for one group turning it into an endless "why?" bit and eating the clock, and for some extremely confident kid winning round one with "because I saw it" — and finally an honest paragraph on which feature of the problem the activity cant reproduce and I'll just have to tell them about. I suspect the time limit is the big one: the go-on-forever view isn't claiming anyone can utter infinitely many reasons out loud, so the game may make it look far stupider than it actually is.

Be straight with me about the setup, though. If you think this is a bad idea — if a structural question about justification can't really be turned into something people feel through a parlour game, or if my live-argument-vs- solitary-believer distinction is muddled and the second round won't do what I want it to — say that up front and tell me what you'd do instead. I'd much rather hear that than get a well-behaved activity that doesn't work.
