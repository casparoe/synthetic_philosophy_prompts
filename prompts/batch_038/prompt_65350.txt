For twenty-nine years I worked as a property-and-casualty claims adjuster, and most of what I did was sit in living rooms and body shops listening to three or four perfectly sincere people describe the same collision in ways that could not all be true. I have no academic training of any kind, but since retiring I have been reading around that problem on my own, and I came across a result in decision theory associated with Robert Aumann to the effect that two rational people who began from the same basic beliefs about the world, and who each knew what the other believed, could not honestly remain in disagreement once they compared notes: whatever disagreement remained would have to be traced to differences in the information they held rather than in their starting points. The assumption that they start from the same basic beliefs is apparently called the common prior assumption, and I gather a good many people think it is doing a great deal of unexamined work.

The film I want to hold up against it is Kurosawa's *Rashomon* (1950), which I have watched perhaps a dozen times and which draws on two short stories by Akutagawa Ryūnosuke. A samurai is murdered in a bamboo grove, and we are given four irreconcilable first-person accounts of the killing: from the bandit Tajōmaru, from the wife, from the dead samurai himself speaking through a medium, and finally from the woodcutter, who admits under the gate that he watched the whole thing happen and lied in court about it. All of this is framed by the woodcutter, a monk, and a sneering commoner sheltering from the rain in a ruined gatehouse, and the film ends with an abandoned baby, the commoner stripping the child of its clothes, and the woodcutter — destitute, with children of his own already to feed — carrying the infant home to raise.

Please write me a reading of the film through that agreement result and its common prior assumption. I do not want a summary of either, and I do not want a reading list. Give me exactly five numbered points, each a sentence or two, with no introduction and no conclusion, addressing in this order:

1. Whether the four accounts are really four answers to one question, or whether some of them are answering something else entirely — and what either answer does to the idea that these witnesses share the same evidence.
2. Which condition of the argument the film genuinely strains: the absence of a shared prior, the fact that each witness holds private evidence the others never had, the fact that none of them ever meets or cross-examines another so that they lack common knowledge of one another's beliefs, or the possibility that they are not truth-directed agents at all.
3. The commoner's insistence that men lie to themselves without knowing they are doing it, which the film seems to bear out — and whether an argument that models belief as honest updating on evidence has anything left to say about people like that, or whether it simply loses its grip.
4. The ending: whether the woodcutter's decision to take the baby is offered as an alternative way of settling the disagreement — trust enacted rather than testimony weighed — and whether that amounts to a philosophical proposal or is a sentimental exit from one.
5. Where the reading breaks down. Tell me plainly what in the film resists being treated as a case about agreement and priors, and whether a careful critic ought to conclude that the picture is not about knowledge in the relevant sense at all.

Two constraints. Do not quote the film's dialogue, since subtitle versions differ and I have no way of checking you; describe the scene you mean instead. And treat *Rashomon* as a film rather than as a thought experiment with actors in it: the fact that we never see the grove except through somebody's telling is part of what it is saying, and a reading that flattens the movie into an illustration of the theorem, or that stretches the theorem until it fits the movie, would not be worth my time.
