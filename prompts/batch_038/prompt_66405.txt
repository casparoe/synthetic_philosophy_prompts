Our epistemology reading group got to the Meno's question — why is knowledge worth more than mere true belief — and then hit the swamping problem, and I tried writing the argument out clean to check I had it. Now it looks like it proves way more then the thing we were assigned.

(1) If the only thing good about X is that X reliably produces something good, then once you have that good thing, X's reliability isn't adding anything to it.

(2) The usual example: two cups of coffee, indistinguishable, one from a machine that gets it right every morning and one from a machine that fluked it. As coffee they're identical. The reliable machine didn't make this cup better.

(3) For the reliabilist, what separates knowledge from true belief is just that the belief came out of a reliable process.

(4) So by (1) and (3), reliability adds nothing to the belief you end up with. Knowledge is worth no more than true belief — which is Meno's worry with the reliabilist answer already ruled out.

(5) But notice (1) isn't about belief at all, it's about any reliable producer, and all that ever exists is particular outputs: this cup, this statement, this diagnosis, this crossing. So my friend's honesty adds nothing to the true things she tells me, my doctor's track record adds nothing to the correct diagnosis she just gave me, good engineering adds nothing to the bridge holding up right now. Every disposition gets swamped by its own outputs.

(6) That's nuts. I'd take the honest friend over the one who happened to tell the truth once, and the doctor with the record over the one who guessed right, and I don't think I'm confused about that.

(7) But if I deny (1) to save (6), I've also killed (4), and then I have no idea what the swamping problem was ever supposed to show or why anyone found it compelling in the first place.

I'd most like to keep (2) and (6). The coffee thing seems impossible to argue with, and so does preferring the reliable doctor. So: which step goes, and what do you pay for dropping it? And is there any way to keep swamping as a problem for reliabilism specifically without it snowballing into a proof that reliability is never worth anything beyond the good it happens to produce?
