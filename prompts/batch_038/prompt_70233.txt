The question as set was:

Question 3 (25 marks).
(a) Explain Strawson's distinction between reactive attitudes and objective attitudes, together with his account of the conditions under which the objective attitude is adopted. (10 marks)
(b) Consider the following case. For two years, Ravi has systematically taken credit for Dana's research and undermined her in departmental meetings. Dana resents him. She then learns from a mutual friend that Ravi was raised by a father who humiliated him for every failure, and that Ravi is, in the friend's phrase, unable to let anyone outshine him. Dana's resentment does not lessen. Is her resentment appropriate on Strawson's account, and does what she has learned change what that account tells her? Explain whether this is a case of exemption or of something else, and say what would have to be true of Ravi for Strawson's account to deliver the answer you give. (15 marks)

The word limit was 500. The script I want marked is:

Strawson begins from the observation that the free will debate has gone round in circles because both sides assume that the truth or falsity of determinism would settle whether moral responsibility is possible. His way out is to look at the attitudes we actually take up toward other people, and he divides them into reactive attitudes and objective attitudes.

The reactive attitudes are those we feel toward others as participants in ordinary human relationships: resentment when someone shows us disregard or contempt, gratitude when they show us more good will than we had any reason to expect, hurt feelings, forgiveness, and, in the self-directed cases, guilt and remorse. They have impersonal analogues as well: indignation, or moral disapprobation, is what we feel when the disregard is shown not to us but to a third party. Strawson's key claim is that these attitudes embody a demand that others show us a reasonable degree of good will. To feel them at all is to see the other as a member of the moral community, as someone whose attitude toward me means something.

The objective attitude is what we take up when we stop seeing the other as a participant and see them instead as an object of social policy: something to be managed, treated, trained, avoided or cured. We adopt it toward small children, toward people with severe mental illness or significant intellectual disability, and temporarily toward anyone under extraordinary strain. Strawson's point is that these are exemptions and not the rule. We cannot live inside the objective attitude, because doing so would cost us the ordinary warmth of human relationships, and that cost would be more than we could bear.

Applied to Dana, what she learns does change things. Ravi's behaviour is now fully explained by his history, so we can see that it was caused in the relevant sense, and Strawson's central idea is precisely that when we see behaviour as caused we shift toward the objective attitude. Dana should therefore come to see Ravi as someone damaged rather than as someone who has wronged her, and to respond accordingly — by keeping her distance, perhaps, or by encouraging him into therapy. Her continuing resentment is understandable but inappropriate, because resentment presupposes that Ravi could have done otherwise, and once she has the explanation she no longer has that.

The same goes for the head of department's indignation when Dana reports the pattern. Indignation is already an objective attitude, since the head is not personally involved and views the two of them impartially; this is why formal procedures feel colder than personal confrontation.

Strawson's position is thus a compatibilist one, though distinctive: he thinks our practices of holding one another responsible are vindicated by the value of the relationships they make possible, so the truth of determinism cannot undermine them. What he adds is an explanation of why we could not simply decide to give up our reactive attitudes even if we accepted this.
