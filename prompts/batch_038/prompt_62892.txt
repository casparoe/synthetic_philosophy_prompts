I would like to work out what principle, if any, unifies my intuitions about whether the physical scale of a nervous system bears on moral status. I have been collecting verdicts rather than arguments, and I suspect they are inconsistent. Here they are, stated as concretely as I can manage.

1. Absolute mass, rejected. A whale's brain is several times the mass of a human brain, and I do not think a whale thereby has greater moral status than a person, or even straightforwardly equal standing in a one-to-one comparison.

2. Absolute mass, accepted. A mouse versus an elephant: I do think the elephant's interests count for more, and when I interrogate myself, part of the reason feels like the sheer difference in neural machinery.

3. Relative size. A capuchin's brain is a small fraction of a cow's, and I give the capuchin more weight. But my reason for preferring relative measures is that encephalization quotients and similar statistics are computed against an allometric baseline fitted to the very data they are then used to explain, which makes the baseline look arbitrary. So my verdict tracks a quantity I have independent reason to distrust.

4. Neuron counts, regionally weighted. I have read that roughly 97.5 percent of an elephant's neurons — something on the order of 251 billion out of 257 billion — are located in the cerebellum, and that the same holds in a milder form for the human brain, where most neurons are cerebellar rather than cortical. This made me want to say that what matters is neuron count in the cortex or pallium, not in the brain as a whole.

5. Organoids. Suppose a laboratory fuses cortical organoids until the culture contains more neurons than a mouse brain, with spontaneous electrical activity, no body, no sensory organs, and no behavior. I say this has no moral status whatsoever — not a diminished degree of status, but zero.

6. Distributed nervous systems. An octopus has a large share of its neurons in its arms, which learn locally and semi-autonomously. I nonetheless think an octopus has substantial moral status.

7. Within-species invariance. A person born with microcephaly and a brain a third of typical volume has, in my view, full and equal moral status.

8. Within-species variance. Yet I reduce status for an anencephalic newborn, and I reduce it somewhat for a person in the late stages of a dementing illness with marked cortical atrophy.

9. Persistent vegetative state after hypoxic injury, cortex largely destroyed, brainstem intact: significantly diminished status — though I suspect my verdict here is driven by what remains of the person and of their relationships rather than by the tissue itself.

10. Hemispherectomy. A person who lost one cerebral hemisphere in childhood and functions surprisingly well has, for me, unchanged full status. This sits badly with the proposal in 4.

11. Small, dense brains. Corvids and parrots have very small brains but reportedly pack neurons into the pallium more densely than mammals do, and I give them more status than a rodent of comparable brain size. Consistent with 4.

12. Non-biological systems. A trained model with more parameters than any brain has neurons: I think its moral status is a genuinely open question rather than obviously zero, even though parameters are not neurons and there is no tissue at all.

The pairs that trouble me most are 4 with 5 (a count-based criterion that works in brains and fails in a dish), 7 with 8 (size is irrelevant within a species until it is not), and 5 with 12 (why the biological substrate seems to do all the work in one case and none in the other).

What I want from you:

- One principle, or a small set of competing candidates, stated precisely enough to generate verdicts — conditions or a scalar function, not a slogan to the effect that complexity is what matters.
- For each clause, the case above that motivates it.
- Whether the principle is a gate or a gradient: does it determine who counts at all, how much they count, or both, and if both, what relation holds between the two?
- Its verdicts on four cases I have not listed: a split-brain patient after corpus callosotomy; a vascularized cortical organoid attached to a body-like chassis that reliably withdraws from noxious stimuli; a dwarfed insular primate with an eight-gram brain but otherwise typical primate organization; and a whole-brain emulation running a hundred times faster than the original.
- Which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate. Where you must overturn one, say so plainly; where you can save it only by adding a clause, state the clause and what it costs in generality or in ad hoc-ness. I would rather lose a verdict than acquire an epicycle, but tell me which epicycles you think are worth buying.

Finally, tell me what would change your mind: which argument, or which empirical finding about nervous systems, would lead you to recommend a different principle; which of my verdicts you would defend even against my own reconsideration of it; and where in your reasoning you think you are most likely to be wrong. Please write in American English.
