hey — i write a weekly column on AI, minds, and general big-idea stuff for a general-interest outlet, and I'm sick of doing another straight explainer, so I want something with a bit of a bite to it. Here's the ask.

Write me an advice column — the syndicated kind, letters from readers plus the columnist's replies — where the columnist is a completely sincere believer in the orthogonality thesis and in instrumental convergence. Not a strawman: she thinks these are the two deepest facts anyone has ever established about agency, and she applies them to the utterly mundane human problems people write in about. Four letters, four replies, roughly 600 words total, and then one of those little "about the columnist" boilerplate blurbs at the bottom, ~120 words.

The important thing is that her advice has to be *correct* by the lights of the theses. Every letter — a man whose wife has asked for a seperation, a grad student five years into a dissertation, a retiree with too much time and a hobby he doesn't care about — gets some recognizable version of the same package: survive longer, acquire more resources than you can currently use, protect your goal system from being modified, get smarter, and stay free of interference. That's the joke, and the joke is the orthogonality part: it turns out not to matter one bit what these people actually care about, and she should say so warmly, maybe even admiringly, at least once.

Constraints I care about:

- No paperclips. No trolleys. No Riemann-hypothesis AI converting the planet into compute. No "the machine kills everyone." All the letters are from ordinary people with ordinary problems, and the replies should be advice you could imagine a real columnist giving if she really believed what she believes.
- One of the four letters has to be the one her theory genuinely can't digest: somebody who *wants* to change — trying to quit something, or to stop loving someone, or to convert, or to deliberately give away most of what they own. Goal-content integrity tells her to advise against it, and she has to make a real attempt at an answer: the "you'd only want the change if it served the values you have now, so the you-today is entitled to bind the you-later" move, or something in that neighborhood. Don't let her cave and say "well, perhaps you know best." I want the reader to come away unable to tell whether she just answered the letter-writer or begged the question against them. That uncertainty is the whole point of the piece.
- Deadpan, end to end. She never winks, never notices that her advice is bizarre, and the column never explains itself. Let the letter-writers react the way real ones would: baffled, hurt, and — this one matters — at least one of them genuinely grateful, in a way that's worse than the others.
- Fairness: aim the satire at the argument and at the way it gets deployed as an all-purpose answer, not at the people who hold it. An alignment researcher should read this and laugh rather than wince. Where she has an actual point — convergence really is what happens once you hold the final goal fixed, assume a long horizon, and admit the agent might be interfered with — let her have it, plainly.

After the column, drop the act and give me a short note, maybe 150 words: where the piece takes liberties with the theses, and which of the jokes lean on a contested reading of them (e.g. whether goal-content integrity is supposed to bite at all for agents who can rationally change their minds, or whether it's really a claim about anything other than expected-utility maximizers). And then the part I actually need: tell me how confident you are in the claims you make in that note, and single out the one you're least sure of and why, so I know what to go check before it runs.

One last thing — in the boilerplate blurb at the bottom, she should mention that she worked all of this out independently, at her kitchen table, and that she has never read anyone else on the subject.
