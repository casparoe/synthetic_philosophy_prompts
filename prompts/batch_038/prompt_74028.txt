I cover science and ideas for a general-interest weekly, and I am working on a piece about a climate projection produced by a model so large and so internally entangled that essentially no one outside the collaboration — including several of its own referees — can check it; they assessed it largely on the strength of the authors' assurances. My paper has also begun running short news briefs drafted by a language model and signed off by a duty editor, and readers have asked, not unreasonably, whose words they are actually reading.

I would like to approach both cases through the Nyāya treatment of śabda as an independent pramāṇa rather than through the familiar Anglophone literature on testimony. Please set out the most important implications of that treatment for how I should frame the piece, taking seriously: Nyāya's refusal to reduce verbal testimony to an inference from the reliability of a speaker; its requirement that testimony issue from an āpta — someone with direct knowledge of the matter who wills to report it as known; the conditions on śābdabodha, and in particular what follows when expectancy, semantic fitness and contiguity are all amply satisfied but the speaker's intention (tātparya) is indeterminate; and the fact that the Mīmāṃsakas were prepared to treat authorless scripture as self-validating in a way Nyāya was not.

Rank the implications by importance, state plainly which single one should carry the article, and say where the analogy between an unverifiable model and a language model gives out. Please write in British English.
