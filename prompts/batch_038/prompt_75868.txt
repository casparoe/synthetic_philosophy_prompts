Reading group argument, imprecise probabilities, and I can't tell whether it's a cute result or a trainwreck. Numbers first so you can check my arithmetic. Two coins get flipped behind a screen. H = the second coin lands heads. E = the two coins land the same way, both heads or both tails. My credal set has exactly two members, and here they are in the order HH, HT, TH, TT:

P1: .05, .05, .35, .55
P2: .45, .25, .15, .15

So my prior lower/upper credence in H is [.4, .6]. Conditionalize each member on E and you get 1/12 and 3/4. Conditionalize each on not-E and you get 3/8 and 7/8. Both posteriors strictly contain the prior interval. Whichever of E or not-E I get told, I end up less determinate about H than I was before. Classic dilation, fine, I can live with that. But here's where my friend takes it:

(1) If learning E can never improve your doxastic state with respect to H, then E isn't evidence about H at all.
(2) Improving your state w.r.t. H can only mean ending up closer to a settled view on H: a strictly narrower interval for H is strictly better, a strictly wider one strictly worse, other things equal.
(3) In the setup above, whichever of E or ¬E I learn, my interval for H goes from [.4, .6] to something strictly wider.
(4) So by (1) and (2), E is irrelevant to H here, and getting evidence bearing on E leaves me strictly worse off about H.
(5) Nothing there turned on these particular numbers. It turned only on H and E being dependant in the wrong direction under every member of my set — and for any H you care about I can extend the algebra with an E built to do exactly that.
(6) An imprecise Bayesian is committed to non-singleton credal sets whenever the evidence doesn't single out one probability function, which on their own story is nearly always.
(7) So for an imprecise Bayesian no evidence is ever relevant to any hypothesis, and going out and collecting evidence strictly degrades their beliefs.
(8) And here's the part that scares me: (1) and (2) never mention imprecision. They're supposed to be constraints on any theory of evidence, and a precise Bayesian conditionalizes too. So if (7) refutes imprecise credence, it refutes the claim that conditioning on evidence ever improves anybody's epistemic state.

(8) is obviously too much and (7) is too much, and I want to know where the rot starts. My instinct is (5) — cooking up a dilation pair in an extended algebra isn't the same as showing real evidence is irrelevant — but I can't say why (5) fails without also giving up (3), which is just arithmetic I've triple checked. So: which step do you cut? Why did that step look innocent on a first read?
