write me an advice-column piece — one of those "Ask ..." agony-uncle features in the back pages of a sunday magazine — where the columnist is a hardline reductionist about testimony. His whole shtick: you're only ever justified in taking someone's word for something if you have positive reasons to think that specific person is a reliable truth-teller, reasons that don't themselves come from anyone's say-so, plus no undefeated reason to think they're lying or confused on this occasion. He's turned this into a life philosophy and brands himself on it.

format: a couple of sentences of intro in his column voice, then four reader letters with his replies, around 750–800 words all told. Give the column a title and the columnist an invented name and credential — and let him run into trouble from that credential immediately, since he can't establish he holds it without trusting the institution that granted it. Letters signed first name plus city, all invented, and make the letter-writers sound like ordinary people with ordinary problems rather than grad students.

the jokes have to come from getting the epistemology right, not from anybody being a moron. So his replies should be technically correct applications of the view, and the view should visibly eat itself:

- one letter should force him to notice that pretty much all his evidence about how reliable people generally are came from testimony in the first place — surveys, news reports, what teachers told him, his therapist's account of human nature — so his positive reasons bottom out in exactly what they were supposed to justify. he should not be allowed to notice this out loud as a problem, just to paper over it awkwardly;
- one letter reports a miracle, and he does the proper weighing (would it be stranger that the witnesses were deceived or lying than that the thing happened), and finds that running the same procedure on his own background beliefs leaves him unable to say he has ever reliably witnessed anything at all;
- one letter is from a parent whose four-year-old believes literally everything anyone tells her, and the parent is worried she'll get eaten alive; he has to end up conceding, without ever stating it, that the kid's default-to-believe setup is better grounded than his own, since it only gets weakened by actually being lied to;
- one letter is from a reader on the other side of the debate who says she simply believed everything in last month's column and now wants him to confirm one factual claim in it. he must be unable to answer without either taking her word for what he wrote or leaning on his own memory, which he's already treated as testimony from a past self of dubious reliability.

he never caves, never gets converted, and never winks at the reader — no "of course this is a paradox", no closing paragraph explaining what was funny, and don't turn it into a defence of gullibility either. deadpan all the way through, treating the paralysis as rigour. don't put any verbatim quotation from Hume, Reid, Fricker or anyone else in anybody's mouth — paraphrase only, and keep proper names to a couple of passing mentions at most, since I cant check them and neither can the reader. no bibliography, no further reading, no footnotes. Someone who's never heard the word "reductionism" should still laugh; someone who works on the epistemology of testimony should recognise every single argument and think the satire is fair.
