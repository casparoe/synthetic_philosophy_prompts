I'm putting together an application for a nine-month fellowship at an interdisciplinary center (philosophy with some sociology and econ attached) and I need the proposal part written up. Here's the half-formed idea, which came out of an argument with my brother, who runs a small charity. Pretty much every philosophical use of signaling theory assumes a signal is trustworthy for one of two reasons: either it's wasteful, so only the genuinely well-endowed can afford it (the handicap line, Zahavi via Grafen, and the whole Veblen-married-to-biology tradition), or it's an index — physically hooked up to what it indicates, so faking it is impossible. But there's a third regime that I think gets ignored. A signal can be cheap or even free to produce and still be reliable, because lies are detectable and get punished. Audited accounts, reference checks, degree verification, escrow, public reputational records. Nothing is burned; the reliability comes from an audit infrastructure rather than from waste. Biologists apparently already accept that honesty doesn't require cost — cheap talk can be stable — but the ethical and political literature still runs almost entirely on the waste intuition, and it treats conspicuous waste as an epistemically load-bearing nuisance that we have to put up with.

What I want the proposal to argue for is that the migration of human signaling institutions from the wasteful regime to the audited one is not morally neutral, and that this is genuinely open rather than settled. The optimistic read: if audit does the same trust-building work, then conspicuous waste is pure deadweight and cutting it back is an improvement. The pessimistic read, which is where I think the real content is: audit capacity is a scarce, expensive, institutionally concentrated resource, so the switch doesn't eliminate the cost, it just moves it onto whoever runs the means of verification — and people outside those systems lose the ability to signal at all, since the old wasteful signals were at least producible by anybody who happened to have resources and no paperwork. That's why my rough question is whether replacing cost-backed with audit-backed signaling redistributes the power to certify, and whether that changes what we should say about waste as such.

So write the proposal. Put the question in a single sentence at the very top, then use headed sections: why the existing positions (the handicap-based accounts of conspicuous consumption, plus the cheap-talk results) leave it open rather than jointly answering it; the method, meaning what I'd actually do month by month across the nine months, and why conceptual work on cases beats the obvious alternatives of building a formal model or commissioning a survey; what a result would look like and what would count as the project failing; and an honest note on what could make it too big for the time available. Please take on the objection I already know is coming — that verification is itself costly, so nothing has been saved and I've just hidden the waste in an office somewhere — and say what would settle it.
