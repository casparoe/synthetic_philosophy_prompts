Still can't figure out what the absent-minded driver problem is supposed to prove, and it's been kicking around since the late 90s. Setup as I remember it: Piccione and Rubinstein's driver gets 4 for exiting at the second of two identical-looking exits, 0 for exiting at the first, and 1 for blowing past both; he can't tell which one he's at, so the ex-ante optimal plan is to exit at each exit with some fixed probability (1/3, i think?). But once he's actually sitting at an exit he can start reasoning about whether he's likelier to be at the first or the second, and then the plan stops looking right — except that the probability he should assign depends on the very plan he's questioning, which feels circular. People respond in at least four incompatible ways: the ex-ante plan is authoritative and the exit-side reasoning is the mistake; the exit is where the real decision happens and the plan was never anything he could choose; the example just shows you should give up dynamic consistency when you can't remember; or there's no paradox at all, because his choice at one exit and his choice at the other are the same decision rule applied twice, so treating them as independent is the error.

What kind of disagreement is this, actually? My hunch is that they're not arguing about the driver at all — half of them are scoring plans and half are scoring acts — and underneath that sits an unacknowledged epistemic question about whether an agent with imperfect recall even has a well-defined credence about where he is, which each side answers differently without noticing. Tell me whether that's right or whether there's a genuinely substantive disagreement about what rationality requires. Not a survey of the four responses: a diagnosis, plus what would count as progress — a theorem, a distinction, a reframing, or nothing, because the example is ill-posed.
