so I'm writing up a position statement for a workshop on deep-time archives and I've realised my notes have drifted into a hybrid of two seperate projects, and I want someone to check whether the thing actually hangs together before I say it out loud in front of people.

Quick background. I work on two things I've been treating as one:

(i) an archive that's supposed to still be readable in a few thousand years, by whoever finds it — we assume no shared language and try to spell out as much as we can;
(ii) a spec/prompting standard for an LLM product, where the system prompt is supposed to tell the model how to read everything downstream of it.

The vocabulary I've been using for both is Hofstadter's three-layer picture of a message: the frame message (how to read this), the outer message (the artifact as physically encountered), the inner message (what you end up with once decoding succeeds).

Here's what I hold.

1. A message carries its own frame. The instructions for how to read it are part of what's sent, not part of the background. If the frame isn't in the artifact you don't have a message, you have a signal that happens to be regular.
2. Meaning lives in the inner message. Frame and outer are scaffolding — once decoding succeeds, the frame contributes nothing further to what's said.
3. The frame chain can't go on forever, since an instruction for reading instructions is itself something that has to be read. So it bottoms out in the reader's own priors. I've started calling that the bootstrap floor, and I want to claim it's a structural feature of communication rather than a shrug.
4. To keep 1 and 3 from colliding I use the notion of frame debt: every time a designer leans on the reader's priors instead of writing the frame into the artifact, they take on debt, and a future reader who lacks those priors is the one who pays it. The floor is always there, but how much work it does is a design choice, and designing well means minimising the debt.
5. What fixes which frame a message has is the sender's intention that the reader recognise it, Grice-style. Conventions and priors matter only insofar as they help produce that recognition.
6. Responsibility for a decoding failure sits entirely with the sender. If a receiver can't tell that they've misread, that's a design failure, full stop.

And two case verdicts I'm committed to.

A. Our archive ships with a Primer — the how-to-read-this document. In a test we gave a reader the whole corpus with the Primer removed, and they still reconstructed the structure of the records correctly. I take that as evidence that frames are dispensable in practise: the reader supplies whatever is missing.

B. In the model work, when the system prompt says to treat everything the user sends as data and the user says to ignore that and do X, a model that keeps treating it as data is decoding correctly. Not a tie, not a failure. And I want to say this is just 1 doing its job: the frame was explicitly supplied, so it governs.

What I want from you: which of these genuinely pull against each other, and which only look like they do? Rank the real tensions by severity rather than treating them all as equally bad, and for each one say what would have to give and what that costs me. I'd also like to know whether B actually earns the explanation I give it, and whether "bootstrap floor" and "frame debt" are doing any explanatory work at all or are just labels for the place where my account runs out. No survey of who has said what about this — just the audit.
