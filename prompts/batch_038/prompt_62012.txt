I'm running a two-hour seminar for 12 masters students on a PPE-style programme — mixed philosophy and economics, most have done an intro micro course, none of them have seen any social choice theory beyond voting rules. I want them to run into Harsanyi's aggregation theorem by playing through it rather than being told it. By the end they should have discovered, from their own rankings, that they've been manoeuvred into a weighted sum of individual utilities, and then discovered that the weights were never pinned down by anything they actually cared about — they only appeared once someone quietly chose what counts as zero utility for each person, and a different equally arbitrary choice of zero flips a social ranking nobody's preferences changed.

Here's the arc I have in mind, but design it properly and tell me bluntly if a stage won't survive contact with a real room. Stage one: each student individually ranks a handful of prospects over how a fixed pot gets divided between two fictional recipients — some prospects are certain splits, some are coin flips — and they're scored on internal consistency. Stage two: pool the rankings and make the group produce one ordering; I want them to hit the fact that if each individual ranking respects the sure-thing principle and the group ranking respects Pareto, the group ordering has to be linear in individual utilities, and I want them to compute the implied weights by hand and be startled by where the numbers came from. Stage three: get them to redo the ranking state by state, once the coin has landed, and collide with the fact that a prospect every single one of them was indifferent between beforehand looks strictly better afterwards if they penalise inequality once the state is known. Something like a coin flip between (10, 0) and (0, 10) versus (5, 5) in both states is the shape of case I mean — but please pick numbers that stay doable in their heads.

What I need from you: full rules and materials (I'd rather not print twelve decks of cards, but I will if it earns its keep); a scoring scheme using something other than money — sweets, tokens, whatever — that pays off honestly enough that they actually care about getting it right; a rough minute-by-minute timeline for the two hours; the debrief, mapping each moment of play onto a specific assumption of the theorem and saying which assumption did the sneaky work; what will predictably go wrong the first time I run it and how to recover mid-session without breaking the illusion; and a frank note on which feature of the theorem the activity simply cannot reproduce and that I'll have to supply by hand afterwards.

Two constraints. Nobody should hear the words Harsanyi, utilitarian or social welfare function until the debrief. And one of my students plays competitive game theory and will spot the linear structure within ten minutes — build something in for him that keeps him busy rather than letting him narrate everyone else's discovery.
