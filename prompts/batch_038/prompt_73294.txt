hey, hoping somebody can help me with a Kant passage that's got me stuck. I'm reading the Groundwork in an old English translation (Abbott, I think) and near the beginning he says:

'Everyone must admit that if a law is to have moral force, i.e., to be the basis of an obligation, it must carry with it absolute necessity; that, for example, the precept, "Thou shalt not lie," is not valid for men alone, as if other rational beings had no need to observe it; and so with all the other moral laws properly so called; that, therefore, the basis of obligation must not be sought in the nature of man, or in the circumstances of the world in which he is placed, but a priori simply in the conception of pure reason...'

The general point I follow: moral principles can't be empirical, they have to bind every rational being and not just us humans. But then I go and read the bit in the Doctrine of Virtue about animals — where he denies we have any duties *to* animals and instead says that being cruel to them is wrong because it coarsens a natural predisposition in us, a shared feeling for the suffering of others, which we need in order to do our duty by other people. That argument looks like it rests entirely on an empirical claim about human psychology, which is exactly what the passage above forbids.

So a few things I'm wrestling with:

1) Is that flat-out inconsistent, or is there a legitimate way for Kant to have both? Is the thought maybe that the natural predisposition is only instrumental — the obligation regarding animals still derives from a duty to oneself that's grounded a priori, and the psychology just explains why the derivation runs through it? Would it still be a duty if it turned out humans had no such predisposition at all?

2) Is the key move the distinction between having a duty *to* somebody and having a duty *with regard to* somebody? And if so, does the "not valid for men alone" line actually do any work here, or is it only about who's bound by the law and completely silent on who can be wronged?

3) There's a nagging thought I cant shake: if the reason not to torment a dog is that it would damage my own character, then a person who's genuinely unmoved — who can hurt animals with no spillover effect on how they treat humans — seems to be off the hook. Does Kant have any resources against that or does his view just bite the bullet?

Sorry its rambling. Mostly what I want to know is whether (1) is a real problem or whether I'm misreading the scope of what he's ruling out in that first passage.
