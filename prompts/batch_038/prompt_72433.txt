I'm a backend engineer, zero philosophy training, so this may be a dumb question, but I've been maintaining the triage bot that decides when a new bug report is a dupe of an existing ticket for about two years, and after overruling it by hand that whole time I've realized I don't actually know what I mean by "the same bug." Here are my verdicts on cases, mostly in the order I formed them:

1. Two reports, near-identical stack traces, one on iOS 17 and one on iOS 18, one bad line in our code behind both → same bug, close as dupe.
2. Two reports that both say "app crashes on launch," with completely unrelated causes → not the same bug, even though the user-visible description is basically word for word the same.
3. One bug in a shared library that shows up as a crash on mobile and as silently wrong totals on the web dashboard → I open two tickets, but I'd say out loud that it's "one bug," and I don't think I'm being sloppy when I say that.
4. A bug we fixed in March that comes back in September because someone reverted the fix → new ticket, not a dupe, even though the failure and the cause are literally identical to a closed one.
5. A failure that looks identical to an old closed one but was introduced by a different commit during a rewrite → also a new ticket. But 4 feels more like a new ticket than 5 does, which I cant explain.
6. A bug that only reproduces when the device language is Hebrew → same bug as the English one if its the same code path, even though only RTL users ever hit it.
7. Same bug reported by 400 people in three days vs. by one person once a quarter → same bug, obviously, but I'd never call those the same situation and the second one sits at priority 4 for a year.
8. A crash that only happens when two already-ticketed bugs interact, each one harmless on its own → third ticket, not a dupe of either, and I genuinely can't say what it is a duplicate *of*.

So: is there a principle behind these, or a small set of them? "Root cause identity" is the obvious candidate and 2 and 4 both break it. I'd like you to (a) state the principle or principles precisely enough that I could tell where a new case lands, and say which of my cases motivated each clause, (b) tell me straight which of my verdicts it can't save and which one I should give up, and (c) tell me what it says about a few I haven't listed: the same bug in a fork we don't control, two bugs with an identical cause where one is a security hole and the other isn't, and a report whose cause we will never figure out.

One more thing that might be relevant. There's that old puzzle from Hofstadter's Copycat work — abc becomes abd, so what should xyz become — and the answers people find clever are the ones where "rightmost" quietly slides into "leftmost" to fit the new situation. Cases 3 and 4 feel like that to me: the concept moves to fit what's around it, and it doesn't feel like me being inconsistent. So is the slippage a defect my principle should eliminate, or is it the concept working correctly, and if it's the latter, what does a principle that *keeps* the slippage look like? You don't need to point me at papers or anything, I won't read them — I just want the principle.
