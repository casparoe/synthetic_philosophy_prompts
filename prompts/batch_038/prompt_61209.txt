Our jurisprudence reading group spent the last two sessions on the Hart–Devlin exchange — Devlin's 1959 Maccabaean lecture on the enforcement of morals, and Hart's response in Law, Liberty and Morality plus his shorter piece on immorality and treason — and then somebody dragged in a case neither of them had in mind and it turned into a two hour fight. I'm reporting it because I was the one taking notes and I genuinely couldnt tell who was winning.

The case: Florida's 2024 law making it a crime to manufacture or sell cultivated (cell-cultured) meat, plus the copycat bills in a handful of other statehouses. The public justifications mix three strands: consumers will be confused about what they're eating; it'll wreck the cattle industry and the ranching way of life; and, in a lot of the surrounding rhetoric, this isn't real food, it's an insult to what we eat and how it's grown.

Rob and Meera went at it. Roughly, in order:

1. Rob: this is textbook legal moralism of the kind Hart was worried about. Strip out the labeling worry, which a disclosure rule handles, and what's left is either protecting incumbent producers from competition — which isn't harm in any sense the harm principle was built to catch — or a bare appeal to how unnatural the thing feels to the majority. Which is exactly what Hart said Devlin was smuggling in through the reasonable man's indignation and disgust.

2. Meera: that's the caricature of Devlin, not the argument. The disintegration thesis isn't about yuck reactions. It's that a society is held together by a shared web of practices, and some of those practices — Devlin's own example was marriage, but nothing stops it being how a community feeds itself and works its land — are doing constitutive work. A law protecting one of those isn't enforcing an opinion against harmless conduct, it's protecting an institution, more like preventing sabotage than like policing taste. If you think that's illegitimate you owe an account of why institutions don't matter and only individual states of mind do.

3. Rob: then Hart's charge lands harder, not softer. Devlin slides between society-as-its-members and society-as-its-way-of-life, and only the second one supports the ban; the first doesn't, because no individual's life goes worse from cultivated meat existing. And the disintegration claim is an empirical prediction — let the practice spread and the shared fabric comes apart — which Devlin asserted and never evidenced. Florida's legislative record doesn't evidence it either. What it evidences is cattlemen wanting protection from a competitor.

4. Meera: fine, call it empirical. But then Hart owes the mirror image. His framework needs it to be true that whether a food culture survives or dies makes no difference to the moral texture of a community, and he never argued that either — he just assumed that the interests that count are ones individuals can point to from inside their own lives. And that assumption is doing all the work here, because whose loss counts as a harm is precisely what the two sides disagree about. In one state a disappearing ranching way of life is a harm worth legislating against; in the next state it's just competition. So the harm principle isn't the neutral referee it's presented as, it's already loaded with a view of what a society owes its members.

5. Rob: if that's right the harm principle has no limiting content left. Any interest a culture happens to recognize gets promoted to a harm, and then you can legislate against anything — which is the exact result Hart spent the book trying to block. And practically speaking, reaching for Devlin to defend this bill hands a rent-seeking statute a philosophical dignity it hasn't earned.

And tell me what both of them are missing — including whether this case even belongs inside the Hart–Devlin frame, or whether they've spent two hours arguing about a law that's mostly economics with moralism as garnish.

Last thing: when you hand down your verdicts, mark which of them are more or less settled among people who actually work on this debate and which are you picking a side on something that's genuinely contested.
