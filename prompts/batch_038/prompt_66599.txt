I have been developing what I would call a strict ordinalist account of utility, and before circulating it I would like an audit of whether my commitments hang together rather than a survey of the surrounding literature. Here is the package:

(1) Preference attributions are behaviourally constituted: to say that an agent prefers A to B is to say that, presented with the pair, they take A. There is no further inner state that such talk reports.

(2) Utility is nothing over and above an ordinal representation of a complete and transitive preference ordering; claims about the intensity of preference, and interpersonal comparisons of utility, are not merely difficult but meaningless.

(3) Money-pump arguments vindicate transitivity as a requirement of practical rationality, and the parallel dominance arguments in the risky case vindicate the expected-utility axioms.

(4) Framing effects and preference reversals show that a choice sometimes fails to reveal the agent's preferences, so in those cases preferences must be inferred from evidence other than choice.

(5) With Sen, I hold that an agent can, out of commitment, choose A while ranking B above A, and that this is a coherent phenomenon rather than a confusion.

(6) For policy evaluation I am a Kaldor–Hicks welfarist: an allocation counts as an improvement when the gainers could compensate the losers, gains and losses being measured by willingness to pay, and I take this to involve no interpersonal comparisons of utility.

Which of these genuinely conflict, which only appear to, and where exactly does each conflict bite? Rank the tensions by severity rather than listing them as equally bad, and for each say precisely what would have to be given up and what is lost by giving it up. Do not assume I have any prior view about which item is expendable. Then identify the single revision that would clean up the most, say which of your severity judgements you are least confident in, and tell me what argument or piece of evidence would move you to a different verdict there — or where you think your own reasoning is most likely to have gone wrong.

Close with a final line in exactly this form: Verdict: revise (n); genuine = k — where n is the number of the commitment whose revision does the most work, and k is the number of tensions you count as genuine rather than merely apparent.
