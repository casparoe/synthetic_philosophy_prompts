I teach a 200-level philosophy of AI course — 28 students, mixed majors, roughly half have written some code and half have never touched it — and we get one 80-minute session on the idea that you can work out what people want by watching what they do. Inverse reinforcement learning, preference inference from behavior, call it what you like. I don't want to lecture them about it. I want them to *be* the inference engine and get burned by it.

I've got a half-designed activity and I want you to finish it, fix the parts that won't work, and tell me where I'm fooling myself. Here's the skeleton. Each round: one student is the "human" and gets a private card listing some things they genuinely want, some hard constraints they can't violate (it isn't theirs, they promised someone, they'd get sick), and — this is the bit I care about most — the card tells them their wants shift partway through the round. Another student is the "agent," never sees the card, only watches the human pick among options that get put in front of them, and at the end writes down what they think the human wants and then makes one final choice on the human's behalf. Everybody else is an observer who also writes down a guess, so we can see the room split. Scoring is candy and index cards, no real money, no phones or laptops, and it has to work in a room with movable chairs and one whiteboard.

The thing I actually want them to run into: the agent gets scored on how the *human* rates the agent's final choice afterwards, not on how well it matches the card. Which means at least once per session the agent is going to have inferred the behavior perfectly and still get zero, because the human looks at the choice and says no, that's not what I wanted. I've started calling that the disavowal gap, which is maybe a bad name, but you get the idea. That's the moment I want the whole session built around, and I want them to hit it before anyone says a word about it.

So, concretely:

- Write out the actual card contents for at least three rounds, escalating. What I need is options designed so that one and the same choice is compatible with at least two very different explanations — wanting X versus being unable to have X versus saving X for later — and the observers should split down the middle with both sides feeling smug.
- Give me a scoring rule that makes the agent's best strategy *not* be "guess whichever preference is most common in the room," and that rewards the agent for hedging or for asking, without letting them just interrogate the human and collapse the game. Say explicitly how many points are available where, and how the human's rating gets collected so it can't be argued with afterwards.
- A minute-by-minute timeline, a printable materials list, and what should be on the board when they walk in.
- A debrief that maps specific moments from the rounds onto the real structure of the problem — behavior underdetermining preference, the confound between what someone wants and what they can do, a moving target that any estimate only averages over, and then the harder question of what it even means to get it right. Order it so the naming comes last.
- What will predictably go wrong the first time I run this, and what to do in the moment. I've run games like this before: someone hoards the candy, someone breaks character, someone reads the card off the human's face, and one group finishes four minutes early and starts heckling.
- And be straight with me about which feature of the actual problem this game cannot reproduce and I'll have to supply by hand. I already suspect it's the fact that a real system is inferring from thousands of people at once and never gets to ask.

One last thing, and I'm not sure the game can carry it on its own. I want them to leave unsettled about whether "the human would disavow it" is really the right test. If the agent's guess tracks the behavior flawlessly and the human afterwards insists that's not what they wanted — is the agent wrong, or is the human wrong about themselves, and is there any difference between a preference someone has and a preference someone would endorse having? I'd like at least one round where that question is unavoidable and where nothing printed on any card settles it. If you think that's too much to ask of 80 minutes with 28 sophomores, tell me so and tell me what you'd cut to make room for it. No need to point me at papers, I've got my own stack.
