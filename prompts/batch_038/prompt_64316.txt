Three weeks ago I became the person who signs off on whether our agent goes out to the next cohort of customers, which sounds grander than it is except that I turn out to be the actual bottleneck and nobody else wanted the job. We're about 30 people. The product is a tool-using coding/ops agent that customers let into their own infrastructure — it can open a shell, run migrations, touch prod. Almost all of the time it's great. My problem is I've been making these go/no-go calls by gut, and I've started logging them, and the gut is not consistent. I keep flip-flopping between wanting an average-case guarantee and wanting a worst-case one, and I want to know what rule I'm actually applying.

The record so far:

1. A build that scored 8% better on our usefulness evals but had a red-team-found path where it could be talked into piping customer secrets to an attacker-controlled endpoint: no-go. I didn't care that it was maybe one in a million and the expected harm was tiny.
2. A build with no known catastrophic path at all but 12% worse in aggregate, because it asks for confirmation before every destructive action: go, shipped it, felt good about it.
3. Two weeks later: option A had a worst case of one permanent data loss (drops a table, no backup) at maybe 1 in 10^8 per action; option B had a worst case of a recoverable multi-hour outage at maybe 1 in 10^4. I picked A. Which looks like it flatly contradicts 1 and 2 — there I ignored an expected-value argument, here I made one.
4. Harm that lands entirely on the customer who opted in and read the docs, versus harm that lands on someone who never agreed to anything (a bystander whose data ends up in a log we ship offsite). Same magnitude, very different verdict from me, and I think I'd hold that even if the bystander harm were smaller.
5. A failure mode we found once in red-teaming and could never reproduce, versus a whole region of behavior we've never probed and can't bound: the unreproducible one I refuse to ship past, the unprobed one I shrug at. I know how dumb that sounds.
6. Four thousand customers each losing an afternoon of work versus one customer losing their entire business: same total, I'd take the first without blinking and block the second even at a much lower probability.

My best guess at my own rule is that irreversibility and third-party exposure are what I actually care about, and probability only earns a vote when the harm is bounded and consensual. But 3 breaks the first half and I don't know what breaks it with.

The one I have to decide by Thursday: a release where the agent runs a scheduled job that, if it double-fires, corrupts a downstream billing system in a way that takes a human about 30 hours to fully unwind — estimated 1 in 500,000 runs. The alternative is holding four weeks, which we're confident costs more total harm in the ordinary sense, including one customer who plausibly goes under without the new version.

Is there a principle, or a small set of them, that gets most or all of these — stated precisely enough that I could hand it to a colleague and get the same calls out? I'd want each clause tied to the case that forces it, plus what it rules on cases I haven't listed: worst case with respect to whom (adversarial users? actual traffic? the distribution we can even measure?), what happens when there's no safer alternative at all, and whether the bar is "nonzero catastrophic chance" or "no worse than a human doing the same job." I'd much rather you say which of my verdicts it can't save than build something baroque that saves all six. Also tell me how confident you are in what you land on, and where you think I'm reporting a principle when really I'm just reporting squeamishness.

And if you think the framing is broken, say that instead. If there's no interesting principle here because these are six different questions in a trenchcoat, or if "worst case" is meaningless until you fix a distribution and my list is unanswerable as posed, I want to hear that rather than a dutiful synthesis.
