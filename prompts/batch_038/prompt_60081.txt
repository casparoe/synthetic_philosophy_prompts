Ok so I'm prepping notes for a reading group on diachronic self-trust and I built an argument that was supposed to motivate a restricted version of Reflection, and then it ran away from me. It now seems to show that no credence is ever irrational, which can't be right, and I cant find the exit. Case first, because step (3) leans on it:

There's a five-year residential program — call it the Institute — run by people who push a doctrine D. I'm confident of four things. Every member of the last four cohorts came out with credence about .95 in D. I've been accepted, I'm certain I'll go, and I'm certain I'll finish. The program works entirely by social means — isolation, sleep loss, charisma, sunk cost — so nobody leaves it with evidence for D they didn't arrive with. And D is false on evidence I already have; call my present credence .1.

(1) Reflection: if you're now certain that your credence in p at some later time t will be x, you should now have credence x in p.
(2) (1) is meant to hold for all p, all x and all t, and nothing in it turns on how your future self gets to x. Its whole appeal is that it's a coherence constraint linking you-now to you-then, in the same family as Conditionalization, not a thesis about which belief-forming sources are good ones.
(3) So in the Institute case I should now be at .95 in D — despite being certain that the .95 will be manufactured by a process I already regard as evidence-blind, and despite being at .1 now on decent evidence.
(4) Generalize: for any p and any x I can get myself into the Institute position with respect to p and x, because I can volunteer for whatever conditioning will reliably leave me at x in p. So for any p and x, I can make it the case that I ought now to have credence x in p.
(5) If I can make it the case that I ought to have credence x in p, then credence x in p isn't ruled out for me by my present evidence.
(6) So nothing is ruled out: there's no p and no x such that x in p would be an irrational present credence for me. Credences are never constrained by evidence at all.
(7) But that's absurd — here's a die I know to be fair; .95 in six on the next throw is flatly irrational for me now, and by (4)–(5) I could have arranged for it to be required of me.
(8) Hence (1) is false: Reflection is refuted by the mere fact that you can always engineer your future credences.

Two things I can't get past. First, (4) seems to work just as well if I substitute my sister, who I'm certain will go to the Institute, finish, and come out at .95 in D, and who I trust at least as much as I trust myself. If the substitution changes the verdict on (3), then (2) is wrong and Reflection was never a coherence constraint, and I lose the diachronic Dutch book argument that was the reason anyone took it seriously — and I don't see how to repair that book without smuggling the restriction in. Second, if the fix is to restrict (1) to future credences whose provenence you now endorse, I want to know why that isn't just abandoning Reflection and relabelling it evidentialism with an extra step.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look innocent going in? Price the alternatives for me — I'd rather keep (1) than (7), and I want to keep the Institute case exactly as described because I think it's doing real work — but tell me what each option costs. And if the answer is that (4) and (5) are where it breaks, say precisely what the difference is between a future credence I can bring about and a future credence I must defer to, because right now I can't see that there is one.
