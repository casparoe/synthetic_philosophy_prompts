I serve as principal clerk to the Second Panel of a transitional tribunal established last year in a country that has just emerged from nineteen years of rule by a regime that seized power in a coup and thereafter governed through its own legislature, its own courts, and its own land and civil registry. Our charter confers a single power: on petition, to declare that a specified act of the former authority — a conviction, a registration, a decree, an agreement its courts enforced — is without legal effect in the new order. The panel may not modify, reduce, or rewrite such an act, and it may not invalidate anything done by the present legislature.

In its first year the panel has disposed of seven petitions. Its reasons are two or three sentences long, it has never articulated a general principle, and it has never cited a theorist. The record is as follows.

1. A conveyance of farmland recorded in the regime's land registry in its fourth year, between two private parties, under formalities indistinguishable from those of the civil code in force before the coup. Upheld. Reason: the act would have been valid under the law as it stood before the interruption of constitutional order.
2. A conviction for defeatist speech under a decree issued in the regime's seventh year, where the conduct occurred in its sixth and the decree was not printed in the gazette until three months after the trial. Declared without effect. Reason: the panel cannot treat as law a rule the subject had no means of consulting.
3. Marriages solemnized, and births and deaths registered, by officials whose appointments were made without constitutional authority. Upheld. Reason: a defect in the source of authority does not travel to routine acts that merely record what is independently the case about private lives.
4. A civil action to enforce an agreement that was formally flawless under the civil code and freely entered, by which the claimant undertook to identify tenants of a designated ethnic origin to the security police in exchange for a waiver of arrears. Enforcement refused. Reason: this tribunal will not lend its machinery to a bargain whose performance consisted in delivering persons to a persecutory power.
5. Applications to annul pension entitlements, the contributions having been levied under the regime's fiscal statutes — published, prospective, applied exactly as written, widely resented as confiscatory, and a principal source of revenue for the security apparatus. Refused. Reason: where a rule was published, prospective, and applied as written, the panel will not inquire into its justice.
6. A conviction for harbouring a fugitive under a published statute fixing a maximum of three years, where the trial court imposed nine. Declared without effect. Reason: an official act that departs from the rule it invokes is not an application of that rule at all.
7. A conviction for burglary tried under the pre-coup criminal code continued in force by the regime, with regular procedure and legal representation. Upheld. Reason: an ordinary wrong does not cease to be one because the court that tried it was usurped.

The eighth petition is set down for next month. In its ninth year the regime enacted a Law on the Preservation of Civic Unity, printed in the gazette four months before the conduct at issue. It criminalized knowingly giving shelter to a member of a proscribed organization. The drafting was unusually exact: the elements were enumerated, the penalty was fixed at six years with no judicial discretion and no aggravating or mitigating factors, and the statute's own preamble announced its purpose as the eradication of internal enemies. The petitioner sheltered her brother for eleven days; she was tried in the ordinary courts, appealed, and was affirmed on the law. A study commissioned by the new ministry of justice found this the most uniformly applied statute of the nineteen years: of some four thousand prosecutions, no sentence departed from the fixed penalty and no similarly situated defendant was treated otherwise. The proscribed organization's members were subject to mass detention and extrajudicial killing. The petitioner lost her medical licence on conviction, and the licensing statute revokes automatically upon conviction of a criminal offence, so the licence can be restored only if the conviction is declared without effect. Her counsel argues that the panel's own fifth decision commits it to upholding a rule that was published, prospective, clear, and applied as written, and that if the panel now voids the conviction it can do so only on the ground that the content was monstrous — the very inquiry it disclaimed a year ago. The government argues the opposite. The present legislature is not expected to reach retroactive relief before the next election.

Please write a memorandum of roughly 1,100 words for my presiding member that does the following.

First, recommend the disposition, and draft the panel's ratio in the two or three sentences of the kind it actually writes.

Second, identify which of the seven decisions is the closest analog to the eighth, and which the second closest, saying what does the work in each comparison.

Third, state at least two general rules — formulated precisely enough to be applied — that account for all seven dispositions. For each, say what it implies about the eighth petition and which single precedent it explains least well; if no rule covers all seven without strain, say so and say where the strain falls.

Fourth, say which rule you would bet on and why, and in particular whether the panel can retain the reason it gave in the fifth decision if it grants this petition.

Fifth, locate the panel's implicit rule within the debate between legal positivism and natural law: whose position its record vindicates; whether an account of legality built on publication, prospectivity, clarity, and congruence between declared rule and official action can really deliver the fourth decision or this one; and whether the panel has in practice been operating a content-based view of intolerable injustice while describing its work in formal terms.

Sixth, address the alternative of refusing the declaration and recommending an explicitly retroactive statute of the new order: what the panel gains and loses by that route, whether its charter permits it to recommend it, and how this compares with the choice Hart put to the postwar German courts between leaving an informer unpunished and punishing her retrospectively.

Seventh, describe one further hypothetical petition, concrete enough to be decided on these materials, that would discriminate between your two leading rules, and state how each rule would decide it.

Please argue from the record given and from positions you state in your own words. Do not supply citations, a bibliography, or quoted passages from any source, and do not add facts about the regime beyond those set out above. Where the precedents genuinely underdetermine the outcome, say so plainly rather than presenting a single rule as compelled.
