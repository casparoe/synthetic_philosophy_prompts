I am putting together a seminar exercise on Frederick Douglass as a constitutional interpreter, in which the point is method rather than conclusion. Here is the track record I will hand the students — all from after his break with Garrison in the early 1850s, given as verdicts rather than arguments:

- The preamble's declared objects (justice, tranquility, common defense, general welfare, liberty) govern the construction of every clause, and no clause may be read so as to defeat them.
- Because the document nowhere names slaves or slavery, no provision can be construed to create or protect property in persons; the euphemisms the framers resorted to show that they knew the thing could not be said.
- The clause about persons bound to service or labor who escape into another state reaches apprenticed or contractually bound laborers, confers no power on anyone, and cannot cover a person held by force rather than by obligation.
- The clause counting three-fifths of the enslaved population toward representation is a disability on the slaveholding states rather than a reward, since it strips two-fifths of their people from the basis of their power and so discourages the institution.
- The clause barring Congress from prohibiting the African trade before 1808 shows that the trade was expected to end, and the framers' own published commentary rejected any constitutional idea of property in men.
- The due process guarantee speaks of persons and therefore protects the enslaved; slavery exists only where state positive law creates it, and federal law does not.
- The guarantee to each state of a republican form of government obliges the federal government against any domestic institution incompatible with republican government.
- Nothing in the text authorizes carrying slavery into the territories.
- Had the text contained a pro-slavery provision, the amendment power would supply the remedy; its silence is no evidence of complicity.

In his Garrisonian years he returned roughly the opposite verdicts on the same clauses — reading the fugitive provision as a federal guarantee of rendition, the three-fifths provision as slaveholder power, and the compact itself as something an honest abolitionist should refuse to swear allegiance to.

Now the new case, on which his published readings give no direct ruling: the clause providing that the writ of habeas corpus shall not be suspended except in rebellion or invasion when the public safety requires it, and a president's unilateral suspension of it early in a rebellion whose prosecution he believes will end slavery.

Please do four things. Identify the two to four interpretive rules that would each account for the whole post-1851 set, stated so that someone who does not share Douglass's conclusions could apply them; say whether the earlier Garrisonian set shows the rule changing or the same rule meeting different premises about what the text says. Then predict his verdict on the suspension case, naming which rule you are running to get it. Finally, identify the clause or controversy that would most cleanly separate your leading candidates, and say where his record is internally unstable — the appeal to the framers' published views sits oddly with the insistence that the document's own declared objects are the only legitimate intention — and which way you think that instability resolves. Reasoning, not references: no bibliography, no citations.
