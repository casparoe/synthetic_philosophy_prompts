My uncle Ray died two weeks ago and the family asked me to do the eulogy, I guess because I'm the one who writes things down for a living. Here's the complication. About a year before he died, a geneology site plus a shoebox of letters proved that Ray — who ran a hardware store in a town of four thousand people for forty years and never mentioned any of this — was the same man as a poet who put out one well-regarded book under a pen name in the seventies and then dropped out of that world completely. Two names, two lives, one body. My aunt is wrecked by it and keeps saying "that wasn't him, that was somebody else." Two of my cousins are analytic philosophers and will hear every mistake I make in the metaphysics; the other fifty-odd people in the Methodist hall will hear philosophy and switch off. And somebody has tracked down the man's old editor, who's coming, and he only ever knew the poet.

So write the eulogy, about four minutes. It has to hold the line that if the identity is true it's necessary — I can't stand up there and give them two men, or let my aunt off with the comforting idea that the hardware store Ray and the poet are different people who happened to share a face — without telling a room full of grieving people that what they're feeling is a confusion or a mistake. My own hunch, which you can use or bin: the interesting thing isn't the necessity, it's the two baptisms. Two entirely separate chains of people handing a name along, converging on one referent, and your grief runs down whichever chain you happen to be standing in — which is why my aunt isn't wrong about the feeling, only about the facts it seems to report. Take that or throw it out. No vocabulary above my aunt's head; the philosophers should catch the argument from its shape rather than its labels. And nothing in it that I'd have to retract as false at the graveside.
