I chair the rules committee of a three-hundred-member student society, and on Thursday I deliver the closing remarks immediately before the membership votes to adopt the new constitution my committee has spent a year drafting. Two features of the situation make this harder than an ordinary ratification speech. First, the old constitution's sole provision for its own replacement says it may be superseded by majority vote of the membership — so the authority we are invoking to adopt the new document is an authority the new document abolishes the moment it takes effect, and my own standing to convene this meeting rests on the same expiring rule. Second, the new text contains a two-thirds amendment clause, entrenches one article against any future amendment, and then provides that the entrenchment article may itself be repealed by two-thirds: it authorizes the removal of its own permanence. A small, articulate faction intends to vote no on precisely these grounds, calling the whole arrangement circular. Roughly a dozen law students in the room will follow the argument closely; the rest came for the free food and will resent a lecture.

Please write those remarks: four to five minutes, meant to be spoken, free of jargon. They must take the faction's objection seriously rather than wave it off, must not pretend the circularity is merely apparent, and must not resolve it by flat. They should be deliverable by someone who holds no view about whether the faction is right, and should hand the no-voters at least one true reason they can vote yes.

After the speech, add a brief note flagging where it takes liberties with the underlying problem, and distinguish what you say that reflects settled ground among people who work on self-amendment and legal self-reference from what is genuinely contested there. I do not want a live dispute dressed up as consensus.
