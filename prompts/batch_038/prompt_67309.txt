Compose an internal HR memo of roughly 550 words from the People Analytics team of a mid-sized firm, announcing a new Utility-Calibrated Thermostat Policy for the open-plan office. The memo should take interpersonal utility comparison entirely seriously and attempt to operationalise it: a single unit in which thermal comfort is denominated, an elicitation procedure for each employee's marginal utility of warmth, an aggregation rule for the competing claims on the shared thermostat, and a route for appeals.

The humour must come from the welfare economics being right rather than from obvious gags — the unobservability of other people's utilities, the fact that a von Neumann–Morgenstern representation is unique only up to positive affine transformation per person and therefore fixes no common zero or scale, willingness-to-pay elicitation quietly weighting every complaint by the complainant's salary, the incentive to inflate one's own sensitivity, an extended-sympathy answer to the sceptic, and the discovery that the tie-breaker has collapsed back into a vote — while the memo itself names no theorist and concedes no difficulty. Keep the register deadpan, corporate, and sincerely proud of the policy's fairness.

Close with a three-question FAQ; one quoted sentence from a sceptical colleague in Finance whose objection is Robbins's in all but name; and a footnote explaining why one employee's preferences proved unmeasurable and what that implies for their share of the thermostat.
