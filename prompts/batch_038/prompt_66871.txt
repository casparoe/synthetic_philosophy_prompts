Write a parody of an institutional style guide — the fussy, comma-governing sort of house manual that tells press officers, drafters, and spokespersons which words they are permitted to use — for a wholly invented international body: *Nomenclature: The Style Manual for the Description of Events*, eleventh edition, issued by the Secretariat's Office of Terminology. Roughly 950–1,150 words, deadpan throughout, in the register of a usage guide crossed with a treaty secretariat.

The manual's subject is the vocabulary of mass atrocity, and its governing anxiety is that the nouns carry obligations. Two features of the real law should drive everything, and you may reproduce these two passages verbatim, and only these two:

Article I of the 1948 Genocide Convention: "The Contracting Parties confirm that genocide, whether committed in time of peace or in time of war, is a crime under international law which they undertake to prevent and to punish."

Article II defines genocide as "any of the following acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such," and then enumerates five acts. Note what the enumeration leaves out and what it adds: political and social groups are not protected categories, and the crime requires a purpose to destroy a group rather than a pattern of killing however large.

Invent the manual's apparatus, and let the humour come from its internal logic rather than from anything that happens to anyone. Suggested furniture — use it, extend it, or replace it, but keep it that specific:

- a Substantiation Ladder, Rungs 0 through 5, governing which nouns may be printed at which level of evidentiary support, with Rung 4 and above requiring an annex countersigned by a legal officer, which has never yet been countersigned;
- a Provisional Adjective Bank, drawn down each quarter, reported in this edition as nearly exhausted, with the Editorial Board recommending a return to verbs;
- a rule that a protected noun may appear only in the past tense and only after the relevant chamber has closed, since the gerund would imply an obligation still running;
- mandatory passive voice wherever the agent is a party to the instrument, on the stated ground that agency is a matter for the Security Council;
- a Moratorium Register of words retired this cycle, and a standing entry for the phrase ethnic cleansing, admissible precisely because it appears in no treaty and therefore obliges no one.

Format: a short note from the Editorial Board; ten to fourteen alphabetized entries, each carrying the same fixed subheads (Usage / Register / Clearance / See also); one boxed sidebar; and a closing italicized disclaimer in the manual's own voice.

Constraints. Do not name any real state, conflict, tribunal, case, or living official, and do not refer to any treaty other than the Convention; do not describe any invented atrocity in comic detail. The manual should never mention victims except as a drafting problem, and that refusal is the satire. Every *See also* must point to another invented entry in the manual — never to real scholarship, real judgments, or a reading list; include no bibliography or footnotes to real authorities. Two entries must work against the grain. One is the manual's best genuine case: that a word which triggers an undertaking to prevent is a word that should be spent only where the undertaking could be honoured, and that promiscuous use devalues the accusation for those who will need it later — argued soberly enough that a reader cannot simply dismiss it. The other is an entry in which the rules break down, as when a witness uses the noun aloud in testimony and the Office must decide how to render it in print. The piece must never wink: a reader should be able to reconstruct unaided that the manual has come to treat the description as the hazard rather than the event, and that its ideal document would consist entirely of adjectives. Target the practice of euphemistic naming and the assumption that the noun itself creates the duty — not the victims, not the Convention's drafters, not any identifiable government. It should be funny to someone who knows the special-intent requirement and the Article I undertaking, and legible to someone who has never heard of either.
