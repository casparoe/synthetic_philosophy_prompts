I cover science and ideas for a general-interest magazine, and my guest next week is an economist who defends what she calls the tractability thesis: that Mises's calculation objection was at bottom a claim about computational limits, so it dissolves once you grant modern hardware, sparse linear programming, and detailed input-output accounting — the Cockshott–Cottrell line, on which labor-time bookkeeping can now be solved at machine speed, with Lange's parametric trial and error simply running faster than Mises could have imagined. Draft exactly five interview questions, numbered, each one or two sentences, in American English, with no introduction or conclusion. Build them to press the thesis at its joints rather than let her restate it: I want one on Hayek's separate point that the operative knowledge is tacit, situational, and never arrives in a form that can be tabulated at all; one on what trial-and-error pricing can mean when no one owns the capital goods whose scarcity the prices are supposed to register; one on the gap between solving a system of equations and knowing which equations to solve, including the reliability of the data fed into the solver; one on her favorite illustration — that a large retailer already plans internally across thousands of outlets without any internal market — and whether that example works only because it borrows genuine market prices from outside its own boundaries; and one on discovery of new goods and production methods as against the allocation of a given endowment. Each question must be phrased so that her stock reply, that whatever remains is an engineering difficulty rather than a theoretical one, cannot simply be used to wave it away. No warm-up questions about her intellectual formation or the state of the field.
