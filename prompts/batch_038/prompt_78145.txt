A podcast episode on decision theory that I listened to last week summarised Lara Buchak's risk-weighted expected utility theory as follows:

"Expected utility theory tells you to pick the option with the highest probability-weighted average of utilities, and it copes with the fact that most of us dislike gambles by letting the utility function be concave — an extra thousand matters more to you when you are poor than when you are rich. Buchak's point is that this manoeuvre cannot do the work, because people's choices in the Allais experiments flatly contradict the linear weighting of probabilities. So she introduces a second function: rather than weighting each outcome by its own probability, you weight it by the probability of getting that outcome or something better, passed through a risk function. Make the risk function the identity and you get expected utility theory back, which shows that expected utility theory is just the limiting case of a theory in which the agent is not risk-averse at all. The upshot is that maximising expected value was never a requirement of rationality; and since risk-weighted agents violate the sure-thing principle, they can be walked through a sequence of individually acceptable trades that leaves them strictly worse off, which is why most decision theorists still treat the view as refuted."
