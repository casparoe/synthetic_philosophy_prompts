I am preparing a lecture section on superrationality and have run into an obstruction I cannot resolve. Consider Hofstadter's Platonia dilemma: a benefactor offers a very large prize to whichever member of a specified group of twenty people — forbidden from communicating with one another — sends him a message before a fixed deadline, with the stipulation that the prize is paid only if exactly one of the twenty sends; if none sends, or if more than one does, nobody is paid. Consider alongside it the Luring Lottery he publicized: readers of a magazine could enter by postcard, a single winning entry was to be drawn at random, and the prize consisted of a fixed pot divided by the total number of entries received.

The Prisoner's Dilemma template is familiar: because like-minded agents in like situations are supposed to reach like conclusions, each may reason as though her choice settles the others', and cooperation comes out ahead. I want to explain precisely where that template fails in the two cases above, and I have not been able to get the explanation into a form I am willing to say out loud.

Please address the following.

(i) In Platonia, sending weakly dominates not sending for each individual: someone who does not send cannot be the unique sender, whereas someone who sends wins whenever the other nineteen do not. Yet the superrational recommendation in this case is that each send with probability one in twenty. Where does that figure come from, and what exactly is it a recommendation of, given that the superrational principle is ordinarily stated as a claim that like reasoners choose alike rather than that they assign alike probabilities?

(ii) If the twenty are alike in their reasoning, what licenses the assumption that their twenty chance outcomes are uncorrelated? Trace what becomes of the argument if that assumption is withdrawn.

(iii) Does the analysis carry over to the Luring Lottery, where the relevant group is not a specified twenty but an unknown fraction of a readership, each member of which is estimating that fraction while simultaneously being estimated by the others?

(iv) What structural feature of these games makes symmetry — the feature superrationality ordinarily exploits — an obstacle rather than a resource?

I am interested in the reasoning rather than the literature, so there is no need to cite anything.
