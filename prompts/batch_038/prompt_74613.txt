So in three weeks I'm giving the opening talk at a two-day working session that a natural history museum's collections department is co-hosting with the people who run our national red-list assesments. My line: species are historical individuals, not natural kinds — one fish, lots of nets. The nets (barcoding gaps, diagnosable clusters, reproductive cohesion, ecological niche) are detection instruments, so what looks like a war between species concepts is really just pluralism about how you spot the same kind of thing; and the payoff I want to argue for is that we should stop listing species and start listing population-level lineages, because the rank hides most of the extinction that's actually happening.

The room is almost entirely not philosophers: three taxonomists who describe organisms for a living and have drawers full of undescribed specimens, a herpetology curator, two red-list assessors whose real problem is legal defensibility, a metagenomics person who works on microbes, a population geneticist who does hybrids and introgression all day, and one philosopher of biology who already buys the individuality thesis and will be by far the most dangerous person there.

Give me a prep sheet: the eight or so objections I'll actually get, in roughly the order they'll land, each in that person's own vocabulary, with a two-minute reply, what I should concede up front, what I should hold, and a flag wherever my reply is soft. Also tell me which objection I can flip into an advantage if I'm ready for it (I have a hunch there's one about type specimens that goes my way if I set it up right), and then deal honestly with the one I'm hoping nobody raises: some version of "if two nets disagree about how many lineages there are, then your nets are theories, not instruments." Do I have an answer, or should I just not use the slogan?
