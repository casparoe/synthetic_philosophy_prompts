I'm a novelist — literary fiction, mostly third person, one book out with a small press and a second one giving me trouble. You can think of me as somebody who has spent twenty years thinking about characters exclusively in craft terms: what does this person want, what do they believe about themselves that's wrong, where's the desire-line, is the motivation legible by page forty, does the turn land. That's my native language and I'm fluent in it to the point of not noticing it. My new problem: the narrator of the book I'm drafting is a neuroscientist who has genuinely talked herself out of thinking that anybody, including herself, has beliefs or desires, and my editor's note basically says the first hundred pages have no people in them. Fair enough. So I've been reading around eliminative materialism — the Churchland-ish line that folk psychology is a stagnant theory whose central posits won't line up with anything a finished neuroscience says, so they should go the way of phlogiston and witches rather than get reduced to brain states — plus the instrumentalist alternative that treats belief-talk as a useful predictive strategy instead of a description of anything, plus the pushback that eliminativism undercuts itself, and the pushback that folk psychology isn't really a theory in the relevant sense to begin with.

Here's what I want. Act as a philosopher of mind who reads a lot of fiction and is willing to get technical with a smart non-specialist. First, do the prep work out loud for a paragraph or two: line up the main claims and moves in that debate against the craft concepts I actually use — motivation, desire-line, interiority, free indirect style, the unreliable narrator, dramatic irony, character arc, foreshadowing, what "show don't tell" is even showing. Think carefully here and don't rush to a verdict; I want to see the mapping before the conclusion. Then restate the eliminativist case itself in that craft vocabulary, as faithfully as you can manage: what does the claim that beliefs dont exist look like when the only things you're permitted to say are things about how characters get built and how readers track them? Keep the translation honest. Don't quietly assume craft talk is already non-committal, or already instrumentalist, just because that makes your job easier, and don't smuggle your own preferred resolution in and label it translation.

Then the part I actually care about, which is the residue report. Go claim by claim: which pieces of the eliminativist argument survive intact in craft talk, which survive only if you bend something, and which have no expression at all in the way I think about fiction. Where something doesn't survive, tell me which side the failure sits on — is the craft vocabulary missing something real (it has no way to ask whether an attribution is true, only whether it reads), or does the philosophical claim start looking less substantial once you notice it was arguing against a theory-shaped caricature of what ordinary people, and novelists, are doing when they say someone wanted something? End on the practical question, but without turning it into a pep talk: given your residue report, is my narrator's position writable in third person at all, or is the reason those first hundred pages feel empty that I'm trying to render in scene something that isn't the sort of thing a scene can hold? No bibliography and no reading list please — I'm mostly offline and I don't want invented page numbers, just the argument. Naming philosophers is fine when it helps me keep the positions seperate.
