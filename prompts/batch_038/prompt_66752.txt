ok so I'm deep in Politics book 1 — the money chapters, where he's separating running a household from making money for its own sake — and instead of a summary I want to hand my reading group a poem that does the argument. Full sestina please, all 39 lines, strict permuation, no cheating and no free verse sneaking in halfway through.

The six end-words, in the order they should appear in the first stanza: shoe, use, need, weight, stamp, gold.

Here's the stuff I want the form to actually carry, not just decorate:

- the word 'use' has to slide. In the early stanzas it should mean the obvious thing — a shoe is for wearing, grain for eating, the proper job of the item. By the later stanzas that same end-word should have drifted into meaning turnover, being traded on, keeping the thing moving through hands. Same word at the end of the line, different sense each time. That drift is the whole point: exchange-value starts out parasitic on use-value and then walks off without it, and I want people to feel it happen through the repetition instead of being told about it.

- one stanza should stage why money turns up at all: barter falls over when the two needs don't line up in time or place, so people settle on something handy and useful in itself — iron, silver — and at first it's weighed out, and then somebody stamps it so nobody has to weigh it every single time. That's where 'weight' and 'stamp' earn their slots.

- another stanza should hold the uncomfortable part: the token only counts because everyone agrees it counts. Money is there by convention, not by nature, and the moment people stop honoring the stamp it's a lump of metal that can't feed anybody. I want that fragility on the page, not glossed over.

- and by the end it has to be clear the two ways of getting things differ in kind, not just in degree. A household's needs are bounded — they get satisfied and then stop — but money-making has no stopping point, so it starts treating the limit as an obstacle and the means as the goal. The sestina's own rotation, six words coming back forever in shifted order and never resolving, should feel like that second kind. The envoi is what finally breaks the loop.

- for the envoi: Midas. Everything gold, nothing edible. Three lines, and the point I want landed is that a thing whose entire function is to be exchanged fails at the exact moment you need it to be used — gold comes back around to the shoe and the shoe is still just a shoe.

Stay inside the ancient frame: agora, obols, stamped silver, the oikos. No crypto jokes, no fiat-currency sermon, no modern names. One glancing nod to the present is allowed if it's really glancing. Someone who knows the chapters should nod; someone who's never read them should just get a good poem that leaves the distinction stuck in their head.
