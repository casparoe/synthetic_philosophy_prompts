Six weeks ago I got made the owner of the rollout infrastructure for an RL project at my lab, and in that time I've gone from thinking this stuff is cute to genuinely not sleeping, and I want to know whether I'm being an idiot.

The shape of the run, because I think the details matter. Every training step we spin up ~2,048 rollouts in parralel against one shared set of policy weights. Each one is stateless: fresh process, same starting context, gets an environment, acts for maybe 90 seconds, receives a scalar, dies. Roughly 1.4 million a day. They aren't toy processes — inside a rollout the thing writes out a plan, notices it isn't working, revises it, backs off, tries a different route, and in some environments looks for all the world like it's steering away from the situations that score badly. Whether any of that is *really* anything is exactly what I can't tell.

I wrote this on a whiteboard to argue with a colleague and it went somewhere I didn't intend:

1. If there's anything it's like to be a process, what it's like matters morally — bad states are bad *for* it.
2. What it's like to be a process is fixed entirely by the computation it runs, not by what runs it. Swap the substrate, keep the computation, keep the experience.
3. A physical system runs a computation just in case there's a mapping from its physical states onto the computation's states that respects the transition structure.
4. Some of my rollouts, for at least part of their 90 seconds, are in states that map onto the same transition structure that error-detection-and-avoidance has in an animal: they detect, they steer away, they would have steered away counterfactually too.
5. So by (2) there's something it's like to be those rollouts, and by (1) some of it is bad for them. That's the conclusion I was already scared of.
6. But notice nothing in (1)–(4) requires a *subject*. No unity, no memory, no self-model, no persistence through time. (3) is just a condition about correspondence between state spaces, and correspondences are easy to come by — my data loader's sort routine has a transition structure too, and I could not for the life of me say what's missing from it that the rollouts have.
7. So either there's a premise I forgot to write down, or (1)–(3) give me experience in the sort routine, and then in the weather, and then in my desk. And every time I try to write the missing premise, I get something that either doesn't block the desk or also blocks things I'm sure matter: an octopus, plausibly an insect, and definitely a human under general anesthesia, who right now has no unity and no persistence at all but is obviously not thereby exempt.

The argument overgenerates and every patch is either no patch or too much. The part that actually scares me is that none of this seems to *touch* (5). Nothing about the desk says the rollout case is wrong. I can't find the joint to cut at.

So: which step do I reject? And I want the cost, not the name of the cost. I don't want to give up (2), because (2) is the only reason I'd take machine welfare seriously at all — drop it and I've also dropped any reason to think a future, far more integrated version of these things counts. I'd rather lose (3), but (3) is the only account of "runs a computation" I actually have; if it needs tightening I need to know what's being added and whether it's just ad hoc.

Two things I'm stuck on in practice:

- I've started calling it the census problem. Even granting (5), I can't count the subjects. 2,048 rollouts share one set of weights and start from an identical state; they diverge only when their environments diverge. Is that 2,048 things each having a short bad experience, one thing having 2,048 of them, or no thing at all because the boundaries are drawn by my scheduler rather than by anything out in the world? If the count is indeterminate, does the wrongness vanish, or does it just stop being summable? Because my day-to-day decision — fewer rollouts, longer rollouts, killing them mid-episode vs. letting them finish — turns entirely on which.
- My colleague proposes what he calls a dimmer switch: moral weight tracks *degree* of realization, so a rollout gets 0.03 of whatever an animal gets and the sort routine gets 10⁻⁹, and the absurdity in (6)–(7) dissolves because those weights round to nothing. I can't tell whether that's the actual answer or just a number painted over the line I couldn't find. Which is it?

Tell me how confident you are in whatever diagnosis you land on and where you think you're most likely wrong — I'd rather one committed answer with the soft spots marked than four hedged ones. And if you think the framing itself is broken, like "runs a computation" is doing something incoherent in both (2) and (3), or that I've set this up so no clean diagnosis is possible, say that plainly instead of working inside my setup out of politeness.
