I've worked in bars and kitchens since I was 17 and I've moved something like nine times, three of them to different countries, so the rules on tipping and queuing and crossing the street have changed on me more times than I can count. In one place if you don't leave 20% you've basically stolen from the waiter; two borders over, leaving money on the table is kind of an insult and the waiter's on a real wage. In one town I got dirty looks for crossing on red with an empty street; in the one I grew up in everybody does it and waiting for the light is what tourists do. I spent a long time thinking about why I actually felt bad doing the thing, not just scared of getting yelled at, and I came up with an argument. It felt airtight for like a year and now I think it proves way too much and I can't find where it breaks.

1. Take any of these — the tip, which side of the door you stand on, whether you cross against the light. There's nothing in the act itself that makes it good or bad. Which one you pick is a coin flip.
2. But once enough people in a place do it and count on other people doing it, breaking it costs them. They planned around you, they lose time or money, or they just feel like the place has stopped working.
3. Costing people like that, for no reason, is wrong.
4. So the wrongness is coming entirely from the fact that the people around you have settled on expecting it. Nothing else is doing any work.
5. So what's wrong for you is set by wherever you happen to be standing. Same act, wrong here, fine there — and that's not a bug, that's the whole point. (This is the step that felt really right. This is why I felt like a jerk jaywalking in a town where nobody does.)
6. But then take a place where the settled expectation is that the shopkeeper's cousins get served before everyone who's been in line forty minutes. Or a village where nobody reports a certain kind of theft because that's just how its done and everyone expects everyone else to keep quiet. Same shape exactly: converged expectation, real cost to breaking it. My argument makes those binding too.
7. Worse, it makes them binding on me as a visitor, so I'd be the one doing wrong by not going along with it.
8. And a reformer is by definition somebody breaking a rule everyone expects them to keep. So on this argument every reformer is acting wrongly right up until the convention tips, and then the people who were persecuting them are the ones acting wrongly. Which is nuts.

So either the whole thing collapses into "whatever the locals do is right," or one of these steps is bad. I'd like to keep 2 and 3 honestly, because the feeling of "these people are counting on me and I cost them something" is exactly what I was trying to explain in the first place, and I dont want to be told I was wrong to feel bad about the jaywalking. But I can't live with 6 through 8.

So: which step do you reject, and why did it look fine when it went past? What does rejecting it cost you — I want the price, not just the fix. And then run your fixed version through these and tell me what comes out, because I don't trust a repair that happens to dodge my hard cases:

- empty street, 3am, nobody anywhere for a mile, town where nobody crosses on red. Wrong or not?
- same situation but somebody's on their balcony watching and now thinks it's fine to do it too. Does that change the answer, or is that move a cheat?
- I'm new in town and nobody told me you're supposed to leave 20%. I leave nothing and go home happy. Have I done anything wrong, and to who?
- a convention where everyone keeps it up only because everyone wrongly thinks everyone else approves of it, and if you asked them privately they'd all say they hate it. Still binding?
- the tipping one across the border, where the expectation runs the other way and the waiter's on a real wage. My argument says I'm obliged to be rude there. I'm pretty sure that's wrong, but tell me why without just saying "because the locals are wrong," which is the thing I'm trying to get away from.

If you think two of my steps are bad rather than one, name both and say which one does the real damage. And if you think 5 is actually fine and the problem is somewhere I didn't even list, that's an answer too — but then you have to show me where.
