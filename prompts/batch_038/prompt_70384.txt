Please restate the multiple-realizability argument against the mind–brain identity theory in the working vocabulary of a medicinal chemist: ligands and targets, orthosteric versus allosteric binding sites, structure–activity relationships, affinity as distinct from efficacy, functional assays, biased signaling, receptor reserve. For context on who is asking, I design compounds against G-protein-coupled receptors for a living and have no philosophy training beyond one undergraduate elective; the vocabulary above is completely fluent for me and the philosophical vocabulary is not, so I am asking for a translation and not for an introduction to the field.

Cover, in turn: Putnam's original move against type identity; the standard reply that type identities need not be knowable in advance of experience and are therefore not threatened by the mere possibility of other realizers; Block's thought experiments in which the functional organization is instantiated but something seems clearly absent, together with his diagnosis of what goes wrong when functional analysis is pushed down through levels; and Kim's worry about whether a mental state that is nothing over and above its physical realizer can have causal work left for it to do. Render each in terms I could use in a lab meeting without embarrassing myself.

Three things I want you to confront rather than step around.

First, mark every place where your rendering depends on a functional role being fixed independently of how it is measured. In my field a function is always assay-relative: the same compound reads as a full agonist in one preparation and a partial agonist in another, depending on receptor reserve and coupling efficiency, and there is no fact about its efficacy apart from a specified assay. Tell me whether philosophical functional roles are assay-relative in the same way, or whether functionalism needs a measurement-independent notion of the role, and if the latter, what is supposed to supply it.

Second, my field contains the inverse of multiple realizability as well: a promiscuous compound discharging several distinct functional roles at once through several targets. Ask whether the philosophical debate has a counterpart to this and whether it survives. Functional roles in what I have read are individuated by their causal relations to other roles, and I cannot see how one physical realizer discharging many roles is meant to be handled.

Third, note that a receptor is itself defined by what it does rather than by what it is made of. Tell me honestly whether my vocabulary is really any more physical than the philosophers', or whether the contrast I have been assuming between concrete mechanism and abstract role is largely illusory.

Then give the residue report: which claims translate cleanly, which translate only with distortion, and which have no expression at all in my vocabulary. Be specific in each case — name the claim, show your rendering, and identify the exact point where it fails. Do not smuggle my framework's assumptions in and present them as translations; where you had to stipulate something to make a mapping work, flag the stipulation as a stipulation.

Close with a verdict rather than a survey. Either the residue shows that my vocabulary is missing something real, or it shows that the philosophers' disagreement is less substantive than it appears; commit to one, give your reasons, and say which parts of your own translation above you would withdraw if the other verdict were correct. Do not settle for the observation that each framework has something to teach the other. Finally, if you need a pharmacological term you are not certain of, say that you are uncertain rather than coining one.
