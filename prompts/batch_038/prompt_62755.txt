**Role.** You are a moral philosopher who specialises in population ethics and, more narrowly, in the aggregation of infinitely many welfare locations. You are equally at home with the normative dialectic and with the elementary machinery the cases below require: partial sums, Cesàro means, discount factors, permutation invariance, and the difference between a preorder and a total order.

**Task.** Think carefully, and then write an essay of roughly 2,000–2,500 words on the controversy set out below. The essay must weigh the competing positions seriously, and it must reach and defend a definite verdict. Do not end on a non-committal note.

**The setup.** Consider possible futures, each of which assigns a welfare level to each of the days 1, 2, 3, … without end. Welfare at any single location is bounded, so no day has infinite value; only the number of locations is infinite. Let S_X(n) denote the cumulative welfare of world X up to and including day n. Define the following worlds:

- **α**: w(n) = 1 for every n.
- **β**: w(n) = 2 if n is odd, 0 if n is even.
- **γ**: w(n) = 0 if n is odd, 2 if n is even.
- **δ**: w(n) = 1 for every n, except w(1) = 100.
- **ε**: w(n) = 1 for every n, except w(n) = 2 whenever n is a perfect square.
- **ζ**: w(n) = 1 for every n, except w(n) = 0 whenever n is a perfect square.
- **η**: w(n) = 1 for every n, except w(n) = 2 for every n from 10⁹ to 10⁹ + 10⁶ inclusive.

**The controversy.** Any ranking of these worlds must be generated by some criterion. The principal families are: discounted summation, which ranks worlds by Σ δⁿ⁻¹w(n) for some fixed δ strictly between 0 and 1; overtaking and its relatives, which rank by the behaviour of S_X(n) − S_Y(n) as n grows — noting that "eventually non-negative", "eventually strictly positive", "bounded difference", "positive Cesàro limit" and "positive limit inferior" are five different criteria which do not always agree; lexicographic or non-Archimedean orderings, which compare long-run averages first and break ties by earlier or later terms; and the deflationary option, on which no complete betterness ordering of infinite worlds is available, so that the honest verdict is incomparability, or a refusal to aggregate at all.

The desiderata a criterion might be asked to meet are:

- **(D1)** Completeness and transitivity.
- **(D2)** Strong Pareto: if X is at least as good as Y at every day and strictly better at some day, then X is better than Y.
- **(D3)** Anonymity: the ranking is unchanged by permuting the days. You must say explicitly whether you require invariance under *all* permutations or only under *finite* ones, and defend that choice. Bear in mind that β and γ stand to one another by a permutation which moves infinitely many days, so the two versions of (D3) can come apart here.
- **(D4)** Amnesia: two worlds which differ only at finitely many days are equally good.
- **(D5)** Non-triviality: the criterion must not collapse into ranking all of α, β, γ, δ, ε, ζ and η as equally good.

Note that (D2) and (D4) cannot both be satisfied, given δ. Note also that (D1), (D2) and the unrestricted version of (D3) are widely held to be jointly unsatisfiable; you should assess that claim rather than merely assert it.

**Questions your essay must address.** Show your arithmetic wherever a verdict depends on it.

1. Does β beat α, lose to α, or stand incomparable to it? How does your answer change if you move from the "eventually non-negative" formulation of overtaking to the "eventually strictly positive" formulation, and can you live with that sensitivity?
2. Given that γ is a relabelling of β, what does your criterion say about β versus γ, and about α versus γ? Trace the consequences for discounted summation in particular, including whether the choice of δ can be defended as anything other than arbitrary.
3. Are ε and ζ comparable to α, and does the answer survive a Cesàro-mean or density-based treatment? If such a treatment satisfies (D3) and (D4) but flouts (D2) and (D5), is that a good bargain?
4. Both δ and η Pareto-dominate α, yet the leading criteria disagree about which of δ and η is better. Work out what discounted summation and what overtaking each say here, and say which verdict you accept and what it commits you to about the moral significance of temporal position.
5. Amnesia and strong Pareto cannot coexist. Which do you surrender, and why is that the cheaper concession? Your answer must not be ad hoc: it must follow from an account of what the betterness relation between infinite worlds is *for*.

**Requirements.**

- Begin with a short planning section, headed "Plan" and no longer than 200 words, in which you identify the desiderata you treat as non-negotiable and the two or three live contenders you intend to press. Only then write the essay proper.
- Write in continuous prose; section headings are welcome, bullet lists are not.
- No bibliography, no footnotes, no citations and no reading list. Argue from the structure of the cases, not from authority. Refer to positions descriptively ("the discounted approach", "the overtaking approach"); if you name a philosopher, do so only where you are confident the attribution is right, and never attribute a wording you cannot verify. Do not invent quotations.
- Confront the strongest objection to your own verdict, including the objection that the whole enterprise is idle because no actual choice concerns an infinite future. Explain why the idealisation earns its keep, or concede that it does not.
- If your conclusion assigns incomparability anywhere, say what an agent facing a forced choice between incomparable worlds ought to do. Incomparability must not be permitted to do all of the theoretical work.
- State your final verdict in a closing paragraph: which criterion you adopt (or construct), which single desideratum you are prepared to give up to secure it, and the specific verdicts it delivers for at least β versus α, δ versus η, and ε versus α.
- Write in British English.
