My brother-in-law got into this "time average vs ensemble average" thing and by the end of Christmas dinner he'd refuted all of decision theory. His argument, as numbered as I can manage:

(1) Rational choice has to be about what actually happens to you as your own life runs forward once, not about an average over imaginary copies of you in parallel universes.

(2) Expected utility theory ranks options by exactly that imaginary-copy average, so it's evaluating the wrong quantity.

(3) When wealth changes multiplicatively, the two come apart. Repeat this gamble forever: each round your wealth is multiplied by 1.5 or by 0.6 on a coin flip. The average multiplier is 1.05, so expected wealth grows, but the growth rate is sqrt(1.5 × 0.6) = 0.949, so the typical path loses about 5% a round and crawls toward zero.

(4) So expected utility tells you to play a game that ruins you, which means its not just incomplete, it's false.

(5) The replacement isn't a matter of taste either: maximizing the growth rate is the one criterion that isn't eventually beaten with probability 1, so the fraction of your wealth to stake — the Kelly fraction — is fixed by the arithmetic of the gamble alone.

(6) Which means your preferences, your time horizon, and whether you'll ever face the bet again are all irrelevant to how much risk you should take.

(7) So my brother, who needs his savings for a house deposit in three years and can't stand variance, is making an arithmetic error rather than a value judgement when he stakes less than the growth-optimal amount.

I'd like to keep (1) and the numbers in (3) if I can help it, and (7) I flat out reject. Which step goes, why did it look fine going in, and what do I pay for killing it? Give me exactly five numbered points, a sentence or two each, no preamble and no wrap-up.
