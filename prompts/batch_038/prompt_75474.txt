I run a small philosophy-of-science podcast and next month's guest is a stratigrapher-turned-philosopher — call her Dr. R. Her view is that the Anthropocene debate keeps going in circles because everybody assumes a boundary has to be a signal you can point at in a deposit, and she wants to swap that for a counterfactual test: the epoch starts at the first point after which the Earth system's trajectory would have been markedly different had humans never existed. On that test she lands on 1610 and the CO2 dip in the ice-core record rather than the mid-century bomb spike, and she thinks the ICS subcommision was the wrong body to settle it anyway — she'd rather have some mixed panel of geologists, historians and Indigenous representatives vote. She's charming, slippery, and very good at saying "it's a change of kind, not degree."

Give me eight to ten questions that actually put pressure on that, no softballs, each with the follow-up keyed to the answer she's most likely to give. I want at least one on how you'd detect a counterfactual in a core sample, one on why her test doesn't just get satisfied eight thousand years ago by rice paddies and methane, one on whether a marker caused by the deaths of tens of millions of people raises problems the plutonium spike doesn't, one on what the 2024 rejection of the formal epoch does to her position, and one on whether her criterion collapses into "as many Anthropocenes as there are questions you ask." Plus a who-decides question she can't get out of by saying "democracy."

Then end with one line in the form Verdict: <hold the line | move to another date | drop the counterfactual criterion> — your honest read of where the interview leaves her.
