The exam question was:

"Distinguish a contradiction in conception from a contradiction in the will in Kant's Formula of Universal Law, and use two of Kant's own four examples from Groundwork 4:422 to illustrate the difference. Then say whether you think the universal law test yields determinate results, or whether everything turns on how the maxim gets described."

Their answer, transcribed from the scan (apologies, the missing apostrophes are my typing, not theirs):

"The Formula of Universal Law says to act only on that maxim that you can at the same time will to become a universal law of nature. There are two ways a maxim can fail. First, you cant even conceive of a world in which everyone acts on it — the maxim would destroy itself, which is what Kant means by a contradiction in conception, and this generates perfect (strict) duties. Second, you can conceive of such a world without contradiction but you cant consistently will it, a contradiction in the will, and this generates imperfect (wide) duties, which leave latitude about when and how much.

Kant gives four examples. A man who kills himself out of self-love fails at the level of conception, because a law of nature whose principle was to destroy life by means of the very feeling whose job is to promote it would contradict itself — a perfect duty to yourself. Someone who lets his talents rust fails at the level of the will: you can picture a South Sea islander world where nobody develops anything, but as a rational being you necessarily will the development of your capacities, so you cant will that as a universal law — an imperfect duty to yourself. The false promise is a contradiction in the will too, though, and this is the important point: the reason it fails is that if everyone made promises they had no intention of keeping, nobody would believe promises any more and the whole institution would collapse, so my maxim defeats itself in practice. That is a practical contradiction, not a logical one. Which shows the two failure modes track two different kinds of wrongness — contradiction in conception is logical impossibility, contradiction in the will is closer to imprudence, and thats why imperfect duties read more like strong advice than obligations.

On determinacy: Kant gets described as a deontologist, but the universal law test is obviously sensitive to consequences, since what makes false promising fail is the state of affairs that universal adoption would produce. That is a problem, because if the test looks at outcomes it isnt giving you anything distinctly moral. Worse, the description of the maxim does all the work. Ask 'may I make a false promise?' and you get a contradiction. Ask 'may I make a false promise on a Tuesday, to a stranger, for 500 euros, to pay a debt that would otherwise ruin me?' and you get a world where that happens now and then and nothing collapses at all. Any maxim can be made universalizable by narrowing it, so the test comes out empty — that is basically Hegel's empty formalism charge, and it's why Kant has to bring in the Formula of Humanity to do any actual work, even though he says the formulas are all equivalent. So the test is either trivially satisfiable or arbitrary, and it cant produce determinate duties."
