our reading group has been working through heuristic arguments — the kind where you get a number or a qualitative prediction without a proof, by treating the parts of a problem as roughly independent and multiplying the pieces together. half the group thinks this can be given real mathematical footing; the other half thinks it's irreducibly a matter of judgment, and that trying to formalize it would just relocate the judgment. we're stuck on one step in particular, which i've taken to calling the presumption of independence: you have estimate A and estimate B, you say their errors don't line up, so you combine them — but you can't prove the errors don't line up, and usually you haven't even looked. my own gloss on what practitioners actually do is a mechanism-naming rule: you're entitled to assume no coupling until somebody can point at a specific mechanism that would produce one, and then you correct for exactly that mechanism and nothing else. whether that rule is a deep fact about the world or a cheap trick that usually happens to work is the fight.

the test case i brought last week was the twin prime heuristic. naively, a random integer near n is prime with probability about 1/ln n, so a pair (n, n+2) should be twin with probability about 1/(ln n)², and summing gives about x/(ln x)² pairs below x. that's obviously wrong as stated — it doesn't know about parity, and it doesn't know that n ≡ 1 mod 3 forces n+2 ≡ 0 mod 3. the standard repair is the Hardy–Littlewood correction, the product over odd primes p of (1 − 1/(p−1)²), about 0.66, with the count coming out as 2·C₂·x/(ln x)². the interesting question for us is what that repair proves. our formalizer says: see, there was a probability space all along — uniform on 1..N with N→∞ — the two events were genuinely dependent, we conditioned on the small-prime residue classes and got an exact constant, so independence was never presumed, it was computed; that's what a heuristic argument looks like when it succeeds. the other camp says that's backwards: nobody derived the correction from a space, somebody assumed independence, noticed the estimate was off by a factor, went hunting for a named mechanism, found divisibility by small primes, and patched only that — the space is post-hoc furniture, and the actual epistemic work was done by the presumption plus the search.

two wrinkles, because these are what broke the session and i want them in the piece. first, there's no uniform distribution on the integers, so the formalizer's space has to be natural density, and natural density isn't countably additive and doesn't even exist for every set — perfectly describable sets have no density, like the numbers whose binary expansion has an odd number of digits, or the numbers whose decimal expansion starts with 1. so "the space was there all along" is doing a lot more work than it looks like. second, the parity barrier: sieve methods provably can't distinguish numbers with an even number of prime factors from numbers with an odd number, which is a big part of why the twin prime conjecture is still open even though everybody trusts the heuristic. that's a case where we know there's a coupling we can't name-and-correct, so the mechanism-naming rule doesn't merely lack a justification, it fails visibly — and people still bank on the prediction.

so: write me a dialogue between two characters. one is a number theorist who wants heuristic arguments reduced to something rigorous, and who holds that "independent" has to mean independence in an explicit space you can write down — a random integer up to N, a random hash function, a randomly drawn model — and that a heuristic is only as good as the space behind it. the other defends the presumption as a defeasible default: no coupling until a mechanism is named, and any demand that the default itself be earned is either impossible or circular, because the proof would have to lean on further independence assumptions it can't discharge any more than the first one did. give them names and put them somewhere concrete — my suggestion is a whiteboard in an empty seminar room twenty minutes after everyone else has left, with the twin prime product still half-erased at the top, but you can do better if you have an idea.

some things i need from it:
- neither one is a pushover and neither one is the villain. each has to answer the other's actual last move rather than the easy version of it, and both should be allowed to concede where conceding is right — but don't let it dissolve into a handshake where they realize they meant the same thing all along. end somewhere specific and slightly uncomfortable.
- the twin prime correction, the natural density problem, and the parity barrier all have to be engaged with substantively, not name-dropped. someone who knows sieve theory should recognize what's being said about parity, and someone who doesn't should still be able to follow.
- the formalizer has to face the regress objection head-on: the space they write down encodes independence assumptions it can't justify either. the presumption theorist has to face the objection that mechanism-naming is unfalsifiable in practice — "no one has named a mechanism" is perfectly compatible with an enormous unnamed correlation, so their default licenses exactly the confident errors that matter.
- transpose the whole argument at least once onto a machine-learning-flavored heuristic, since that's what half the group actually cares about. use this one: a paper argues that a system with a planner and a seperately trained value function will rarely both be badly wrong about the same action, so joint failure is roughly the product of the marginals, say 1% × 1%; the rejoinder is that both components were trained on overlapping data against the same benchmark and shaped by the same reward model, so their errors are correlated by construction — the way n and n+2 share residue classes whether or not you've noticed. don't let the characters settle it with a slogan about correlation.
