I am auditing a joint module this term between our politics department and our drama school, and my own formation is in political theory rather than performance studies, which means I have read Rousseau's letter to d'Alembert on the theatre twice without ever feeling that I have properly reconciled its two halves: the long argument that the stage pleases rather than improves, flatters public taste instead of correcting it, and leaves the spectator inert, and then the sudden turn at the end towards open-air republican festivities in which the assembled people are meant to be their own entertainment. What I want to test that tension against is a real episode: on the evening of Friday 18 November 2016, Mike Pence, then vice-president-elect, attended a performance of *Hamilton* on Broadway, was greeted with a mixture of booing and applause as he entered the house, and at the curtain call the actor playing Aaron Burr, Brandon Victor Dixon, stepped forward and delivered a prepared address from the stage to him by name, speaking on behalf of a deliberately diverse company and asking the incoming administration to hear them and to uphold what the cast called American values. Pence heard little of it in the room and was briefed afterwards; the following morning Donald Trump posted a demand that the company apologise to him, and the episode thereupon occupied several days of national news coverage. The address was not improvised: it had been composed in advance and cleared with the show's author.

Please give me a disciplined mapping before you give me a verdict. First, reconstruct what Rousseau's account of the theatre actually commits him to about such a moment, taking seriously at least four distinct elements of it: his rejection of the purgative defence of drama, on the grounds that the stage relieves us of passions rather than instilling or redirecting them; his claim that a performance pleases by conforming to what its audience already admires, so that the moral effect of a successful evening in the theatre is the audience's satisfaction with its own sentiments; his treatment of Molière's misanthrope as the case in which an honest man is made ridiculous while the accommodating man of the world is made sympathetic; and his institutional worry about a theatre in a small, industrious republic, where spectatorship competes with the civic activity a republic requires of its citizens. Say which of these bite on the *Hamilton* curtain call and which do not, and be explicit about the fact that a commercial Broadway house in a metropolis of eight million is not the Genevan setting Rousseau had in mind — if that difference disables part of his analysis, say so rather than smuggling it past.

Second, and this is where I would like you to work with a distinction I have been developing rather than one you will find in the literature, so please treat it as a proposal to be assessed and not as established terminology. I want to distinguish the 'sealed auditorium', in which everything that happens is framed as performance and discharged as such, from what I am calling the 'addressable stage', in which a named political actor is present in the house and the frame is deliberately broken by an utterance aimed at him as an official rather than as an audience member. On my proposal, the curtain call address is an instance of the second, and the interesting question is whether breaking the frame converts a spectacle into something like the civic gathering Rousseau recommends at the end of the letter — that is, whether the people on stage and the people in the seats briefly ceased to be a divided pair of performers and spectators and became instead a single body addressing one of its own. Please apply what I will call the spillage test to the episode: did the address produce any act that was not itself a performance; did it alter anyone's dispositions to act outside the theatre in a way that can be traced; and did the subsequent coverage simply return the act to the status of spectacle, with the president-elect's demand for an apology functioning as a rival performance competing for the same audience? Then tell me, honestly, whether Rousseau's framework can accommodate that distinction at all, or whether I have imposed a category his text does not supply. I would especially like your view on the awkward possibility that the episode confirms rather than refutes him — that the address moved nothing, that the pleasure the house took in its own righteousness was the real product of the evening, and that what looked like civic speech was only spectacle at a higher order of recursion. Note finally where the theory goes silent: Rousseau has a great deal to say about actors, about women in the audience, about the expense and idleness of theatregoing, and about festivals, but you should be candid about which features of a modern, media-saturated, ticketed performance he simply has no resources for. Please write the whole answer in British English.
