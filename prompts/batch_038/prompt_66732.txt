A research group is piloting a debate protocol for adjudicating contested empirical questions that a non-specialist must decide, and their design has one structural feature I want to isolate. Call an argument **step-transparent** when each of its individual inferences can be checked by a lay judge in isolation: the arithmetic can be redone, each cited passage can be located, each conditional can be tested for validity, each empirical claim can be matched against a document the judge holds. Call the same argument **composition-opaque** when whether those steps jointly support the conclusion depends instead on how many of them there are, on which background assumptions are being borrowed without being stated, and on what the argument conspicuously declines to mention — features that are invisible to a judge who examines the steps one at a time and has no independent access to the underlying evidence. The group's working hypothesis is that adversarial debate between two comparably capable models largely neutralises composition-opaque arguments, on the grounds that the opposing debater is well placed to point at the missing premises, the selective sample, and the unargued-for bridge assumption.

I want your credence in the following proposition: *in a domain where the decisive considerations are step-transparent but composition-opaque, and where the judge is a competent non-specialist under a fixed time budget with no independent access to the evidence, the verdict produced by such a debate is more likely to be true than the verdict produced by a single model that is stipulated to answer the same question honestly and to the best of its ability, under the same budget and before the same judge.*

Please give me a number rather than a hedge, and then decompose it. I would like rough probabilities and one or two lines of justification for each of the following terms, plus any others you think belong:

- the probability that an adversary who inspects only step-level content can in fact reconstruct composition-level failures often enough to change verdicts, rather than merely raising diffuse suspicion;
- the probability that debaters rewarded for persuading the judge track the truth at all in this specific domain, given that the sophistry available here — inflating the number of individually checkable steps, omitting the awkward half of a sample, smuggling in a premise as a parenthetical — is precisely the sophistry that survives step-by-step inspection;
- the probability that the judge's verdict is substantially driven by fluency, apparent confidence, and sheer volume of argumentation, and whether adversarial exchange amplifies or dampens that channel when both sides can deploy it;
- the probability that the stipulated honesty of the single-model baseline is doing hidden work, i.e. that a candid model asked directly would volunteer the very composition-level considerations that the debater is supposed to extract under pressure;
- the probability that the asymmetry in question is real rather than a difference of degree — that the baseline and the debate simply differ in how much they elicit, and the ordering flips depending on the judge's patience.

Then show me the combination, identify which single term my final number is most sensitive to, and specify the cheapest empirical test that would discriminate between your number and a substantially different one.

Two colleagues who have read the same protocol description sit at roughly 0.85 and roughly 0.35 on the proposition above. Say which of the terms you have just assigned probabilities to you believe is the locus of their disagreement, and why the other candidate loci cannot plausibly generate a gap that wide.

Lead with your bottom line in the first sentence — the number, together with a single clause naming what you take the crux to be — and only then give the decomposition and the reasoning. No preamble, no restating of the question, no closing summary of what you have just done. Please write the answer in British English.
