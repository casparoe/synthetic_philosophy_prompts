Retired three years ago from a government lab where I spent most of my career measuring trace metals in estuary sediments, and I've fallen into philosophy, mostly the part about classification, which turns out to be exactly what I argued about at work for thirty-five years without knowing there was a name for it. I've been keeping a list of my own verdicts on which categories cut nature at the joints and which ones are just us tidying up. I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

The list, in roughly the order I wrote them down:

1. Gold, oxygen, the elements. Real, no hesitation. Though I'll admit gold in my lab meant a mix of isotopes and nobody cared.
2. Heavy metal. Not real. Every textbook I own draws the line somewhere different, density over five, atomic number, chemical behaviour, toxicity, and the resulting lists don't overlap much. Useful word, fake joint.
3. Mineral. Real, even though as I remember it a committee has to vote on the borderline cases and has voted yes on things that looked to me like they shouldn't count, crystals grown by bacteria, phases out of slag heaps. The voting feels like it's tracking something rather than inventing it.
4. Vitamin. Not real. They're grouped by the deficiency disease they cure, not by what they are. Vitamin D is a precursor your own skin makes and behaves like a hormone once it's there. Chemistry can't find the group.
5. Soil classes, say podzols. Real. Yes, a survey draws a line at some clay percentage and some depth to a given horizon, and different countries put the lines in slightly different places. But I've stood in a pit in Finland and in Quebec and it was the same object, made the same way.
6. Those salamanders ringing the Central Valley in California, interbreeding all the way around except where the two ends meet and act like two species. I think there's no fact of the matter there and I'm comfortable with that.
7. But two populations of some bird that are identical in the hand, never meet, and come out deep apart on the mitochondrial DNA: I think those are two kinds. Nothing you can see separates them and I still want to say the difference is real.

Six and seven is the pair I can't reconcile. Also three and five against two: all three are threshold-and-cluster categories partly set by committee, and I've said yes to two of them and no to one.

My own tentative principle, which I know is crude: a term picks out a real kind when two independent methods, measuring different properties, land on the same partition, and when knowing something is a member lets you predict properties you never measured. Run that against seven and it says not-two-kinds, which I refuse to accept, and against six it also says no fact, which I do accept. So it's doing something right but can't be left as it stands.

So: is there one principle, or a small set of candidate ones, that gets most of these? State it precisely, what each clause is doing and which case forced it in. Then tell me which of my verdicts it can't keep, and don't try to rescue all seven, I'd rather lose one honestly than be handed a machine with eleven dials. And run it on two or three kinds I haven't listed, picking ones where I'd actually be able to tell if you're hand-waving. The one I keep going back and forth on is microplastics: a size threshold plus a polymer family plus a shape, which sounds exactly like heavy metal to me, and yet there's a real process making the stuff and it behaves consistently in the water column. I don't know which side of my own line it lands on.
