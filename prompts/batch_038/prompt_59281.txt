so I have to turn in a one-pager for a Groundwork reading group next week, and two of the people in it go straight for the jugular, so I want a consistency check before it's on paper. Here's where I stand, roughly in the order I'd defend it:

1. The universal law formula does actual work. It isn't empty formalism — some maxims fail on their own terms, and I don't need the humanity formula standing behind it doing the real argument.
2. Contradiction in conception gives you perfect duties, contradiction in will gives you imperfect ones, and I take that mapping seriously rather than as window dressing. It's supposed to cover the self-regarding cases too: developing your talents as an imperfect duty, suicide as a perfect one.
3. On the mechanics I'm on the practical side, not the logical one. A maxim fails when you cant coherently will it as a universal law together with the purpose you have in acting on it. The false promise — borrowing money you know you won't repay — fails because if everyone did that there'd be no such thing as a promise left for me to exploit. My own end defeats itself.
4. But I want to be really clear this isn't consequentialism wearing a trenchcoat. Wrongness never comes from the balance of good and bad in the world that results; nothing about how things go is what makes an act wrong. The free rider story isn't "universal lying would make the world worse," it's "my will comes apart."
5. On maxim description: you test the maxim the agent actually has, at the level they actually conceive it, and no gerrymandering it afterward until it comes out permissible. And the test is impartial in the strong sense — same maxim, same verdict, whoever's asking and whatever their circumstances happen to be.
6. I accept Kant's verdict in the murderer at the door case; lying is off the table even there. What I don't accept is the reason he gives in that essay, the bit about not being able to foresee what your lie will lead to, because that's exactly the outcome appeal I banned in 4. I keep the verdict, drop the argument.
7. Methodologically, the test has to be usable by an ordinary person in real time as a decision procedure, not just something a philosopher reconstructs afterward.
