I should say at the outset that I have no academic background in any of this. I manage a building-supplies yard, and the only reason I know the word "microdomain" is that a few years ago I picked up a popular book about the Copycat program — the one Hofstadter's group built to solve letter-string analogies — and I have been working through those puzzles on scrap paper ever since, usually with my sister, who is considerably less patient than I am. The game as I understand it: you are told that abc → abd, and then you are handed some other string and asked how it goes. What stuck with me from the book was not the answers but the machinery behind them — that a description of the change can slip to a neighboring concept when it fails to apply (successor slipping to predecessor, letter slipping to run of letters), that several descriptions of the same string are alive at once and compete rather than being consulted in a fixed order, and that the designers kept insisting the right answer is the one that falls out of the most coherent perception of the string rather than the one produced by the first rule you happen to think of.

I have now accumulated a list of my own verdicts, arrived at independently, and I want to know what principle, if any, they add up to. Holding abc → abd fixed as the given: (1) ijk → ijl — right, and obviously right, nothing else is in the running. (2) aabbcc → aabbcd — right; its rival aabbdd is perfectly describable, since you can advance the rightmost run of identical letters instead of the rightmost letter, but I reject it, because it alters two letters where one would have done. (3) abcabcabc → abcabcabd, for the same reason; abdabdabd alters three. (4) xyz → xyy — acceptable, and I think best; the slip from successor to predecessor is forced here and feels like a short step, whereas wrapping around to the front of the alphabet, xyz → xya, feels like a long step and I reject it, because the alphabet is a line and not a ring. (5) zzz → zzy — not acceptable; here I want to say the puzzle simply has no answer, or that every answer is arbitrary. I cannot defend this. Structurally it is the identical move to (4): rightmost letter, no successor, slip to predecessor. Yet (4) feels compelled and (5) feels like cheating. (6) cba → dba — this is what I would write, the c turns into a d wherever it happens to be sitting; but cba → cbb, advancing the rightmost letter, is just as describable, alters the same number of letters, and requires no slip at all, so I cannot claim my answer slips less, and I honestly do not know what I am claiming. (7) mrrjjj → mrrkkk — I prefer this to mrrjjk even though it alters three letters instead of one, which flatly contradicts (2), where I preferred the one-letter change over the two-letter one. The only thing I can offer in my own defense is that the runs in mrrjjj are one, two and three letters long, which looks like a pattern in its own right, whereas aabbcc is two-two-two, which looks like nothing at all. (8) Two I have not made up my mind about: aabbccc, which I cannot decide is more like aabbcc or more like mrrjjj; and a variant in which the alphabet is the days of the week, so that the successor of sun is mon — does my refusal to wrap around in (4) survive that change, or was it about letters rather than about edges?

Please take this on as an expert would: someone who has actually worked on models of analogy-making and conceptual fluidity, and who is equally comfortable doing the slow business of testing a principle against a batch of intuitions and then revising either. Think it through carefully rather than reaching for the standard answers to these puzzles — I have read those, and I want the principle that generates *my* verdicts, not the program's. Begin by brainstorming, before you commit to anything, four or five candidate principles that could account for some of this pattern, noting for each which verdicts it delivers and which it misses; include at least one deliberately crude candidate (a single quantity, such as fewest characters altered, or least conceptual distance slipped) and at least one deliberately baroque one, so that I can see what the added complexity actually purchases. Then give me your best candidate, stated precisely enough that I could apply it by hand to a string neither of us has ever seen: what quantities it compares, in what order, what breaks a tie, and what it says when nothing is admissible at all. Attach each clause to the verdict that forced it on you. Then run it on the two open cases in (8) and on two or three strings of your own choosing that you think would put it under the greatest strain, and tell me what it predicts I would answer — I will let you know if you are wrong about me. Finally, be candid about the cost. Verdicts (2) and (7) look to me as though no simple principle can keep both, and I suspect (4) and (5) cannot both be kept by any principle whatever; tell me which you would overturn, why, and whether what I am responding to in the ones I keep is something a rule of this kind could ever state — or whether I am merely squeamish about strings that look ugly. I am not after citations, a bibliography, or a survey of the literature; I want the principle and the reasoning that gets you to it.
