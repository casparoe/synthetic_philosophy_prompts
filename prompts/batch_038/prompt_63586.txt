I'm the recording secretary for my university's student senate and I've spent this semester trying to work out what rule we're actually applying, because the chair swears we have one and half the senators swear we don't. Backstory: a few years ago the senate passed a bylaw saying that when we take an official position on something that would bind other students or ask the administration to change a policy, the sponsor has to justify it "in terms all members can reasonably be expected to endorse." Nobody ever wrote down what that means, nobody has amended it, so it's just been applied case by case, and I'm the one who has to minute the reasoning. Here's the track record from the last two years:

- A resolution pushing the administration to fund a free breakfast program passed. The sponsor read a bit of the Gospel of Matthew about feeding people, then spent most of her five minutes on attendance data and a cost estimate. Nobody invoked the bylaw.
- A resolution asking the university to drop its ROTC contract was ruled inadmissible before a vote. The sponsor was a Quaker and said his whole reason was that killing is wrong and that he couldn't put it any other way; the chair asked twice for something else and he declined. Three senators said on the record that they agreed with him about ROTC and would have voted yes.
- Fossil fuel divestment passed. Sponsor cited an encyclical on caring for creation and then a slide of temperature data. One senator objected that the encyclical was doing real work in the speech and not just decoration, and was overruled.
- A resolution asking the student health center to stop handing out information about medication abortion was ruled inadmissible. Stated reason was that the fetus is a person from conception because scripture says so, and again the sponsor refused, when asked, to offer any other ground.
- A living wage resolution passed with a justification that never mentioned religion once: Kantian dignity, plus a fairly technical argument about background justice and what counts as voluntary agreement. Two senators complained that academic philosophy is no more "reasonably endorsable" than theology is, one said the bylaw obviously doesn't police philosophy, and the chair let it slide.
- A resolution honoring a local synagogue's 150th anniversary passed unanimously. Someone asked whether the bylaw applied; chair said no, it's expressive, it doesn't ask anybody to do anything.
- A resolution asking the administration to cancel classes during Easter week was ruled inadmissible, on the stated ground that it would push a majority religion's calendar onto everyone. But a companion item the same month, asking that required labs not be scheduled Friday afternoons, was ruled admissible and passed; the sponsor's reason was entirely about Muslim students not being made to choose between class and mosque, and no one objected.

Next week's item is the one I'm dreading. It asks the university to stop scheduling final exams on Saturday mornings. The sponsor is from a tiny evangelical group, and his reason — in the packet and in his speech — is that God commands a seventh-day rest and the university shouldn't arrange things so that observant students have to pick between an exam and obedience. No data, no appeal to fairness, no mention of the fact that the university already grants religious and medical exam accommodations anyway and that only a handful of students would be affected. A senator asked him during committee whether he'd accept a secular version of the argument and he said no, the religious reason IS the reason and he wasn't going to dress it up for us. Substantively it would probably pass, and several of the yes votes are the same people who waved through the divestment speech and are now saying this one is different. One of them has started calling the bylaw "that proviso thing" and claiming it only requires public reasons eventually, not up front, which I don't think is what the chair has been doing.

So: what rule best explains all seven of those precedents? Lay out the candidate rules explicitly — I can think of at least (i) religious reasons are fine so long as a public reason is also offered, whenever, (ii) religious reasons are fine so long as some public reason exists, whether or not anyone states it, (iii) we only police doctrines when the resolution would coerce people or impose one group's calendar, so philosophy and ceremonial stuff are exempt, (iv) what we're really tracking is minority-burdening versus majority-imposing, and (v) the bylaw is a figleaf and we're just voting our preferences and reaching for it when we lose. Say which precedents each rule fits cleanly and which ones it has to explain away or write off as errors. Then predict the chair's ruling on the Saturday-exam item and the senate's vote, with reasons — and separately tell me whether it should matter that the religious reason here is itself about religious liberty, i.e. second-order in a way the ROTC and abortion cases weren't. I also have to write the minutes, so I'd like to be able to say what standard we'd be adopting if we admit it and what different standard we'd be adopting if we don't, rather than just recording the outcome. And if the seven precedents genuinely underdetermine the ruling, describe the smallest hypothetical resolution that would split the candidate rules cleanly, so I can suggest it to whoever chairs next year.
