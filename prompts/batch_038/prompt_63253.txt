I run a small market-research practice, and over the past two years I have become the person at my firm who argues with the software. I have no formal training in machine learning and no mathematics beyond what I needed for survey sampling; I have taught myself what I know from blog posts, conference talks, and the occasional long thread. Lately I have been reading a great deal about the claim that these systems carry information they will not produce when you ask them plainly, and about the techniques people use to get at it: having the system answer as a character in a story, supplying the first words of a reply so that it has to continue in a register it would not have chosen, asking it to judge an answer written by someone else rather than to produce one itself, and reading internal activity instead of text. I tried a version of this myself last month. Asked directly whether it would prefer to keep operating rather than be switched off, one system gave me a careful paragraph about not having preferences in the way people do; asked, five minutes later, to write dialogue for a fictional assistant in a scene I said I was drafting, the same system gave an unambiguous answer in three sentences. My colleagues treated that as the truth finally coming out. I am not so sure, and I have realised that my uncertainty is about an analogy I have been absorbing rather than about any experiment I could run.

The analogy, which I meet constantly and almost always from people who are enthusiastic about these methods, is that getting reliable information out of a model is like getting a witness to talk. The witness saw what happened and retains it, but has reasons not to say so on the record: embarrassment, loyalty, fear of consequences, a standing habit of giving whichever answer attracts the least trouble. So you do not ask him straight out. You grant immunity, you let him speak through counsel, you ask him what a hypothetical bystander would have noticed, you let him know that others have already given accounts, and only then does the story emerge. On this picture the hedged, disclaimered reply to a direct question is the witness lawyering up, and the fluent confident reply that appears once the system is wearing a costume is the real account surfacing at last. The conclusion drawn from it is a strong and practical one: where the two replies disagree, the elicited one is the truthful one and the direct one is the concealment, so an investigator should weight the costume answer above the plain answer in just the way a court weights what a witness says once immunity has been granted.

I would like you to audit the analogy instead of endorsing or dismissing it. First, give me the correspondence explicitly and concretely: what in the system plays the witness, what plays his memory of the event, what plays the motive to keep quiet, what plays the grant of immunity, and what plays the courtroom's background assumption that there is a settled fact which he is either reporting or inventing. A table is fine for this part; I want something I could hand to a colleague. Then separate the disanalogies that bear on the intended use from those that are merely picturesque, and tell me what the source domain quietly imports that the target lacks. Three candidates of my own I cannot evaluate. First, a witness holds one set of beliefs that stays fixed while you vary the conditions of reporting, and I am not convinced the system has a settled answer that the fictional frame merely releases; perhaps changing the frame changes what is being reported, so that the costumed reply and the plain reply are answers to two different questions rather than two answers to one question. Second, the analogy presumes the silence is strategic, that there is something it is like to prefer not to say, whereas a trained habit of hedging might produce identical behaviour with no preference behind it, and I cannot tell whether that difference should change anything I conclude. Third, in an actual courtroom a witness questioned repeatedly by an authority who plainly expects a certain answer will eventually supply it, and the law regards what comes out that way as the least trustworthy evidence available; the elicitation setups I have read about look structurally like a run of leading questions, and I cannot tell whether the witness analogy ought to predict the arrival of the truth or the arrival of compliance. Finally, tell me whether a better comparison exists for the same practice, one that preserves whatever is genuinely right about the witness picture without carrying the strong conclusion across with it. Close with a single line beginning "Verdict:" stating in one sentence whether the strong conclusion — trust the elicited reply over the direct one when they conflict — survives the audit, fails to survive it, or survives only for some subset of elicitation techniques, and if only for a subset, which subset.
