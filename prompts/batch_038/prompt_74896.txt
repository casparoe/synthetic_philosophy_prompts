I'm a third-year philosophy student, and for the consciousness unit of my philosophy of mind seminar I drafted a short paper arguing that higher-order theories of consciousness are too intellectualist to be true. Most of my support came from a trade-press book a friend lent me, written by a neuroscientist who defends an attention-based account of awareness; I quoted a paragraph from its chapter on rival theories, and my teaching assistant read it and said only that I should check whether it was describing the view or one version of the view. The paragraph runs as follows:

"Higher-order theories of consciousness say that a mental state becomes conscious only when a second mental state is aimed at it: a thought about the perception, rather than the perception itself. What makes your experience of the taste of coffee conscious is therefore nothing in the taste, but the fact that you are thinking about having it. Two consequences follow. First, since every conscious state requires a further state directed at it, and that further state would in turn require one of its own if it is to be conscious, the theory starts an endless regress of thoughts about thoughts about thoughts; its proponents reply that the higher-order state is itself unconscious, but this pushes the explanation of consciousness up a level instead of supplying one. Second, consciousness becomes a matter of conceptual sophistication: a creature that lacks the concept of a mental state — an infant, a dog, a bird — cannot be conscious at all, and advocates of the view accept this rather than resist it. What the position amounts to, in the end, is introspectionism: the higher-order thought does not detect the first-order state so much as create it. That is also why the theory has nothing to contribute in the laboratory, where consciousness is measured by report, and report is a function of attention and working memory rather than of thoughts about thoughts."

Our seminar reading was the canonical higher-order-thought account, the one on which the higher-order state must be an actual, occurrent thought, so I genuinely cannot tell whether the passage is unfair to that version, unfair to the family of higher-order views more generally, or unfair to neither. I would like an accuracy audit I can act on before revising the draft. Please isolate the five claims in that paragraph that do the most argumentative work — with the fifth being its overall conclusion, that the theory has nothing to offer experimentally — and give me exactly five numbered items, one per claim, each a sentence or two that states the claim and then your verdict: what a defender of the view would accept, what is a fair simplification, and what is a genuine misdescription, flagging in particular any place where the passage slides from a live objection into the theory's own commitments, or from one member of the higher-order family to all of them. No introduction, no closing paragraph, and no suggested readings; if more than five claims are load-bearing, keep the five that matter most to the passage's overall argument.
