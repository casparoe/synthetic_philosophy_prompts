Third week into the sovereignty unit in my second-year political philosophy seminar — 22 students, a mix of politics and philosophy people, one 80-minute slot on a Tuesday — and I've decided the readings aren't going to do the work by themselves. Everyone can now recite the democratic-deficit charge (no European demos, nobody can vote the Commission out, turnout in European elections is a shrug) and they all think they've understood it, and none of them have. What I actually want them to feel is the two-sided structure of the thing, which the literature states but never makes vivid: the very same delegation of decision-making up to the European level that leaves citizens with no one to hold accountable up there also hands their national executives a route out of accountability down here. A minister who gets outvoted in Council can go home and present a rule they helped draft in a windowless room as something done to them by Brussels, and get *rewarded* for it domestically. That inversion — losing sovereignty abroad and gaining impunity at home in the same move — is the thing I want them to run into from the inside rather than be told about.

So design me a two-round in-class negotiation. Same fictional instrument both rounds: something concrete and low-glamour that has obvious domestic winners and losers, e.g. a regulation on cross-border road haulage or on product labelling or on who has to store a certain kind of data. Give each student a country, and a private card with their governing coalition, their most salient domestic constituency, what that constituency rewards and what it punishes, and their next election date — and make the cards asymmetric enough that a couple of ministers genuinely need a deal and a couple genuinely profit from blocking one. Round one under unanimity, round two under a double-majority rule of your choosing (calibrate the threshold so that at least three or four students end up on the losing side, that's the whole point of switching). Then, somewhere in round two, pull three students out into a corner to cut a compromise between a "Council" position, a "Parliament" position and a broker, and bring it back to the room as a single package that can only be accepted or rejected whole, no amendments — I want the plenary to feel that their formal vote was already hollow by the time it happened. And here's the bit I care about most: before the vote in each round, every minister writes down on a slip, in secret, the one line they'll give their domestic press afterwards. Read them out at the end. If the game is working, the same student who negotiated for the compromise will have written something that assigns them no responsibility for it, and the scoring should make that pay off — I'd rather they discover that the blame-shifting move is rationally attractive than hear me assert it. Give me the full rules, the materials list, the slip format, a point scheme that needs no money and no grading, and a minute-by-minute timeline that fits in 80 minutes including the debrief.

For the debrief, map each moment onto the distinctions I want them to leave with — pooled versus delegated sovereignty, the difference between having no one to vote for and having someone to blame, input versus output legitimacy (some of them will argue, correctly, that the deal the room produced under majority voting was better than anything they could have got at home, and I want that argument on the table rather than crushed), and whether the ever-closer union idea even requires the kind of transfer we just staged. Then be straight with me about what will go wrong the first time I run it: my suspicion is the unanimity round collapses into an instant lowest-common-denominator non-agreement that nobody feels anything about, that one or two loud students will steamroll the corner negotiation so the trio just relays what the room already wanted, and that some conscientious student will refuse to defect from their card because it feels like cheating the game. Tell me how to structure against each of those, or what to say when they happen anyway. And be frank about the feature of the real thing the activity can't reproduce and that I'll have to supply by hand — I know it can't capture the fact that real ministers interact for decades under a shared legal culture and real enforcement, and I suspect it can't touch the absence of a common European public sphere at all, which may be the actual core of the deficit rather than the institutional plumbing we're playing with. Don't send me a bibliography or a reading list, I've got the readings sorted; I just need something I can photocopy on Monday.
