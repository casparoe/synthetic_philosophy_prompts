Six months ago I left an academic post in political theory to run the integrity function of a development agency working across four countries, and I have spent that time discovering that the question I assumed was settled — what actually makes an act corrupt — is not settled at all among the people I now work with. Our code forbids staff to accept anything of value from anyone with business before them, which is why a country director was formally admonished for accepting a woven mat from a village council after signing off a water scheme. The same code permits staff to accept funded travel to conferences from firms that bid for our contracts. Our field colleagues regard the first prohibition as absurd and the second as a loophole; my own family, in the country where I grew up, would regard refusal of the mat as a grave insult to the people who offered it. I want an instrument that locates these disagreements precisely, so that a mixed team of auditors, programme staff and local partners can see where they actually part company. I do not want a policy document, and I do not want an adjudication from head office.

Please write a diagnostic decision tree, with a scoring key, that sorts respondents into positions on what counts as corruption. The whole thing should be in British English.

Requirements:

1. **Eleven or twelve branch nodes**, each a concrete case with two or three answer options, plus a scoring key that maps answer patterns onto leaves. No node may use the words corrupt, corruption, bribery, gift, integrity, impartiality or norms: everything must be phrased in the plain idiom of fieldwork and office life. No node may ask the respondent which principle they accept or which label fits them — the sorting has to come out of what they say about cases.

2. At least the following **branch points** must appear somewhere in the tree: whether the operative standard is the formal rules attaching to the office or a deeper requirement to treat comparable cases alike; whether that standard is itself society-relative or holds across societies; whether the assessment attaches to single acts, to standing practices, or to whole institutions; whether consequences for the worst-off belong inside the definition or only count as evidence about it; whether what is transferred matters at all, or only the structure of the exchange (its timing, its expected reciprocity, whether refusal is itself an offence); and whether favouring one's own people can be a legitimate mode of political exchange rather than a spoiled version of an impartial one.

3. The **cases** should not all involve money. Please include hospitality of a kind that is customary locally, a hiring decision favouring a relative of a benefactor, something of value offered long after any decision has been taken, a small payment that is legal where it is made but illegal at our head office, and a politician who steers a project to her own constituency.

4. **Leaves**, each carrying the name of the position if it has one in standard use and a descriptive label plus the word "unnamed" if it does not, and each with one sentence on the commitment its holder will find hardest to defend: a legalistic office-based universalism; an impartialism that condemns practices local rules permit; a social-norms or contextualist view on which nothing can be corrupt where the norms constituting the office allow it; the grease-the-wheels consequentialism; the consent-and-contract view on which two willing parties and no harmed third party leave nothing to object to; the view that corruption consists in degrading the good a practice exists to serve; the critique that anti-corruption standards are a culturally particular export presented as universal; clientelism treated as a distinct category rather than as a species of corruption; and a deflationary view on which the term picks out no unified phenomenon. Do not attribute any of these to a named author, work or date unless you are certain, and do not supply a bibliography.

5. Note the **unoccupied combinations**: which answer patterns your nodes permit but which have no defenders you know of, each diagnosed as incoherent, unattractive, or merely unexplored. Flag explicitly any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate, rather than pretending it can. Include a residual branch for respondents who fit nowhere, and close with a short statement of the tree's systematic blind spot.

6. Finally, outside the tree: my own candidate criterion is that an act is corrupt when the office-holder could not publicly avow the reason for which she acted without thereby undermining the office. Tell me where this would attach — as a new branch, as a rival to an existing one, or as a redescription of one — and give me the strongest objection to it from whichever leaf it most threatens.
