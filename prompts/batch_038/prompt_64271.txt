For fifteen years I have read the rationalist corpus on precommitment — Ulysses contracts, Schelling points, the treatment of one's future self as a trading partner who can be bound today by a better-informed earlier version of oneself — and I have absorbed, largely by osmosis, the assumption that a clear-eyed earlier self has presumptive authority over a later self who is predictably going to be in a worse epistemic and motivational position. Advance directives for dementia look like the paradigm application of that picture, and yet the case has features the standard precommitment story does not obviously handle. Rather than keep inheriting my confidence from the analogy, I want to see it taken apart.

Here is the case, stipulated in full so that no vagueness about the facts is quietly doing normative work.

A woman, at 52, wrote a directive after watching her mother's twelve-year course of Alzheimer's and after receiving a genetic result making early-onset disease likely. The directive refuses artificial nutrition, refuses antibiotics for pneumonia, and is conditioned on a threshold: any life-prolonging treatment once she can no longer recognize her daughter. It also contains a clause anticipating the obvious objection, and this is the part I want you to weigh carefully. She wrote, in substance, that if her later self appeared content — enjoying music, affectionate with staff, untroubled — that contentment was not to be read as a change of mind or of values, but as further evidence of the impairment that had destroyed the person whose values were in question. She said she was not motivated by fear of suffering, that she expected there to be little, and that her reason was that a life organized around a shrunken present and no longer capable of carrying out her projects was not a life she wished to continue having.

Now she is 68. Moderate-to-severe dementia, no terminal illness, no pain. She does not recognize her daughter, who is her named proxy and who wants the directive enforced. She is by all reports content, is attached to one particular caregiver and becomes distressed when that caregiver leaves, and retains enough capacity that the team routinely honors her contemporaneous refusals of blood draws and blood pressure cuffs. She has developed aspiration pneumonia; a course of antibiotics would very likely return her to her current baseline and buy perhaps two or three more years.

What is your credence that honoring the directive is morally required here — where required means that a team which treated the pneumonia anyway would be acting wrongly, and not merely exercising permissible discretion? Give me the number and then the decomposition, with a rough probability and a sentence of justification for each term you take to be doing real work. At minimum I would like to see you separate: the probability that the woman in the bed is a subject of morally weighty interests that are not simply the earlier woman's interests continued under a new name; the probability that a reductionist view of personal identity is true, and — separately — the probability that it cuts in favor of the directive's authority rather than against it; the probability that the distinction between critical and experiential interests is coherent and carries the weight assigned to it; the probability that her anticipatory clause deserves any discount, given that anyone in her position writing such a directive would have written the same clause; and the probability that the absence of any subject left with a complaint is decisive. Say how you combine these, whether multiplicatively or by weighting, and which single term the final number is most sensitive to, and which you are least confident about. Then tell me what would move it most: an argument, or an empirical result — for instance, good data on self-reported quality of life in moderate dementia, and whether such data could ever be distinguished from a response shift.

My own credence is 0.62. I would like your assessment of where that number probably comes from. Is a reader with my priors more likely to be reaching 0.6 because of the ethics, or because the Ulysses-contract frame makes binding a future self feel like the default and any other answer feels like a failure of nerve? If the latter, how far should that prior be discounted here — and is there any version of the precommitment framework that survives the dementia case intact, or does the case show that the framework was never about identity at all?
