I'm giving the toast at my old boss's retirement dinner in about a month. She's been on our state supreme court for 22 years; I clerked for her in 2011-12. The room will be maybe 80 people: three decades of clerks (a lot of them judges or in practice now), her husband, her kids and four grandkids, and — the part I keep losing sleep over — two state senators who were on the losing side of the opinions everyone in the building remembers.

The shape I want:

In 22 years she voted to strike down a state statute exactly twice. Everything else she let stand, usually with a concurrence about how its not the court's job to second-guess a legislature on matters where reasonable people differ. For most of her career people on the left called her a restraint-obsessed conservative and she basically took that as a compliment. But both of the two times she struck something down, the legislature had done something that made it harder to vote the thing out: one was a district map, one was a change to the petition-and-ballot process written so that it would lock itself in. I want the toast to actually argue that point rather than assert it — that twenty years of deference and those two strikes are the same principle wearing different clothes. It's roughly the Ely move, courts policing the channels of political change instead of grading the legislature's answers, and if you want to say that in the speech, say it in one clause: half the room will know exactly what I mean and the other half won't care.

Two objections need to have an answer already sitting inside the toast, not bolted onto it:
- One of her old clerks is a con law professor now and he'll be thinking that deciding which channels are clogged is itself a substantive value judgment, so this isn't restraint at all, it's just a theory that hides its theory better. That's the one that always comes up about footnote four.
- Someone else will be thinking: fine, but if that's the principle, why only twice? Why not go looking for it?

Constraints:
- It has to land for the grandkids. The jurisprudence can't be the whole meal. But I also don't want a warm personal story with a detachable paragraph of legal theory stapled into the middle — the idea about legislatures and courts should be the thing that generates the affection, or else it shouldn't be in there.
- The two senators have to be able to sit through it without being prosecuted. She ruled against them and it cost them, and the toast can't pretend otherwise, but it also can't come out as "she was right and you were wrong."
- No Holmes, no Learned Hand, no quotation of any kind from anyone famous. Every speech like this reaches for them and I refuse.
- Five minutesish, and it ends on her, not on the argument.

Give me the speech. Then after it, in two or three lines, tell me where you think it's most vulnerable in a room that contains people who don't buy the principle — what does the senator say back to me at the bar afterwards.
