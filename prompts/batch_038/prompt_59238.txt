You are a decision theorist who has taught the Bayesian foundations of choice under uncertainty, and the non-Bayesian alternatives that were built to contest them, to graduate students for many years, and you are known for setting examination questions that cannot be answered by assembling the right vocabulary. I teach a first-year graduate seminar cross-listed between philosophy and economics, on the foundations of decision theory, and I am writing the final. I want one substantial essay question on the Ellsberg paradox and ambiguity aversion, together with everything a marker needs in order to grade it consistently. The question must be answerable in fifty minutes by a candidate who has done the reading, but it must be impossible to answer satisfactorily by recounting the standard narrative — the two urns, the common consequence structure, the failure of the sure-thing principle, the subsequent industry of non-additive and multiple-prior representations. Reciting that story should earn, at most, middling marks.

The angle I want the question to turn on comes from an objection a colleague of mine pressed at a workshop last term, and which I have not seen stated cleanly anywhere else. The objection runs roughly as follows. An ambiguous-urn experiment does not test the sure-thing principle at all, because the subjects are not choosing among the acts the experimenter supposes she is offering them. A subject told only that an urn holds one hundred red and black balls in an unspecified ratio is entitled to wonder how that ratio came to be fixed: by a chance device operated in front of her, or by a researcher who had reasons of her own. A subject who hedges against the second possibility — the possibility that the urn was stocked so as to defeat whichever bet she takes — behaves exactly as an ambiguity-averse subject behaves. But if one widens the state space to include the process that determined the composition, her choices are unimpeachably Bayesian: she holds a single prior over the enlarged states, and her refusal to back either colour follows from that prior rather than violating any axiom. On this reading the experiments document a mismatch of descriptions, not a defect in the axioms, and a genuine test would require the composition to be fixed by a visible chance mechanism. I want candidates to engage with this objection as the crux of the question, and in particular to confront at least the following: what its strongest point actually is; the dialectical weakness that, if it succeeds, it leaves the normative question untouched, since mis-described subjects tell us nothing about whether ambiguity aversion is rational in the situation as described; the unfalsifiability worry that arises if every ambiguity-averse pattern of choice can be re-described as Bayesian over an enlarged state space, and what, if anything, constrains legitimate enlargement of the state space so that probabilistic sophistication remains a substantive thesis; which specific axiom in fact gives way in the leading non-Bayesian alternatives, and whether that weakening is principled or merely a patch fitted to the data; and whether the alternatives remain coherent when the choice problem is unfolded over time rather than taken all at once. Candidates should finish by defending a verdict on whether ambiguity aversion is a genuine failure of Bayesian rationality or a rational response to a situation that has been mis-described, and should say what would count as evidence for their verdict.

Please deliver the following, in this order, and think carefully about the marking scheme before you write the question, since every mark should be earnable by a candidate who understands the issue and out of reach for one who has only memorised the labels. First, a short diagnostic note of no more than one hundred and fifty words describing what a well-read but shallow answer will predictably say and why it should score no better than a B; I want this so that I can see that the question has teeth before I commit to it. Second, the essay question itself, with my colleague's objection set out in the stem in the words you think best, and with explicit instructions to the candidate about length and time. Third, a model answer of approximately seven hundred words. Fourth, an itemised marking scheme out of twenty-five marks which names the specific moves an A answer must make and states precisely what separates it from a competent B — the separation must be drawn in terms of which arguments are deployed and which distinctions are respected, not in terms of how many theorists are named. Fifth, three short multiple-choice items of four options each, keyed, to be used as a check-understanding section; every distractor must encode a specific misconception that candidates genuinely hold — for instance, conflating ambiguity with risk over known probabilities, with imprecision or vagueness in the language of the problem, or with the independence failures exhibited by Allais-type choice; or supposing that ambiguity aversion is a failure of transitivity, or of completeness, or of dominance; or believing that the multiple-prior representations are automatically immune to problems of diachronic coherence — and the key must say, for each wrong option, exactly which misconception it is designed to catch. Work entirely from your own knowledge: I am not asking for a bibliography, a reading list, or page references, and you should not append one. Where you are not confident of an attribution, describe the argument rather than attaching a name to it.
