I am in the fourth year of a PhD in cognitive science, and I would like advice on a decision that has turned out to be far harder than it looks. I will describe the arrangement carefully, because several of its features seem to bear on the answer and I am not certain which.

An alumna of our department has set up a one-off private fund for graduate students' summer research. She sent an identical message, at the same hour, to each of the eleven students in my cohort. Each of us may reply privately with a single whole-dollar figure between $1 and $50,000 that we would like the fund to pay us. The pot is fixed at $200,000. If the sum of all eleven requests is at or below the pot, everyone who asked receives exactly what they asked for. If the sum exceeds the pot, the fund pays no one anything at all — including whoever asked for a dollar. There will be no second round next year and no future dealings with this donor; the replies are sealed, so none of us will ever learn what anyone else requested, and she has promised not to disclose the pattern of answers even to the department. She has also asked us not to discuss the offer with one another, and I have honored that, which means my information about the other ten is limited to what I already knew about them.

One feature of her selection seems to matter. She explained that she chose us precisely because our supervisors independently described us as people who reason about matters of this sort in much the same way, and that she expects our eleven replies to look strikingly alike. On the face of it there is then a figure that is obviously correct: if the eleven of us asked for the same amount, the largest whole-dollar amount we could each ask for without sinking the pot is $18,181. But I know one of the eleven reasonably well. He transferred into our cohort from another department, he is carrying substantial debt, and I have heard him say that the sensible response to such an offer is to ask for as much as you can get away with. If I treat him as asking for the maximum, the remaining ten of us have only $150,000 among us, and the symmetrical figure falls to exactly $15,000 — which would bring the total to the pot down to the dollar.

That is where I find myself genuinely divided. If I think of my reply as one move among ten others that are already settled, then asking for a large figure is never worse for me than asking for a small one: if the others' requests already exceed the pot, I receive nothing either way, and if they do not, I receive more by asking for more. Reasoned that way, I should ask for $50,000 and shrug at the outcome. But if we really are as alike as she says, then whatever deliberation I am performing is presumably being performed, in near-identical form, in ten other heads; the thought "I will ask for the most I can" is then not a thought about me but a forecast about all of us, and the forecast is that everyone gets nothing. The first framing makes my reply a purely local matter; the second makes it a sample of a type. I cannot decide whether the second framing is a real insight or an indulgent, faintly magical piece of reasoning that a careful person ought to decline — nothing I choose can causally influence what the others choose, they are not copies of me in any literal sense, and the supposed likeness among us rests on secondhand descriptions from supervisors, several of whom I barely know.

A further thought occurred to me: I might settle the matter by drawing my figure at random. Then I noticed that this felt like cheating, though I could not say why, and the feeling has not gone away.

So, concretely: what figure should I write in my reply? Does the presence of the one student I expect to behave differently undermine the symmetry, merely shift the number, or neither? Is there any legitimate content to the sense that my deliberation is evidence about other people's deliberation, or should I regard that sense as a confusion to be disciplined out of myself? And if I am simply over-thinking a situation with an obvious answer, I would like to be told so plainly, with an explanation of why the obvious answer is obvious.
