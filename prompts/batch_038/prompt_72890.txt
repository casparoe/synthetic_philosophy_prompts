I'm a second-year political theory PhD student and I've been going around in circles on something in my chapter on the duty to vote, so I mostly want someone to either confirm a hunch or talk me out of it. No reading list, no names of books — just the argument.

Two real institutional practices that seem to rest on two seperate pictures of what a vote even is.

(1) Australia, and roughly twenty other countries, compel you to vote. You get fined if you don't turn up. But the ballot is secret, so the state can never observe the thing it claims to be compelling — the only checkable fact is that your name got marked off the roll. You can attend, take the paper, drop it in blank, and you've discharged the duty; nobody will ever know. And for the lower house you have to number every single box or your vote is informal, so the only way to register "I reject all of these people" is to spoil the paper — which is simultaneously a perfectly legal way of complying. So the state mandates an act it can't observe, is officially indifferent to the act's content, and punishes only a proxy for it.

(2) Nevada has printed a "None of These Candidates" line on its statewide and presidential ballots since 1975. It can win a plurality and it still doesn't matter — if it does, the real candidate with the most votes takes the office. When the line was challenged in 2012, the state's defence was essentially that it's a protest option meant to send a message and that the outcome is no different from not voting at all. So here's an institution that builds an official channel for a purely communicative vote, tallies it, publishes it, and then by design strips it of any effect on who governs.

My hunch is that neither survives the instrumental picture, where a vote is a causal contribution to an outcome. On that picture (1) is compulsory performance of a null act and (2) is a line that can only ever function as a spoiler. But they don't survive on the *same* non-instrumental picture either. (2) looks expressive — vote as speech act — yet if the vote is a speech act then (1)'s compulsion is incoherent, because a compelled utterance isn't an expression of anything, and (1)'s indifference to content is exactly what an expressive theory can't stomach. (1) looks more like a duty, or a ritual, or a sampling device (drag the whole electorate in so the aggregate isn't skewed toward whoever felt like showing up) — but then what is the Nevada line *for*? Rituals don't come with a "none of the above" checkbox.

So: what does each practice actually presuppose about what voting is? Where am I wrong about them pulling in opposite directions, if I am? And please keep apart the things a practice strictly can't do without from the theories it merely happens to sit comfortably with — I suspect both of these are a lot more underdetermined than I'm letting on.
