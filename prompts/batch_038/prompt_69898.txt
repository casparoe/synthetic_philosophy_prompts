You're going to be my case-design specialist for this — think of yourself as the person philosophers call when they need a scenario that survives a hostile room. Think carefully, and don't jump straight to the scenario: start with a short brainstorming section where you list three or four candidate mechanisms for making the variable I care about bite, pick one, and say why before you write anything else.

Context. I'm running a workshop session on the inefficacy objection — the line of thought where my individual contribution to some big collective harm makes no difference, so it can't be wrong. My audience has already had the standard diet: Parfit's hundred torturers, "your one steak doesn't change what the factory does," the voting case, the one-flight-emissions case. So I want a pair of cases from a setting they haven't seen, built so that exactly one variable differs.

Setting: a strike at a manufacturing plant, and an outside worker deciding whether to take a shift as a replacement. Ordinary people, ordinary pay, no cartoon villains. The collective harm is what the strike's failure costs the bargaining unit — say the pension clause gets stripped — and it's identical in both cases.

The variable to isolate: whether the substitute is **specific and named** or **diffuse and anonymous**.

- Case A: there's an identified individual on the staffing agency's call-down list who has already been phoned, has already said yes, and will definitely take the shift if our agent turns it down. Same effect on the strike, to the same degree, on the same timeline.
- Case B: everything is the same except no one has been identified or called. The agency is contractually obliged to supply the plant a full crew and always has; the pool is deep. Someone will fill the shift, but there's no particular person who would.

Held fixed across both: same agent, same knowledge, same motives, same pay, same cost of refusing, same alternatives realistically available to her (she can turn it down in either case).

Now the constraints, which are the part that makes this worth doing:

1. One paragraph each, 150 words max, readable by a smart non-philosopher.
2. No verdict words inside the scenarios — no "harmless," "innocent," "complicit," "wrong." No character gets to voice the moral, and no narrator aside. A case that stipulates its verdicts instead of generating them from the theories is a failed case.
3. Close the loopholes out loud: refusing must not cost the substitute anything (nobody loses a paycheck), and taking the shift must not create any extra harm in A that isn't also in B. State the causal facts in plain language — who does what, what the agency does, what happens to the picket line — because the room will poke at any vagueness.
4. No perfect predictors, no magic certainty. The certainty in Case A has to come from boring ordinary facts: the guy's been called, he said yes, he's a full-time agency contractor whose income doesn't depend on this shift.

Then the audit:

- A table: for each of (i) act consequentialism / expected-harm reasoning, (ii) a participation or complicity view on which it matters that the harm runs through *your* act rather than someone else's, and (iii) a fairness or free-rider view keyed to the collective good, give the verdict in each case plus the one-line reason.
- Walk through the reasoning in enough detail that I can check you didn't rig it. Specifically: show me that marginal expected harm is zero in Case A, and whether it's *also* zero in Case B given the contractual guarantee. If it isn't, revise the case until it is.
- Tell me which feature is doing the work on people's intuitions about the pair. My guess is that it's the identifiability of the doer, not the causal structure at all — but argue it, don't just agree with me, and tell me if I'm wrong. Then dial it: what happens to the pair if the substitute is only 85% likely, or if he's named but hasn't been called yet? Which of the three theories comes out worst?
- Finish with the single best attack that a defender of that worst-off theory has on my case pair, and whether it actually succeeds.

If you conclude the variable turns out to be morally inert — that A and B are just the same case described two ways — say so plainly and tell me what variable I should have isolated instead. I'd rather have an honest dud than a faked difference. And if the strike setting genuinely can't satisfy all four constraints, say that and propose the smallest change that can, rather than quietly dropping one.
