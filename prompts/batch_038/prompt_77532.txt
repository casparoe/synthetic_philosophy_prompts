ok so I'm in a political theory seminar this term on liberalism and empire and we just finished the Mill unit — the thirty-five years at East India House, the chapter in *Considerations on Representative Government* about how a free state ought to govern a dependency, the bit early in *On Liberty* where he carves out an exception for societies he thinks aren't ready to govern themselves. And I have this problem where I can't unsee it anymore, because I've been asked to give a speech in three weeks that is basically a live re-enactment of the reading.

Context: I've done two summers and a part-time semester in the country office of a development agency — I'm the one who writes for the student paper, which is apparently why this landed on me. Our country director is retiring in the spring after fourteen years in-country and I'm giving the remarks from the junior staff at her farewell. Four minutes, so 600ish words. About 120 people in the room: the office (mostly locals, several of whom she hired straight out of university), ministry counterparts, four people flying in from headquarters, her husband and two grown children, and the other two interns.

Here's the thing. She's genuinely excellent. She made every new officer spend three months in a district office before they were allowed to open a spreadsheet. She speaks the language and still corrects people's pronunciation of place names. She held the program together through a currency collapse and an epidemic.

And in 2016 the ministry of health decided to fold our maternal-health program into a different model run out of the capital. She didn't fight it openly. She got two other donors to keep funding the old model, moved it into an NGO, and ran it in parallel in the same districts for four years until the ministry's version visibly underperformed and quietly came back around. The numbers were good — that part isnt ambiguous. I asked her about it last year and she said, with no hesitation at all, that it wasn't her call to lose a program over someone else's org chart, and that they'd have caught up eventually. She has never expressed an ounce of regret and she's not going to start at her own party.

So: I dont want to give the speech where she's just brilliant and tough and we'll miss her, because after this seminar I can't say that without hearing the argument she's been living out loud — a permanent professional cadre, secure tenure, no electorate to please, judged by peers and by results, trained in the language, governing a place whose people have no vote in the decisions, and held to be legitimate only because it's supposed to be temporary. A trust that's meant to work itself out of a job. But I also don't want to turn a retirement lunch into a platform for scoring points about empire in front of her husband. And one of the HQ people is here partly because a governance review is coming up and 2016 is in an annex, so nothing I say can be pointed at as an admission of wrongdoing.

The constraints I actually need help with:

- it can't deny 2016, can't litigate it, and can't apologize for it on her behalf. But three people in that room were on the other side of that decision, one of whom wrote the memo she went around, and I want at least one line that they'll hear as an acknowledgment that they were never actually asked. It has to be a line HQ can't quote back at her.
- build the whole thing on the "only legitimate as something that ends" idea, because that's the one piece of Mill's case that lets you be honest and generous in the same breath: the trust was supposed to expire, and she's expiring with it, on schedule, which is more than the theory ever promised.
- it has to sit with the fact that the tutors are the ones who decide when the tutelage is over, and nobody in the room can hand that back to her as a compliment.
- Mill's name can appear at most once. No quotes from him, no book titles, nothing anyone who hasn't read him would have to look up. It should sound like a 22-year-old who likes her.
- her husband asked for warm and short. HQ comms has banned the words "legacy" and "stakeholder." No jokes about her being scary, everybody does that one.
- use at least one detail that has nothing to do with work. Options: she's kept the same broken desk chair for fourteen years and refuses a replacement; she brings a thermos of the terrible local instant coffee on every field visit because she says it'd be rude not to.

Can you also tack on three or four sentences after the speech telling me which line is most likely to land wrong and who specifically it lands wrong with? I'd rather find that out now than while I'm standing up there.
