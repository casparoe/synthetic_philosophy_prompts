The middle question on this year's two-hour, closed-book final for our senior seminar on well-being has been left to me. I am a third-year doctoral student, still taking coursework of my own, and this is my first teaching post; the instructor has already drafted questions one and three — the first on whether prudential value can be irreducibly plural, the third a short-answer item on Nozick's experience machine — so mine has to carry the unit we spent weeks three to five on: the metaphysics of pleasure, rather than the value theory built on top of it. Over those weeks we read Sidgwick's characterisation of pleasure as a feeling that is at least implicitly apprehended as desirable by those who feel it, Ryle's complaint that pleasantness cannot be a distinctive feel attached to particular sensations because the same sensory quality can be enjoyed on one occasion and not on another, Feldman's move to a purely attitudinal notion of being pleased about something, and — rather out of keeping with the rest of the syllabus — a stretch of the affective-neuroscience literature on incentive salience, which treats hedonic impact and motivational pull as separable processes that can come apart in either direction, in some clinical and experimental cases "wanting" persisting after "liking" has gone and in others the reverse. Half the seminar has come away believing that the last of these settles a question the first three leave open, and that is exactly the inference I want the exam to test rather than reward: a student who can recite the heterogeneity objection and then list the family-resemblance, dispositional, and attitudinal replies should not be able to reach a pass with it.

To that end I have invented a position the students cannot possibly have encountered, and I would like the question built around it. Call it bare-tone hedonism. It has three clauses. First, there is a single simple phenomenal property — a "hedonic tone" — present in every case of sensory pleasure and the same property in each of them, as numerically alike in the pleasure of warm bread, in a stretch after sitting too long, and in following a piece of music to its cadence, as redness is in a tomato and a fire engine on a naive view of colour. Second, hedonic tone is not a separable ingredient of experience: it is never instantiated on its own but only as a way in which an experience presents its object, so that two pleasures differing in what they are of differ in the manner of instantiation and never in the tone itself. Third, it follows that all pleasures are commensurable — differing in degree of tone, not in kind — and that nothing about the object or manner of a pleasure bears on its value, which is fixed by tone alone; on this view Mill's higher and lower pleasures collapse into a difference of quantity. Students will have this statement in front of them on the paper, so please write the question as they would actually see it, with the view stated and then the task, and make the task turn on whether the second clause gives bare-tone hedonism a genuine answer to the heterogeneity objection or merely a way of declining to answer it — that is, whether the tone/manner distinction can be drawn from inside the view rather than being presupposed by it. There are two further things a first-class answer should reach that I deliberately do not want signposted in the wording: that the dissociation of liking from wanting is normally deployed against feeling-based accounts like this one, yet on the view's own terms it may instead be evidence for the unity the view postulates, since a separable hedonic core is just what the view wants to exist; and that the second clause sits uneasily with the third, since a tone that cannot be instantiated apart from a manner is not obviously something a wirehead arrangement could supply indefinitely, which either rescues the view from the experience machine at the cost of its own coherence or forces it to bite a bullet it advertised itself as avoiding.

What I need from you is three deliverables. The exam question itself, self-contained and pitched at roughly forty minutes of a closed-book paper, with no length constraint beyond about twelve hundred words. A model answer of around seven hundred words, which I take as showing the shape and reach of an excellent response rather than as a specimen anyone could handwrite in the time, and which should argue rather than survey. And a marking scheme out of twenty-five, organised around five must-hit moves with the marks each carries, followed by three specific mistakes that should lose marks — each with its penalty and a line on why the mistake is tempting, since I intend to use the scheme in feedback rather than just in grading. Please close with a short paragraph contrasting the A-range answer with the B-range one in terms of which moves each contains, not in terms of fluency or style. No reading list and no citations anywhere in any of the three pieces: the students write from memory, and Sidgwick, Ryle, Feldman, and the incentive-salience material should appear in the model answer only where they are doing real work in an argument, never as decoration.
