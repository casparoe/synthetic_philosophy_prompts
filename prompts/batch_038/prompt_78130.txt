I'm a second year philosophy PhD student — this term I'm TA-ing the undergrad intro to philosophy of science course and also sitting in the graduate seminar on Bayesian confirmation theory, so the prof has let me write this week's practice quiz for my section. I want to do one question on the problem of old evidence, but every version I've draft ends up being answerable by someone who just memorised the sentence "when the evidence is already known, conditionalization can't confirm the theory" and then padded it out with names. Can you write the question itself, a model answer of about 650 words, and a marking scheme out of 20?

Constraints: third-year level, 45 minutes, one question worth 20 marks. The question text has to be completely self-contained — no philosopher names, no paper titles, nothing the students would have to recall from the reading. They should get all the setup and all the numbers inside the question. Please present it the way it'd appear on the paper, parts (a)–(c) with the mark allocation next to each part, then the model answer, then the marking scheme as a table.

Here's the skeleton I want you to use and sharpen:

(a) An agent's total evidence includes E, and their credence function has P(E) = 1. They encounter a hypothesis H for the first time and come to believe that H entails E. Ask them to show, using the definition of conditional probability, exactly what conditionalizing on E does to P(H) in this case — and to say why this is a problem for the idea that confirmation is always conditionalization on new evidence.

(b) Now give them numbers: P(H) = 0.1, and E is not quite certain, P(E) = 0.9999, with H still entailing E. Have them derive the general formula for P(H|E) whenever H entails E, plug in, and get roughly 0.10001. Then ask what that tells us about when old evidence is "too old" — I want them to see that the size of the boost is governed entirely by 1/P(E) and so shrinks to nothing in the limit.

(c) This is the part I actually care about. Give them the following proposal verbatim and ask them to evaluate it: the Rollback Rule — if you come to believe that H entails E, where E is already in your total evidence and P(E) = 1, then you should set your new P(H) equal to the value that conditionalizing on E would have given you had you started from the credence function you held immediately before E first entered your evidence base. Ask whether the rule is even well-defined, and whether adopting it costs the Bayesian anything else.

For the marking scheme I want five or six must-hit points with marks attached, and for each one the specific bad answer students actually write and what it should cost. In particular reserve marks for: noticing that the rule is re-appliable, so that every further hypothesis that entails E earns you another boost and you can pump your credence indefinitely out of a single old observation; noticing that it makes current credence depend on your learning history, so two agents with identical total evidence end up with different credences in H, and saying whether that's a bug or a feature; and noticing that the rule needs a superseded credence function the agent may no longer have access to. Also I want an explicit deduction for anyone who writes that conditioning on a probability-1 proposition is undefined — half my section did that last year and it's just wrong.

And if you think part (c) should turn on the fact that what's genuinely new is the agent's learning the entailment rather than the evidence, please build that in. I suspect that's the real crux but I'm not sure how to word it so third-years can get there without me telling them.
