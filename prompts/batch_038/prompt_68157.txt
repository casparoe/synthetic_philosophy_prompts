I serve on the five-person standing committee that is rebuilding our department's required first-year survey, History of the World Since 1300. The dean's charge to us was deliberately awkward: produce a course that does not take any one region's vantage point as the default. Two years in, we still have no written criterion for what counts as Eurocentric framing — only a pile of rulings on individual proposals, each recorded with its reasons in the minutes — and I have been asked to write the memo recommending how we should vote next Thursday on the last unresolved item. The trouble is that the more closely I read the minutes, the less sure I am that we have been applying one rule rather than several rules that have never yet been forced apart. The record so far:

1. Rejected, unanimously: the old opening week, The Voyages of Discovery, which treated 1492 as the hinge of the course. Recorded reason: that vocabulary makes Europeans the only actors and everyone else the scenery they arrive to.
2. Accepted with revision: a unit on the post-1492 exchange of crops, pathogens, and metals, provided it is organized around consequences in the Americas, West Africa, and Ming and Qing China rather than around voyages. Recorded reason: the subject matter was never the objection to item 1; the center of gravity was.
3. Rejected: a draft chapter introducing Benin, Kongo, and Great Zimbabwe in the fifteenth to seventeenth centuries, each polity entering the narrative at the moment of Portuguese contact and characterized by what it sent to Lisbon. Recorded reason: on that structure, non-European pasts begin when Europeans show up and borrow their significance from them.
4. Accepted: the comparative unit in week 10 built on the contrast Pomeranz pressed between England and the Yangzi Delta around 1800 — two advanced regions under similar ecological constraints, separated afterwards by coal and by access to colonial land. Recorded reason: it denies Europe an internal destiny, and it treats the two as commensurable cases rather than as one normal and one belated.
5. Rejected: an examination question asking students to explain why some regions modernized earlier than others. Recorded reason: it converts one region's trajectory into a clock and grades everybody else against it.
6. Accepted: a unit on Lancashire's industrialization whose explanatory weight sits on plantation slavery, raw cotton, colonial markets, and the displacement of Indian Ocean textile production — Europe as the site of the event rather than its cause. Recorded reason: a European setting is not by itself an indictment; what we object to is the endogenous-superiority story, and this is the reverse of what Blaut attacked as diffusionism.
7. Accepted, three to two: keeping a single chronological spine, with all regions discussed in the same weeks, rather than running four parallel regional strands that never meet. Majority reason: simultaneity is itself the corrective, and a course that never converges is five parochialisms side by side. The dissent, recorded in full, was that a single spine requires a single narrator and that there is no vantage from which the whole can be told fairly.

The item I have to write about is this. A colleague proposes as the closing week a capstone titled Why Europe? The Global Origins of the Modern World. Its content is almost entirely non-European — the Potosí mita and the silver that reached China through Manila, Caribbean sugar and the labor that produced it, Ottoman and Qing fiscal strain, coal geography, Indian textile output and its eclipse — but it keeps Europe as the grammatical subject of the question, it is placed last so that the preceding thirteen weeks can be read backwards as the run-up to it, and it ends by asking students to adjudicate among rival answers. I cannot tell which way our own record points. Items 4 and 6 look structurally similar and were approved; items 3 and 5 look similar in some other respect and were rejected; and my intuitions flip depending on which minute I read last.

So: what should the memo recommend, and on what ground? Please (a) lay out the candidate criteria we could be running — agency and perspective, diffusionism, norm-and-deviation or historicist time, the shape and endpoint of the arc, proportion of coverage, endogenous versus global causation — and test each against all seven rulings rather than only the ones that flatter it, saying where it fits cleanly and where it strains; (b) identify which of items 1 through 7 is the closest analog to the capstone and defend that choice against the runner-up; (c) give me your best prediction of the vote, including the likely margin and which members the argument would have to move; (d) state plainly what principle we would be adopting if we approve and what different principle if we reject — in particular, whether approving commits us to holding that a question may have a European subject provided its answer is global, and whether rejecting commits us to reopening items 4 and 6, which share that structure; (e) tell me whether the dissent in item 7 is a live objection to this capstone or a worry that dissolves once you see what the capstone actually does; and (f) construct one further proposal we have not yet seen on which your two leading criteria give opposite verdicts, so that we can force the issue before the vote rather than after it. Rank the criteria by how much work each is doing in the record; please do not report all of them as equally plausible.
