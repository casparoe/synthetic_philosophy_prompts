I record a long-form conversation each month for a university podcast on philosophy of religion, and next month's guest is a philosopher whose work argues that the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda should be read as a substantive epistemology rather than as a pious counsel of tolerance. Her stated position, as I understand it: every assertion is made from a standpoint (naya) and is legitimate only as conditionally predicated (syāt); unqualified, one-sided assertion (ekānta) is always defective; objects genuinely possess a plurality of aspects — substance and its modes — so this is a metaphysical thesis about the many-sidedness of reality and not merely a confession of human ignorance; and the view is emphatically not relativism, because standpoints can be objectively better or worse at capturing the aspect they target, with only kevala jñāna grasping all of them at once. She is also very quick to absorb an objection by saying that the objector has himself fallen into ekānta.

Please draft ten questions for that conversation, arranged in three movements — what the thesis commits her to, where it comes under pressure, and what could count against it. For each question give me three things: the question itself, the reply I should expect from a defender with this exact profile, and a follow-up that is keyed to that reply rather than being a restatement. Where a question has an obvious escape hatch, the follow-up should close it.

The set must cover, at minimum: whether a syāt-qualified claim is compatible with every possible state of affairs and therefore tells the audience nothing; how her ontological pluralism about aspects differs from plain scepticism about our access to them, and whether she can hold the two apart; what happens when two standpoints deliver outright contradictories about the same subject under the same description, and whether her account is then a form of indexicalism, of truth-relativism, or of something more radical; what the "inexpressible" modes in the sevenfold scheme of conditioned predications are doing — marking a real feature of the object, or only our failure to describe it; how she ranks standpoints at all if no human being ever occupies the complete one; and the asymmetry created by her own claim that the Jain account is the most adequate, which sounds like a one-sided assertion about one-sidedness. Close with a question that asks her, concretely, what argument or discovery would persuade her that anekāntavāda is false, not merely incomplete.

No biographical or warm-up material, no requests to define Sanskrit vocabulary (the audience receives a gloss sheet), and no requests for sources or further reading. Every question should turn on the content of the view as described above, and each should be answerable by someone who holds it without needing to comment on Jain history or on interfaith politics.
