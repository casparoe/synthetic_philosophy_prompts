The question the students were given:

> Essay (20 marks, ~600 words). Frequentists and propensity theorists both hold that probability is objective; subjectivists deny this. Explain what each of the three interpretations takes a probability claim to be about, and use the example of a single toss of a coin you know to be biased to explain how much philosophical work the objective/subjective distinction actually does in separating them.

Here's the answer:

> All three interpretations want to say what a probability statement means, but they disagree about whether probability is something out in the world or something in us.
>
> On the frequency interpretation, the probability of an outcome is just the relative frequency with which that outcome turns up in a long run of repetitions of the same experiment — in the ideal case, the limit that the relative frequency approaches as the series gets longer. So saying that the probability of heads is 0.6 for this coin is saying that in the hypothetically infinite sequence of tosses of it, the proportion of heads converges to 0.6. Probability on this view is completely objective: it doesn't depend on anyone's evidence or opinion, and two people can disagree about it with one of them simply being wrong about a matter of fact. The cost is that it struggles with cases that don't repeat. The single toss I'm about to make either lands heads or it doesn't, and if the coin is melted down afterwards there is no long run left to make my claim true, so the frequentist has to say either that the claim was really about a class of similar tosses rather than about this one, or that it has no truth value at all.
>
> The propensity interpretation is the standard fix. A propensity is a disposition of the physical situation to produce a given kind of outcome, roughly the way fragility is a disposition to break. Popper's point was that propensities are irreducibly single-case: the arrangement has a propensity of 0.6 to produce heads on this very toss, whether or not the toss is ever repeated, and long-run frequencies are how we discover what the propensity is, not what it consists in. So propensities are objective too, and the propensity theorist and the frequentist agree about that; they disagree only about what makes the number true. Notice though that if you analyse a propensity the way you'd analyse fragility — as a counterfactual about what would happen if the situation were repeated indefinitely — you have smuggled the frequency view back in through the back door, and the propensity view loses the advantage that motivated it.
>
> On the subjective interpretation a probability is a degree of belief: how strongly an agent is disposed to bet on a proposition. Ramsey and de Finetti both held something like this. It looks like it makes probability arbitrary, but the coherence requirement does a lot of work here: if your degrees of belief violate the axioms, a bookie who knows them can offer you a package of bets that you'd each accept and that loses you money however things turn out, so subjectivism is not "anything goes." Moreover, de Finetti's representation theorem shows that if your beliefs are coherent and exchangeable they can be represented as a mixture over possible chances, and as you accumulate data your conditional beliefs converge on the true objective chance. In the limit, then, the subjective and the frequentist interpretations give the same answers, which suggests the objective/subjective divide is shallower than it looks. What subjectivism cannot escape, though, is the reference class problem. To get a degree of belief about this toss you have to decide which other events are relevantly similar to it and should be pooled with it, and there's no fact about which class is the right one, so the number you end up with is underdetermined. The frequency and propensity views don't have this trouble, because the experimental arrangement itself fixes the probability, so there is a definite class to look at.
>
> Applied to the single toss of the biased coin: the frequentist can only say something about it by treating it as a member of a class of similar tosses, the propensity theorist says the coin has the disposition, and the subjectivist says 0.6 was my credence all along and I should now bet accordingly. So the objective/subjective line does much less work than the question implies — it doesn't seperate the frequentist from the propensity theorist at all, and the subjectivist can be brought into agreement with the frequentist in the long run anyway.
