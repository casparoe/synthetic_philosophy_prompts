So here's a view I've talked myself into over the last year or so, mostly through arguments with a friend who builds GPU clusters and keeps telling me the difference between me and a sufficiently detailed emulation of me is just substrate chauvinism. I actually agree with the standard gradual-replacement move — the one where you swap out neurons for functionally equivalent synthetic parts one at a time and the fading/dancing qualia argument does the heavy lifting. Replace them slowly enough and you're never in a position where anything, including me, could detect a discontinuity, so I think consciousness and personal identity survive. But I've ended up with a twist that my friend thinks is either an improvement or a fudge, and I can't tell which. My twist: what's doing the preserving isn't the abstract functional organization, it's the continuity of the actual dynamical trajectory. Same causal roles, same clock, same continuous deformation of the physical process. So a perfect functional isomorph implemented on a different timebase — running a thousand times faster, or in a different order of updates, or a scan-and-rebuild that reproduces the exact organization but boots it up from a cold copy — would not on my view be me, and might not be conscious in the same sense, even though no functional test could tell. Thats why gradual replacement feels fine to me and Star Trek transporters don't, and I want to know whether that's a principled distinction or just squeamishness wearing a metaphysics wrapper.

What would count against it? I want the whole map of exposure, not the top two hits, and I want it sorted from fatal to merely awkward. Where you can, say whether each item attacks the twist (the dynamics-continuity requirement) or the base view (gradual replacement preserves consciousness and identity), since those come apart and I care much more about the twist. Some things I'm already worried about: a replacement schedule where each prosthetic is dynamically equivalent but very slightly rescaled in time, so there's no single moment of discontinuity but a smooth drift into a different clock — does my view have to say qualia fade there, and if nothing observable changes for anyone, is that a reductio? The spare-parts problem: if I keep every removed neuron and reassemble them into a second working brain, which one is me, and does the dynamics story help or hurt with fission? The collapse worry from both directions — if the dynamics constraint never makes a difference except in cases that are physically impossible or permanently unknowable, it's doing no work and I'm just a functionalist with extra vocabulary; if it does bite, I'm committed to undetectable qualia drift and to splitting hairs about update order that nobody can cash out. So where does that leave me relative to plain functionalism on one side and Searle-style biological views on the other? Also, what would actual neuroscience or anesthesia research have to show for my view to be in trouble, or to be supported — is there anything in the real study of consciousness that speaks to the timebase question at all, e.g. work on how the speed, rhythm, or synchrony of neural processing relates to whether there's experience going on? And the sorites: how slow is slow enough, is there any principled answer, and if not does that matter for a view like mine?

I'd also like to know which of these objections a committed defender of my view would happily concede as counting against it versus wave off, and whether the whole thing has been qualified into unfalsifiability — and honestly, whether that's even a problem here, given that a theory of consciousness arguably can't be falsified by anything a subject or an outside observer could measure. The thing that would personally move me most is an argument that the twist isn't just unearned but incoherent: that there's no stable notion of "the same dynamical trajectory" that survives the replacement process I'm leaning on, since by the end literally none of the original matter is left and the trajectory only counts as continuous because I chose to describe it that way. If you think that's the real kill shot, say so plainly and don't pad it out with the lesser objections. This is for a reading group writeup, not a paper, so I care way more about being told where I'm wrong than about getting a balanced survey.
