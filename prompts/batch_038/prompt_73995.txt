So — I TA an upper-level undergrad reading group on population ethics, and next week is our last session, on the mere addition paradox and the repugnant conclusion. Instead of another round of "ok but does the intuition survive reflection," I want to close by reading a poem aloud. The room has terrible acoustics and two of the students are pre-law and will absolutely pounce on anything sloppy, so it needs to be audible and it needs to be right. Please write that poem. Act as someone who is both a working poet with real formal discipline (you can hold a stanzaic pattern for seven stanzas without drifting) and a philosopher who has taught this exact material many times — accurate enough that a specialist wouldn't wince, and good enough that someone who's never heard of Parfit would just find it moving.

Here's the conceit, and the form has to do the argument rather than decorate it. I want a cumulative poem: stanza 2 is stanza 1 plus exactly one new line at the end, stanza 3 is stanza 2 plus one more, and so on for six or seven stanzas, so that the poem literally grows by addition and the final stanza is a big block containing every line the poem ever had. The lines already written must come back verbatim, unchanged, every time they recur — nobody in the poem is ever made worse off by what happens later, that's the whole point of the step. The one exception is a hinge stanza somewhere around the middle: there, and only there, you're allowed to reach back and rewrite the thinnest lines upward, so that the whole block gets better on total, better on average, and less unequal all at once. That hinge should feel like the most obviously good move in the poem, the one no reasonable person could object to, and it should be what tips the descent. Rule for every *added* line: taken on its own it can't make anything worse. It's another life worth living, another small good, somebody for whom existing beats not existing. Every step locally defensible, the kind of thing a benevolent planner signs off on without a second thought. But the accumulated whole at the end should be something the reader finds worse — or at least much harder to love — than the four or five lines the poem opened with. Locally non-worsening, globally repugnant; that gap is the poem. And the added lines should thin out progressively, not in syllable count but in what they actually give you: early ones have particular texture (a name, a specific want, a morning), later ones are near-interchangeable and could be shuffled without loss, and the last few should barely be worth their ink.

Please write it in the voice of whoever is doing the adding — a colony founder, a planner with a ledger, a minor god, your pick — someone with excellent reasons at every step who ends up holding that huge final stanza and cannot locate the step where it went wrong. Don't let them diagnose it, don't let them repent, and don't let any line name the paradox or any -ism; the argument has to live entirely in the images and the accumulation. If you want the escape hatches to flicker past, pick at most two and keep them oblique: maybe that the chain of better-thans might not carry all the way around, or that the last block isn't worse but just a different kind of thing, or the nastier one where you'd sooner add someone miserable than leave room for the happy few. Leave it genuinely open — we did not resolve this in seminar and I don't want a poem that pretends we did.

Before you write, think carefully and give me a short plan, like six to nine lines: what each added stanza contributes, where you put the hinge, and where you think the poem crosses from charming to bad and why that's the right crossing point for the reader to feel. Then the poem itself. 40–55 lines total is about right, no title needed (or a plain one), and don't give me a bibliography or a list of sources at the end — I just want the plan and the verse.
