I am revising the axiomatic-bargaining unit of a first-year doctoral micro sequence, and I need a worked case built to order rather than an illustration. My students have had concave programming but no fixed-point theorems, so everything on the slides must be checkable by hand in exact arithmetic. Right now the unit shows the Nash solution and the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution on a symmetric triangular feasible set with the disagreement point at the origin; both pick the midpoint of the hypotenuse, and the class reasonably concludes that the quarrel between the two axiomatics is empty. I want to replace that slide with a single example that separates the two solutions and then, in a second stage, exhibits the axiom each one is giving up. Please build it to the following specifications, and treat the specifications as binding: a set of numbers that merely asserts the verdicts I want, or that produces the divergence from an arithmetic quirk rather than from the shape of the frontier, is no use to me.

Stage one. The setting must be a recognizably real two-party negotiation with non-transferable utility — a transit authority and its drivers' union, a hospital and a physician group, a department and a candidate it is trying to hire, something of that order — described in one paragraph of no more than about 200 words, and that paragraph must say what concrete device makes convex mixtures of agreements feasible (a randomized rotation, a phased implementation, a split contract year) instead of simply assuming convexity. Utilities are cardinal von Neumann–Morgenstern utilities, separately scaled for each party, and you must say what each party's numbers are numbers of. Give the feasible set as the comprehensive convex hull, from the disagreement point, of at most five vertices with exact rational coordinates; the disagreement point must be strictly positive for both parties and must be strictly bettered by some feasible agreement. The Pareto frontier must have exactly one kink; the ideal point must be asymmetric, so that the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution is not just an equal-utility split; and the Nash point and the Kalai–Smorodinsky point must be distinct, must both sit in the relative interior of a frontier segment rather than at the kink or an endpoint, and must be far enough apart to be visibly separate points on a slide. Stage two. Introduce one new feasible agreement — a technology, a funding stream, a regulatory change, your choice — together with its convex combinations with what was already available. This expansion must (i) leave both parties' maximum attainable utilities unchanged, (ii) make party 2's best attainable payoff weakly higher at every level of party 1's payoff and strictly higher on some interval, and (iii) deliver the verdicts that make the slide worth having: under the Nash solution party 2 ends up strictly worse off than she was before the expansion and party 1 strictly better off, while under the Kalai–Smorodinsky solution party 2 ends up strictly better off. Also arrange that neither of the two new solution points belongs to the original feasible set, so that nobody in the room can say the shift is really about independence of irrelevant alternatives rather than monotonicity.

For the deliverable, give the vertex list and the equation of each frontier segment for both problems, then compute the ideal point, the Nash point (showing the first-order condition and checking the remaining segments and the kink), and the Kalai–Smorodinsky point (intersecting the ray from the disagreement point to the ideal point with the frontier, and saying which segment it lands on), in exact fractions throughout. Check your Kalai–Smorodinsky points against the equal-proportional-gains characterization, and check that both solution points transform correctly when you apply a positive affine rescaling to one party's utility, so that nothing in the example depends on your normalization; name explicitly which axiom each solution satisfies and which it violates in stage two. Then be honest with me about the design. Which structural feature of the feasible set is doing the work? What happens if I remove it — in particular, if the frontier is straightened into a single segment, or the disagreement point is pushed back to the origin, do the two solutions collapse onto each other, and does the stage-two result survive? Is the stage-two expansion genuinely an improvement in party 2's options, or does it smuggle in something a skeptic would reject as infeasible, non-credible, or covertly utility-comparing? And how would a defender of the Nash solution attack the example — including the charge that monotonicity makes the outcome depend on payoff levels no one can actually reach, and whatever incentive that creates for a party to manufacture options it never intends to use — and how would a defender of Kalai–Smorodinsky answer that charge without begging the question?
