My brother in law is a philosophy grad student and over the holidays I mentioned that I'd finally set up a standing donation to a global health charity, plus a few hours a week on something aimed at the long-run future, and he thought the second part was hilarious. Then he wrote this out on the back of an envelope to show me neither part means anything:

1. If consequentialism is right, one act is better than another just in case it brings about more total wellbeing, summed over everyone who ever lives.
2. Whether the universe is infinite in time or in space is an open empirical question, and if it's infinite there are infinitely many people.
3. Infinitely many people each with a finite amount of wellbeing adds up to an infinite total.
4. Anything I do happens against that same infinite background, so whichever act I choose, the total wellbeing that results is infinite.
5. Infinite equals infinite, so no act I can perform produces more total wellbeing than any other. (I asked whether the order you add the lives in matters and he said no, infinity is infinity.)
6. So it makes no moral difference whether I keep the donation or cancel it, and the same goes for basically anything else I might do.
7. And even if 6 doesn't hold in the actual world, I can't rule out that it does, so I can never know that one of my acts is better than another.
8. A view that says morality is about producing the most good can't tell me what to do, and that's a fatal problem for it.

I couldn't find the hole, mostly because 3 through 5 feel like arithmatic rather than philosophy. Which step should I reject, and what does rejecting it cost? I'd much rather give up 7 or 8 than 1 — I don't want to stop thinking of myself as a consequentialist — but if 1 is what has to go, tell me why. Also: what would your fix have to look like for it to actually rank two of my real options, and does it survive the version where some of the infinitely many lives are awful instead of good? I want to know whether this is a genuine problem or a word trick.
