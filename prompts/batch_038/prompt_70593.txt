I'm a second-year economic history PhD student and I've been handed a two-hour slot in our MA historical methods module. Topic is contingency: for want of a nail, counterfactual history, the lot. The students are mostly first-year MAs with no maths past school, and they've all read Carr's dismissal of counterfactual speculation as unhistorical, so half of them arrive thinking the whole subject is a game and the other half think it's the only honest way to do history. Don't explain the material to me — I need a plan for the explanation.

What I'm after:

- The order the pieces go in and what depends on what. I keep tangling three claims I want them to hold separately by the end: that some historical processes show sensitive dependence (tiny differences in starting conditions, wildly diverging outcomes), that some are path-dependant in the economists' sense (lock-in, increasing returns), and that most outcomes are massively overdetermined, so subtracting one cause changes nothing. I honestly can't tell whether the first two are the same idea in different clothes, and if they aren't, which has to be in place before the other will stick.

- The one case that carries the whole session, and what it has to have and must not have. I was going to build on the Sarajevo wrong turn, but I've started to suspect that's a trap — it'll teach them history turns on individuals and accidents, which is closer to the opposite of what I want. Tell me if that's right and what to use instead.

- The misconceptions in the order they'll surface, and where in the sequence to head each one off. I'm braced for 'chaos just means randomness', and 'if it's unpredictable there's nothing to explain', plus one ex-actuary who'll insist it's a technical term and demand Lyapunov exponents. Do I concede to him?

- A handle I've been leaning on: the spark and the kindling, trigger versus standing conditions. Does that survive the overdetermination problem, or does it make things worse?

- One question I can ask at the end that shows whether it landed, and what I should openly refuse to explain at this level and say I'm refusing. They all want to know whether they're allowed to use counterfactuals in their dissertations, so whatever you give me there should answer that practically.
