I'm a third-year evo bio PhD student and our journal club has turned into a weekly knife fight between the two senior people in the lab, one of whom treats the 1979 spandrels critique as settled method and the other of whom treats it as a rhetorical device for avoiding evidence. I've been asked to write the handout for next term's reading group: a checklist of what a claim of the form "trait T is an adaptation for function F" has to satisfy before it deserves to be printed. I want the goalposts written down before we read any more papers, because right now both sides move them whenever they're losing. So give me the full spec. Which evidential and conceptual requirements does such a claim have to meet, what's the case or consideration behind each one, and which of them are genuinely non-negotiable versus preferences of one camp dressed up as method? Please also mark where a requirement shifts depending on what the person is actually asserting — half our arguing is people talking past each other because "adaptationism" can mean a research program, a claim about how much of the phenotype selection built, or a claim about how close to optimal the result is, and the bar isn't the same for those three.

Anchor the list to one running example so it doesn't float off into generalities, and let me hand you a made-up system on purpose so nobody can answer this out of the literature. Say there's a genus of small diurnal tree-dwelling rodents in open hardwood canopy somewhere with a brutal hot season. Adults have a bare, flat, fleshy pad on the underside of the tail about a third of the way from the base: hairless, heavily vascularised, pressed flat against the branch whenever the animal stops moving. Four explanations get offered in my group. (1) Radiator — it's an adaptation for dumping heat, which matters because they're most active in the hot season. (2) Grip — the tail is used as a fifth limb and the pad is an adaptation for that, with the heat story bolted on later. (3) Mechanical byproduct — a weight-bearing tail thickens the ventral skin, and thick skin needs a blood supply, so both features come free. (4) Developmental byproduct — the signalling that lays down footpads reaches far enough back that a pad-shaped region appears wherever the tail is long enough, and no selection was involved at any step, a spandrel in the strict sense. The data: the pad is fully formed at birth, before any weight bearing or heat stress; animals raised in the lab, kept cool and never allowed to climb, grow normal pads; a montane sister species living in permanently cold cloud forest has one too; across the genus the pad sits at a fixed fraction of tail length no matter how long the tail is; and yet its vascular density is roughly five times that of the surrounding tail skin, with flow through it measurably rising when the animal is warm.

A few things I want specifically. For each requirement, say whether my case meets it, and be explicit about the one requirement that does the most damage to the most of the four explanations — I suspect it isn't the one the two senior people keep arguing about. The checklist also has to run in both directions, because the byproduct camp asserts just as confidently as the adaptation camp: what does it take to establish (3) or (4), as opposed to merely noticing that (1) is unproven, and is the burden of proof between them symmetric? If your requirements are asymetric, say which way the tilt goes and defend it. I'd also like a requirement, if there is one, about the unit of the claim — can you say "the tail pad is an adaptation for heat dumping" as a single sentence when the position and shape look like a byproduct and the physiology looks designed, or does the list have to force you to break the structure into aspects and give each its own verdict? And if some subset of your requirements can't all be met at once, especially for groups with no fossils and no manipulable populations, tell me which subset and what the honest options are: drop one, or accept that "adaptation" is a word you can almost never earn. A numbered list is fine, it doesn't have to be prose. Don't give me a reading list or try to tell me who said what, I've got the bibliography, I just need the standards, and take the architectural metaphor as given — I'm not interested in re-litigating whether the surfaces in Venice are pendentives or spandrels or whether the mosaics came first. If you want to finish with two lines on which of my four explanations survives your list, fine, but keep the weight on the requirements themselves.
