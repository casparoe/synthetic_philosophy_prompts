> Consider this argument. (1) Human beings naturally tend to form lasting pair bonds and to invest heavily in the care of their offspring. (2) Whatever accords with our natural tendencies is good. (3) Therefore forming pair bonds and caring for your offspring is good.
> In about 350 words, explain what the naturalistic fallacy is, say whether this argument commits it, and explain which premise is doing the work.

The student's answer, reproduced in full:

> Hume noticed that writers on morality spend a while establishing facts, about God or about human nature, all in the indicative, and then suddenly switch to sentences about what ought or ought not to be the case. His complaint is that this new relation appears with no explanation of how it follows from the ones that came before, and that authors should attend to it. Since an argument can only be valid if the content of its conclusion is already contained in the premises, purely descriptive premises can never yield a normative conclusion on their own. You need at least one premise that is itself evaluative — a bridge premise.
>
> Moore later gave this problem a name, the naturalistic fallacy, and put it in terms of definition rather than inference. His open question argument is that if 'good' just meant something like 'conducive to pleasure' or 'in accordance with human nature', then it would make no sense to ask whether those things really are good — but it plainly does make sense to ask, so 'good' cannot be defined in natural terms at all. Goodness is a simple, unanalyzable, non-natural property, which we come to know through a kind of moral sense or faculty of intuition rather than through observation of the world.
>
> The argument above does commit the naturalistic fallacy, and premise 2 is where it happens. Premise 1 is descriptive and harmless. Premise 2 tries to make 'accords with our natural tendencies' equivalent to 'is good', which is exactly the move the open question argument blocks: anyone can intelligibly ask 'pair bonds accord with our natural tendencies, but are they good?' So the argument is invalid, the conclusion doesn't follow, and adding premise 2 doesn't rescue it, because a premise that defines good in natural terms is itself fallacious rather than a genuine bridge. The only way to argue for the conclusion would be to begin from a premise that already asserts something evaluative, and then the argument would tell us nothing new about why pair bonds are good.
