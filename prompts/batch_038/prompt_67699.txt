I'm three quarters of the way through a novel whose narrator finds out at forty that she was made from a donor embryo — her parents' own embryos all failed and the clinic had a leftover from another couple — and the whole book turns on whether the woman she "would have been" is somebody she lost or nobody at all. To write it I've had to pin down what I actually believe about where a person comes from, which turns out to be a mess, so I've been keeping a list of cases and my gut calls on them in the back of a noteboook. I'd love to know what principle, if any, they add up to.

Here's the list. Same egg, different sperm: not me — that guy is my half-brother, and it doesn't matter that he'd have my mother's face. Same sperm, different egg: also not me, symmetrical, no hesitation. My embryo — my actual parents' actual gametes — frozen in a tank for twenty-two years and then implanted: me, absolutely, the delay is irrelevant. Same embryo carried by a surrogate instead of my mother: still me. A somatic-cell clone of me made tomorrow from a cheek swab: NOT me, and I feel this one hard, even though he'd have my nuclear DNA. An embryo built from my nucleus but with a donor egg's mitochondria, the three-parent thing: I want to say this one is closer to me than the clone, maybe actually is me, which I know sits badly with what I just said. And the one the novel hangs on: my embryo splits at day four into identical twins, one of them stops developing at nine weeks and gets absorbed, and the survivor carries some of his cells in her blood for the rest of her life. I want to say she's me and I want to say she's partly him, and I dont think both can be true.

So: is there a principle that gets all of these, or nearly all? I know the Kripke-ish line that where you come from is essential, that you couldn't have come from different gametes, and at first glance that seems to deliver the frozen embryo and the surrogate for free while killing the clone, since he comes from a cheek cell and not a gamete — but then the mitochondrial case ought to come out "not me" too, and I'm not willing to give that one up, or maybe I should be. Give me the cheapest principle that fits the pattern, state it precisely enough that I could apply it to a new case without squinting, and tell me which case motivated which clause. If saving everything costs you clause after clause of machinery, say so and tell me which verdict to throw overboard — I suspect it's the clone, which would wreck a subplot. Then run whatever you land on through two cases I havent decided: an embryo where a single base pair of mine is corrected before implantation (is the corrected one me, and if the edit gets bigger, say a whole gene added, where does it flip?), and an embryo assembled from lab-grown sperm and egg derived from my own skin cells, so the gametes are made out of me rather than out of my parents. No reading list please, I've got a deadline and zero interest in footnotes — just the reasoning, and an honest account of where it breaks down.
