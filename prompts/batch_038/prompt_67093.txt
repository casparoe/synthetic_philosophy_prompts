I have been asked to lead the Thursday-evening History of Ideas circle at our county historical society — a standing group of about fourteen, mostly retirees including three former schoolteachers and a retired army colonel, none of them with any Arabic or any training in medieval history. I am not a historian either: I spent my career in organizational turnaround work, which is presumably why the group let me choose a session on the theme of institutions succeeding themselves out of their own strengths. My fifty-five minutes will be devoted to Ibn Khaldun's account of dynastic rise and decay in the Muqaddimah — asabiyyah, the contrast between the hard conditions of the periphery and the soft conditions of the settled city, the way royal authority is seized by the group with the strongest solidarity and then consumes that same solidarity in the enjoyment of it, and his arithmetic of a dynasty's natural life at roughly three generations of forty years each. I have a whiteboard and a marker, no handouts, no slides, and no expectation that anyone in the room has read anything beforehand. Two members have worked through Gibbon and consider themselves well informed about decline; a third raises Spengler at every session whether or not it is relevant.

What I want from you is not an explanation of the theory but a plan for how I should teach it. Give me the dependency structure: which pieces have to be fixed in place before the cycle becomes intelligible at all, in what order, and precisely what goes wrong if they are introduced out of sequence. My own guess is that the claim that solidarity is manufactured by material conditions rather than expressing the fixed character of a people has to be secured before any talk of decline is even possible, and that the idea of a dynasty having a lifespan of its own, distinct from the lifespans of the men inside it, has to come before the generational arithmetic — but tell me where that is wrong and, more importantly, what I have left out. Then choose the single case that should carry the whole session and defend the choice against the alternatives: an actual Maghrebi dynasty nobody present has heard of, Rome, the Ottomans, a polity I invent live on the whiteboard, or a modern analogue drawn from my own world of firms, mergers, and founders. Say what that case has to contain and what it must conspicuously not contain, and be blunt about the point at which the modern-analogue route stops illustrating the theory and starts falsifying it.

Next, map the misconceptions in the order I should expect them to surface, and tell me the exact place in the sequence at which to head each one off in advance rather than the place at which to argue it down afterwards. My provisional list is certainly incomplete and possibly badly ranked: that asabiyyah is simply team spirit, or tribalism in the pejorative sense; that this is a theory about Islam, or about Arabs, or about nomads as such; that the corruption of the strong by luxury is a moral sermon rather than a causal claim about where solidarity comes from and what spends it; that the three-generation figure is a forecast, so the theory is either fatalist or refuted by every dynasty that outlasted it; and, from the Spengler partisan, that all of this is a biological organism in fourteenth-century dress. Tell me as well where Polybius belongs — before the main sequence as a foil, partway through, or in the closing ten minutes — and what the contrast between a cycle of constitutional forms and a cycle of group cohesion is supposed to establish for people who arrive with Gibbon already installed. Close with one thing I can put to the room at the end that would show me the mechanism landed and not merely the vocabulary, and with an explicit list of what I should refuse to cover at this level. I am thinking his economics of labor, prices, and the profit of the ruler; the role of a religious calling as an amplifier that cannot substitute for solidarity; and the methodological attack on credulous historians with which the book opens. Say which of those can safely be cut, and whether cutting any of them quietly guts the thing I am trying to teach. Please do not recommend readings; nobody will do them, and I would rather spend the minutes at the board.
