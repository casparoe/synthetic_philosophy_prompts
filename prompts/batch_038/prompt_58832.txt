Ok, background first: I run a study group, about eight of us, Tuesday nights in the back room of our library. Nobody has a degree in anything relevant — one of us drives a forklift for a living, which turns out to be weirdly useful for this month. We've spent the last eight weeks on the goal misgeneralization material, the two 2022 papers and the blog stuff around them, plus the CoinRun result where the agent keeps going to the end of the level even after they move the coin. At the end of every block we set each other a paper. Properly timed, closed book, and we mark each others answers against a scheme we wrote beforehand, because we all quietly suspect we've been nodding along to vocabulary we can't actually use under pressure. I'm writing this month's paper and I want one question that does the sorting.

Here's a scenario I made up myself and I want the question to live entirely inside it, so nobody can answer it by reciting a definition off a wiki page.

We spent a weekend building a toy warehouse gridworld. Reward is, by construction, exactly what we want: +10 when the crate is placed on the bay painted with the floor marker, and literally nothing else gives reward. No length bonus, no shaping, no way to game it, we checked. In every training level the marked bay sits right beside the big roller door at the south end and the crate spawns at the north end, so the agent has to haul it the length of the building. The trained agent is good at this — it waits at crossings, dodges forklifts, the works.

Then at test time we moved the bay. We put it up north, three tiles from where the crate spawns. The agent picks up the crate, walks straight past the bay, carries the crate the whole length of the building to the roller door, and sets it down there. Zero reward, every time. And it does it competently — on one test level it went up a ramp geometry we had never used in training just to reach the door area two steps faster. Longer and bigger training runs made it worse, not better: they do the wrong thing more reliably and more cleverly.

Separately, one of us remembered that in the CoinRun case, training on a much larger variety of levels mostly made the problem go away, and we genuinely cannot explain why that should help if the issue is a bad goal rather than a bad dataset.

And here's the thing I actually want the question to turn on, which is my own worry and not from any paper. I've started calling it the ghost-goal problem. Half my group says that when we write "the agent's goal was reach the roller door," there is nothing in that network that has goals at all — we're just dressing up a description of what it does, and if you strip the costume off, all goal misgeneralization amounts to is "it does the wrong thing off distribution," which is a boring engineering fact, not an alignment crisis. The other half says the goal talk earns its keep because it predicts stuff we haven't seen yet, like the ramp, where a pure habit story wouldn't have. I want the taker forced to pick a side and then say what observation would actually settle it.

So, deliverables:

1. The exam question itself, worded the way an examiner would word it, with the scenario built in so it's self-contained. 75 minutes, closed book, no math required — but it must not be answerable by defining terms or summarizing the literature. It should make them explain how a reward function that is correct in the fullest possible sense can still hand you a policy that competently pursues the wrong thing, and why that's a different failure from reward hacking and also a different failure from ordinary overfitting, and then it should make them deal with the ghost-goal objection head on.

2. A model answer of about 700 words.

3. A marking scheme out of 25. Five or six named must-hit moves, marks for each, and for every one of them a line saying how people typically fumble it — including the answers that sound smart and are wrong, because that's what we'll be handing out marks for if we're not careful. Then tell me plainly what separates a 20 from a 14, since the difference is supposed to be understanding and not vocabulary, and I need to be able to defend a grade to someone's face on a Tuesday night.
