I am assembling the second session of an upper-level seminar on the philosophy of death, and I want to spend the whole of it on a single exchange in Book XVIII of the *Zhuangzi* (the chapter traditionally titled "Perfect Happiness"), in Herbert Giles's Victorian English, which I reproduce below with its anachronisms left in place. My graduate students have read the exchange a dozen times and still produce only paraphrase, so I would like a proper dissection of the argument rather than another summary of Daoist equanimity.

> When Chuang Tzŭ's wife died, Hui Tzŭ went to condole. He found the widower sitting on the ground, singing, with his legs spread out at a right angle, and beating time on a bowl.
>
> "To live with your wife," exclaimed Hui Tzŭ, "and see your eldest son grow up to be a man, and then not to shed a tear over her corpse,—this would be bad enough. But to drum on a bowl, and sing; surely this is going too far."
>
> "Not at all," replied Chuang Tzŭ. "When she died, I could not help being affected by her death. Soon, however, I remembered that she had already existed in a previous state before birth, without form, or even substance; that while in that unconditioned condition, substance was added to spirit; that this substance then assumed form; and that the next stage was birth. And now, by virtue of a further change, she is dead, passing from one phase to another like the sequence of spring, summer, autumn, and winter. And while she is thus lying asleep in Eternity, for me to go about weeping and wailing would be to proclaim myself ignorant of these natural laws. Therefore I refrain."

A few pages later, in the same chapter, Giles renders this:

> "Do you loathe this?" asked the hunchback.
>
> "Not I," replied the other, "why should I? Life is a loan with which the borrower does but add more dust and dirt to the sum total of existence. Life and death are as day and night; and while you and I stand gazing at the evidences of mortality around us, if the same mortality overtakes me, why should I loathe it?"

Here is what I should like from you. First, reconstruct the reply to Hui Tzŭ as an explicit sequence of premises and a conclusion, and identify the load-bearing inference — the step without which the move from cosmology ("phases, like the sequence of spring, summer, autumn, and winter") to conduct ("Therefore I refrain") does not go through. Second, tell me what the opening concession is doing: he admits that at the moment of the death he could not help being affected, and only later recollects the genealogy of form, breath, and birth. Does that concession mean the sage is exempt from spontaneous feeling, or only from its persistence and its public performance — and if only the latter, is Hui Tzŭ's complaint then not about the badness of death at all, but about a violated social office of mourning (note that his reproach is built out of roles: wife, son, corpse, the visitor who has come to condole)? I have been pressing exactly that reading on my students, and I want to know whether the text will bear it or whether I am smuggling my own conclusion in. Third, attend to the charge that the argument is trivially circular: if continued wailing is itself the symptom of failing to grasp the transformations, then to say that wailing proves such a failure cannot be informative. Offer me the strongest non-question-begging reading available, or say plainly that there is none and that the passage is doing something other than arguing. Fourth, compare the structure of this reasoning with the Stoic treatment of grief as resting on a mistaken assent about what counts as bad: both make mourning a cognitive failure, but the two accounts seem to locate the error differently, and I want to know whether Zhuangzi's premises support a claim about the value of death for the deceased, or only a claim about the mourner's epistemic position — and whether "lying asleep in Eternity" is doing quiet work for the first of those, work that the four-seasons analogy alone does not do. Fifth, and this is where the second excerpt matters: if life is a loan by which the borrower merely adds dust and dirt to the sum of things, that looks like a negative valuation of existence, in tension with the neutralism of the phase-sequence in the wife passage. Are these two passages reconcilable within one outlook, or does the chapter simply contain two incompatible attitudes toward being alive?

Two constraints. I am not asking for a bibliography, a list of commentaries, or citations of any secondary literature, and I do not want the Chinese characters reconstructed from memory — work only from the English above and from general philosophical reasoning, since that is what my students will have in front of them in class. And because Giles is a loose and doctrinally confident translator, please be candid about the fragility of your conclusions: for each of the five points, tell me how confident you are, distinguish which of your readings depend on loaded choices in this rendering (terms such as "substance," "spirit," "Eternity," and "these natural laws" are doing a great deal of unexamined work) from those that would survive a flatter, more neutral translation, and finish by singling out the one claim in your answer you are least sure of and explaining what would settle it.
