I write literary fiction, and the institute where I have been invited to apply for a year-long residential fellowship funds writers doing research rather than drafting; the application requires a proposal for a nonfiction project, which in my case would be a book-length essay. I would like you to draft that proposal, roughly 1,500 words, from a half-formed idea I have been circling for some time.

The idea belongs to the debate over aesthetic testimony, and it grows out of my working life. Novelists are industrial-scale suppliers of aesthetic testimony: I spend months trying to make a reader who has never stood in a particular harbour at dusk register something about how it is there, and then readers write to me saying that they now know what that harbour is like. Yet much of the philosophical discussion holds that testimony of this sort cannot do what testimony ordinarily does — that a report on how something looks, sounds, or reads cannot be a proper ground for judging it, that acquaintance is required, and that there is something second-hand and faintly disreputable about settling an aesthetic question on somebody else's say-so.

My suspicion is that the debate is stuck because both sides treat aesthetic testimony as one kind of speech act, when in practice it divides in two. There are property-ascribing reports, which attribute an aesthetic character to the object: this novel is profound, the cathedral is overwhelming, the late quartets are austere. And there are effect reports, which describe what the object does to whoever meets it: I wept in the last chapter, that movement is unbearable, reading it feels like being held. The second kind transmits with no apparent resistance. If a trusted friend tells me a film will leave me shaken, I form the belief, I am usually right, and no one accuses me of any aesthetic failing — indeed I seem entitled to plan my evening around it. The intuitive force of the acquaintance principle, I want to argue, attaches almost entirely to the first kind. If that is correct, a good deal of the puzzle is a category conflation rather than a deep epistemological problem, and the interesting residue is much narrower than the literature suggests.

The residue is what gives the project its spine. If effect reports really do transmit knowledge, then a work's dispositional profile — the ordered set of things it does to the sorts of people who meet it — can be mapped from a sufficient accumulation of them, by someone who has never encountered the work. So: is an aesthetic property exhausted by that profile, such that mapping it is mapping the property? Or does acquaintance contribute something irreducible, so that a fully informed effect-map and an encounter are knowledge of two different things? I have a standing test case from my own correspondence: readers who assured me they knew a city from my descriptions of it, travelled there, and were disappointed. Their disappointment seems to show both that something genuine was transmitted and that what was transmitted was not what the visit supplies. I would like the proposal to build an account that explains why both halves of that can be true.

Please write the proposal so that it contains, in this order:

**The Question** (about 200 words): state the question precisely enough that it has a determinate answer, and say explicitly which question you are treating as primary — the status of the distinction, or the supervenience issue it opens onto.

**Why It Has Not Been Settled** (about 300 words): explain why the available positions leave this open — those who insist acquaintance is necessary for aesthetic judgement, those who deflate the whole problem by treating aesthetic testimony as testimony like any other, and those who relocate it from epistemology to the propriety of the believer. Show that none of them has the distinction between property-ascription and effect-reporting in view, or show where they gesture at it without exploiting it.

**Method, Month by Month** (about 400 words): what I would actually do across twelve months — sorting a corpus of real critical and reader language into the two kinds of report, constructing and testing cases, and building the argument about dispositions. Say why this approach rather than the obvious alternatives: dissolving the topic into a general theory of testimony, importing the psychology of imagination and mental imagery, or reframing everything as a problem in the ethics of belief. I want the alternatives named and refused for reasons, not ignored.

**What Would Count as a Result, and What Would Count as Failure** (about 250 words): describe the best outcome and the honest worst ones — among them that the distinction turns out to be merely verbal, and that effect reports are property ascriptions in disguise, so that nothing has been gained.

**Scope** (about 200 words): a realistic assessment of what twelve months and a single researcher can support, and what I would cut if the project began to spread.

**On the Applicant's Standing** (about 150 words): an argument for why a working novelist is well placed to ask this — and a candid statement of the limits, that is, whether practitioner experience counts here as evidence or only as a source of cases.

I will supply all references myself, so the proposal should identify positions, arguments, and examples descriptively rather than by citation, and it must not invent attributions, titles, or dates. Please write it as a finished document in my voice: measured, plain, and specific rather than grand.
