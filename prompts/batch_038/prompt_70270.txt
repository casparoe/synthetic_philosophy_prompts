I'm helping to run a small reading group attached to a municipal archive's public programme on contested monuments and street names, and the first session is supposed to be about what we are entitled to say about historical agents: Jefferson's eloquence about liberty alongside his ownership of hundreds of enslaved people, Churchill's wartime leadership alongside his conduct and rhetoric during the Bengal famine of 1943, Columbus's voyages alongside the tribute and labour regime imposed on the Taíno. The stock move in these conversations is the "man of his time" defence, and I find it unsatisfying but not obviously false. What has unsettled me is that a friend in the group — a historian with a taste for argument — produced a derivation that, if it works, shows the defence is not merely weak but self-destructive, and then uses that to conclude the defence is never available at all. Since I can neither swallow his conclusion nor return to the defence I started with, I'd like a careful diagnosis rather than a survey of the usual presentism-versus-anachronism debate, which I know well enough.

His argument, tidied up by me but I think faithful to what he said:

(1) Blame is fitting for an agent's conduct only if the agent could reasonably have been expected to know that the conduct was wrong.
(2) What an agent could reasonably have been expected to know is fixed by the epistemic situation they occupy: the evidence actually within their reach, together with the interpretive norms available to them for making sense of that evidence.
(3) Interpretive norms are not chosen; they are absorbed from the community in which one is raised and in which one's conduct is judged.
(4) Therefore, where a community's norms permitted or required a practice, an agent raised in that community could not reasonably have been expected to know the practice was wrong.
(5) Hence such an agent is not blameworthy for taking part in it.
(6) The rejoinder that dissent existed — a Las Casas, an abolitionist pamphlet, a slave revolt — does not restore blameworthiness, because dissent that is socially marginal, dangerous to voice, or addressed to an audience unable to act on it never enters the ordinary agent's epistemic situation in a usable form; a norm that a society punishes you for questioning is not, for you, an available alternative to it.
(7) Now generalise: every agent occupies some community and inherits some norms, and for any practice, however monstrous it looks from here, there has been a community whose norms permitted it.
(8) So no agent is ever blameworthy for taking part in a practice that their own community's norms permitted.
(9) By a parallel argument, no agent is praiseworthy for conforming to their community's norms either, since conformity demanded no insight the community did not already supply; and whatever credit the dissenter gets reduces to a lucky difference of temperament, upbringing, or exposure.
(10) Therefore, if the "man of his time" defence is sound at all, it is sound without limit, and both blame and praise lack proper objects when we turn them on historical agents.
(11) But they do not lack proper objects: we are unwilling to say that the abolitionist and the slaveholder who lived in the same town and read the same papers stand on exactly the same moral footing.
(12) Therefore the "man of his time" defence is unsound, and social context never mitigates anything; Jefferson, Churchill, and Columbus are answerable for what they did in precisely the way we are answerable for what we do.

Step (12) strikes me as flatly implausible, and (10) strikes me as intolerable, so somewhere the machinery must break — but I cannot locate the break, and I am not sure whether the trouble is a bad step in the middle or something wrong with the very idea, in (2), of an epistemic situation being "fixed" in the way he needs. Could you tell me which step or steps I should reject, and for each one explain why it looked perfectly acceptable when read on its own, in isolation from the work it is later made to do? I would also like the price tags: what do I lose — about fairness, about the role of luck in moral appraisal, about what the practice of blaming is even for — by giving up each candidate step, and is any of those losses worse than living with (10) or (12)? Two further questions. First, does the argument still do useful work even if it fails as a refutation — that is, does it kill a strong version of the defence while leaving a restricted version standing, and if so, what would that restricted version have to say about the three cases I named, given that they differ from one another in how much was contested around them while they were acting? Second, is (9) actually needed for the move to (10), and does it follow from what precedes it? If you have to choose, I would rather keep (1) and (11) than anything else, but I want to know what I am paying for that preference, and I want you to be explicit about which reading of "could reasonably have been expected to know" in (1) you are working with and whether the derivation still goes through on that reading. Please give me the reasoning rather than a list of further reading; nobody in the group has library access, and I would rather argue about the steps than about who said what.
