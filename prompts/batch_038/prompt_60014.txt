I'm the junior analyst who takes notes for a small working group at a public health institute, and last Thursday two members got into it in a way I couldn't referee. Context: we have to pick a rule for ranking candidate allocation policies for a scarce treatment. Each policy is a gamble over who gets treated and how they end up, and our model gives, for every outcome, a well-being number for each of the N people, built up from that person's own choices over gambles.

Here's the exchange as best I can reconstruct it.

(1) Ines said: rank policies by the expected sum of those numbers. She insisted this isnt a moral preference but a theorem — if every individual's ranking over the lotteries satisfies the expected-utility axioms, and the group's ranking does too, then any group ranking that respects unanimous preference has to be a positively weighted sum of the individual utility functions. So a concave rule, like summing square roots to favour the worse off, must violate unanimity somewhere, and she sketched a two-person, two-lottery case where everybody prefers a lottery her opponent's rule rejects.

(2) Glen said the theorem is fine but proves less than she thinks: it's linear in whatever units you choose, and each person's utility numbers are only fixed up to multiplying by a positive constant and adding a constant. Multiply Ines's own numbers by ten and the theorem still holds, with her weight a tenth as large — so the theorem never delivers equal weights, and it doesn't rule out prioritarianism either, because summing concavely transformed indices is still a positive weighted sum of perfectly legitimate utility representations. Her counterexample, he said, only shows the concave rule violates unanimity about the *transformed* numbers, which nobody claims measure well-being.

(3) Ines: that's what the veil-of-ignorance device is for. Add an impartial observer with an equal chance of turning out to be each of the N people, who inherits that person's beliefs and preferences and ranks policies by expected utility. That pins the scales down and gives equal weights.

(4) Glen: but now the observer is one expected-utility maximiser with her own attitude to risk, and that attitude is doing the work. Make her risk averse about the who-will-I-be lottery and what comes out is a concave function applied to individual utilities — prioritarian, maybe close to maximin. The veil doesn't rescue the theorem, it hides the choice inside an undefended parameter.

(5) Ines: that mixes up two different risks. Which person you turn out to be isn't a benefit or a harm to anybody, so risk aversion there is a category error, not a moral stance; the morally relevant risk is over states of the world, where you use people's own utilities untransformed. Besides, concave-of-each-then-sum and concave-of-the-sum are different rules, and only the second is what risk aversion about identity produces.

(6) Glen: and the unanimity condition itself is doing more work than she admits. She needs unanimity over lotteries, not over sure outcomes, which is the contentious ex ante version and can clash with the ex post one. Worse, our patients don't share a belief model — each has her own view of her own prognosis — and when beliefs differ, people agreeing about some lottery is often just their disagreements cancelling out, so unanimity bites in very few real cases. He said he'd give up ex ante unanimity, keep the ex post version plus a concave weighting, and own the consequence.

(7) Ines: then say that to the families. If every affected person, on her own beliefs, prefers policy A to policy B and you pick B, you owe them something better than "the theorem's assumptions didn't hold."

Can you go through this move by move? Rate each of (1)–(7) from clearly mistaken to clearly right and say why. I especially want to know whether Glen's scaling point in (2) really does neutralise Ines's counterexample, and whether her claim in (5) about which rule risk aversion over identity yields is correct. Also: is the package Glen ends up with in (6) actually coherent, or does dropping ex ante unanimity quietly take ex post unanimity with it? And what are both of them missing — including about where the weights are supposed to come from at all, and about whether the numbers we actually have are the right kind of utility to be summing? No need to name papers or people, just work the arguments through.
