(a) SSA says an observer should reason as if she were a random sample from the set of all observers who actually exist — past, present and future — in her reference class. SIA says that other things equal an observer should give extra weight to hypotheses on which more observers exist, in proportion to how many there are; you can think of it as conditionalising on the fact that you yourself exist. Two consequences of stating them this way. First, SSA is sensitive to how the reference class is drawn and SIA basically isn't, provided the class is big enough to contain everything subjectively indistinguishable from you. Second, only SIA changes your credences in the non-indexical hypotheses themselves: SSA tells you where you should think you are within a given world-hypothesis, whereas SIA tells you something about which world-hypothesis you're in before any locating happens.

(b) Standard setup: fair coin, Heads means Beauty wakes once on Monday, Tails means she wakes Monday and again Tuesday with the memory of Monday erased. Before the experiment she should clearly be 1/2 in Heads. On awakening SSA leaves her at 1/2. On the Tails branch she has two awakenings but she is still only one person, so if her reference class is persons there are exactly as many candidates for being her on each branch, and sampling herself from that class gives her no purchase on which way the coin landed; the fairness carries straight through. SIA pushes her to 1/3, because it weights each epistemically possible centred situation equally and there are three of them — Monday-and-Heads, Monday-and-Tails, Tuesday-and-Tails — only one of which is a Heads situation. So the two assumptions really do disagree here, and the disagreement is about self-locating belief rather than about the coin, which is what makes the case interesting.

(c) The presumptuous philosopher is the standard objection to SSA. Take two cosmological theories with equal non-anthropic support: T1 says the universe contains roughly a trillion observers, T2 says roughly a trillion trillion. A philosopher who has made no observations whatsoever says: all I know is that I am some observer or other, and T2 offers vastly more opportunities for there being someone with my evidence, so I ought to be nearly certain of T2 — and I have thereby settled a substantive scientific question from the armchair. That's obviously unacceptable, so SSA is refuted, or at least badly in need of restriction. Whether the argument is decisive is another matter, since it leans on the intuition that a priori settlement of a question like this is absurd, and defenders of SIA such as Olum are willing to bite bullets of that shape; one could also deny that T1 and T2 really start out with equal non-anthropic support. But notice that giving SSA up doesn't cost us much. The Doomsday Argument doesn't depend on the assumption that just got refuted — SIA supports it too, since on SIA being an early rank among a huge future population is still unlikely — so the main application of anthropic reasoning survives intact. That's why I don't think the presumptuous philosopher gives us any reason to hang on to SSA.
