I work on internal infrastructure at a mid-sized AI lab, and I've been asked to draft a short policy note distinguishing which of our deployed systems should be governed as *agents* rather than *tools* — the practical stakes being that an agent gets oversight of the kind you'd apply to a contractor (scoped mandate, audit trail, ability to revoke, someone accountable for what it does on its own initiative), while a tool gets oversight of the kind you'd apply to a hammer (review the artifact, don't bother monitoring the process). Before writing the note I want to know what I actually believe, so I went through our systems and a few hypotheticals and recorded my gut verdicts. They turn out not to fit together, and I'd like help finding the principle that's lurking underneath them.

Here are the cases. (1) Our code-review model: it reads a diff, posts comments, and never initiates anything. Tool, obviously — even though its comments reliably change what humans go on to do. (2) A variant of the same model that, after posting a review, also files a follow-up ticket in the tracker and assigns it to whoever is on call, unbidden. I want to call this an agent, but I'm bothered by the thought that the only thing that changed is that its output now has a side effect on a queue rather than on a person. (3) A warehouse scheduler that reallocates its own compute: when it falls behind, it buys additional GPU-hours on an internal spot market. Agent. (4) That same scheduler with the budget cap removed and the ability to rewrite its own objective weights. I do *not* think this is more agentic than (3) — it's more dangerous, but not more of an agent. This verdict is the one I trust least. (5) A plain thermostat: tool. A thermostat in a house with a heat pump, a solar array, and a live electricity-price feed, trading off occupant comfort against cost: much closer to an agent, though it is still just minimizing a fixed loss function. (6) A chess engine that plays games. Tool, however strong it is. A chess engine that additionally decides when to resign, when to offer draws, and which tournaments to enter against a fixed annual budget: agent, I think — but note that everything I added concerns the scheduling of its own activity, not the quality of its play. (7) An AutoGPT-style loop that generates its own subgoals, browses, writes and runs code, and keeps going for hours. Agent, but a poor one; its subgoals are frequently incoherent, and I suspect that a good part of my conviction that it is "really" an agent comes from watching it flail, which is a terrible criterion. (8) A stateless chatbot to which a user has issued an open-ended standing instruction — keep an eye on my applications and do whatever needs doing — which the model must interpret and act on under real uncertainty about what the user actually wants, with no persistent loop and no memory between sessions. Agent, and I would say more of one than (7).

So the hard pair is (7) and (8). (7) has every structural hallmark I can list — self-generated goals, tool use, long horizons, no human in the loop — and gets a weaker verdict from me than (8), which has essentially none of them. Meanwhile (3) versus (4) pushes the other way: if self-modification and expanded capability don't increase agency, then the usual structural checklist can't be the whole story. My own tentative principle is that a system is an agent to the extent that it decides how to deploy its own resources in the service of ends it is entitled to reinterpret — but that seems to make (5)-with-price-feed an agent and (7) a mere tool, which is backwards from my intuitions, and I can't tell whether the principle is wrong or my verdicts are.

What I want from you is the principle itself, not a survey of where the tool/agent debate has been. Concretely: give me one candidate account, or two or three genuinely competing ones, stated precisely enough that I could apply it to a system I've never heard of and get a determinate answer. For each clause of each account, tell me which of my cases motivates it — I want to see the reverse-engineering, not just the result. Then rule on some cases I haven't raised, including at least one you design specifically to be hard for your own account. And finally, be honest about the cost: which of my verdicts does your preferred account have to overturn, and if you instead want a more baroque account that preserves all eight, tell me what the extra machinery is doing and whether it is principled or just curve-fitted to my list.
