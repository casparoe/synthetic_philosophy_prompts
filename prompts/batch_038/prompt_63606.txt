You are an expert in the foundations of decision theory, with particular depth on Hofstadter's superrationality, the Platonia dilemma, and the Luring Lottery, and on the subsequent debate over whether superrational reasoning can be absorbed into causal decision theory rather than treated as a rival to it. Please think carefully before answering, and do some of your own analysis first: work through the logical relations among the claims below before you evaluate them. Do not reach for a stock summary of superrationality or of its standard objections.

I am drafting the position section of a thesis chapter and want a consistency audit before these commitments go on paper. Here is the package, stated in my own words. I have deliberately not signalled which items I think are the weak ones, and I have no prior about which I would sacrifice; that is what I am asking you to determine.

1. Superrationality is not a rival decision theory to causal decision theory. What survives in it is a claim about causal structure: when a group of agents is running the same decision procedure, their choices share a common cause in that procedure, and a causal decision theorist who models this correctly will arrive at what Hofstadter calls the superrational choice. The fact that my act is evidence about yours does no independent justificatory work.

2. That common-cause structure is an empirical and contingent matter, not something readable off the game matrix. I treat others as running my procedure only where I have positive evidence that they share my information, my values, and my style of reasoning. Where I lack that evidence, they are background parameters of my decision problem, on a par with the weather.

3. Granted the superrational premise, the answer to a superrational problem is unique. Agents who share the premises and the information must land on the same act. I take this to be an assumption about rationality rather than a theorem of decision theory, but I accept it; and I hold that when two self-described superrational thinkers disagree about what to do in the same case, at least one of them has made a mistake.

4. In the Platonia dilemma — an eccentric trillionaire gathers twenty people, offers a billion dollars to the sender of the one and only one reverse-charge telegram arriving by noon the next day, nothing to anyone if none or more than one arrives, and forbids communication — the deterministic reasoning is decisive. All twenty will do the same thing; "all send" and "none send" are the only two answers available to a deterministic reasoner; both yield nothing; so the superrational verdict is not to send. Hofstadter's alternative, that each should send with probability one in twenty, needs a further premise: that twenty agents who share a decision procedure can nevertheless generate independent random draws. On my own account of item 1, I do not think he is entitled to it, since what makes them superrational is that the same procedure on the same inputs produces the same output.

5. In the Luring Lottery, however — the lottery run from Hofstadter's column, in which a reader posted a card bearing either the number one or the number of entries desired, the million-dollar prize was divided by the total number of entries received, and one entry was then drawn at random — I think the superrational recommendation was correct and I would have followed it: estimate how many readers were likely to respond, roll a die with that many faces, and post a single entry only if the die comes up one.

6. In that same case I think the verdict is properly sensitive to your estimate of the reference class. A reader who expected a large fraction of the readership to respond and a reader who expected a small one could each reason impeccably and end up doing different things, and I would not call either of them mistaken. I also think the actual responses — cards claiming astronomically many entries, including a googolplex — are good evidence that most respondents lay outside the superrational reference class, and that this is a legitimate input to the calculation rather than a failure of nerve.

7. Superrationality never asks me to accept a genuine loss. Where its verdict coincides with ordinary expected-value maximisation it has nothing to say; where it differs, the sacrifice is only apparent, because the outcome I am said to be forgoing was never in fact available to me.
