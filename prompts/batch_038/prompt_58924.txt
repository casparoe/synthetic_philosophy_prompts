so my sister runs a tiny "longevity desk" — people pay her to tell them how much longer an institution has left. Investors mostly, but also a couple of museums and two city governments. She's weirdly good at it and she never explains the method, she just gives a number and shrugs. I've been keeping a private log of her calls for about four years because I want to work out what shes actually doing, and now I've got a case I care about and I want to predict her before she opens her mouth.

The log. I've included the class file she had open, because she lets me watch.

- scooter-share startup, 2 years old, well funded, celebrity founder. File: 60 companies from that wave, half dead by month 20; of the ones still rolling at month 24, half dead by month 48. Call: gone by its fourth birthday. Wouldn't move for the funding round — "cash changes when you die, not whether."
- mutual (policyholder-owned) insurer, 70 years old. File: class median total lifespan 45 years, but of the ones that reached 70, half reached 140. Call: still writing policies at 140.
- personalist populist party, 6 years old. File: 38 parties of that type, half dead by year 9; of those alive at 6, half dead by 12. Call: a name on a ballot paper until 12, not until 20. Asked about the leader's charisma she said the leader is already in there, that's *why* it made it to six.
- 500-year-old university in a funding crisis. She refused to open the "all universities ever" file on the grounds that institutions founded before print don't have the same death curve, and used a class of 22 of them instead: of those that reached 500, half were still going at 1000. Call: outlives all of us, only question is what "it" refers to in 2200.
- bar bet on whether the current top regional military-commercial power keeps its position. She said the honest class is 5 cases with a median tenure around 90 years, too small to bet on, so she widened to roughly 20 cases of "decisive regional lead," median tenure 55, then conditioned on having already held it 45 years, which landed her at 90 total. Her words: rather be roughly right with twenty cases than precisely wrong with five.
- 12-year-old open-source library with one unpaid maintainer. File: half of such projects abandoned by year 5; of those alive at 12, half dead by 30. Call: another decade, twelve years at the outside, then somebody forks it. Note that's *below* what the file's conditional number would give, and she also said a fork isn't a death, so her caveat pushes one way and her number the other.

I've already ruled out the dumb rule (class median total minus current age) because that one says the insurer died 25 years ago. What I can't reconcile is two things she's said at different times: "age is the only variable I trust" and "anyone quoting an unconditional median is doing it wrong, you always condition on having survived this long." On every call above those two give the same answer, which is exactly why I can't tell them apart.

My case: a 45-year-old daily newspaper in a city of about 400,000. Has never missed a publication day. Circulation down around 70% from peak. As of last month it's majority-owned by a nonprofit with a $60m endowment. Files she could plausibly open: (a) all ~1,700 US dailies ever, median total lifespan 58; (b) of dailies that reached 45, half reached 72; (c) but (b) is mostly papers that turned 45 before 1990 — for papers that turned 45 after 2000, half were gone within 9 years; (d) the narrow class, dailies founded 1975–85 in metros of 250–600k, n = 11.

So: what's her number, and what procedure produces it. Lay out the rules consistent with the whole log — I can see "expected remaining life equals current age" and "read off the conditional median for the class," but I think there's a third one hiding about when you're allowed to widen versus narrow the reference class (she did both, in different cases, and the paper makes those two instructions point in opposite directions), and maybe a fourth about which inside-view facts are admissible. Give me what each rule yields for the paper. They should not all agree; if two of them happen to land on the same number here, say so rather than padding the list. Then commit to one and tell me what she'd have to be assuming about the shape of the death curve in each class for her log to come out the way it does, because I genuinely can't tell whether she thinks everything ages like a startup or like a tree.

Second question: is the endowment a fact she's allowed to use? On the scooter she said no. But this looks different — an endowed nonprofit arguably doesn't have the same failure mode at all, which might mean the paper isn't in any of the four files and she has to build a fifth one. Would she do that, or is that exactly the move she calls cheating?

Last thing. I see her next month and I get to ask about one more case to break the tie. Which is most diagnostic and why: a 90-year-old family hardware store in a town that's growing again; a 4-year-old charter school in a state that just rewrote its renewal rules; or a 25-year-old bilateral fisheries treaty both of whose signatories just changed governments. If none of the three actually splits the candidate rules, build me one that does.
