I am facilitating a two-hour workshop for a grantmaking organisation whose staff divide, roughly, between moral philosophers and quantitative analysts. The session concerns how the possibility of an indefinitely long future, and of unbounded total value, should bear on the evaluation of interventions. Two reflexive moves dominate the room, and they pull in opposite directions: one faction holds that against an infinite backdrop any finite contribution is negligible, so nothing funded can really matter; the other holds that because the stakes are infinite, any option with even a slight probability of success outweighs every finite alternative, so expected-value arithmetic settles everything. I want a diagnostic decision tree that sorts participants by what they actually believe about infinite aggregation, rather than by which of these two reflexes they repeat.

Please build the instrument to the following specification.

First, set out the logical space: a short list of the independent questions your nodes are drawn from, with the answer options each admits, so that it is visible how the leaves are placed rather than merely enumerated.

Fixed starting node: the participant accepts the workshop's framing — that the value of the world could be unbounded, and that this raises a real question about how outcomes are compared — and wants to know where they stand.

Six to eight branch questions. Each must be a concrete case or a plain-language claim answerable on first reading: two worlds differing only by a finite amount of good spread across an infinite span; whether the verdict may depend on the order in which the parts of an infinite series are added; whether an agent who must choose should be told that for some pairs of options there is no fact of the matter; whether a discount rate is a moral claim or a bookkeeping device. Technical vocabulary is banned from the question text — no term of art from measure theory, non-standard analysis, or the aggregation literature may appear at a node, though such terms may appear in your key. No node may name a view, an author, or a position.

Leaves: at least seven terminal positions, including treating the problem as one that arises only in an idealisation the actual world never instantiates; discounting, presented both as a substantive moral claim and as a mathematical repair; comparison by way of finite truncations of an infinite series; comparison by way of quantities richer than the reals, on which some infinite totals are larger than others; the view that infinite outcomes are not comparable and that evaluation must be re-founded on local, act-by-act considerations; the view that the paradoxes indict the decision procedure rather than the theory of value; and the view that several individually plausible requirements are jointly unsatisfiable, so one must be surrendered, together with a statement of which. Where a leaf has a standard name, give it; where it has none, describe it functionally and mark it as unnamed. Do not invent attributions.

For each leaf add two sentences: the commitment its defender finds hardest to defend, and what it implies for how this organisation should treat a long-run intervention that carries a small probability of an enormous benefit.

Finally: (a) identify any pair of leaves your tree cannot genuinely separate by means of answerable questions, and say why; (b) list at least two combinations of answers that no leaf occupies, and for each say whether the gap exists because the position is incoherent, because it is unattractive, or because nobody has developed it; and (c) state which single branch does the most work in telling the negligibility reflex apart from the infinite-stakes reflex, and whether any position in your tree licenses both.
