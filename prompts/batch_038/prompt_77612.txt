I sit on my high school's student council. Last week a school-wide referendum passed 54 to 46 to eliminate our peer-counseling program, and when several of us asked the council to take up the objections raised by the 46 percent, the president cut us off with "vox populi, vox Dei." My Latin teacher told me that one of the earliest surviving appearances of the phrase is in a letter from Alcuin of York to Charlemagne in 798, in which Alcuin says that people who keep repeating it should not be listened to, since the riotousness of the crowd is always very close to madness — so the proverb's first recorded use is an argument against it.

I would rather answer him properly than just repeat that fact. Could you lay out, as a menu, the distinct precise claims that "the voice of the people is the voice of God" could be making — say, a claim about how reliably a crowd judges, a claim about where legitimate authority comes from, a claim about what makes a decision binding on those who opposed it — and for each say whether it is trivial, defensible, or false, and what it has to assume about who counts as "the people" and about whether a voice is a passing preference or a settled judgment? Then tell me which reading, if any, would actually license ending the argument the way he did, and whether any of the favorable readings survives Alcuin's objection. A numbered list I could bring to the meeting would be ideal.
