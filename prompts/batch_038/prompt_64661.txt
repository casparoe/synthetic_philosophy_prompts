**Question.** State the Church–Turing thesis as precisely as you can. Then say whether it is a theorem, an empirical hypothesis about the world, or a conceptual/definitional claim, and defend your answer. Part of your defense should say what would count as evidence for or against it.

**Student's answer.**

"The Church-Turing thesis says that any function on the natural numbers computable by an effective procedure — a finite mechanical method a person could carry out with paper and pencil — is computable by a Turing machine. Church put it in terms of lambda-definability and Kleene in terms of general recursive functions; these classes were then proved to coincide with each other and with the Turing-computable functions, so the rival formalisms turn out to be notational variants of a single notion.

Strictly it can't be a theorem, because 'effective procedure' isn't a defined mathematical term and there's nothing to prove from. What supports it is Turing's analysis of the human clerk following rules — finitely many symbols, attention confined to one or two at a time, everything else recoverable from what's on the page — plus the fact that eighty-plus years of attempts to write down a procedure that outruns his machines have all failed. So it's confirmed the way a well-supported scientific law is, by the repeated failure of counterexamples, and it's falsifiable in principle: build a device that uses some physical process to compute a non-recursive function and the thesis is refuted. Quantum computers already point in this direction, since superposition lets a machine work over exponentially many states at once, which is why they factor big numbers fast.

Some people call the claim that the physical world only ever realizes computable processes 'the Church-Turing thesis' too, but I think that just is what Turing and Church meant, because the whole point of the 1936 paper is that computation is a physical activity of a person manipulating symbols.

Finally, the thesis overreaches when you apply it to people. Godel showed that any sufficiently strong formal system has a sentence that's true but unprovable in it, so no formal system can capture a mathematician's reasoning; Penrose argues this means the mind isn't a Turing machine. Relatedly, the halting problem shows no algorithm decides of an arbitrary program whether it halts, yet I can often just look at a program and see that it stops. So the thesis is fine about paper-and-pencil procedures but not about human thinking."
