So I'm a second-year PhD student TA-ing a big intro course — "chance, evidence and paradox" — mostly non-majors, and I run one 50-minute discussion section of about 25 of them, sitting at six tables of four-ish. Next week is the principle of indifference and I'm supposed to do Bertrand's chord paradox, but I really don't want to just draw three chord diagrams on the board and talk. Half of them will have half-remembered a webpage about it and the other half will zone out within four minutes.

Can you design me a betting game instead? The shape I have in mind: a few students act as bookies, each one quietly committed to a different procedure for generating the random chord (the usual suspects — random point along a fixed radius as the midpoint, random midpoint anywhere in the disk, random pair of endpoints on the circumference), and everybody else bets on whether the chord that comes out is longer than the side of the inscribed equilateral triangle. I want the odds each bookie posts to be wrong in a way the bettors can actually exploit, so the sharpest table starts making money off arbitrage and the room notices that the bookies are contradicting each other even though nobody has made an arithmetic error. Then the reveal that the contradiction can't be fixed by "just doing the experiment properly," because there is no such thing as the experiment until you've fixed a generator.

The bit I most want you to build in, and which I haven't seen anyone do: settlement should happen through a physical apparatus the class assembles out of what I bring — straws, string, pins, paper circles, a spinner, dice — and at some point the apparatus itself gets chosen by drawing lots out of a hat, so the under-determination pops back up one level higher right when they think its been resolved. If you think that's a gimmick that backfires, say so.

Constraints: 50 minutes, no laptops in the room, I can bring paper, string, straws, pins, a cheap spinner, dice and candy. Candy is the currency and the payoff, plus a tiny participation bump for the winning table — nothing with actual money attached. Give me roles, exact rules, a minute-by-minute timeline, and the real numbers for stakes and posted odds so the arbitrage works out arithmetically — I want to check that myself, so please show the math rather than just asserting it. Then a debrief that maps specific moments from the game onto the principle of indifference, onto the choice of parameterization, and onto the response that says you have to pin down the physical randomizing mechanism before the question is even answerable. Also: what predictably goes wrong the first time you run something like this with 25 people, and what do I say when someone asks "okay but which one is actually right" — because somebody will, within two minutes of the reveal.

And be straight with me about the failure mode I'm most nervous about: that the whole exercise just teaches them that probability is whatever you feel like. If you think the game as I've sketched it will land that way, or that the hat-draw is doing something dishonest, or that something in the premise is confused in a way I haven't spotted, tell me plainly rather than politely building around it — I'd much rather hear that now than in week six. And where you're guessing about whether a piece will actually work on sleepy undergrads, mark it as a guess.
