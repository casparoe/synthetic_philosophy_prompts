I've been working through the dispute over whether an agent's reasons depend on her actual motivations, and one structural maneuver keeps recurring that I cannot find a label for. The analysis on offer is that A has a reason to φ just in case A would be moved to φ under conditions of full information and full rationality — that is, by an idealized version of A. A case then arises in which the idealized A is unmoved by something the theorist takes A plainly to have reason to do: say, A has no interest in the aesthetic merits of a piece of music, and even the fully informed, fully rational A remains indifferent, yet the theorist insists A has a reason to attend to them. The analysis is not withdrawn. Instead the description of the idealized agent is elaborated — she must be vividly, not merely propositionally, acquainted with the facts, or her desires must be systematically unified — and the elaboration is invariably the one that yields the verdict the theorist had already reached. My worry is that the elaboration is selected by the target verdict, so the analysis threatens to say only that A has reason to φ if a fully reasonable person in A's circumstances would want to φ, which is what it was meant to explain.

Does this have a name? I don't think it is simply ad hocness, and I have met talk of a conditional fallacy in this neighborhood, but that seemed to concern the idealizing conditions destroying or distorting the response, not their being tuned to reproduce a fixed list of verdicts. Tell me which established diagnosis fits best and how each near neighbor differs from what I've described; if none fits, coin a term and define it precisely enough to separate a refinement that can be motivated independently of the cases it settles from one that cannot, and give an example on each side of that line.
