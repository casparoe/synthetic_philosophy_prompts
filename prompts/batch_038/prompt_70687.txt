I sit on the professional conduct panel of a state association of licensed engineers, and we hear a case on the fourteenth that has split us three to two. The respondent is a member who was seconded years ago from design work into a public affairs role at a gas utility. Last spring he appeared before a county commission considering a permit for a welded-steel transmission main installed in the late 1960s, and was asked, under oath, whether there was any evidence that the line posed a danger to nearby residents. His answer, per the transcript: "There is no evidence that this line poses a danger to nearby residents." Later in the same hearing he volunteered that the line had an excellent safety record. Both sentences appear to have been literally true as spoken, and that is the whole difficulty. The file discloses three things behind them. First, six weeks before the hearing he signed a deferral of an in-line inspection of the segment to the following fiscal year, on budget grounds; the absence of evidence was partly a product of his own signature. Second, he co-signed an internal memo that reclassified two releases from 2019 from "leaks" to "seepage," which kept them below our association's own reportable-incident threshold and out of the numbers behind the safety-record claim. Third, a contractor's field note from two years earlier described anomalous corrosion at three crossings; he had read it and never circulated it. Our code, in the printed booklet every member is issued, contains two relevant clauses: one prohibiting a member from making, or causing to be made, a statement the member knows to be false, and another prohibiting conduct involving dishonesty or deceit, and conduct that brings the profession into disrepute. Its preamble directs members to treat public safety as the paramount consideration. Available remedies run from dismissal, through private admonition, to public censure, mandatory continuing education, and suspension of membership for up to two years. The respondent's written answer is contrite about the deferral and insistent that the sentence was true.

Please draft the decision memo I will circulate to the other four members before the meeting, in the range of 1,300 to 1,600 words, written in a register the panel could adopt as its own. It needs a clear bottom line: which clause or clauses, if any, are engaged, which specific remedy, and over what period. But the reasoning is what the split members actually need, so I want the memo to say explicitly whether the distinction between lying and misleading should do any work in applying the false-statement clause here, and if it should not, why not — including whether a speaker who controls the evidential base has done something categorically different from one who merely chose words that let a hearer draw his own inference. Propose an operative test the panel could apply in future cases involving literally true statements: something three lay members could administer without a philosopher in the room, with the criteria stated rather than gestured at. Then run your test against each of the three fact patterns separately — the deferred inspection, the reclassification memo, and the unshared contractor's note — and tell us where it treats them alike and where it does not, and why that is the right result rather than an artifact of the test. Finally, give the losing side its strongest case: the argument that the false-statement clause was deliberately drafted narrowly so that members can speak in public at all, that a misleading standard announced for the first time in this proceeding operates retroactively and will chill candid testimony before bodies like that commission, and that our association is not the proper forum for policing what a member says to a county legislature. Say how much weight that carries, and separate what we should punish retrospectively from what we should fix prospectively by amending our guidance.

Approach this as a professional philosopher of assertion and applied ethics who has also served on real disciplinary panels and knows what a document like this has to survive. Before drafting, give me a short brainstorming section — half a page at most — listing the cruxes on which the outcome actually turns, the candidate tests you considered (an intent-to-deceive standard, a reasonable-hearer standard, one keyed to the speaker's control over what evidence exists, one keyed to whether the question invited the whole truth rather than a narrow answer), and which facts in the file would move your recommendation in which direction. Then write the memo, with headers. Do not cite cases, statutes, professional rules from other jurisdictions, or any literature, and do not append a reading list; I want your reasoning from the clause language and facts I have given you, and invented authority would be worse than none. If you think the file is missing something that matters — his actual state of mind about the reclassification, whether anyone at the utility relied on the deferral memo, whether the county had the corrosion note independently — flag it, say what you would assume in the meantime, and note how the recommendation would change if the assumption is wrong.
