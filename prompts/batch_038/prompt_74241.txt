so my city council is voting next month on whether to formally apologize for bulldozing a mostly Black neighborhood in the 60s to run a highway through it, and I went to the public comment night mostly to hear what people would actually say. The weird thing: the exact same sentence got used by both camps, and both used it like it settled the argument — "you can't apologize with somebody else's money."

The people against the apology meant something like: the council didn't do the bulldozing, the people who did are dead, so a resolution read out by current officials isn't an apology at all, it's theatre — and if you attach any payment to it you're taxing people who never did anything wrong to settle a debt that isn't theirs.

The people for the apology meant something like: an apology with no money attached is worthless, because the party who actually owes it — that old administration, the highway people — never pays a cent, so today's council gets to look repentant at zero cost to itself. Words are free, and that's exactly the problem.

Opposite conclusions, identical sentence, and I sat there unable to say what was wrong with either use. Can you break it down? I want the distinct precise claims that one sentence could be expressing, seperated along the joints: who the "somebody" is (the apologizer? the original wrongdoer? the person whose tax money funds it? the person who benefited?), what "apologize" is being assumed to require (a speech act with felicty conditions, an admission of liability, a transfer of something the apologizer would rather keep), and what "can't" is doing — conceptual impossibility, moral impermissibility, or just practical ineffectiveness.

For each reading, tell me whether it's a platitude everyone in that room already accepts, whether it's actually true, and which of the two camps it would help if it were true. Then the question I really care about: is there any single reading both camps could accept, or is the sentence just doing decisive-sounding work with no content underneath? And if the readings that turn out to be true are ones nobody there meant, say so plainly rather than reconstructing everybody charitably.

One more thing — does any of it hinge on the apologizer being a continuing institution instead of a person? I couldn't tell whether the sentence comes out differently for a city than for, say, a grandson apologizing to a family for what his grandfather did, and that felt like it should matter but I couldn't get a handle on why.
