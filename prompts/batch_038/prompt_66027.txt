I write novels for a living — three out, fourth due in the spring — and a literary magazine has asked me for a short position piece on who actually owns a story, timed to some AI conference, and my agent says the editors want my "principles" stated cleanly for once instead of the hedging I do in interviews. So I sat down and wrote them out, and now I honestly can't tell whether they hang together. Here's the list.

(a) Nobody owns an idea. Premises, "what ifs," plot shapes, tropes — all free game, always have been. I tell my MFA students to steal premises shamelessly and worry only about whether they can execute one. (b) Same goes for lives: nobody owns their own story either, so any writer may fictionalize any real person including me, and I owe them nothing. I built a whole section of my second book out of my great-aunt's letters, which I inherited, and I don't think her grandchildren — who are alive and who hated the book — had any standing to stop me. (c) But when a friend I'd workshopped with took the central device I'd talked her through over dinner — a novel made entirely of the marginalia left in one library copy by successive borrowers — and got it out two years ahead of me, I didn't feel like she'd been merely rude. I felt like she'd taken something that was mine. I still call it theft when I tell the story. (d) I also think style theft is real: someone who writes a book so thoroughly in my voice, rhythms and all, that reviewers mix us up has wronged me even if not one clause is copied, and I don't think that's just me being precious. (e) Training a language model on my novels without asking is theft too, even though the model stores no copy of anything and emits no sentences of mine. Something is taken. (f) That said, when the Authors Guild lost the Google Books case in 2015 I said in print that it was the right result — tens of millions of books scanned with no permission asked, and fine, because readers got search instead of substitutes. (g) Fanfiction of my own work is morally fine and shouldn't be litigated or shamed, including the stuff that's better than my books. (h) Copyright's moral point is to let people who write for a living keep writing; where enforcement doesn't serve that, it loses its claim on me.

A friend of mine who does IP law read the list and said "this is a mess, pick three and stop," and my agent said its completely coherent and that the incoherence is the brand. They can't both be right, and I can't tell which one is bluffing. So: go through it as an audit rather than an essay. Which pairs genuinely pull against each other, and which ones only look bad but survive inspection once you say what's really doing the work? For each real tension, give me the cheapest repair and what that repair costs — meaning, which sentence do I have to stop saying in interviews. Rank the tensions by severity instead of dumping them all on me as equally bad, and end by telling me which single revision buys the most consistency for the least damage. I'm not attached to any particular item on the list, I just want to know where the load-bearing wall is and whether I've been leaning on it while telling everyone it isn't there.
