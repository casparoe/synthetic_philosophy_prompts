I spent most of yesterday arguing with a friend about Machiavelli and came away unconvinced by both of us, so I would like a second opinion on where the disagreement actually lies. She runs a small political-risk practice and reads The Prince and the Discourses as operational manuals rather than as rhetoric. My charge against her was that her account of virtù and fortune cannot be falsified: whatever happens, she can say either that the man had virtù and fortune betrayed him, or that he lacked virtù and fortune merely revealed the lack. She denied this, and offered as evidence her verdicts on a series of cases, drawn from clients and historical studies she has worked through, on the grounds that a judgment is constrained if you can point to what would have produced the opposite one. Here is the ledger as I recorded it.

1. A provincial general who raised his own militia from the peasantry, fought a ten-year war of secession, and founded a state with its own tax system and conscript army. He seized power by killing the family of the governor he had served and betraying the commander who had once sheltered him. He died in his bed, having named a successor by written law, and the state outlived him by two generations. Her verdict: virtù of the highest order and a genuine founding, though she will not concede him glory.

2. A competent, honest administrator installed as premier of a dependent territory by the metropolitan power and kept in place by its garrison. He governed well for eight years and was removed without incident the day that power changed its policy. Verdict: no virtù at all; his competence is beside the point, since nothing he stood on was his own.

3. A finance minister who rose through his own party organization and became head of government during a long stagnation. He governed with extreme deliberation and prospered for fifteen years. When a sudden shock demanded immediate unilateral action, he spent months assembling consensus and was swept away. Verdict: he lacked virtù; the times turned and he could not turn with them; the ruin was his, and fortune only supplied the date.

4. A commander of the same deliberate temperament fighting a long war of attrition in which deliberation was precisely what the situation required, with forces largely equipped and paid by an ally. He won. Verdict: full virtù. She insisted here that caution is no defect and audacity no merit in itself.

5. A young heir who inherited a well-ordered state and lost it within two years when two great powers marched across his territory. Nobody could identify a single error of his. Verdict: pure fortune; he was never a candidate for virtù, never having been given an occasion; and the deeper fault lay with his father, since a state that cannot outlive one man's lifetime is badly founded.

6. An exiled leader of obvious talent with his own armed following who, when the crisis he had waited twenty years for finally arrived, spent four months negotiating. The window closed and he achieved nothing further. Verdict: he had the materials of virtù and no achievement; ability that never meets an occasion does not enter the ledger.

7. A slow, unglamorous ruler who governed through a quiet period and spent it building a professional civil service, a standing army recruited from his own population, and a fiscal reserve. A severe shock arrived after his death and the state weathered it. Verdict: the highest form of virtù, better than any victory, because the provisions hold after the builder is gone.

The case she has agreed to rule on next, and the one I want your prediction about, is this. A founder-president who came to power entirely by his own means: his own forces, his own money, his own alliances, no patron. He ruled for twenty-five years with recognized ability, won two wars by long preparation and overwhelming force, wrote the constitution, and built the civil service and citizen army described in case 7. He was by temperament and by unbroken record a man who never moved until every arrangement was complete. In his twenty-second year he wrote a private memorandum, since published, diagnosing the coming crisis with precision: a change of regime in a neighboring state, combined with a shift in the regional balance, would within a few years demand immediate unilateral action undertaken before preparations were complete and against his own lifelong method. He prescribed exactly that course, and he built the machinery it required — a rapid-reaction force and a legal instrument authorizing immediate executive action without legislative approval. When the crisis came three years later, he did what he had always done: four months of assembling allies and waiting for certainty. The window closed. During the critical fortnight his principal ally abandoned the arrangement and he himself fell seriously ill. The state was dismembered within eighteen months and he died in exile. The civil service and the officer corps he created were not destroyed; they persisted under the occupation and formed the nucleus of the restoration thirty years later.

What I want, in order:

- A prediction of her verdict. Was this a man of virtù wrecked by fortune, or a man whose virtù failed at the one moment that counted? Give one answer as your best guess, and say how strongly the ledger compels it.
- The candidate rules her seven verdicts admit. For each, name the conception of virtù it embodies — I have in mind distinctions such as self-reliance and one's own arms, the fit between a man's mode of proceeding and the demands of the times, the provisions laid in calm weather against the flood, the seizing of occasion when fortune offers it, and the separate axis she invokes when she grants empire but withholds glory — and say which of these the earlier cases have already ruled out or demoted to a necessary condition. Cases 3 and 4 look to me as though they damage at least one of them in both directions.
- The point of divergence on the new case. Two of these rules seem to fit everything she has said so far and to come apart here, and there is a further complication inside one of them: the same chapter of The Prince that tells us a man is ruined when his mode of proceeding no longer suits the times also tells us that no man is found able to adapt, either because he cannot deviate from what his nature disposes him to, or because, having always prospered on one path, he cannot persuade himself to leave it. If that second claim is doing work for her, the new case stops looking like a failure of virtù and starts looking like the old Borgia pattern — a man who did everything a prudent and able man could do, whose ruin came through no fault of his own but by an extraordinary malignity of fortune. Tell me which way she goes, and on what grounds.
- Whether my unfalsifiability charge survives the exercise. Is there any verdict on this case that her ledger forbids her, or can every outcome be absorbed?
- The next case I should put to her to discriminate between the two leading readings, together with what she would have to surrender in each branch of her answer.

Please write in British English. I would also like you to be explicit about the standing of your prediction rather than merely confident in it: identify which of my reports of her verdicts, if I have mis-stated or over-simplified it, would flip your conclusion; name the step in your reasoning you regard as most likely to be wrong; and say what argument or consideration would change your mind about the verdict itself rather than merely complicate the picture. If your prediction is leaning on a contested interpretation of Machiavelli instead of on her track record, flag that dependence as well.
