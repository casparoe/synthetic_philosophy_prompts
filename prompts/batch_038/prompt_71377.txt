I write the science-and-ideas column for a general-interest site and I'm stuck on a piece that keeps trying to be about why institutions make you waste something valuable in order to get in. My running examples: a religious order whose novitiate runs for years and produces no revenue for anyone, a startup that torches a chunk of its Series A on a glass-walled office no one needs, a dating app I'm covering where you have to destroy a token you paid real money for before anyone will reply to you, and the whole quiet-luxury thing where the expensive part is deliberately invisible to outsiders and legible only to insiders. I showed the draft to three people and got three different diagnoses. The biologist said this is textbook costly signaling, handicap principle, done deal. The behavioral economist said most of it is cheap talk plus a handful of unfakeable indices, and the "waste" is incidental to what's actually doing the work. The sociologist said the framing was built for peacocks and doesnt travel to humans, who signal to audiences rather than to trading partners. Before I write three thousand words picking a side, I want the spec — the thing I can hold each of them to.

So: what does a theory of honest signaling have to deliver before I'm entitled to call something a costly signal? Give me the desiderata, and for each one the case or consideration that motivates it, plus which are non-negotiable and which are really just the preferences of one camp. It has to be usable as a checklist I can run a live example through in a paragraph or two, not a survey of the field. Stuff I know is in the neighborhood and want you to sort out: the line between a signal, a cue (information leaked rather than sent), and an index (something unfakeable because it's physically pinned to the underlying quality) — and whether a theory is even allowed to treat that as a fixed trichotomy, or whether the boundaries move; why honesty has to be a property of an equilibrium rather than of how the trait was designed; the point that what matters is the marginal cost of signaling more going down as quality goes up, not that high-quality signallers can simply afford to pay more, since affordability isn't a mechanism and I think it also matters that costs and benefits are being traded off in the same currency for that result to go through at all; what the theory owes about cheap-but-honest signals and about common-interest situations where lying wouldn't pay anyway; what it predicts about how much cheating there should be and how much is too much; who counts as the receiver when the audience isn't the party that pays the reward (third parties, eavesdroppers, reputation to a crowd that never interacts with you); and how a trait that started as a leaked cue and got ritualized into a signal is supposed to be handled without the whole account turning unfalsifiable. Then tell me if some subset of these can't be jointly satisfied — my suspicion is that "honesty requires waste" collides with "the theory has to cover honest signals generally," and that the collision is the finding rather than a footnote. Also: which single requirement knocks out the most candidate explanations, including ones that aren't signaling stories at all, like runaway preference feedback or sensory bias or plain screening by the other side. Once the list exists, spend a few sentences each on how a Zahavi-style handicap account, a Grafen-style signaling-game account, and a Spence-style economic screening account do against it — briefly, the weight should stay on the criteria. No bibliography, no reading list, no citations, I'll chase the literature myself.

Last thing and it matters for the piece: keep the settled parts and the contested parts visibly separate. My readers will take whatever I assert as what experts think, so if something is a live dispute among people who actually work on this — whether the handicap principle is a coherent general claim, whether wastefulness is a diagnostic or a red herring, whether any of it transfers from animals to human institutions — mark it as disputed and say roughly who's disputing it, and dont let one camp's position wear a consensus costume. And if my framing is the problem, if "the waste is the signal" is a prejudice I picked up from pop science rather than a requirement anyone serious defends, say that bluntly at the top before anything else.
