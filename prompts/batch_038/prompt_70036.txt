I am in Year 12 and I produce my school's history podcast, whose listeners are mostly sixth-formers and a handful of our teachers. Next week's guest is an archaeologist who has spent her career reading skeletal and settlement evidence from the earliest farming villages of the Near East, and who argues in public that the adoption of agriculture was on balance a catastrophe for human beings: shorter stature, more infectious disease, harder and less varied work, and the first appearance of inequality that could be inherited. I do not want the episode to be forty minutes of her restating that thesis, and I do not want to ask her anything that a listener could answer with a shrug.

Please draft ten interview questions for me, arranged so that the set opens on ground she will happily stand on and becomes progressively harder for her to hold. Within the ten, please make sure there is at least one question on each of the following:

- the difference between an affluence secured by wanting little and one secured by producing more, and whether the first kind of affluence is really the thing we should be valuing;
- who counts: if farming supported vastly more people at a lower average quality of life, in what sense is that a failure, and what she takes her units of comparison to be;
- the archaeological evidence for lethal violence, including massacres, among populations who lived before farming at all, so that agriculture cannot be credited with inventing brutality;
- the fact that some pre-agricultural societies were already sedentary, hierarchical and monument-building, and some kept slaves, which seems to cut the causal link she wants between farming and rank;
- whether "mistake" can honestly be applied to a slow, uncoordinated sequence of locally sensible choices that no one was ever in a position to decline, and if not, what she thinks she is saying when she uses the word;
- irreversibility: whether the trap she has in mind is population density ratcheting upwards, and whether she sees anything structurally similar operating now, since listeners will certainly be wondering.

Before each question, give me a single line stating which pressure point it is aimed at and roughly what she concedes if she answers it badly. Then, for each question, add a follow-up keyed to the answer she is most likely to give — the specific next move rather than an invitation to expand — and write out that expected answer in her own probable phrasing so that I can recognise it live on air. She has two very reliable deflections: an appeal to regional variation (the evidence looks different from valley to valley) and a refusal to rank societies (it is not for us to judge whose life went better). At least three of the follow-ups should be constructed so that using either deflection costs her something she needs.

The tenth question should be the closing one: what discovery in the next twenty years would make her give up the thesis. Please also tell me, in advance, what a non-answer to that question will sound like, so I know whether to let it pass.

Do not include anything about how she came to archaeology, what she enjoys about fieldwork, or advice for students. Please write the whole response in British English, including spelling and idiom, and keep the questions themselves short enough to be read aloud in a single breath.
