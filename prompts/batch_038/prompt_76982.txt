I spent thirty-four years on fisheries research vessels and then another six doing stock assessments from a desk, and since I retired I've fallen into philosophy, mostly by accident. What I want help with is a running argument I have with a friend of mine, Nadia, who did political philosophy properly and now teaches part time. We meet Thursday mornings. She's the one who got me onto this "Moloch" / multipolar trap business — situations where everybody acting sensibly makes things worse for everybody and nobody can get out on their own. I keep bringing her cases from my working life and she rules on whether they count. I've written her verdicts down, because I want to spring a new case on her next week and I'd like to know what she's going to say before she says it. Mainly I want to know whether we're disagreeing about anything real or just about words.

Her verdicts so far, with the asides she made, which are probably the useful part:

1. The Grand Banks cod crash. A dozen distant-water fleets plus the inshore boats, no total catch limit anybody could enforce, and each fleet's own logic said keep fishing. Stock collapsed. Trap — she called it as clean an example as there is. Her aside: "no skipper in that fleet could have stopped and stayed in business."

2. A pulp mill upstream of a town. One identifiable defendant, thousands of identifiable people downstream, one court with jurisdiction over both. Not a trap. "That's a tort."

3. A domestic fishery run on secure individual quotas with observers aboard. Not a trap — "it was a trap until somebody designed it out of being one."

4. Doping in the peloton before testing had any teeth. Trap. I argued that nothing irreversible happened, the sport came back, the riders are mostly fine, so it's a weaker case than the cod. She said that was beside the point entirely, and then added, "the awful part is that half of them hated it and couldn't stop."

5. Ballast water and invasives. The decades before the IMO convention: trap. After the convention, once you had to treat your water or get detained at the next port: no longer a trap, even though the temptation to cheat never went away. Her aside: "the payoffs didn't change. Who could catch you did." As I recall the shipping companies ended up backing one global rule because the patchwork of local port requirements was costing them more than treatment would have.

Here's the case I'm building for next week. It's based on a deepwater fishery I actually worked on, details changed. Long-lived species on seamounts, well over a century old, doesn't recruit until it's several decades in, spawns in tight aggregations that are easy to find and easy to wipe out. All of it inside one country's own waters, so there's one government with unambiguous authority. There's a science agency doing proper assessments, observers on the boats, and a minister who can close a seamount by regulation and has closed two. Six years running the assessment has said the catch needs to come down by roughly half. Six years running the minister has allowed five percent. Three companies hold the quota. Each is thinly capitalised and each says that if its share gets cut it goes under and the boats get bought by a foreign operator who won't be careful at all. Their skippers say the fishing gets worse every year and they'd take a lower quota if everybody else took one. One of the three wrote to the ministry asking for a two-year closure, and what they recieved back was that the ministry can't cut or reallocate shares without legislation it cannot get through the house. Nobody in the industry thinks this is fine. They all think it's a disaster. They just don't see a move available to them.

For what it's worth I think that's obviously a trap, and my reason is the hysteresis — I spent my career telling ministers that once a spawning aggregation is gone it isn't coming back on any timescale that matters to anybody — and she keeps telling me that's not what makes something a trap.

So, what I'd like:

- Which plausible-sounding rule her record actually kills, and which single verdict does the killing. Then tell me whether irreversibility is dead for her as a criterion or just demoted to something else — that's the bit I care about most personally.
- Run each surviving rule on my seamount case. Say which ones call it a trap and which don't, and be precise about where they part company.
- Your best guess at her verdict, and what you'd bet on it. If you think her verdicts genuinely leave it open, say so plainly, and then say what she'd be committing herself to by ruling each way.
- One or two cases I could put to her after that which would separate the surviving rules, ideally by changing one thing at a time, with what each rule predicts about them.

Everything you need is in this email, so don't go hunting for references or hand me a reading list — I want the reasoning. And since you're being asked to guess at a person's principles from five data points: tell me how confident you are in your best guess, and single out the one claim in your answer you're least sure of. If you think I've reported any of her verdicts in a way that loads the dice toward one rule, flag that too, because I probably have.
