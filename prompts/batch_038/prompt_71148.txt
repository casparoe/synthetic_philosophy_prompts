I lead a small team that maintains an internal service producing probabilistic outputs — delivery-date estimates, failure-risk scores, retry-probability predictions — and I am currently redesigning how the service represents and communicates uncertainty. I have no formal philosophy training, so I will state the constraint I want to work under as plainly as I can. I have been reading about Keynes's A Treatise on Probability (1921), and two of his claims seem directly relevant to what we are building:

(a) probability for Keynes is a logical relation between a body of evidence and a hypothesis, and that relation is often neither numerically measurable nor even comparable — for some pairs of hypotheses there is simply no fact about which is the more probable, so any procedure that emits one number for every case may be manufacturing an ordering that was never there; and

(b) the weight of an argument is a second dimension distinct from its probability: as relevant evidence accumulates, the probability of a conclusion may go up or down, but its weight always increases, which suggests that a confident output resting on almost no relevant data differs in kind, not merely in degree, from the same output resting on a great deal of it.

Here are the five components of our current uncertainty stack:

1. Every response carries a single scalar confidence value, temperature-scaled on a validation set, and consumers threshold on it — above 0.8 the estimate ships automatically, below that it is routed to a human.
2. A weekly calibration audit scores each model by Brier score against realized outcomes over a rolling ninety-day window and triggers retraining once the score degrades past a fixed tolerance.
3. Conformal prediction intervals, constructed to guarantee ninety percent marginal coverage over the deployment distribution, are published in place of point estimates for our highest-stakes outputs.
4. An ensemble-disagreement signal — the spread across five models trained on disjoint slices of the historical data — is emitted as a separate field alongside the estimate and is deliberately never folded back into the confidence number.
5. A hard routing rule flags any request whose neighborhood in feature space contains fewer than a threshold number of comparable historical records as lying outside the evidence base, and escalates it to a human regardless of what the model's own confidence reports.

The team is split. One engineer holds that item 3 is the most defensible because it is the only component with an actual guarantee attached; another holds that it is the least defensible, because a marginal coverage rate over a distribution says nothing at all about whether the individual case in front of us was supported by much relevant evidence — which is precisely the confusion Keynes was trying to block.

Rank all five from most defensible on Keynes's own terms to least defensible. Reply with exactly five numbered items, in rank order, each one or two sentences stating why that component belongs at that position and which of the two Keynesian claims above it honors or violates — no introduction, no conclusion, and nothing outside the five items.
