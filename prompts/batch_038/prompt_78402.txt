I'm three quarters of the way through my fourth novel and I've been dreading chapter 22 for two years, so I'm outsourcing the first pass to see if the thing is even doable. The book is set in a busted-down diesel town in upstate New York. Ray Kessler dies at 84, and his daughter Rita gives the eulogy; her younger brother is the POV character, so the speech gets quoted in full while he sits there sweating in the second pew. Here's the situation that makes it hard. For roughly seventy years Ray was a genuinely good man — ran the repair shop on Route 9, funny, mean-tempered in a lovable way, would pull you out of a ditch at three in the morning and then refuse your money and be annoyed about it all week. Then vascular dementia took him for the last eleven, and for most of that stretch he was cruel in a very particular direction: he accused Rita, who quit her job as a respiratory therapist to care for him, of stealing from him, of watering down his food, of being a stranger who'd talked her way into the house. He said things about her dead mother that I'm not going to type out here. And for eleven years everybody — the neurologist, her brother, the priest, eventually Rita herself — told her it wasn't Ray, it was the disease, don't take it personally. She has now decided that advice was exactly wrong, and wrong in a way that has cost her something. She needs those things to have been said *by* him, by a person who might have held his tongue, because otherwise there's nothing to forgive and nobody to be forgiven by, and she's stuck holding a decade of injuries with no one on the other end of them.

So the eulogy has to thread that, and the philosophy has to be load-bearing and completely invisible. I keep going back to Strawson's "Freedom and Resentment" — the distinction between the stance where someone is a participant you can resent, thank, be hurt by, forgive, and the stance where you treat them as a case to be managed, medicated, protected, explained. Build the speech on that distinction, but nobody in that room has read a page of philosophy, so no jargon whatsoever, and no naming Strawson. It should make its case in Rita's register: 58 years old, dry, plainspoken, allergic to feeling-words, notices hands and weather and objects instead. She has to argue — to a room that will not think it's being argued with — that "it was only the disease" was a kind of abandonment of her father as much as of her, and that a funeral is the last available chance to take him back as a person.

The bind is that it can't just invert the received wisdom and announce that he was responsible for every cruel sentence. That's monstrous at a lectern and Rita isn't monstrous. And it also can't dissolve into both/and mush, which is what everybody's going to expect from a eulogy and what I least want. She wants him back as an agent without arriving at blame — closer to having been *wronged by someone* than to having been *damaged by something*. Hard bans from the character, which you have to write around rather than through: she will not say he's at peace, will not say we got him back at the end, will not say any variation of "that wasn't Dad," will not thank God for anything, and there's no scripture and no hymn lyric in it. Two more craft things. One: I want a single sentence that reads as a loving tribute to the shop crowd in the front rows and as a plain accusation to the block of Ray's second wife's family who drove up from Scranton, who only ever met the sick Ray and think this whole service is a nostalgia act — and it should genuinely be both, not just ambiguous. Two: pick two or three concrete objects (his knuckles, a coffee can of bolts, the shop radio, whatever you like) and let them do the emotional work, then end somewhere that leaves the problem standing rather than resolving it. Around 700 words. Then give me 100 words max afterward pointing at the three sentences carrying the most weight and saying what each is doing — my editor thinks chapter 22 is "unclear" and I need to be able to defend it. Don't send me a reading list or citations, I've got the book and its the speech I'm stuck on, not the bibliography.
