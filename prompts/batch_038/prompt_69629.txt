I am a doctoral student in philosophy, and this term I am one of the graders for the final examination in a graduate seminar on social epistemology and the epistemology of markets. The seminar covered, among other things, Aumann's agreement theorem and the surrounding debate about common priors, the no-trade results of Milgrom and Stokey, the Grossman–Stiglitz argument concerning informationally efficient markets, and sequential-trade models in which a portion of the order flow comes from participants trading for liquidity reasons rather than on the basis of information.

The question was worth 25 marks and read as follows.

**Question 4.** Both Aumann's agreement theorem and the no-trade results of Milgrom and Stokey are standardly presented as resting on a common prior assumption.

(a) State that assumption as precisely as you can and explain the role it plays in each of the two results. In particular, identify what each result requires *beyond* a common prior. [9]

(b) It is sometimes argued that the sheer volume of purely speculative trading on organised exchanges falsifies the common prior assumption. Assess this argument. Your answer must give at least two distinct explanations of speculative trade that are fully consistent with a common prior, and must explain why the no-trade theorem does not already rule them out. [11]

(c) Is the common prior assumption best understood as a descriptive hypothesis, as a normative constraint on rational belief, or as something else? Does your answer here make any difference to your answer in (b)? [5]

The candidate wrote:

> (a) The common prior assumption says that there is a single probability distribution P over the state space, and that every agent's beliefs are obtained from P by conditioning on what that agent has come to know. Each agent's knowledge is represented by a partition of the state space: at the true state she knows only the element of her partition containing it, and her posterior for an event is P conditional on that element. The content of the assumption is therefore not that agents agree about much — they usually do not — but that whatever disagreement there is is disagreement about information. Agents who share a prior never differ in how they weigh the same evidence, and they never differ about the probability of an event before any evidence has arrived; so a disagreement that survives the disclosure of all the evidence can only reflect a difference in the priors themselves.
>
> In Aumann's theorem the assumption does most of the work. If two agents share a prior and their posteriors for some event are common knowledge, those posteriors must be equal; the argument runs through the finest common coarsening of their two partitions, since on any element of the coarsening each agent's posterior is an average that must coincide with the common value. Mutual knowledge of the posteriors is not sufficient — the theorem genuinely needs common knowledge, and the case of two agents who each know the other's posterior without those posteriors being common knowledge makes this vivid.
>
> The no-trade theorem of Milgrom and Stokey is the same argument carried over to exchange. A proposal to trade is itself a signal. If a rational agent is offered a bet, she knows that the offerer would not have made it unless it were favourable to him given what he knows; so to accept is to accept something the better-informed party expects to win. Anticipating refusal, no one makes the offer, and speculative trade never gets started. The assumptions are correspondingly light: a common prior, common knowledge of rationality, and the publicity of the proposed trade. On an organised exchange all three are satisfied, since every order is visible to every participant.
>
> (b) That gives a direct empirical test, and the market fails it. Turnover on the major equity exchanges runs to billions of shares a day, and the bulk of it cannot be hedging or portfolio rebalancing: the counterparties on either side of most trades are both speculating on direction. If the no-trade theorem were sound and the common prior held, this trade could not occur. It occurs; therefore the common prior does not hold. Speculative volume is the observable trace of disagreement about probabilities rather than of disagreement about information.
>
> Grossman and Stiglitz reach the same conclusion from the other direction. Their impossibility result is in effect a no-trade theorem about the acquisition of information: if prices fully revealed what the informed knew, nobody would spend resources becoming informed, since no profit would remain; and if nobody becomes informed, prices cannot reveal anything. The market thus unravels in exactly the way it does in Milgrom and Stokey — the public signal, the price there and the offer here, discloses what the discloser knows, and the disclosure destroys the incentive that produced it. Read this way the no-trade theorem is not a falsified prediction but a correct diagnosis, and what has to be given up is the common prior.
>
> (c) The assumption cannot be descriptive. No agent ever actually entertains a distribution over a state space rich enough to encode everything she might come to know, so there is nothing for the hypothesis to be about. It is better read as a normative constraint: an ideal of coherence that agents would satisfy were they to reflect together, and a benchmark against which actual disagreement registers as a failure. One might try to rescue it by enlarging the state space to include each agent's dispositions to form beliefs, so that apparently different priors become a single prior conditioned on different information about belief formation; but this only relocates the difficulty, since the enlarged prior is equally something no one holds and is no more testable. On the normative reading, then, the speculative volume described in (b) does not show that agents are irrational. It shows that the idealisation does not apply to them, which is another way of saying that their disagreement is about probabilities and not about evidence.
