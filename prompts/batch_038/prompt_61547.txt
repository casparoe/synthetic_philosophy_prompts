I volunteer as chair of the appeals committee at a community tool library — we lend out power tools, members pay dues, we've been going since 1991. The bylaws were written by three of the founders and then patched by later boards, so its a mess, and any time a member disputes a ruling it lands on me. Two years of decisions have piled up and I'm trying to work out whether my own reasoning adds up to anything, because looking at it all at once it doesn't feel consistent.

Here are the calls I actually made.

1. Section 3 says no member may check out more than four tools at a time. A guy took four power tools plus a folding workbench from the back room that nobody had ever counted as a tool. I ruled the bench doesn't count. The point of Section 3 is to stop people hogging inventory, and a bench hogs inventory, but the word is "tools" and I wasn't going to stretch it.

2. Section 9 says tools come back within seven days or the member loses borrowing privileges for a month. A woman hit day eight because she'd been in the hospital since day five. I voted to waive the suspension — the deadline is there to keep tools circulating, and she would have brought it back if she could.

3. Section 14 says gasoline-powered equipment may not be stored in the building overnight. Somebody donated a generator, tank drained and dry, and it sat in the back shed. I let it stay. What they were worried about was fire, and there was no fuel in it.

4. Section 6 says no member may use a library tool on a project for a for-profit business. A member who does weekend furniture repair took our belt sander to a customer's door. I upheld the violation and I hated it, because this library was started by carpenters who wanted people doing real work.

5. There's a clause saying the committee shall interpret the bylaws according to the intent of the founding members. We had to decide whether a laser cutter counts as shop equipment and the founders obviously never thought about lasers. I asked myself what they'd have said and wrote the answer down as though it were a fact.

6. The 2016 amendment reads that no member under 18 may operate a table saw. The minutes show the motion was made at 16 and got typed up as 18. I went with 18, the number actually enacted, even though everyone in the room knew what had happened.

Notice that 1 and 6 went with the words against what the rule was for, and 2 and 3 went the other way, and I couldnt tell you what the difference is. Best guess at my own principle: follow the words when they're plain, unless the result is one that nobody who voted for the rule could possibly have wanted, in which case ask what the rule was for. But "could possibly have wanted" is doing all the work and I don't know whether that's a fact about the people who voted or a fact about the rule itself.

One of the other committee members is a retired contracts lawyer who keeps invoking the mischief rule at me and I only half follow him. I know judges argue about this stuff for a living and I'm not after a tour of that debate — no reading list, please, I can't chase sources anyway. What I want:

- Is there one principle, stated tightly, that gets all six verdicts? If it needs more than one clause, tell me which case each clause is doing work on, because I suspect several of them are just patches.
- If nothing gets all six, which verdict do I give up, and what does giving it up cost me — I've already handed down rulings that members have arranged their lives around, so "you were wrong in March" isn't free.
- Is 3 salvageable at all? A generator with an empty tank still seems like gasoline-powered equipment to me, and if I can't explain 3 without explaining away 1, I want to know that.
- Does my principle turn out to be something recognizable, or is it a muddle with a nice ring to it?
- Then run it on the one coming up next month, which I have deliberately not decided yet. Section 11 says library tools may not be removed from the county. A member has converted a school bus into a mobile workshop, parks it at her farm in the county, and wants to take the tile saw and a welder across the state line for a weekend to build a greenhouse and bring everything back. Her argument is that nothing leaves the county permanently so nothing is "removed." I need to know what my own principle makes me say before I hear her out, not after.
- And flag every place the principle depends on evidence I can't actually get — what somebody was thinking in 1991, what a room of volunteers would have wanted about a case nobody raised — because I'd rather know which of my rulings are resting on facts I made up.
