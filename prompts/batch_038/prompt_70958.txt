I am at work on a novel whose central section is a constitutional convention conducted behind an information screen: the delegates are to settle the basic arrangements of a small polity after a collapse, and they settle them without knowing which household, which body, or which trade they will occupy once the screen is lifted. The device is borrowed, and I am not trying to write a treatise. I am trying to write delegates who quarrel for reasons that survive rereading. To that end I have spent some weeks inventing cases and recording my own verdicts on them, and the verdicts have turned out not to add up. I should like to know what principle, if any, they do add up to.

The cases, and what I think about each.

One. A valley with a single mill, owned by one family. The millers are vastly better off than anyone else. But the landless laborers are better off than they would be under any arrangement the convention can actually put in place: better than under a workers' cooperative that could employ only half of them, better than under a parceling of the mill among households. I judge this just, and I judge that no further account of the family's fortune is owed to anyone. My discomfort at the sheer size of the gap strikes me as a sentiment I have no business legislating.

Two. The same valley, the same numbers for the laborers, except that the family's position now rests on a standing prohibition against laborers pooling capital to build a second mill. The laborers remain, as it happens, better off than under anything the convention could implement, since no one has credit. I judge this unjust. I notice that in the first case I looked only at where people ended up, and here I am looking at the rule that put them there, and I cannot say why the second way of looking seems more honest than the first.

Three. A polity that taxes the earnings of a gifted surgeon at a punishing rate to fund clinics for the worst-off, and thereby funds those clinics. I judge this just. The same polity, drafting that surgeon for two years to staff the same clinics, producing exactly the same clinics and exactly the same distribution of goods. I judge this unjust, and the two outcomes are indistinguishable by any measure I have been using. Whatever the difference is, it seems to be about the person rather than about her share.

Four. A polity in which the worst-off group can be raised to the highest attainable level only by a levy that hollows out the middle — clerks, smallholders, the valley's midwives — so that the result is a comfortable floor, a ruined middle, and a small top. The worst off are at their maximum. I judge it unjust all the same.

Five. A polity that has drawn down its aquifer and exhausted its topsoil to raise the bundle of goods going to the least advantaged, who are now better off than their parents were and will have children worse off than themselves. I judge this unjust, and I sense that it is a different species of injustice from the first four, not a distribution among contemporaries gone wrong.

Six. A woman who uses a wheelchair. Under an arrangement that maximizes the goods going to the least advantaged, her bundle is at its maximum and she still cannot get into the assembly hall. I judge the arrangement unjust, and I do not think the remedy is to hand her more of the same goods, though I could not say what it is to hand her instead.

Seven. A delegate from a small religious minority. I have been writing the screen as stripping the delegates of everything particular about themselves, but I find that I do not want it to strip her of the knowledge that her worship requires a building oriented a certain way and that a bare majority would outvote her on it. I want a thinner screen than the one I have drafted, and I am not certain that this is not merely my wanting the novel to have an argument in it.

My own tentative principle, which I suspect is two unrelated halves stapled together: an arrangement is just when those worst off under it are as well off as the convention can make them, and when no one under it can truthfully say that the arrangement was contrived to place them where they are.

What I want back. State the principle, or the small number of genuinely rival candidate principles, precisely enough that a delegate could argue from it against another delegate. For each clause, name the case that earns it. Tell me whether the rivals are actually distinct or one principle fitted with different epicycles. Then give me the principle's verdicts on three cases I have not listed: whether the screen should strip a delegate of knowledge of her own talents; what follows about an arrangement that maximizes the least advantaged group's bundle but delivers it entirely in a form that group does not want; and whether a delegate who knows she belongs to the worst-off group and votes against the maximizing arrangement out of resentment has been treated unjustly. Finally, tell me plainly which of my seven verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and — if I am forced to choose between a principle that buys all seven at the cost of complexity and a spare one that costs me a verdict — which verdict I should surrender first, and why that one.
