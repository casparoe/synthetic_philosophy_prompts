I sit on a small working group drafting an annotation policy for a comparative functional-genomics resource: the rules by which a curator may attach a "function" label to a gene product, an organ, or a trait, and the rules by which she may not. The document is meant to be philosophically explicit rather than leaving the operative concept implicit, and I have been asked to supply that account. My difficulty is that my own verdicts seem to come from neither of the two standard families — selected-effect (etiological) accounts of the kind associated with Millikan, and causal-role (dispositional) accounts of the kind associated with Cummins — and that several of them are flatly incompatible with one another. Below are the verdicts I would defend if pushed.

1. A human heart with severe valve disease, which can no longer pump blood adequately, still has the function of pumping blood. Making that denial is precisely what turns "heart failure" into a mere description of what the organ now does.

2. Suppose a cave-dwelling fish's vestigial eye still expresses photopigment, and that light striking it triggers a measurable response integrated into the animal's physiology. I would still record the eye as having no function.

3. Suppose a mutation arose within the last few generations and confers resistance to a newly encountered pathogen, with a large effect on the bearer's survival. I would record it as functional now, though nothing selected for it and it contributed nothing to any ancestor's fitness.

4. The redness of blood has no function. It has consequences — wounds are legible at a glance, and clinicians exploit haemoglobin's absorption spectrum — but having consequences is not enough.

5. Feathers have the function of enabling flight notwithstanding that they were originally selected for thermoregulation. And the human capacity to read has functional components — letter-shape discrimination, phonological mapping — notwithstanding that reading was never selected for at any point. These feel like a single verdict to me, though I can see that a selected-effect account would treat the two halves very differently.

6. A synthetic circuit inserted into a production strain to secrete a therapeutic protein has the function of secreting it, provided it does so and the strain is maintained for that purpose. But an engineered insert intended to do something that in fact does nothing has no function, whatever its designers meant. Intention therefore looks neither necessary (see 3) nor sufficient.

7. A tumour's induction of angiogenesis was selected for, by ordinary somatic selection within the host, and is well adapted to its role. I nonetheless refuse to record it as a function of the tumour, and I want a principled reason for the refusal rather than a report of my distaste.

8. Components of a trained neural network fall on the same side of my line as the corresponding biological cases: an attention head that ablation shows to be dispensable has no function, though it is causally active, whereas a head whose removal degrades the task metric does. Yet I cannot see how to derive this from selection history, since the only thing gradient descent ever selected was the loss of the whole system.

The pair I cannot reconcile is 3 and 2. A trait with a current causal contribution and no relevant history gets a function; a trait with both a history and a current causal contribution gets none. Any account that saves both looks disjunctive to the point of being ad hoc; any account simple enough to look principled appears to cost me one of them. The verdict I have not yet formed concerns the case the policy will actually be judged on, because curators meet it weekly: a de novo orphan gene, transcribed from previously non-coding sequence, present in a single lineage, whose knockout yields a small but reproducible fitness effect under laboratory conditions.

What I would like from you:

(a) One or two candidate principles, stated as precisely as you can manage — ideally as conditions applicable to a description of a trait, not as slogans — together with, for each clause, the verdict above that motivates it.

(b) For each candidate, an explicit statement of which of 1–8 it cannot accommodate, and whether the cost is a verdict I should be willing to abandon or a genuine defect in the principle. Where a candidate overturns one of my verdicts, argue that the verdict was wrong; do not merely note the mismatch.

(c) A verdict on the orphan-gene case under each candidate, and an account of what makes that case hard — which feature of it my other verdicts leave unsettled.

(d) Your view on whether the ambition behind (a) is coherent. It is arguable that function talk is systematically level-relative and interest-relative, so that no single set of conditions will deliver all of 1–8, and that the right policy separates two registers rather than unifying them. If that is your view, identify the registers, assign each of my verdicts to one, explain how a curator chooses between them when the choice changes the annotation — 7 and 8 look like exactly such cases — and show how the normativity that verdict 1 depends on survives in either register.

(e) Because this is for a policy document: what your principle or pair of registers implies for cases I have not listed, in particular for traits of extinct organisms known only from morphology, and for traits whose contribution runs to a group or a host rather than to the bearer. Two worked examples of each would be more useful to me than general discussion.
