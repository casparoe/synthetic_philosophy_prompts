I've spent more years than I care to admit arguing about supertasks on forums, and Thomson's lamp is the case where the thread reliably degenerates into one side saying it's physically impossible and the other saying that begs the question. Write a short story, in exactly five numbered scenes, about a firm that believes it has built the lamp: a switch thrown at ever-halving intervals converging on noon, and a client who has paid handsomely to read the lamp's state at noon as a yes/no answer that no finite computation could have produced. Build the plot out of the two things I find most interesting and least often dramatized. First, the engineering of interval halving: contact bounce and the finite reset time of any real relay, the arm's tip accelerating past any material limit, and the moment when the time remaining is shorter than light takes to cross the switch gap, so that "the lamp's state at noon" stops being a single observer-independent fact. Second, the underdetermination point in Benacerraf's line: nothing in the description of the switching sequence fixes the state at the limit, because the limit instant is not itself one of the switching instants — a gap the firm's founder treats as a feature rather than a scandal, since a bit that no description determines is a bit you can sell. Keep the founder and the outsider who comes to shut the machine down equally competent and equally immune to rhetoric; neither should win by being smarter than the author. No exposition scenes and no lecture dialogue: the argument should happen through what the machine does and what the client is told. After the fifth scene, add a note, clearly marked as outside the fiction, that separates what the story treats as settled from what is genuinely contested among people who work on this, and flags anywhere you have leaned on a reading of the paradox that a substantial fraction of philosophers would reject.
