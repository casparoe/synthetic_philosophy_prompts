I am presenting a paper at a two-day workshop on the norms of inquiry, and what I need is a preparation sheet for the question period rather than a defense of my view.

The paper's target is the dogmatism paradox. As I formulate it in the talk: if I know that p, then I know that any future evidence against p would be misleading; if I know that a given piece of future evidence would be misleading, then I am permitted to disregard that evidence when it arrives; therefore, if I know that p, I am permitted to disregard any future evidence against p. My claim is that the second step fails, but only in a restricted and structured way, and that the restrictions are not the familiar ones: not a denial of closure, not a denial of the KK principle, not contextualism about knowledge, and not an appeal to the opacity of "misleading."

What I defend instead I call asymmetric preemption. Evidence bearing against p can reach me in two roles: as a first-order indication that p is false, or as a higher-order indication that the process by which I came to know p malfunctioned on this occasion. Knowledge entitles me to dismiss evidence preemptively in the second role but never in the first. Dismissal in the second role is licensed only under a non-circularity constraint: I must be able to establish the unreliability of the channel producing that higher-order evidence without relying on p, and without relying on any belief whose warrant runs through that same channel. Where the constraint is unmet, knowledge licenses no dismissal at all; it only shifts the burden of inquiry onto me.

The room: roughly twenty-five people, mostly formal epistemologists working in Bayesian confirmation theory, two of whom work primarily on learning-theoretic and accuracy-dominance arguments; a small knowledge-first contingent; and three or four who think the entire project of describing what evidence permits is misframed and want zetetic norms instead. The session has a commentator who works on epistemic circularity and easy-knowledge problems and has written that he regards this paradox as a reductio of knowledge-first epistemology in general. Nobody in the audience works on the history of the puzzle.

Give me the eight or nine objections I should expect, ordered by how much damage each would do if I fumbled it, and phrased the way these people would phrase them in a room like this — including the technical form the two learning theorists will put theirs in. For each objection: a reply I can deliver in under two minutes without a blackboard, a candid note on where that reply is softest, and an assessment of whether a modest revision of my view would absorb the objection or whether absorbing it costs me the asymmetry itself. Then tell me what I should concede in my opening five minutes to take the wind out of the most predictable attacks, and which single reply has to land for the paper to be taken seriously.

Finally, name the question I am hoping nobody asks — the one for which I presently have no answer — and sketch what a partial answer would have to look like.

I am not asking for references, a bibliography, or a survey of the literature on the paradox; I know it, and I want the shape of the room instead. Close by telling me what would change your mind about the view: which of these objections you judge to be fatal rather than merely awkward, and where your own reconstruction of this audience is most likely to be wrong — for instance if you have assumed they will reach for a formal model faster than they actually will.
