I've spent the last few months slowly getting talked into a view I used to roll my eyes at, and now I want someone to talk me back out of it -- or failing that, to tell me exactly how much it costs me to hold. The view is the big-worlds / acausal cooperation line: the universe (or multiverse, if you're into that) is large enough that there are lots of other agents out there running decision procedures relevantly similar to mine, and becuase my choice is evidence about what those agents choose, I can have genuine reasons to cooperate in one-shot situations with no causal interaction at all -- and maybe even to shape my behavior around trade with agents I'll never meet, identify, or be able to affect. It's basically the Hofstadter-ish superrationality intuition plus the evidential-decision-theory reading of Newcomb-type cases, pushed up to cosmological scale. I'll call the whole package ECDT. For what it's worth I find the one-shot prisoner's dilemma version fairly compelling and the "compromise between value systems spread across the multiverse" version much less so, but treat them as one package for now -- my hunch is they stand or fall together, and if you think they don't, that's itself part of the answer I want.

So: play it straight as an objection exercise, and tell me what would have to be true for this to be wrong. Sort the candidate defeaters by how much damage they'd do -- which are fatal to the whole thing, which just force a retreat to a much weaker version that nobody would have found interesting, and which are merely awkward and can be absorbed by a qualification. For each one, say what the world would have to look like and whether that's live (something we have positive reason to think might be the case) or merely logically possible. Be explicit about which defeaters a committed defender would themselves concede count against the view, as opposed to what a skeptic would throw at it; I care a lot about that distinction, since these arguments usually end with the two sides disagreeing about what's even up for grabs. Two worries in particular I want handled head-on rather than gestured at. First, the correlation worry: if the reason those distant agents act like me is some common cause -- similar selection pressures, similar environments, similar training -- does that screen off my action as evidence about theirs, and is this objection decisive or is it something the ECDT people think they've already answered? Second, the self-undermining worry: the view tells me to adopt a policy partly because that policy correlates me with agents who reward it, which sounds like it hands leverage to anyone able to manufacture or anticipate the correlation. Is that a real defeater or a cheap trick? Also worth knowing: what it would take to make the similarity requirement do any work at all, since if the relevant class of "agents like me" can be widened or narrowed freely, I suspect the whole evidential support slides around with it.

And then the honest bit: does the view risk anything? Tell me whether there's any observation, argument, or discovery that could ever count against it, or whether every failure gets explained away by "there just weren't enough relevantly similar agents on that branch," and if it is insulated like that, whether that's actually a problem here -- it's meant to be action-guiding practical philosophy rather than an empirical theory, so maybe untestability isn't the sin it would be elsewhere, but I'd like that argued rather than assumed. Lead with your own bottom line in two or three sentences, saying where you think the real failure point is and roughly how confident you are, and only then go into the map. Plain prose please -- no headings, no bullet points, no bold, no tables, just continuous paragraphs like an email to a friend who's already thought about this some. And no bibliography or list of papers to go read: I'm on a train with no laptop and spotty signal, so I'd much rather you give me the reasoning than guess at titles. The thing most likely to move me, incidentally, is an argument that doesn't require me to first settle evidential versus causal decision theory, since I've never been able to make any progress on that question and I'd rather not have the whole thing hostage to it.
