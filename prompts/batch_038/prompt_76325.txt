I am preparing a seminar handout that treats Rousseau's arguments to d'Alembert about whether Geneva ought to establish a theater as a live hypothesis rather than a historical curiosity. Before writing anything about him, though, I want to know what principle my own judgments commit me to, because I suspect they are not straightforwardly his. Below are my verdicts on twelve cases, stated as plainly as I can manage. I have deliberately not explained them, since the explanation is what I am asking you to reconstruct.

1. A professional repertory company resident in a small city, performing classics to a season-ticket audience: on balance good for that city.
2. A school play in which the children act and their own families watch: better than (1), and better than the same play performed by professionals for the same families.
3. A prestige drama streamed alone, nightly, for hours: not good, though I cannot identify anyone it has harmed.
4. A filmed version of a celebrated stage production, seen by thousands who will never see the stage version: worse than (1). I hold this despite the fact that more people see it and it costs each of them less.
5. An immersive promenade show in which the audience is split up, addressed by name, and made to choose: worse than (1). This surprises me, since it is by far the least passive item on the list.
6. A documentary after which a large number of viewers organized, petitioned, and litigated: good, and better than (2) — though I cannot say why, since structurally the viewer sat in the dark and watched other people.
7. A reality competition in which ordinary contestants are humiliated for the audience's amusement: bad, and bad in a way that (3) is not.
8. A comedy that makes a righteous, unbending whistleblower the laughingstock and his accommodating colleague the sensible man of the world: bad. This is the strongest of my verdicts, and the one I would defend last.
9. A municipal festival with no performers, no stage, and no program, at which the townspeople dance and eat together in a square: the best thing on this list.
10. A stadium match attended in person by sixty thousand people, all of them singing: nearly as good as (9), which I did not expect when I started ranking.
11. A subsidized theater newly planted in a town that has never had one: bad. The same theater, thirty years on, with the same repertoire and the same building: fine. I hold this even though the steady states are identical.
12. A naturalization ceremony at which new citizens read a scripted text aloud in unison, having been assigned their places by officials: good, nearly as good as (9), even though nothing about it is spontaneous.

On that basis:

(a) State a single principle that yields these twelve verdicts. It should be decomposed into a small number of named clauses, each tied to the case or cases that motivate it, and each phrased as a test I could apply to an unfamiliar case in under a minute. Build the clauses out of whatever parameters you think are actually load-bearing — I would guess the list includes who performs, whether the performer resembles the audience, whether the audience is co-present with one another, whether the audience is addressed or made to act, whether attendance is repeated and local, what is asked of the viewer afterward, and how the depiction distributes sympathy between the virtuous and the accommodating — but revise that list if you think it is wrong. Tell me which clause carries most of the weight and which one I could delete with the least damage.

(b) Rousseau appears to say two things that pull against each other: that drama draws its material from the morals already in force and so can confirm them but not reform them, and that planting a theater in Geneva would nonetheless alter Genevan habits, by bringing new expenditure, new idleness, and a new public arena in which the young display themselves to one another. Separately, he suggests that the compassion a spectator feels in the dark is a cheap substitute for action — a sentiment that costs the feeler nothing and asks nothing of him. Test my reconstructed principle against both of these. Does it dissolve the first tension by separating what a performance depicts from what the institution of performance does to the habits around it, or does it inherit the tension? And can it explain why (6) escapes the charge of sterile compassion while (3) does not, without an ad hoc clause about whether the subject matter happens to be political?

(c) Tell me which of my verdicts the principle cannot accommodate, and for each whether you would amend the principle or overturn the verdict. I expect (4), (5), (6), and (11) to be the troublemakers, and I want (6) defended if it can be defended at all. Then say whether a leaner principle — three clauses rather than however many you end up with — would preserve, say, nine of the twelve verdicts, identify the three it sacrifices, and tell me whether that trade is worth taking. If two of my verdicts are simply incoherent with one another rather than merely hard to unify, say so instead of contorting the clauses to cover them.

(d) Apply the principle to six cases I have not listed: a weekly community choir that performs badly and often; a livestreamed protest in another country, watched by millions who are in no position to join it; a televised leaders' debate watched in bars and living rooms on election night; a museum installation that records visitors and shows them to themselves and to the room; a stand-up special filmed before a live house audience and then released on a streaming service; and a municipal ceremony marking a defeat, attended by the descendants of both sides.

(e) Flag any pair of my cases the principle cannot separate — that is, any pair on which it delivers my ranking but for reasons I would not accept, or on which it ranks things alike that I rank differently. I would rather know about these than have them smoothed over.

Two constraints on the answer: I want the instrument, not the literature, so no references, no reading list, and no survey of what commentators have said; and write it so that a student who has never read Rousseau could apply the principle to a new case correctly. Where Rousseau's own example matters — for instance, the objection underlying (8), which he pressed against a famous seventeenth-century French comedy — say whether my principle reproduces his objection or shows that it was over-broad, and on what grounds.
