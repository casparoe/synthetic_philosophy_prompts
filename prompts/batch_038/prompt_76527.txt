Been going back through Anscombe on Truman again — the honorary degree fight at Oxford, the pamphlet she put out afterwards, Modern Moral Philosophy a couple years later — and it hit me that she died in January 2001. So basically the entire post-9/11 landscape happened without her: drone wars, signature strikes, the torture debate coming back from the dead, algorithmic target generation. I want the counterfactual done properly, not the cheap version where you say she'd be appalled and stop there.

Here's the setup I want you to run her on. It's stylized, but roughly how these pipelines are said to work:

A military contracts a targeting system. It ingests signals and imagery and outputs a ranked list of candidate targets, each tagged with an estimate of how many non-combatants are probably present at the recommended strike time. Command sets a tolerance per target class in advance — say, up to fifteen unidentified civilians for a mid-level operative, up to three for a low-value courier. The system also generates signature targets: people identified only by a pattern (military-age males, in a particular valley, moving certain shapes between certain compounds at night), with no name and no individual evidence whatsoever. A human officer reviews each recommendation and clicks approve; the machine supplies the description of what will happen. The vendor also sells a harm-mitigation module that re-times or re-routes a strike when the predicted civilian count exceeds tolerance, and the military cites its existence as proof of good faith. Aggregate civilian deaths in the theater drop measurably after deployment.

Now the questions:

1. Which of these killings does Anscombe count as intentional killing of the innocent, and which as merely foreseen? I want the actual machinery — action under a description, intention in acting, the way she thinks double effect gets wielded as a fig leaf. Does the pre-set tolerance number convert what would otherwise have been a side effect into something aimed at? Is there any real difference between approving one strike while knowing fifteen will die, and approving strikes under a standing rule that fifteen is the accepted price?

2. The signature case. Nobody knows who is being killed. Does ignorance of the victim's identity change the description under which the act is intended? My hunch is that she has the resources to say no, and that the anonymity makes it worse rather than cleaner — but I want to know which specific part of her philosophy does that work, because it isn't obvious to me.

3. Moral diffusion. No single human being intends any particular civilian's death; the deaths are the output of a statistical procedure that a hundred people each contributed a small piece to. Does she treat that as an excuse, or as the modern form of somthing she was already attacking in 1958 — a morality with nobody in it? And what about the engineers and the analysts: do they intend what the officer carries out, and can you be guilty of a killing you only made more probable?

4. The mitigation module and the aggregate drop. Her case against Truman had two distinct moves in it: a conceptual one about what you may not do whatever the consequences, and an empirical one about whether the bombing actually shortened the war. Separate those two for me and say which she'd press here. Would a measured fall in civilian deaths count for anything in her view at all, and is there a version of the argument where it does?

5. The bit I find genuinely hard. A modern consequentialist says: absolutism followed at scale kills more innocents — the side that accepts no civilian risk loses, the war drags on for years, and the civilians die anyway under the occupation that follows. Does Anscombe have an answer that isnt just a refusal to run the calculation? Is saying that the calculating stance is itself corrupting a real reply, or a dodge dressed up as profundity? Engage with the strongest form of the objection rather than defending her.

6. Where would she actually have moved between 2001 and now? Give me a few candidates with reasons, and separate them from the ones that look like they'd move her but wouldn't. Include deterrence: as I read her, the conditional-intention point is what sinks nuclear deterrence — you can't intend to do what's forbidden even on a condition — and the threat has now held for eighty-odd years without going off. Does that track record touch her argument at all, or does it only feel like it should?

Two ground rules. Be explicit about where you're extrapolating from her general views versus where she said something that pretty much settles the question; I want to know which parts of your answer are load-bearing and which are you papering over a gap. And don't put anything in quotation marks as though she wrote it — I can't check quotes offline, and a plausible-sounding fake is worse than an honest paraphrase.
