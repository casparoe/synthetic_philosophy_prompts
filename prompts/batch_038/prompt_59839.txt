I'm a third-year working on formal epistemology, and I've assembled an argument for my reading group that seems to demolish considerably more than I meant it to. I'd like a diagnosis rather than a summary of the literature.

(1) A credal state is irrational just in case there is some finite package of bets, each of which the agent would accept at the prices she herself quotes, that guarantees her a net loss.
(2) By the Dutch book theorem, any credal state that is not a probability function over a set of propositions closed under the Boolean connectives admits such a package.
(3) No bounded agent assigns credence 1 to every theorem of a system as strong as arithmetic and 0 to every refutable sentence in it. I cannot presently say which side of a four-hundred-clause formula is the tautology, and my standing attitudes toward unexamined logical consequences of what I believe are neither 0 nor 1.
(4) So by (1) and (2), every bounded agent's credal state is irrational.
(5) But it is no defect of rationality to fail to see that a hard claim is a theorem; we do not convict a mathematician of irrationality for not having derived everything that follows from what she has proved.
(6) Therefore (1) is false.
(7) Yet (1) is the only bridge we have between coherence and irrationality — it is what makes the probability axioms and the updating rules that descend from them look normative rather than merely tidy. Rejecting (1) leaves formal epistemology without its central argument.

(4) and (5) are the claims I most want to keep; (7) is the one I most hate to lose. Which step should go, why did it look innocent, and what does the survivor pay? In particular: does indexing the criterion to traders or bookmakers who are themselves computationally bounded — the move the logical induction program makes — actually rescue (1), or does it swap a normative standard for a choice of threat model? And what does the idealizing reply, that rationality is a limiting notion and (5) is an excuse, have to give up?
