I work as a water-quality analyst for a mid-sized regional utility. While going through eighteen months of routine plant records, I came across a pattern nobody had flagged: on the days we raised the coagulant dose above our usual range, the filter runs before backwashing got shorter rather than longer, which is backwards from what the treatment literature and our own operating manual both lead you to expect. I developed an explanation for it — the dose interacts with raw-water alkalinity in a way that produces light, compressible floc, so the benefit reverses once you pass a threshold that depends on the source water. That explanation also accounts for details I hadn't set out to explain, including why the pattern appears only in the winter months and only at the two plants drawing from the reservoir.

I should be candid that I adjusted the explanation several times while working through those records, and after each adjustment it accounted for more of what was already in them. I then designed a bench study to test it, and over four months the reversal reproduced three times, at the predicted dose ranges, in the predicted direction, with the shape of the curve matching what the explanation implies.

A reviewer at the state association has raised an objection I don't know how to answer. She says the original eighteen months of records cannot count toward my confidence in the explanation, because I already knew what they showed before I proposed it, and something you already knew cannot make you more confident of anything. On her account, only the bench study carries any weight. I want to say she is wrong, but I notice two things pulling in opposite directions: I did fiddle with the explanation until the records fell out of it, and yet the records are real, they are extensive, and my explanation is the only one I have that makes sense of them.

Her principle also seems to have consequences she would not accept. My colleague at the neighboring utility reached the same explanation on his own before anyone showed him our records; he then ran the same bench study and got the same three results. If she is right, his confidence should be higher than mine, even though we now know exactly the same things. And if being already known really disqualifies evidence, it would seem that no body of data we have accumulated can ever raise our confidence in the explanations we build out of it, which would make most of what I do in a day irrational.

Help me think this through carefully. Is the reviewer right that the old records cannot support the explanation, and if she is, what exactly does the bench study add that they lack? Is there a real distinction between a result that came out as predicted beforehand and one that merely fits neatly into an explanation afterward, and if so, why would that difference change what the evidence is worth? Does the fact that I revised the explanation to suit those records disqualify them in a way that their merely being old does not? What should my confidence in the explanation actually rest on now, and what would you have me say back to her that concedes whatever is legitimate in her objection without accepting the part of it that proves too much?
