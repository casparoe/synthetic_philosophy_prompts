I'm not a historian by training — I write narrative nonfiction, mostly about infrastructure and standards — and last month a magazine ran a 3,000-word piece I did on the 1884 conference in Washington where the delegates picked Greenwich as the prime meridian. It's told straight in order, no flashbacks, and I closed it by noting that the timestamp on the readers phone is still, at several removes, an artifact of that vote, and that France abstained and then held out for decades rather than concede. A friend of mine who does history of science for a living read it and said, kindly, that it's Whig history — the whole thing is arranged so that 1884 exists in order to produce my last paragraph. I've been chewing on that for three weeks now, partly because I have a book proposal due and I'm terrifed the same note is going to come back from an editor who won't phrase it as kindly.

The problem is I keep hearing Whig used as a slur and I can't tell whether it's one complaint or six wearing a trenchcoat. So can you lay out for me the distinct claims someone might actually be making when they say a piece of writing is Whig history — a menu, sorted from the ones that are trivially true of literally all narrative history to the ones a specific text could genuinely fail? For each, tell me what it would take for a piece of writing to violate it, whether it's a platitude, whether it's substantive and defensible, and whether it's just false. Then, given that my friend's specific complaint was about my ending rather than about any fact in it, which reading was he most plausibly reaching for? I'd also like the menu to cover the structural stuff, since I suspect some of the charge is about shape: does telling things in chronological order count, does having a single protagonist institution, does the anniversary-issue framing I was commissioned under (the whole issue was built around round numbers)?

My own tentative diagnosis, which I want you to attack rather than nurse: I've been telling myself that Whiggish just means selecting your material by downstream consequences — and if that's the test, every history ever written fails it, because you can't decide what to mention without some sense of what turned out to matter. On that gloss the insult is empty and my friend was just being a snob. Is the gloss right, wrong, or repairable into something non-vacuous? And here's the squeeze I actually want out of: my old advisor used to write antiquarian in the margins of drafts that described things carefully and refused to say why any of it mattered. Is there a target that dodges both notes, or are they incompatible demands that anyone writing about the past just has to trade off against each other? If it's a tradeoff, tell me roughly what buys what.

Two things on the epistemic side. Please mark clearly which parts of your menu are standard usage — Butterfield's 1931 pamphlet and the arguments people have had with it since — and which parts are your own reconstruction, and flag anywhere you're less than confident; I'd rather have a short honest menu than a long confident one. And if you think my whole framing is confused — say, if Whig no longer has stable enough content to precisify and the honest answer is that I should ask my friend what he meant instead of guessing at seven readings — say that plainly rather than complying to look thorough. One thing I'd ask you not to assume: that my last paragraph was factually wrong. It wasn't. Whatever the sin is, it isn't accuracy, and I'd like the menu to be able to say what it is if that's possible.
