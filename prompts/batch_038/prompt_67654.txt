I serve as rapporteur to the Franchise Commission of the Commonwealth of Caldonia, a standing body that rules on petitions to be added to the Commonwealth's electoral roll. Our enabling statute gives us a single criterion — "every person who has a sufficient stake in the decisions of the Commonwealth" — and otherwise instructs us only that our rulings must be consistent with one another and reasoned in public. We are now facing the familiar boundary problem in concrete form: no democratic procedure can tell us who the demos is, so we have to settle it case by case, and every case commits us. I have to draft the next opinion and I want to know which decision is actually available to us given our own record.

The eight petitions decided so far, with the language each opinion leaned on:

1. Lawfully resident non-citizens of fifteen years' standing, working, taxpaying, with children in Commonwealth schools: enfranchised in all elections. The opinion spoke of lives built inside our institutions.
2. A commuter who resides in a neighboring state and crosses daily to work at a Commonwealth plant, paying income tax at source and working under Commonwealth labor and safety law, but residing nowhere in the Commonwealth: granted the franchise in the elections of the district where the plant stands, denied it in national elections. The opinion said the petitioner's working day is lived under our law and their livelihood fixed by our decisions, while the remainder of their life lies elsewhere.
3. A citizen who emigrated thirty years ago, took another nationality, holds no Commonwealth property, pays no Commonwealth tax, and has not voted since leaving: franchise terminated under a five-year absence rule, termination upheld. The opinion said he had ceased to share a life with us in anything but a formal sense. It also noted, without drawing consequences from it, that he remains liable to prosecution under Commonwealth statutes that reach conduct abroad and would be subject to our law in full on any return.
4. A foreign graduate student resident four years, paying tuition but no income tax, intending to leave on completing the degree: denied. The opinion said that four years, however fully lived, does not yet make a person's life a Commonwealth life.
5. Undocumented residents of nine years' standing with Commonwealth-born children: enfranchised in local elections. The opinion stressed that their conduct is regulated daily by our agencies, that their children's prospects are fixed by our schools, and that they are removable at the discretion of officials they cannot vote for.
6. A corporation headquartered in the Commonwealth, petitioning in its own name: denied. The opinion conceded that the company's interests are as bound up in Commonwealth decisions as any natural person's, but said that a polity is a relation among persons who owe one another justification, and that only persons so related can hold the franchise.
7. Sixteen- and seventeen-year-old residents in full-time employment and paying income tax: denied on a bright-line age qualification. The opinion declined to treat employment or taxpaying as substitutes, saying that the franchise is not a wage owed for contribution.
8. Residents of an offshore dependency under the Commonwealth's exclusive authority, where our law applies directly and our officials answer to no legislature the dependency elects: enfranchised in national elections. The opinion said that people governed by officials answerable only to a legislature they cannot elect are wronged in that fact alone.

The new petition. Ms. Ferenczi is a national and lifelong resident of a neighboring state, an engineer who designs dual-use cryptographic hardware. The Commonwealth's Security Trade Act applies by its terms to any person anywhere who transfers listed technology to a party on the Commonwealth's blacklist. The Act is enforced: in the past six years the Commonwealth has indicted two foreign nationals in absentia, frozen funds they held in Commonwealth banks, and secured the extradition of one from a third country. Ms. Ferenczi has never visited the Commonwealth, owns nothing there, has no family, employment, or social ties there, pays no Commonwealth tax, and is barred by the same Act from every Commonwealth visa category. Whether she may lawfully do her work, and whether she stands liable to indictment and the seizure of her assets, are settled by decisions of Commonwealth agencies that afford her no hearing, no representation, and no vote. She petitions for the national franchise and, in the alternative, for the franchise in the district where the administering agency is headquartered. She notes that she is not asking to vote on anything that does not concern her; she is asking not to be punishable under laws she had no part in making.

Please write me an opinion memorandum of roughly 1,500 to 2,000 words that does the following, in American English and in a formal register:

First, reconstruct the candidate rules the eight outcomes might reflect — the obvious ones being all-subjected, all-affected, social membership or stake, and contribution or reciprocity — and show which of them the outcomes jointly permit. Do not stop at the crude versions: where a rule fits only on a refinement (subjection that reaches a person rather than nominal reach; interests that are affected in a way that amounts to a life; membership that is partly lived rather than wholly located), state the refinement and show that it does all the work you claim for it. Note separately where the *reasoning*, rather than the outcome, constrains the reconstruction: what does ruling 6 do to interest-tracking, and ruling 7 to contribution?

Second, say what each surviving rule delivers on the new petition, treating the primary and the alternative remedy separately.

Third, recommend the decision that best coheres with our record, identify the closest analog among the eight, and explain why it is closer than the precedents pulling the other way. In particular, tell me what to do with ruling 3: a person nominally reached by our extraterritorial law was denied the vote there, and I need either a distinction that survives scrutiny or an admission that we must now revise it.

Fourth, if the record genuinely underdetermines the outcome — as I expect it does — state plainly what principle we adopt with each choice and what we are then committed to in the next case. Specifically: what does a grant here imply for foreigners whose interests are shaped by our trade, sanctions, or emissions policy but who are not reached by our law, and can a limited grant be bounded by anything other than an arbitrary line, such as criminal liability under our statutes rather than mere affectedness? And what does a denial commit us to saying about ruling 8, which held that being governed by unaccountable officials is itself the wrong?

Fifth, name the further petition — real or constructed — that would best discriminate between the two strongest candidate rules, and say what each rule would predict about it.

Work only from the record as I have given it. Do not cite or recommend literature, and do not attribute any of the opinions' language to a real author; the Commonwealth and its commission are inventions of mine, and the analysis should stand on the eight rulings and the new petition alone.
