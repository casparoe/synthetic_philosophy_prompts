I'm preparing a reading-group session on conditionals and probability, and the assigned background reading is a chapter from a recent edited volume on reasoning under uncertainty. Its summary of Lewis's triviality result runs as follows:

'Lewis's 1976 paper is usually taken as the death certificate for truth-conditional approaches to the indicative conditional. He starts from what is called Stalnaker's Thesis: the probability of "If A, then C" just is the conditional probability of C given A. Lewis shows that this equation is inconsistent with the ordinary probability calculus. If you treat the conditional as a proposition with a probability of its own, the calculus forces P(C|A) to equal P(C) — it forces your antecedent to be probabilistically irrelevant to your consequent — so any probability assignment satisfying the thesis is trivial. And because delivering the right probabilities is the only job we ever wanted a truth-conditional semantics to do, indicatives turn out to have no truth conditions at all. Adams was therefore right from the start: an indicative conditional is neither true nor false, and what governs it is not a probability of truth but a conditional probability of acceptability. Note also that the proof does no smuggling. It does not require the thesis to hold across all of an agent's possible revisions of her beliefs, and it does not turn on how one treats antecedents of probability zero, so there is no wiggle room for a truth-conditionalist.'
