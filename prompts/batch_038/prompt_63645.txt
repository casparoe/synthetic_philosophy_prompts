I'm throwing together a short teaching dialogue and could use a draft of it.

Two characters. The first holds the view Berkeley argues for in The Querist: money isn't wealth, it's a counter or token whose whole job is to set industry moving, and a country is rich to the extent its people can be got to work and to trade with each other, not to the extent its vaults hold gold and silver. He likes the idea of a national bank issuing paper. The second is a hard-money man: he thinks money has to be, or be convertible on demand into, some commodity that people want for non-monetary reasons anyway, because that's the only thing standing between a government and an unlimited note issue.

Please don't make either of them a fool. Put them both on a commitee in some invented 18th-century kingdom that's badly short of coin, and place in front of them a concrete proposal that neither Berkeley nor his real opponents ever actually faced: notes in a quantity fixed once and for all by statute, convertible into nothing, backed by no land or bullion, receivable only at the tax office and in payments the sovereign himself owes, with the sole anchor being the state's standing demand for them. Have them argue about wether that thing can hold up.

Stuff I want the exchange to actually work through:

- The famine case. Suppose a bad harvest cuts the grain supply by a third. Does the note still excite industry when there's nothing left to excite it toward, or does it just bid up the price of a fixed quantity of food while the hungry starve just the same? I want the Berkeleyan to give a real answer here, not a shrug and not a switch to a different topic.
- The sovereign's temptation. The hard-money man should press the point that a statutory quantity is not self-enforcing, that the first expensive war snaps it, and the Berkeleyan should come back with something better than an appeal to trusting the legislature.
- Then each of them should get one move the other hadn't prepared for. Something like the Berkeleyan turning the metallist's own reasoning around on him (what exactly is gold doing for the hungry in the famine year that paper couldn't do, given that gold's monetary value also depends on what it can be exchanged for), and the metallist finding something inside the Berkeleyan's own position that undercuts the proposal rather than merely disagreeing with it.

They shouldn't end up agreeing. Close with one of them restating the other's view in a way that makes clear what the live disagreement actually is — whether it's a dispute about what gives a token its value, or a dispute about who can safely be given the power to create one. Roughly 1100 words.

One more thing: don't have either character recite lines from The Querist or from any other period text as quotations. Let them make the arguments in their own words; I'd much rather have clean paraphrase than risk a garbled quote.
