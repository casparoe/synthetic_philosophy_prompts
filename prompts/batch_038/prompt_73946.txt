I am a software engineer volunteering on a civic-technology working group, and the proposal currently in front of us is to replace an appointed advisory board with a standing body of five hundred residents drawn at random from the voter roll, serving staggered two-year terms and paid for their time. The proposal's main advocate is a data scientist on the team, and his argument is statistical in form: random selection yields an unbiased sample, so a chamber of five hundred drawn from a city of about four hundred thousand registered voters should reproduce the city's demographic composition and opinion distribution to within a few percentage points, by the same margin-of-error arithmetic we use when sizing an A/B test. He notes that we already accept this logic without much controversy in two familiar settings — jury empanelment and opinion polling — and that bodies of this kind are apparently called mini-publics in the literature, which he takes as evidence that the sampling intuition is doing serious work for serious people and not just for him. On his account, resistance to sortition is therefore unease about randomization wearing a political-theory costume, and the burden of proof sits with anyone who wants to keep a hand-picked board.

I find the argument persuasive in a way I do not entirely trust, and I would like it audited rather than either accepted or waved off. Please treat the sampling analogy as a piece of reasoning and take it apart:

1. Give the correspondence explicitly, ideally as a table: what in the source domain — the sampling frame, the draw, the sample statistic, the confidence interval, the ground truth being estimated, the option to re-run the experiment — plays what role in the target domain of a sortition chamber.

2. Separate the disanalogies that actually bear on his conclusion from those that are merely rhetorical or aesthetic. I expect at least one of each, and I would rather you say plainly which of the objections people raise are not worth his time.

3. Name what the analogy quietly imports from the source domain: assumptions that are true of a well-designed A/B test or a professionally fielded poll and may not be true here. I can think of a defined sampling frame, unit nonresponse, independence between sampled units, a stable estimand, the ability to discard a bad draw, and error bars that mean something because there is a fact of the matter to be wrong about — but I want the full list, including the ones I have missed.

4. Address a gap I cannot close myself. The analogy, if it works, seems to establish something about composition, whereas the proposal is about decisions. Can accuracy in reproducing a distribution carry over into the quality or legitimacy of what the body produces, or has the inference swapped one target for another somewhere along the way? If it swapped, identify the exact step.

5. Tell me whether his conclusion survives at either strength. Narrow version: the drawn chamber will be demographically and attitudinally representative within a few points. Broad version: therefore it should be handed the board's authority. If the narrow version fails, say how large the deviation could plausibly be and what would drive it. If the broad version fails, state the additional premise it would need in order to go through.

6. If sampling is the wrong source domain, propose a better analogy for a sortition chamber, and stress-test your own proposal in the same way rather than presenting it as a fix.

7. Conclude with the cheapest empirical check our working group could realistically run before the next meeting — something that does not require the city to adopt the proposal first — together with the result that would falsify his position and the result that would falsify mine. If no such check exists, say so and explain why the disagreement is not empirical.
