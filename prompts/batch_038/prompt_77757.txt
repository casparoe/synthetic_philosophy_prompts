=== THE QUESTION ===

Question 4 (30 points). (a) State the Lucas–Penrose arguement against mechanism as carefully as you can. Identify each premise, and say what "machine" is being taken to mean. (8) (b) Several writers say the argument needs the machine to be *sound*, not merely *consistent*. Explain what turns on the difference: which premise of your reconstruction is affected, and why a proponent might think soundness rather than consistency is what they need. (8) (c) Now suppose every step in (a) is granted. Does the argument then refute the thesis that the human mind is a Turing machine? Argue for your answer, taking seriously at least one reply available to the mechanist. (14)

=== THE STUDENT'S ANSWER ===

(a) Take a candidate machine M. "Machine" here means a system whose mathematical output is generated by a fixed, effectively specifiable set of rules — something a Turing machine could implement — so that the set of sentences M derives forms a recursively axiomatized theory. Suppose M is offered as an account of one particular human mathematician: whatever she can establish, M can establish.

Construct M's Gödel sentence G(M) in the standard way: arithmetize M's proof relation, then diagonalize to obtain a sentence asserting of itself that M does not derive it. If M is consistent, M does not derive G(M), and G(M) is true. So:

1. M is consistent.
2. We can come to know (1), since we can survey M's rules.
3. From (1) and (2), we can see that G(M) is true and that M cannot derive it.
4. Hence there is an arithmetical truth we can establish that M cannot.
5. Hence M is not adequate to our reasoning.
6. Since M was arbitrary, no machine is.

It's worth noting that Gödel himself didn't argue this way; he framed a disjunction — either the human mind infinitely surpasses the powers of any finite machine, or there are absolutely undecidable problems in mathematics — and treated the disjunction as established without committing to either horn. Penrose takes the first horn and argues for it.

One thing the argument handles well is the objection that we contradict ourselves all the time in practice. Sloppiness in performance shows we're unreliable, not that the idealized system of what we'd accept on reflection is inconsistent, and it's the idealized system the argument is about.

Lucas's version asks less of the machine than Penrose's does. Lucas only needs M to be consistent and for us to know it; there's no soundness premise in his argument at all. Penrose's reconstruction, by contrast, needs the mathematician to be able to see that a whole class of argument-forms is sound.

(b) Soundness is strictly stronger than consistency — a consistent theory can still prove false sentences, as with a consistent but ω-inconsistent theory. But notice that the truth of G(M) only requires consistency: that's all the first theorem uses. So the soundness talk is doing less work than it appears to. What does the heavy lifting is the second incompleteness theorem: M cannot prove Con(M). We, on the other hand, are standing outside M with its code in front of us, and we can see that M is consistent — you inspect the axioms and the rules and there is simply no route from them to a contradiction. So the human can establish about M something M cannot establish about itself, and that is the gap the argument exploits. On this reading soundness is a convenience for Penrose rather than a requirement: consistency, plus our knowledge of it, is the whole requirement.

(c) Granting all of (a), I don't think the argument refutes mechanism as such, and the reason is what I'll call the identification gap. The argument is always run against a specified machine, and premise (2) needs us to be able to survey that machine's rules. The mechanist can therefore say: granted, for every machine you can point to, you can outrun it — but my claim is that you are some machine, and I never said you could point to it. The argument then shows only that we are not any machine we can identify, which is compatible with everything the mechanist actually cares about. Penrose's counter is that a machine which cannot be surveyed even in principle is not the kind of thing that could explain mathematical insight; but that just relocates the argument onto a claim about surveyability instead of answering the retreat.

Where I'd push further is a consequence people usually skip. Since we can recognize the truth of the Gödel sentence of any system we can fully survey, there is no arithmetical statement that lies permanently beyond a competent mathematician: incompleteness turns out to be a limit on formal systems, not on mathematicians. That's what makes the anti-mechanist result exciting — not merely that we beat machines, but that our grasp of arithmetic isn't hemmed in by any fixed set of rules whatever.
