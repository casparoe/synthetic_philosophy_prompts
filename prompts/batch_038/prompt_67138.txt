ok so I'm TAing a second-year social theory seminar — 22 students, one 100-minute session, week 2 of the Foucault unit — and my supervisor has let me build the class around the power/knowledge material. They've read the opening of Discipline and Punish (the execution, then the prison timetable a few pages later) plus the panopticism chapter, and honestly most of them skimmed. Their working mental model right now is basically "power = someone big tells you no and punishes you," and the thing I most want them to *feel* is that this gets Foucault exactly backwards: the regime that trains you, measures you, hands you a vocabulary for yourself and makes you genuinely better at the task is also the regime that governs you most thoroughly. Its not repressive, it's productive. I don't want to lecture them on that sentence, I want them to arrive at it looking slightly sick.

Design the activity for me. You're someone who has actually taught Foucault to undergrads and designs experiential seminars, so think hard about the mechanics rather than the theme. Before you write it up, brainstorm like three candidate mechanics in a few lines each and tell me why you're dropping two — I'd rather see that than just get handed a winner.

Hard constraints:

- 100 minutes, 22 students, seminar room with moveable chairs, index cards, markers, a whiteboard, and one photocopied handout. Phones are banned in this building, so no apps, no live polling.
- They must not hear "panopticon," "disciplinary power," "power/knowledge," or "governmentality" from me until the debrief. Those words go on the board at the end and not before.
- No real personal disclosure. Last year someone ran a confession-style exercise and a student said something true about herself and then felt awful for a week, so everything has to be in-character. Give them a fictional frame — a logistics firm, a certification body, a training scheme, whatever you like — so any self-examination they do is about a made-up person they're playing.
- It has to contain two contrasting regimes, becuase the point is comparative: one governing by prohibition and visible penalty, one governing by training, measurement and self-report. And the twist I'm chasing is that students should *prefer* the second one while they're inside it — find it fairer, more useful, more fun — and only afterwards work out what it did to them. If the activity lets them resent the second regime from minute one, it has failed.
- Something the students themselves produce in the room should turn into the thing that governs them: a rubric they write, categories they invent for sorting one another, a record they keep and read back. That's the knowledge half, and I don't want to just assert it at the end.

What I need back:

1. the fictional setup and the roles, in enough detail that I could hand it straight to them
2. rules and any scoring — keep it simple, no real money, nothing I have to mark afterwards
3. a minute-by-minute timeline for the 100 minutes, including exactly where the switch between regimes happens and what triggers it
4. materials list, plus what's actually printed on the handout
5. the debrief: specific questions in a specific order, each one anchored to a moment that genuinely happened in the room, and where I finally name the concepts
6. an honest list of what will go wrong the first time I run this and what I do in the moment. I've never facilitated anything like it and 22 twenty-year-olds with no phones is a lot of dead-air potential.
7. the part I care about most: tell me flat out which feature of Foucault's view this activity *cannot* reproduce and will actively mislead them about, and what I say to repair it. My worry is that any game like this makes discipline look like a scheme somebody designed on purpose, for a bounded group, who can leave at 4pm — which is pretty much the conspiracy model of power he's arguing against. So don't tell me the activity is faithful. Tell me where it lies to them.
