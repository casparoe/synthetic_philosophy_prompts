I am putting together a set of problems for a graduate seminar on Bayesian confirmation theory, and I need a thought experiment that probes the problem of old evidence in a configuration the canonical examples do not reach. The standard version of the problem bundles two features together: the evidence was known long before the hypothesis, and the hypothesis itself was newly devised. I want a case in which the second feature is stripped away, so that we can see whether any of the intuitive force of the problem remains.

Please construct a case satisfying all of the following:

1. The setting is mundane: an ordinary person reasoning about something in her own life — a workplace, a household, a small business, a clinic. No scientists, no laboratories, no theory choice, no exotic physics.
2. The hypothesis H is not new. The agent has entertained it for years, at a stable middling credence (say between 0.3 and 0.6), and what the hypothesis says does not change or get refined during the case.
3. The evidence E is not new either. She has known it for years and, by stipulation, assigns it credence exactly 1 before, during, and after whatever happens in the case.
4. She has long known that H entails E. The entailment relation is not a discovery in the story; nobody points it out to her for the first time.
5. No new empirical information arrives: nothing she sees, hears, measures, reads, or remembers for the first time.
6. Nevertheless there is an intuitive judgment, which the case should make vivid rather than merely assert, that E now confirms H — that her credence in H ought to rise.

The design difficulty is that conditions 4, 5, and 6 pull against one another: almost any trigger you reach for will look like a new proposition smuggled in under the label of old evidence. Finding a trigger that is not a disguised novelty is the point of the exercise. If you conclude that no such trigger exists, that is a legitimate result, and I would much rather have it stated plainly than have a case that cheats.

Once the scenario is written, please do the following. First, lay out the bookkeeping: the relevant propositions, the priors, and what update, if any, is applied. Show explicitly why strict conditionalization leaves P(H) untouched here, and whether Jeffrey conditionalization has any purchase at all — if it does, name the partition whose probabilities shift and say whether that shift is itself covert new information. Second, give the verdict of each of these three responses on your case: the erasure or abstraction strategy (drop E from the evidence base and conditionalize on what remains, in the manner Glymour proposes); the strategy that relaxes logical omniscience (add a new proposition to the effect that H entails, or explains, E, assign it credence below 1, and conditionalize on coming to accept it — condition 4 is aimed precisely at blocking this, so say whether it in fact does); and the deflationary reply that the entire difficulty is an artifact of idealizing a rational credence as exactly 1. Third, identify which single feature of your case is doing the work, and run the removal test on it: what do the verdicts become if that feature is taken away, and in particular, does putting the novelty of the hypothesis back collapse your case into the familiar astronomical one or produce something genuinely different? Fourth, supply a control case — same setting, same old evidence, same long-known entailment, same absence of new information — in which the intuitive verdict is that nothing is confirmed. Say what distinguishes it from the first case and whether that difference is one any of the three responses above can represent in its formalism. Finally, give the strongest attack on your case from a Bayesian who thinks there is no problem to solve, and state whether the attack succeeds.

For reference on what counts as cheating, since it fixes the standard the case has to meet: stipulating the rise in credence instead of generating it from the theories, letting a fresh fact enter under the heading of evidence already in hand, and allowing H to quietly change content so that the "old" hypothesis and the confirmed one are not the same proposition. If any of the six conditions turns out to be jointly unsatisfiable with the others, say so and show where the contradiction arises.
