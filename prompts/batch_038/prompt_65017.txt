I'm co-teaching a graduate seminar that sits between formal epistemology and the philosophy of economics, and the week on common priors reliably goes flat: students read Aumann's result on agreeing to disagree as idealized mathematics with no purchase on actual disagreement, and my attempts to convince them otherwise have mostly involved toy urn examples. This year I want to build the session around a real episode so that the students have to argue about whether the theory bites at all. The episode I've chosen is Goldman Sachs's ABACUS 2007-AC1, the synthetic CDO whose marketing materials presented the reference portfolio of subprime mortgage-backed securities as the selection of an independent agent, ACA Management, without disclosing that Paulson & Co. — which was taking the short side of the same portfolio — had a substantial role in shaping it. The principal long investor, the German bank IKB, took a near-total loss on its position; the SEC brought civil fraud charges in 2010, Goldman settled for what was then a record penalty (I recall five hundred and fifty million dollars, but the figure is irrelevant to what I want), and Fabrice Tourre, the trader who assembled the deal, was subsequently found liable.

What I'd like from you is a disciplined analysis of that episode through the common prior assumption, and specifically through the no-trade result that Milgrom and Stokey derived from it: with a common prior, common knowledge of rationality, and an initial allocation already Pareto optimal relative to those priors, voluntary speculative trade should be impossible, because each party reasons that the other would accept only on the basis of different beliefs, and that reasoning itself unravels the trade. So I want the mapping first, before any verdict. Who in this episode counts as the agents whose beliefs are at issue — Paulson, ACA, Goldman's structuring desk, IKB, the rating agencies, and in what combinations? Is there any defensible sense in which they shared a prior over the default behavior of the reference pool, or are the differences better described as differences in likelihood functions, in the weight given to a common body of evidence, or in priors over each other's incentives and honesty? What was private information here as opposed to public: the composition of the reference pool was in principle disclosed to the long investors, while the identity and direction of the party that helped select it was not, and I suspect that asymmetry is doing most of the work, but I want you to say whether it is Bayesian information about a common state of the world at all, or rather information about an agent's intentions. And then: which assumption actually fails, if any — the common prior, common knowledge of rationality, the Pareto-optimality precondition, which seems plainly inapplicable to a hedger and a manufactured instrument rather than to risk-averse traders rebalancing from an efficient endowment, or the requirement that trade be speculative rather than a deliberate transfer of risk that both sides want?

Then I want the analysis run in the other direction, from case back to theory. Does ABACUS tell us anything at all about whether the common prior assumption is true of real agents, or does it show only that the assumption is not the operative constraint in this setting? A recurring objection from my students is that the assumption is unfalsifiable: any observed disagreement can be absorbed by positing different priors, and any observed trade can be absorbed by denying one of the theorem's side conditions. I'd like you to say whether that objection is right, and whether any episode of market disagreement could in principle discriminate between the agents lacking a common prior and the agents sharing one but updating on it badly — under motivated reasoning, under pressure from internal incentive structures, or from non-partitional information. If you think the better test case lies elsewhere in the same crisis, say so and analyze that instead or alongside: for instance the divergence between institutions' internal mark-to-model valuations of subprime-linked positions and the continuously quoted prices on the ABX indexes, where the market's valuation was public and yet banks, insurers, and their auditors resisted updating for a long time, or the collateral-mark disputes involving AIG Financial Products and its counterparties. Tell me which of these sub-episodes genuinely puts pressure on the common prior assumption and which one merely dramatizes ordinary private-information trade, and be explicit about where the framework is silent rather than stretching it to speak.

Please begin with your bottom line in the first sentence — a single sentence stating what, if anything, this episode shows about the common prior assumption — and then give the reasoning: the mapping, the assumption-by-assumption diagnosis, and the verdict on which framework handles the case better. No preamble and no restatement of my question. Close by telling me what would change your mind: which argument, consideration, or correction to my account of the facts would push you to a different verdict, and where you think your own reasoning is most likely to fail — in particular, flag every step where you are leaning on your recollection of the ABACUS history rather than on the structure of the theorem, since I can check the history but not your inference.
